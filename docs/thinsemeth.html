III, 31 col. 285 CHAPTER 23. ¶

Probatur, תנשמת thinsemeth esse Noctuam.

It is proved that תנשמת thinsemeth is the little owl. ¶

Vox תנשמת thinsemeth Hebraeis est valde ambigua. Nam Levit. XI, 30. est col. 286 inter lacertorum species, ut suo loco dictum. At ejusdem capitis versu decimo octavo, in quo versamur, avis immundae genus est. Graeci πορφυρίω-να explicant, avem aquaticam in Lybia et Comagene Syriae, sic dictam a colore rostri et pedum: nam pennae sunt coeruleae (1) Hieronymus cygnum hic ha-bet. Ionathan, אותיא otja, id est, otum, ut quidem puto, avem, ab otide natura diversissimam, quamvis nomine vicinam. Otus enim nocturna est avis, de qua sic Philosophus libri octavi capite duodecimo; Ὁ δὲ ὠτὸς, ὅμοιος ταῖς γλαυξὶ, καὶ περὶ τὰ ὦτα πτερύγια ἔχων. ἔνιοι δὲ αὐτὸν νυκτικόρακα καλοῦ-σιν. ἔστι δὲ κόβαλος, καὶ μιμητὴς, καὶ ἀντορχούμενος ἁλίσκεται, περιελθόν-τος θατέρου τῶν θηρευτῶν, καθάπερ γλαύξ. Otus autem noctuis est similis, et circa aures habet pinnulas (unde illi nomen). Nycticoracem nonnulli vocant. Scurra autem et imitator est. Et contra saltantem unum aucupum vicissim saltans capitur ab altero circumeunte, ut noctua (2). Onkelos בותא bautha, vel bavetha, etiam avis nocturnae genus, quae Graece βύας dicitur, Latine bubo, et Arabice البوه albuho a cantu. Quamvis etiam apud Arabes parum constet, quid proprie sit البوه albuho. Damir, البوه طاير يشبه البوم الا انه اصغر منه والانثي بوهة ويشبه بها الرجل الاحمق Albuho avis est similis τῷ album, seu buboni, nisi quod minor est. Foemininum est albuha. Cum ea confertur, qui stupidus est. Quod postquam probavit exemplo, ex poëta quodam petito, addit, وقيل البوه ذكر البوم وقيل البوه اكبر من البوم Ab aliis albuho dicitur esse mas al-bum, et rursus ab aliis esse major album, id est, bubo. Quod verissimum. Omnino enim البوه albuho est Graecum βύας, id est, bubo, quae avis in nocturnis est maxima. Neque aliud Chaldaeum בותא, quod linguae more factum ex בוא: ut עותא, iniquitas, ex עוא: et כותא, fenestra, ex כוא.

The word תנשמת thinsemeth is highly ambiguous among the Hebrews. For in Lev 11:30 it is col. 286 among the species of lizards, as was said in its place. But in the eighteenth verse of the same chapter, with which we are dealing, it is a kind of unclean bird. The Greeks explain it as πορφυρίωνα [porphyriōna], a water bird in Libya and Commagene in Syria, so called from the colour of its beak and feet, for its feathers are blue R1. Jerome here has swan. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] has אותיא otja, that is, otus, as I at least think: a bird very different in nature from the bustard (otis), although close to it in name. For the otus is a nocturnal bird, about which the Philosopher writes thus in book 8, chapter 12: Ὁ δὲ ὠτὸς, ὅμοιος ταῖς γλαυξὶ, καὶ περὶ τὰ ὦτα πτερύγια ἔχων. ἔνιοι δὲ αὐτὸν νυκτικόρακα καλοῦσιν. ἔστι δὲ κόβαλος, καὶ μιμητὴς, καὶ ἀντορχούμενος ἁλίσκεται, περιελθόντος θατέρου τῶν θηρευτῶν, καθάπερ γλαύξ. [Ho de ōtos, homoios tais glauxi, kai peri ta ōta pterygia echōn. enioi de auton nyktikoraka kalousin. esti de kobalos, kai mimētēs, kai antorchoumenos halisketai, perielthontos thaterou tōn thēreutōn, kathaper glaux.] “The otus is similar to little owls, and has little feathers around its ears” (hence its name). “Some call it the night raven. It is a buffoon and a mimic. And while it dances in turn opposite one of the fowlers who is dancing, it is caught by the other, who goes round it, like the little owl” R2. Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] has בותא bautha, or bavetha, also a kind of nocturnal bird, which in Greek is called βύας [byas], in Latin bubo [eagle owl], and in Arabic البوه [al-būh] albuho, from its cry. Yet even among the Arabs it is hardly agreed what البوه [al-būh] albuho properly is. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: البوه طاير يشبه البوم الا انه اصغر منه والانثي بوهة ويشبه بها الرجل الاحمق [al-būh ṭāyir yushbih al-būm illā annahu aṣghar minhu wa-l-unthī būha wa-yushabbah bihā al-rajul al-aḥmaq]: “Albuho is a bird similar to album, that is, to the eagle owl, except that it is smaller. The feminine is albuha. A man who is stupid is compared to it.” After proving this by an example taken from a certain poet, he adds: وقيل البوه ذكر البوم وقيل البوه اكبر من البوم [wa-qīla al-būh dhakar al-būm wa-qīla al-būh akbar min al-būm]: “By some albuho is said to be the male of album, and again by others to be larger than album,” that is, the eagle owl. Which is most true. For البوه [al-būh] albuho is certainly the Greek βύας [byas], that is, bubo, the largest of the nocturnal birds. Nor is the Chaldee בותא anything else; it is formed, after the manner of the language, from בוא, as עותא, iniquity, from עוא, and כותא, window, from כוא. ¶

III, 32 It is now worth knowing why the Syriac renders the Hebrew תנשמת as ܟܘܟܒܝ [כוכבי] cocabi, which at first sight will seem to mean ἀστερίαν [asterian], starry, because ܟܘܟܒ [כוכב] cocab is ἄστρον [astron], star; and the name ἀστερίας [asterias] is common to many birds. For sometimes it is an epithet of the larger eagle, which others call chrysaëtos. Aelian, History of Animals, book 2, chapter 39, col. 287 Ἀκούω δέ τι καὶ γένος αἰετῶν, καὶ ὄνομα αὐτῶν χρυσαίετον ἔθεντο· ἄλλοι δὲ ἀςερίαν τὸν αὐτὸν καλοῦσιν [Akouō de ti kai genos aietōn, kai onoma autōn chrysaieton ethento; alloi de asterian ton auton kalousin]. So also Michael Apostolius, Century 2, Proverb 6. Sometimes it is a kind of hawk. Thus in Aristotle, History of Animals, book 9, chapter 36, among the species of hawks, after the buteo, the aesalon and the circus, the fourth is reckoned ὁ ἀςερίας [ho asterias]. It is also among the kinds of heron. Aristotle, book 9, chapter 11: Τῶν δ᾽ ἐρωδιῶν ἐςὶ τρία γένη· ὅτε πέλλος, καὶ ὁ λευκὸς, καὶ ὁ ἀςερίας καλούμενος [Tōn d’ erōdiōn esti tria genē; ho te pellos, kai ho leukos, kai ho asterias kaloumenos]. Hence Pliny, book 10, chapter 60: “Of little herons there are three kinds, the leucon, the asterias, the pellos.” Aelian too, History of Animals, book 5, chapter 36, which begins thus: Ὄνομά ἐςιν ὄρνιθος ἀςερίας [Onoma estin ornithos asterias], etc. There are therefore several birds of this name. Indeed, all birds that are painted and marked with spots like stars are called asteriae, or starry. Accordingly, since asterias is an epithet of many birds, it will be difficult to determine which one in particular the Syriac designates by the word ܟܘܟܒܝ [כוכבי] cocabi, if indeed it means starry. What of the fact that ܟܘܟܒܝ [כוכבי] Cocabi itself can also mean Cocabene, that is, born at Cocaba? There are several towns of this name in Syria and Arabia. One is in Basanitis, of which Epiphanius speaks in the Nazaraeans, writing that their sect flourished ἐν τῇ Βασανίτιδι τῇ λεγομένῃ Κωκάβῃ, Κωχάβῃ δὲ Ἑβραϊςί [en tē Basanitidi tē legomenē Kōkabē, Kōchabē de Hebraisti]. And in the Ebionites: Ἄρχεται μὲν τὴν κατοίκησιν ἔχειν ἐν Κωκάβῃ τινὶ κώμῃ ἐπὶ τὰ μέρη τῆς Καρναΐμ, Ἀρνὲμ, καὶ Ἀςαρὼθ ἐν τῇ Βασανίτιδι χώρᾳ [Archetai men tēn katoikēsin echein en Kōkabē tini kōmē epi ta merē tēs Karnaim, Arnem, kai Astarōth en tē Basanitidi chōra]. And below: Ἀπὸ Μωαβίτιδός τε, καὶ Κωχάβων τῆς ἐν τῇ Βασανίτιδι, ἐπέκεινα Ἀδραῶν [Apo Mōabitidos te, kai Kōchabōn tēs en tē Basanitidi, epekeina Adraōn]. And in the Archontics, of Eutactus, the founder of the sect: Κατῴκησεν ἐν τῇ Ἀραβίᾳ ἐν Κωχάβῃ, ἔνθα αἱ τῶν Ἐβιωναίων τε καὶ Ναζωραίων ῥίζαι ἐνήρξαντο [Katōkēsen en tē Arabia en Kōchabē, entha hai tōn Ebiōnaiōn te kai Nazōraiōn rhizai enērxanto]. There are other Cochabeni, or Cauchabeni, in Arabia Deserta, of whom Ptolemy speaks, book 5, chapter 19: Κατέχουσι δὲ τῆς ἐρήμου ταύτης Ἀραβίας, τὰ μὲν παρὰ τὸν Εὐφράτην ποταμὸν Καυχαβηνοί [Katechousi de tēs erēmou tautēs Arabias, ta men para ton Euphratēn potamon Kauchabēnoi]. And a little after: Ὑπὸ μὲν τοῖς Καυχαβηνοῖς Αἰσῖται [Hypo men tois Kauchabēnois Aisitai]. There is also in the valley of Damascus the town كوكبا [Kawkabā] Caucaba, which the Nubian Geographer mentions, Clim. [=climate (section) of al-Idrīsī's geography, in the Latin Geographia Nubiensis] 3, section 5. And in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] كوكب [Kawkab] Caucab is a fortress of Tiberias, and كوكبان [Kawkabān] Caucaban one of Arabia Iemamitica. Some bird originating from such a place could therefore have been called כוכבי cocabi, or caucabi. Yet we conjecture something else, namely that כוכבי cocabi is the little owl, and that the Syriac translator here agrees with the Chaldees, for whom תנשמת thinsemeth is אותיא, otus, or בותא, eagle owl, birds very like the little owl. This III, 33 conjecture of ours rests on the following reasons. Little owls are said to cucubare [hoot]. The author of the Philomela: ¶

Noctua lucifuga cucubat in tenebris.

“The light-shunning little owl hoots in the darkness.” ¶

Hence it is very likely that the Syriac ܟܘܟܒܝ [כוכבי] cocabi here stands for the little owl R3. But I think that the Samaritan ࠒࠐࠅࠐࠄ [קפופה] kaphupha also belongs here, as does the Chaldee קפופא kephupha. So the two Chaldees, the Syriac and the Samaritan understand nearly the same thing by the Hebrew word תנשמת thinsemeth. ¶

But in that language it can happen that the same word has two origins, one with respect to its form, the other with respect to its meaning. And you will find not a few words which take their form from the defective Pe-Nun verbs, such as נשם nasam, but their meaning from the verbs doubling the second radical, such as שמם samam. Thus נחילות nechiloth, a kind of pipe, Ps 5:1, has its form from the verb נחל nachal, to possess, but its meaning from the verb חלל chalal, to pierce, whence also חליל chalil is a pipe, Isa 5:12, and חללים chalalim, that is, pipes, Jer 48:36. Petrus Crescentiensis libri decimi De plantis et animalibus capite decimo sexto, quod est De gimo et gimetta, id est, noctua mare et foemina: Admirantur eas reliquae aves et videre cupiunt, ceu rem novam ac insolitam. III, 38 The evening-loving little owl is of a wily nature. It is worth observing her tricks. Mocking the birds the whole day, and displaying herself to be looked at in various forms, she fawns upon the smaller winged creatures. Palladius, book 10, chapter 12: Now (in the month of September) the fowling with the little owl is prepared in the snares, together with the other instruments of capture, so that it may be practised around the Calends of October. Petrus Crescentiensis, On Plants and Animals, book 10, chapter 16, which is On the gimo and the gimetta, that is, the male and female little owl: The other birds marvel at them and long to see them, as something new and unusual. For this reason men, seeing the other birds flying around the gimo and gimetta and gazing at them with the greatest eagerness, devised a new method of fowling: namely, by setting out these birds as a spectacle, to catch for themselves the others thus lured, with birdlime or nets. Rightly, then, is the little owl called תנשמת thinsemeth, as being astounding and wonderful, that is, to those little birds which, struck with stupor, it draws to itself. Accordingly not only the Hebrews, but among our own scholars Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Schindler etc. render it "little owl". And the two Chaldee versions, the Syriac and the Samaritan follow the same. Someone was mocking the little owl. She, however, parried each of the jibes with clever speech. Obj. What a head! Resp. But what sort of head does Jupiter have? Obj. And the grey-blue colour? Resp. This is proper to Minerva, the goddess with grey-blue eyes. Obj. You sing unpleasantly. Resp. The magpie sings even more unpleasantly. Obj. How (supply will it be excused) the thinness of your foot?
