III, 154 col. 379 CAP. IV.

III, 154 col. 379 Chapter 4. ¶

Probatur, עכשוב achsub esse Aspidem.

It is proved that עכשוב achsub is the asp. ¶

Quaeritur, quid sit עכשוב achsub, in his Psal. CXL, 4. חמת עכשוב תחת שפתימו, Venenum achsub sub labiis eorum. Chaldaeus exponit, אורסא דעכוביתא, venenum araneae, quasi עכשוב κατ᾽ ἀναγραμματισμὸν sit idem, quod עכביש. Arabs سم الافاعي, venenum viperarum. Alii venenum aspidis: quibus assentior potius, quia, praeter Graecos interpretes, et Latinum, et Syrum, et Apollinarem, et Hieronymum, et ex Hebraeis Kimchium, Mardochaeum, et Pomarium, insuper Paulum, Apostolum, suffragatorem habent, qui Psaltis verba sic effert Roman. III, 13. Ἰὸς ἀσπίδων ὑπὸ τὰ χείλη αὐτῶν. Videri possit עכשוב achsub alludere ad vocem עכס eches, quam alii serpentis venenum, alii viperae speciem explicant. Prius his verbis asserit Rabbi Selomo in Esa. III, 16. עכס הוא ארס של נחש, Eches est venenum serpentis. Posterius auctor Mechiltae, ex quo Aquinas, עכס הוא מין אפעה שכשרואה העוף פורח באויר יתחבר אל צלו ואיבריו נושרין וזהו צלמות צל ועמו מות, Eches est viperae species, quae, cum avem volantem videt, consociatur umbrae ejus, et pennae ejus defluunt, et haec est umbra mortis, seu umbra mortifera. Sed uterque fallitur. Nam עכס eches nihil quidquam est, quam Graecum ἔχις, id est, vipera mas. Neque illam vocem Graeci ex Hebraeis, sed Hebraei recentiores ex Graecis mutuati sunt. Itaque nugatur, quisquis עכשוב achsub, vocem vere Hebraeam, conatur ad hanc revocare.

The question is what עכשוב achsub is in these words of Psal. CXL, 4: חמת עכשוב תחת שפתימו, The poison of achsub is under their lips. The Chaldee renders it אורסא דעכוביתא, the poison of the spider, as though עכשוב were, κατ᾽ ἀναγραμματισμὸν [kat᾽ anagrammatismon], the same as עכביש. The Arabic translator has سم الافاعي [samm al-afāʿī], the poison of vipers. Others have the poison of the asp; I rather agree with these, because, besides the Greek translators, the Latin, the Syriac, Apollinaris and Jerome, and among the Hebrews Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], Mardochaeus and Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], they also have Paul the Apostle on their side, who renders the words of the Psalmist thus in Roman. III, 13.: Ἰὸς ἀσπίδων ὑπὸ τὰ χείλη αὐτῶν [Ios aspidōn hypo ta cheilē autōn]. It might seem that עכשוב achsub alludes to the word עכס eches, which some explain as the poison of a serpent, others as a species of viper. The former is asserted by R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Isa 3:16 in these words: עכס הוא ארס של נחש, Eches is the poison of a serpent. The latter by the author of the Mechilta, and from him Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: עכס הוא מין אפעה שכשרואה העוף פורח באויר יתחבר אל צלו ואיבריו נושרין וזהו צלמות צל ועמו מות, Eches is a species of viper which, when it sees a bird flying, joins itself to its shadow, and its feathers fall off; and this is the shadow of death, or the deadly shadow. But both are mistaken. For עכס eches is nothing other than the Greek ἔχις [echis], that is, the male viper. Nor did the Greeks borrow that word from the Hebrews, but the later Hebrews from the Greeks. Therefore whoever tries to trace עכשוב achsub, a genuinely Hebrew word, back to it is trifling. ¶

Sed verum ejus etymum mihi videor ex Arabica lingua deprehendere, qua عكس achasa est retorquere, et reflectere rem ad principium suum, sive id fiat recta linea, ut in repercussu solis, qui dicitur انعكاس in Avicenna a), sive per circulos et gyros, ut cum, capite ad caudam obverso, serpens in spiram se colligit. Hinc Alcamus, تعكس في مشيته مشي مشيا الافعي Incessit incessu viperae, id est, in gyrum se convertendo. Forte Hebraeis idem fuit עכש, unde עכשוב, litera Beth in fine adscita. Quomodo ex כרך, ambire, factum כרכוב, ambitus, Exod. XXVII, 5. Et a Chaldaeo תעל, Arabicum ثعلب, vulpes. Et ערבוב, mistio, a Verbo ערב, miscere. Et זרבוב, fusorium, a Verbo זרב, fluere. Et si quae alia sunt hujusmodi. Aspi- col. 380 dis certe naturae plane convenit hoc nomen; cum, Nicandro teste b), soleat κατ᾽ ἐναντίον ἕρπειν, in contrarium serpere, id est, بالعكس c). Ut taceam, quod idem Nicander pugnantem ita describit versu 166.

But I think I detect its true etymology from the Arabic language, in which عكس achasa means to turn back and to bend a thing back to its starting point, whether this happens in a straight line, as in the reflection of the sun, which in Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] is called انعكاس [inʿikās] a), or by circles and coils, as when a serpent, its head turned toward its tail, gathers itself into a coil. Hence Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: تعكس في مشيته مشي مشيا الافعي [taʿakkasa fī mashyatihi mashā mashyan al-afʿā] He walked with the gait of a viper, that is, turning himself in a circle. Perhaps among the Hebrews עכש was the same, whence עכשוב, with the letter Beth added at the end. Just as from כרך, to go around, comes כרכוב, circuit, Exod 27:5. And from the Chaldee תעל the Arabic ثعلب [thaʿlab], fox. And ערבוב, mixture, from the verb ערב, to mix. And זרבוב, a casting-vessel, from the verb זרב, to flow. And any others of this kind. This name certainly fits the nature of the asp col. 380 perfectly, since, as Nicander testifies b), it is wont κατ᾽ ἐναντίον ἕρπειν [kat᾽ enantion herpein], to crawl in the contrary direction, that is, بالعكس [bi-l-ʿaks] c). Not to mention that the same Nicander describes it fighting thus, in line 166: ¶

III, 155 Ὁλκῷ δὲ τροχόωσαν ἅλων᾽ εἰλίξατο γαίῃ. Σμερδαλέον δ᾽ ἀνὰ μέσσα κάρη πεφρικὸς ἀείρει· Describit tereti sinuosa volumina tractu, Horrendumque caput medio sic tollit ab orbe.

III, 155 Ὁλκῷ δὲ τροχόωσαν ἅλων᾽ εἰλίξατο γαίῃ. Σμερδαλέον δ᾽ ἀνὰ μέσσα κάρη πεφρικὸς ἀείρει· [Holkō de trochoōsan halōn᾽ eilixato gaiē. Smerdaleon d᾽ ana messa karē pephrikos aeirei] It traces winding coils with rounded trail, and thus raises its dreadful head from the middle of the circle. ¶

Ita etiam Etymologus, Συστρέψαν ἑαυτὸ πολεμεῖ, καὶ μάχεται· Contorquens se ipsum bellum gerit, et pugnat. Συστρέφειν ἑαυτὸ Arabice est تعكس. Sic Oppiani Cynegeticῶn lib. 3. versu 446. ichneumon aspidem dilacerat

So too the Etymologicum: Συστρέψαν ἑαυτὸ πολεμεῖ, καὶ μάχεται [Systrepsan heauto polemei, kai machetai]; Twisting itself together, it wages war and fights. Συστρέφειν ἑαυτὸ [Systrephein heauto] in Arabic is تعكس [taʿakkasa]. Thus in Oppian's Cynegetica book 3, line 446, the ichneumon tears apart the asp ¶

— — — ἑλισσομένην ἑκάτερθε,

— — — ἑλισσομένην ἑκάτερθε, [helissomenēn hekaterthe] ¶

hinc inde convolutam. Et in Phylarcho, quem citat Aelianus lib. 17. cap. 5. mansuetae aspides τὴν μὲν λοιπὴν σπεῖραν ἐῶσι κατὰ τοῦ δαπέδου, ἀρᾶσαι δὲ τὴν κε-φαλὴν περιλιχμῶνται, reliquam quidem spiram humi relinquunt, capite autem sublato circumlingunt. Idem animal εἰς κύκλους πολλοὺς ἑλισσόμενον καθεύδειν, in plures circulos convolutum dormire, asserit Aristophanis Scholiastes in Vespis. Hi circuli Graece dicuntur Οἶμοι. Hesychius, Οἶμον, οἶμος· ὁδὸς, κύκλος, τρίβος. ὅθεν καὶ τῆς ἀσπίδος κύκλους οἴμους ἐκάλεσαν. Arabice انعكاس recte dixeris. Proinde Hebraeum aspidis nomen עכשוב videtur inde deduci, qua ratione diximus. Neque alia est ratio Graeci nominis. Ἀσπὶς enim tam pro clypeo, quam pro serpente sumitur. Rotundos autem fuisse Graecorum clypeos, vel ex Poeta disce, a quo Polyphemi oculus orbicularis describitur d),

coiled on this side and that. And in Phylarchus, whom Aelian cites in book 17, ch. 5, tame asps τὴν μὲν λοιπὴν σπεῖραν ἐῶσι κατὰ τοῦ δαπέδου, ἀρᾶσαι δὲ τὴν κεφαλὴν περιλιχμῶνται [tēn men loipēn speiran eōsi kata tou dapedou, arasai de tēn kephalēn perilichmōntai], leave the rest of the coil on the ground, but with head raised they lick round about. That the same animal εἰς κύκλους πολλοὺς ἑλισσόμενον καθεύδειν [eis kyklous pollous helissomenon katheudein], sleeps coiled into many circles, is asserted by the Scholiast of Aristophanes on the Wasps. These circles are called Οἶμοι [Oimoi] in Greek. Hesychius: Οἶμον, οἶμος· ὁδὸς, κύκλος, τρίβος. ὅθεν καὶ τῆς ἀσπίδος κύκλους οἴμους ἐκάλεσαν [Oimon, oimos; hodos, kyklos, tribos. hothen kai tēs aspidos kyklous oimous ekalesan]. In Arabic you would rightly say انعكاس [inʿikās]. Accordingly the Hebrew name of the asp, עכשוב, seems to be derived from this, in the way we have said. Nor is the reason for the Greek name different. For Ἀσπὶς [Aspis] is used both for a shield and for a serpent. That the shields of the Greeks were round you may learn even from the Poet, by whom the round eye of Polyphemus is described d): ¶

Argolici clypei aut Phoebeae lampadis instar.

Like an Argive shield or the Phoebean lamp. ¶

Hinc in Homero passim e) ἀσπίδες εὔκυκλοι, et ἀσπίδες ὀμφαλόεσσαι, ubi Scholion, στρογγύλαι, περιφερεῖς. Et Diodoro f), τὸν τῆς ἀσπίδος κύκλον μιμη-σάμενος, clypei circulum imitatus. An clypei nomen ἀσπὶς a serpente sit ductum, ut placet Scholiasti Aristophanis, aut serpentis a clypei forma, quam spiris suis videtur exprimere, ut vult Etymologus, nobis disquirere non libet; quia ad rem nostram non facit *).

Hence in Homer everywhere e) ἀσπίδες εὔκυκλοι [aspides eukykloi] and ἀσπίδες ὀμφαλόεσσαι [aspides omphaloessai], where the Scholion has στρογγύλαι, περιφερεῖς [strongylai, periphereis]. And in Diodorus f), τὸν τῆς ἀσπίδος κύκλον μιμησάμενος [ton tēs aspidos kyklon mimēsamenos], imitating the circle of the shield. Whether the name ἀσπὶς [aspis] for the shield was derived from the serpent, as the Scholiast of Aristophanes holds, or the serpent's name from the shape of the shield, which it seems to reproduce with its coils, as the Etymologicum would have it, we do not care to investigate, since it is not relevant to our subject *). ¶

Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791) and Clavis über den Jesaias (1793)], in his Clavis in Psalmos, p. 282, thinks that the word עכשוב, a ἅπαξ λεγομενον [hapax legomenon], owes its origin to a faulty division of words: instead of what we now have, חמת עכשוב תחת שפתימו, we should read חמת עכשו בתחת שפתימו, they gather wrath (compare Arabic عكش [ʿakasha], to gather) under their lips. This conjecture seems to me to be rejected chiefly for this reason: in the first part of the verse there had preceded שננו לשונם כמו נחש, they sharpen their teeth like serpents, so that the parallelism of members requires in the other part of the verse another name of a serpent; and that this is עכשוב itself Bochart, I think, sufficiently shows in this chapter.
