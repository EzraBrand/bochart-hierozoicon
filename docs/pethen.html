III, 156 col. 380 Chapter 5. ¶

Probatur, פתן pethen esse Aspidem.

It is proved that פתן pethen is the asp. ¶

Aliud est aspidis nomen פתן pethen. Ita enim hanc vocem plerique reddunt. Et veram esse hanc versionem, suadent aliquot loca de veneno פתן pethen *). col. 381 Sic Iob. XX, 14. מרורת פתנים בקרבו. Ubi Seniores, Χολὴ ἀσπίδος ἐν γαστρὶ αὐτοῦ· et Vulgatus, Fel aspidum intrinsecus. Et paullo post versu decimo sexto, ראש פתנים יינק. Ubi Seniores, Θυμὸν δὲ δρακόντων θηλάσειεν. Sed Graeci interpretes alii, Χολὴν ἀσπίδων μυζήσει· et Hieronymus, Caput aspidum suget. Rursus Deut. XXXII, 33. וראש פתנים אכזר, Et venenum pethanim crudele. Pro quo Graece, Καὶ θυμὸς ἀσπίδων ἀνίατος· unde Vulgatus, Et venenum aspidum insanabile. Ac si pro אכזר legerint אין זר, non est curatio. Nam זר est curare vulnus, Esa. I, 6. Et מזור, vulneris curatio, Ier. XXX, 13. Nempe venenum aspidis est omnium acutissimum, et vitalia celerrime penetrat. Aeliani verba sunt lib. 9. cap. 61. Ὀξύτατόν ἐστι τὸ ἐξ αὐτῆς φάρμακον, καὶ διαδραμεῖν ὤκιστον. Unde vulgatum adagium in Apostolio et Erasmo, Δῆγμα ἀσπίδος, Morsus aspidis, pro immedicabili vulnere; quia, qui ab aspide demorsus est, raro sanatur. Quod testatum faciunt scriptores, non contemnendi. Aristoteles lib. 8. Historiae cap. 29. Τὰ δὲ τῶν ὄφεων δήγματα πολὺ διαφέρουσιν. ἥτε γὰρ ἀσπὶς ἐν Λιβύῃ γίνεται, ἐξ οὗ ὄφεως ποιοῦσι τὸ σηπτικόν· καὶ ἄλλως ἀνιάτως. Serpentum morsus multum differunt. Aspis enim in Africa nascitur, III, 157 ex qua faciunt unguentum putrefactivum: et alioqui immedicabiliter, supple mordet. Plinius lib. 8. cap. 23. Colla aspidum intumescere (vulgatum est) nullo ictus remedio, praeterquam si confestim partes contactae amputentur. Aelianus lib. 1. cap. 54. Ἀσπίδος δὲ ἀκούω μόνον δῆγμα ἀνίατον εἶναι, καὶ ἐπικουρίας κρεῖττον· Aspidis solum morsum immedicabilem esse audio, et omni remedio potentiorem. Et lib. 6. cap. 38. Τῶν ὑπ’ ἀσπίδος δηχθέντων οὐ μνημονεύεται οὐδεὶς ἐξάντης γεγο-νέναι τοῦ κακοῦ. Ἔνθεν τοι καὶ τοὺς βασιλεῖς ἀκούω τῶν Αἰγυπτίων ἐπὶ τῶν δια-δημάτων φορεῖν πεποικιλμένας ἀσπίδας, τῆς ἀρχῆς αἰνιττομένους τὸ ἀνίκητον. Eorum, qui ab aspide morsi sunt, nemo a malo (id est, a morte) traditur fuisse immunis. Unde est, quod Aegyptiorum reges in diadematibus pictas aspides ferre audio, ut invictum imperii robur sic significent. Et infra, Οἱ δηχθέντες ὑπ’ ἀσπίδων οὐ περαιτέρω βιοῦσι τετάρτης ὥρας· Qui ab aspidibus morsi sunt, ultra quartam horam non vivunt. Phile cap. 59.

Another name of the asp is פתן pethen. For so most render this word. And that this rendering is correct is suggested by several passages on the poison of the פתן pethen *). col. 381 Thus Job XX, 14. מרורת פתנים בקרבו. Here the Elders have Χολὴ ἀσπίδος ἐν γαστρὶ αὐτοῦ [cholē aspidos en gastri autou], and the Vulgate translator The gall of asps within. And a little later, in verse sixteen, ראש פתנים יינק. Here the Elders have Θυμὸν δὲ δρακόντων θηλάσειεν [thymon de drakontōn thēlaseien]. But other Greek translators have Χολὴν ἀσπίδων μυζήσει [cholēn aspidōn myzēsei], and Jerome He shall suck the head of asps. Again Deut 32:33. וראש פתנים אכזר, And the cruel poison of pethanim. For this the Greek has Καὶ θυμὸς ἀσπίδων ἀνίατος [kai thymos aspidōn aniatos]; whence the Vulgate translator has And the incurable poison of asps. As if for אכזר they had read אין זר, there is no cure. For זר means to treat a wound, Isa 1:6, and מזור the treatment of a wound, Ier. XXX, 13. The asp's poison is, of course, the sharpest of all, and penetrates the vital organs very swiftly. Aelian's words, book 9, ch. 61, are: Ὀξύτατόν ἐστι τὸ ἐξ αὐτῆς φάρμακον, καὶ διαδραμεῖν ὤκιστον [oxytaton esti to ex autēs pharmakon, kai diadramein ōkiston]. Hence the common proverb in Apostolius and Erasmus [=Desiderius Erasmus, Adagia], Δῆγμα ἀσπίδος [dēgma aspidos], The bite of an asp, for an incurable wound; because one who has been bitten by an asp is rarely healed. This is attested by writers not to be despised. Aristotle, book 8 of the History, ch. 29: Τὰ δὲ τῶν ὄφεων δήγματα πολὺ διαφέρουσιν. ἥτε γὰρ ἀσπὶς ἐν Λιβύῃ γίνεται, ἐξ οὗ ὄφεως ποιοῦσι τὸ σηπτικόν· καὶ ἄλλως ἀνιάτως [ta de tōn opheōn dēgmata poly diapherousin. hēte gar aspis en Libyē ginetai, ex hou opheōs poiousi to sēptikon; kai allōs aniatōs]. The bites of serpents differ greatly. For the asp is born in Africa, III, 157 from which they make a putrefying ointment; and besides, incurably—supply it bites. Pliny, book 8, ch. 23: That the necks of asps swell (it is commonly known), and there is no remedy for their stroke, except if the parts touched are immediately amputated. Aelian, book 1, ch. 54: Ἀσπίδος δὲ ἀκούω μόνον δῆγμα ἀνίατον εἶναι, καὶ ἐπικουρίας κρεῖττον [aspidos de akouō monon dēgma aniaton einai, kai epikourias kreitton]; I hear that the bite of the asp alone is incurable and stronger than every remedy. And book 6, ch. 38: Τῶν ὑπ’ ἀσπίδος δηχθέντων οὐ μνημονεύεται οὐδεὶς ἐξάντης γεγονέναι τοῦ κακοῦ. Ἔνθεν τοι καὶ τοὺς βασιλεῖς ἀκούω τῶν Αἰγυπτίων ἐπὶ τῶν διαδημάτων φορεῖν πεποικιλμένας ἀσπίδας, τῆς ἀρχῆς αἰνιττομένους τὸ ἀνίκητον [tōn hyp’ aspidos dēchthentōn ou mnēmoneuetai oudeis exantēs gegonenai tou kakou. enthen toi kai tous basileis akouō tōn Aigyptiōn epi tōn diadēmatōn phorein pepoikilmenas aspidas, tēs archēs ainittomenous to anikēton]. Of those bitten by an asp, none is recorded to have escaped the evil (that is, death). This is why, I hear, the kings of Egypt wear asps depicted on their diadems, to signify thereby the invincible strength of their rule. And further below: Οἱ δηχθέντες ὑπ’ ἀσπίδων οὐ περαιτέρω βιοῦσι τετάρτης ὥρας [hoi dēchthentes hyp’ aspidōn ou peraiterō biousi tetartēs hōras]; Those bitten by asps do not live beyond the fourth hour. Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)], ch. 59: ¶

Τῆς ἀσπίδος τὸ δῆγμα δῆγμ’ ἔστι θανάτου. Μὴ, μὴ, Γαληνὲ, μηδὲ φάρμακον χέῃς. Repente mortem morsus infert aspidis. Ne, ne, Galene, pharmacum ne infuderis.

Τῆς ἀσπίδος τὸ δῆγμα δῆγμ’ ἔστι θανάτου. Μὴ, μὴ, Γαληνὲ, μηδὲ φάρμακον χέῃς. [tēs aspidos to dēgma dēgm’ esti thanatou. mē, mē, Galēne, mēde pharmakon cheēs.] The bite of the asp brings sudden death. Do not, do not, Galen, pour in a drug. ¶

Quem frustra sibi objicit, cum id abunde confirmetur ipsius Galeni suffragio lib. 1. De Theriaca capite 8. ubi de aspidibus haec habet: Πολλάκις ἐθεασάμην ἐγὼ ἐν τῇ μεγάλῃ Ἀλεξανδρείᾳ τὸ τάχος τοῦ ὑπ’ αὐτοῦ γιγνομένου θανάτου. ὅταν γάρ τινα, τούτῳ τῷ νόμῳ τῆς κολάσεως κατακριθέντα, φιλανθρώπως καὶ ταχέως ἀποκτεῖναι θέλωσι, προσβάλλοντες αὐτοῦ τοῖς στέρνοις τὸ θηρίον, καὶ ποιήσαντες ὀλίγον περιπατῆσαι, οὕτω ταχέως ἀναιροῦσι τὸν ἄνθρωπον. In magna Alexandriae urbe saepe vidi, quam cito mortem inferant. Quoties enim aliquem, hujus supplicii lege damnatum, humaniter citoque interimere volunt, ad-motam pectori bestiam aliquantulum inambulare jubent, et hominem hac ratione stastim enecant. Proinde aspides calcare maxime periculosum est. Et Psaltes, ut virum bonum etiam inter hostes infensissimos a Deo servari significet, illum aspides et dracones ait impune calcaturum, Psal. XCI, 13. Quale est, quod apud veteres Onirocriticos in Suida,

He addresses Galen in vain, since this is amply confirmed by the testimony of Galen himself, book 1 On Theriac, chapter 8, where he says this of asps: Πολλάκις ἐθεασάμην ἐγὼ ἐν τῇ μεγάλῃ Ἀλεξανδρείᾳ τὸ τάχος τοῦ ὑπ’ αὐτοῦ γιγνομένου θανάτου. ὅταν γάρ τινα, τούτῳ τῷ νόμῳ τῆς κολάσεως κατακριθέντα, φιλανθρώπως καὶ ταχέως ἀποκτεῖναι θέλωσι, προσβάλλοντες αὐτοῦ τοῖς στέρνοις τὸ θηρίον, καὶ ποιήσαντες ὀλίγον περιπατῆσαι, οὕτω ταχέως ἀναιροῦσι τὸν ἄνθρωπον [pollakis etheasamēn egō en tē megalē Alexandreia to tachos tou hyp’ autou gignomenou thanatou. hotan gar tina, toutō tō nomō tēs kolaseōs katakrithenta, philanthrōpōs kai tacheōs apokteinai thelōsi, prosballontes autou tois sternois to thērion, kai poiēsantes oligon peripatēsai, houtō tacheōs anairousi ton anthrōpon]. In the great city of Alexandria I often saw how swiftly they bring death. For whenever they wish to kill humanely and quickly someone condemned under the law of this punishment, they put the beast to his breast and make it walk about a little, and in this way they kill the man at once. Therefore treading on asps is extremely dangerous. And the Psalmist, to signify that a good man is preserved by God even among the most bitter enemies, says that he will tread on asps and dragons unharmed, Ps 91:13. Similar is the saying of the ancient interpreters of dreams in the Suda: ¶

Ὄφεις πατεῖν, τὰ κέντρα τῶν ἐχθρῶν λύει· Qui calcat angues, vitat insultum hostium.

Ὄφεις πατεῖν, τὰ κέντρα τῶν ἐχθρῶν λύει· [opheis patein, ta kentra tōn echthrōn lyei] He who treads on snakes escapes the assault of enemies. ¶

Neque ullus est, qui ad aspidis aut basilisci foramen ultro accedat. Aut, si quisquam ita insaniat, is magnum adit vitae discrimen. Hinc, ut doceat Esaias, magnam fore sub Christi regno rerum conversionem, Tum, inquit, ludet infans על חור פתן, super foramen aspidis: et super cavernam basilisci puer, a lacte col. 382 depulsus, manum injiciet, Es. XI, 8. Id est, Gentes cum Iudaeis in unam ecclesiam III, 158 convenient: et tutus patebit accessus ad eos, qui fidelibus olim erant tam terribiles, quam sunt pueris anguium maxime venenati et pestiferi, aspides puta et basilisci. Quamvis etiam ad literam hoc aliquando fuisse impletum, his verbis narret Sulpicius Dialogi primi cap. 5. Panem Abbas per duos pueros miserat, quorum major habebat annos aetatis quindecim, minor duodennis erat. His ergo, inde redeuntibus, aspis mirae magnitudinis fit obviam; cujus occursu nihil perterriti, ubi ante pedes eorum venit, quasi incantata carminibus, coerula colla deposuit. Minor e pueris manu adprehensam, ac pallio involutam, ferre coepit. Deinde monasterium quasi victor ingressus, in occursum fratrum, inspectantibus cunctis, captivam bestiam, resoluto pallio, non sine jactantiae tumore, deposuit. Sed et jam olim in Aegypto mansuetas aspides cum pueris innoxie vixisse, et digitorum crepitu vocatas ex cavernis prorepsisse, narrat Phylarchus in Aeliano lib. 17. cap. 5. Verba sunt, Τοῖς παιδίοις (τὰς ἀσπίδας) συντρεφομένας μηδ-ὲν ἀδικεῖν, καὶ καλουμένας δὲ ἐξέρπειν τῶν φωλῶν, καὶ ἀφικνεῖσθαι· κλῆσις δὲ αὐτῶν ὁ τῶν δακτύλων ἐστὶ κρότος. Idem de Psyllis refert Lucanus lib. 9. versu 909.

Nor is there anyone who would willingly approach the hole of an asp or a basilisk. Or, if anyone were so mad, he would run a great risk to his life. Hence, to teach that there will be a great reversal of things under the kingdom of Christ, Isaiah says: Then the infant shall play על חור פתן, over the hole of the asp, and the child weaned from milk col. 382 shall put his hand on the den of the basilisk, Isa 11:8. That is, the Gentiles will come together with the Jews into one church III, 158, and safe access will lie open to those who were once as terrifying to the faithful as the most venomous and deadly of snakes, namely asps and basilisks, are to children. Yet Sulpicius, in the first Dialogue, ch. 5, relates in these words that this was at some time also fulfilled literally: The abbot had sent bread by two boys, of whom the elder was fifteen years old and the younger twelve. As they were returning from there, an asp of wondrous size met them; they were not at all terrified at its approach, and when it came before their feet, as if charmed by incantations, it laid down its dark-blue neck. The younger of the boys seized it with his hand, wrapped it in his cloak, and began to carry it. Then, entering the monastery like a victor, he went to meet the brothers and, while all looked on, he unfolded his cloak and set down the captive beast, not without a swell of boasting. But Phylarchus, in Aelian, book 17, ch. 5, also relates that long ago in Egypt tame asps lived harmlessly with children, and when called by a snapping of the fingers crept out of their holes. His words are: Τοῖς παιδίοις (τὰς ἀσπίδας) συντρεφομένας μηδὲν ἀδικεῖν, καὶ καλουμένας δὲ ἐξέρπειν τῶν φωλῶν, καὶ ἀφικνεῖσθαι· κλῆσις δὲ αὐτῶν ὁ τῶν δακτύλων ἐστὶ κρότος [Tois paidiois (tas aspidas) syntrephomenas mēden adikein, kai kaloumenas de exerpein tōn phōlōn, kai aphikneisthai; klēsis de autōn ho tōn daktylōn esti krotos]. Lucan, book 9, line 909, reports the same of the Psylli: ¶

— — — — pignora gentis
Psyllus habet, si quis tactos non horruit angues,
Si quis donatis lusit serpentibus infans.

— — — — the Psyllian holds these as pledges of his race: if any infant did not shudder at the snakes he touched, if any infant played with the serpents given to him. ¶

Et Aelianus Historiae lib. 1. cap. 57. eosdem ait legitimos liberos suos in dolium, cerastis plenum, injectos, ex eo agnovisse, quod ad illorum contactum cerastae ἐμαράνθησαν, extabescerent. Quin hodieque in Africa circa Segelmessam montes quindecim esse perhibentur, quorum incolae cum serpentibus familiariter degunt, ita, ut cum iis pueri ludant. Ita narrat Vincentius le Blanc partis 2. cap. 23.

And Aelian, History, book 1, ch. 57, says that the same people recognized their legitimate children by throwing them into a jar full of cerastes, from the fact that at their touch the cerastes ἐμαράνθησαν [emaranthēsan], wasted away. Indeed, even today in Africa, around Segelmessa, there are said to be fifteen mountains whose inhabitants live on familiar terms with serpents, so that children play with them. So relates Vincent le Blanc, part 2, ch. 23. ¶

In Aegyptiis serpentibus aspides esse multo maximas, scriptores docent. Ammianus lib. 22. Serpentes quoque Aegyptus alit innumeras, ultra omnem perniciem saevientes: basiliscos, amphisbaenas, et scytalas, et acontias, et dipsadas, et viperas, aliasque complures: quas omnes magnitudine et decore aspis facile supereminens, nunquam sponte sua fluenta egreditur Nili. Nicander μῆκος longitudinem iis tribuit ὀργυῇ μετρητὸν, orgya (seu ulna) mensurabilem. Quod sequutus Strabo lib. 17. Διττὴ δ᾽ ἔστιν, inquit, ἡ μὲν σπιθαμιαῖα, ἥπερ καὶ ὀξυ-θανατωτέρα· ἥδ’ ἐγγὺς ὀργυιᾶς. Duplex est aspis: una quidem dodrantalis, quae celerius necat; altera vero fere orgyiae. Apud Aelianum libri 16. cap. 39. Aegyptii dicunt ἀσπίδας τετραπήχεις γίνεσθαι πολλάκις, aspides saepe esse quatuor cubitorum. Sed et, eodem teste lib. 6. capite 38. γίνονται καὶ πεντα-πήχεις, sunt etiam cubitorum quinque. Aëtii Tetrab. 4. Serm. 1. cap. 21. tres aspidum censentur species; terrestres, chelidoniae et ptyades: quarum terrestres III, 159 majores sunt, et quandoque usque ad quinque cubitorum longitudinem reperiuntur. Quomodo in Avicenna de aspide sicca dicitur, هي في قدرها ما بين ثلثة اذرع الي خمسة اذرع Est in quantitate sua a tribus ad quinque cubitos. Quam molem serpentes cum attigerunt, incipiunt dici dracones. Hinc Aëtius, in capite De Draconibus; Alii cubitorum quinque sunt, alii decem: sunt et, qui ad triginta cubitos se extendant, et quidam etiam majores narrantur. Et Avicenna, اصغر اصناف الثنانين علي ما ذكره بعضهم خمسة اذرع واما الكبار فتكون col. 383 من ثلثين ذراعا الي ما فوق ذلك Minima draconum species, ut quidam referunt, est quinque cubitorum: maximi vero sunt triginta cubitorum, et supra. Cum igitur crescant aspides usque ad draconum molem, non immerito Hebraeum nomen פתן pethen draconi fuit cum aspide commune. Proinde pro felle פתנים pethanim Syrus habet, ܡܪܪܬܐ ܕܬܢܝܢܐ, et Arabs مرارة التنين, fel draconis: et Graeci θυμὸν δρακόντων. Illi Iob. XX, 14. hi paullo post versu 16. Addo, פתן pethen, et תנין thanin (vulgare draconis nomen) ut fere synonyma poni Psal. XCI, 13. et Deut. XXXII, 33. Quo loco pro Plurali פתנים pethanim, Ionathan habet פיתוניא pithunja, tanquam a Singulari פיתון pithun. Atque huc alludere videtur Graecum PYTHONIS nomen, quo caesum in Parnasso draconem ab Apolline poetae vocant. Draconem tamen non fuisse, sed hominem, scripsit Ephorus, ex quo refert Strabo lib. 9. Apollinem sagittis confecisse χαλεπὸν ἄνδρα, Πύθωνα τοὔνομα, ἐπίκλησιν δὲ Δράκοντα, virum atrocem, Pythonem nomine, Draconem cognomento, postquam scilicet caedibus et latrociniis diu fuisset Parnassiis infestus. Idem pro Pythone Delphynes nominatur a Suida a) post Apollonium b). In his tenebris si quid video, fuit is praedo insignis in Phocide, cujus verum nomen erat Delphynes. Python cognomen inditum a Phoenicibus, in vicina Boeotia tum degentibus, ob tumultus concitatos et latrocinia commissa. Nimirum apud Arabes (quibus Phe est loco Pe, quia litera Pe carent) فتن phathana est concitare tumultus: فتنة phithna, seditio, caedes: فتان phatthan, seditiosus, latro, grassator. Sed idem ex grassatore draco factus est a fabularum architectis; quia Syris ܦܬܢ pethen, vel ܦܝܬܘܢ pithun, draconem significat. Itaque etiam Python dictus est. Ab hoc Pythone Pythii ludi; et Delphorum urbs Πυ-θὼ, vel Πυθών atque ipse Apollo Πύθων vocatus. Macrobius lib. 1. cap. 20. Hoc nomen (Πύθων) ex nece draconis inditum Deo Graeci fabulantur. Itaque III, 160 Actor. XVI, 16. πνεῦμα Πύθωνος est spiritus Apollinis, quo daemone mulier illa ἐγγαστρίμυθος plena esse credebatur. Unde ipsos etiam ventriloquos Εὐρυ-κλέας πάλαι, νυνὶ Πύθωνας appellant, ait Plutarchus in libro De defectu Oraculorum. Eo sensu, pro Pythone, Hebraei פיתום Pithom scribunt, voce leviter deflexa. Atque ariolorum id genus non ex ventre, sed ex axillis, vocem emisisse somniant. Talmudici in Sanhedrin cap. 7. fol. 65. a. בעל אוב זה פיתום המדבר משחיו, Baal Ob idem est, qui Pithom, seu qui loquitur ex axillis suis. Et ibi Glossa, מעלה את המת מן הארץ ומושיב לו בשחיו תחת זרועותיו ומדבר Mortuum ex terra educit, quem sub axillis et brachiis suis constituit, ut loquatur. Proinde Rabbi Selomo in Deut. XVIII, 11. explicans, quid sit שואל אוב, consulens Ob (seu Pythonem), זה מכשפות ששמו פיתום ומדבר משחיו ומעלה את המת בבית השחי שלו, Haec est divinatio, quae vocatur pithom, qua loquitur quis ex axillis, mortuum ad axillarum locum evocans. De hoc ritu, qui plura volet, Maimonidem consulat in tractatu De Idololatria, statim initio cap. 6. Auctor Aruch פיתום Pithom dici vult quasi פי־תהום pi-thehom, os abyssi. Quin פיתום pithom, et Πύθων idem sunt: et utrumque factum ex פתן pethen, col. 384 vel פיתון pethun, ut diximus.

Writers teach that among the Egyptian serpents the asps are by far the largest. Ammianus, book 22: Egypt also nourishes countless serpents, raging beyond all destructiveness: basilisks, amphisbaenas, and scytales, and acontias, and dipsades, and vipers, and many others; all of which the asp easily surpasses in size and beauty, and it never of its own accord leaves the streams of the Nile. Nicander assigns them a μῆκος [mēkos], a length, ὀργυῇ μετρητὸν [orgyē metrēton], measurable by the orgya (or ell). Strabo, following him, says in book 17: Διττὴ δ᾽ ἔστιν, ἡ μὲν σπιθαμιαῖα, ἥπερ καὶ ὀξυθανατωτέρα· ἥδ’ ἐγγὺς ὀργυιᾶς [Dittē d' estin, hē men spithamiaia, hēper kai oxythanatōtera; hēd' engys orgyias]. The asp is of two kinds: one a span long, which kills more quickly; the other nearly an orgyia. In Aelian, book 16, ch. 39, the Egyptians say ἀσπίδας τετραπήχεις γίνεσθαι πολλάκις [aspidas tetrapēcheis ginesthai pollakis], that asps are often four cubits long. And, on the same witness, book 6, chapter 38, γίνονται καὶ πενταπήχεις [ginontai kai pentapēcheis], there are also some of five cubits. In Aëtius, Tetrab. 4, Serm. 1, ch. 21, three species of asps are reckoned: terrestrial, chelidonian and ptyades; of which the terrestrial III, 159 are larger, and are sometimes found up to five cubits in length. Just so Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] says of the dry asp: هي في قدرها ما بين ثلثة اذرع الي خمسة اذرع [hiya fī qadrihā mā bayna thalathat adhruʿ ilā khamsat adhruʿ] In its size it is from three to five cubits. When serpents have reached this bulk, they begin to be called dragons. Hence Aëtius, in the chapter On Dragons: Some are five cubits, others ten; there are also some that extend to thirty cubits, and certain ones are even said to be larger. And Avicenna: اصغر اصناف الثنانين علي ما ذكره بعضهم خمسة اذرع واما الكبار فتكون col. 383 من ثلثين ذراعا الي ما فوق ذلك [aṣghar aṣnāf al-thanānīn ʿalā mā dhakarahu baʿḍuhum khamsat adhruʿ wa-ammā al-kibār fa-takūn min thalāthīn dhirāʿan ilā mā fawqa dhālika] The smallest species of dragons, as some report, is five cubits; but the largest are thirty cubits and more. Since, then, asps grow to the bulk of dragons, it is not without reason that the Hebrew name פתן pethen was common to the dragon and the asp. Accordingly, for the gall of פתנים pethanim the Syriac has ܡܪܪܬܐ ܕܬܢܝܢܐ [מררתא דתנינא], and the Arabic مرارة التنين [marārat al-tinnīn], the gall of the dragon; and the Greeks θυμὸν δρακόντων [thymon drakontōn]. The former at Iob. XX, 14, the latter a little later at verse 16. I add that פתן pethen and תנין thanin (the common name of the dragon) are set as virtual synonyms in Ps 91:13 and Deut 32:33. In the latter passage, for the plural פתנים pethanim, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] has פיתוניא pithunja, as if from a singular פיתון pithun. And to this the Greek name PYTHON seems to allude, by which the poets call the dragon slain on Parnassus by Apollo. Yet Ephorus wrote that it was not a dragon but a man; from him Strabo, book 9, reports that Apollo killed with arrows χαλεπὸν ἄνδρα, Πύθωνα τοὔνομα, ἐπίκλησιν δὲ Δράκοντα [chalepon andra, Pythōna tounoma, epiklēsin de Drakonta], a savage man, Python by name, Dragon by surname, after he had long harassed the people of Parnassus with murders and robberies. The same figure is named Delphynes instead of Python by Suidas a) following Apollonius b). If I see anything in this darkness, he was a notorious brigand in Phocis, whose real name was Delphynes. The surname Python was given him by the Phoenicians, then living in neighbouring Boeotia, because of the tumults he stirred up and the robberies he committed. For among the Arabs (with whom Phe stands in place of Pe, since they lack the letter Pe) فتن [fatana] phathana is to stir up tumults; فتنة [fitna] phithna, sedition, slaughter; فتان [fattān] phatthan, seditious, a robber, a highwayman. But this highwayman was turned into a dragon by the fabricators of fables, because among the Syrians ܦܬܢ [פתן] pethen, or ܦܝܬܘܢ [פיתון] pithun, signifies a dragon. So he was also called Python. From this Python come the Pythian games; and the city of Delphi was called Πυθὼ [Pythō] or Πυθών [Pythōn], and Apollo himself Πύθων [Pythōn]. Macrobius, book 1, ch. 20: The Greeks tell the fable that this name (Πύθων [Pythōn]) was given to the god from the slaying of the dragon. Thus III, 160 in Actor. XVI, 16. πνεῦμα Πύθωνος [pneuma Pythōnos] is the spirit of Apollo, the demon with which that ἐγγαστρίμυθος [engastrimythos] woman was believed to be filled. Hence ventriloquists themselves were formerly called Εὐρυκλέας [Eurykleas] and are now called Πύθωνας [Pythōnas], says Plutarch in the book On the Failure of Oracles. In this sense, for Python the Hebrews write פיתום Pithom, the word being slightly altered. And they dream that this kind of soothsayer uttered his voice not from the belly but from the armpits. The Talmudists in Sanhedrin, ch. 7, fol. 65a: בעל אוב זה פיתום המדבר משחיו, Baal Ob is the same as Pithom, that is, one who speaks from his armpits. And there the Gloss: מעלה את המת מן הארץ ומושיב לו בשחיו תחת זרועותיו ומדבר He brings up the dead from the earth, and sets him under his armpits and arms, so that he speaks. Accordingly R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Deut 18:11, explaining what שואל אוב is, one who consults Ob (or a Python): זה מכשפות ששמו פיתום ומדבר משחיו ומעלה את המת בבית השחי שלו, This is the divination called pithom, by which one speaks from the armpits, calling up the dead to the place of the armpits. Whoever wants more on this rite should consult Maimonides [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] in the treatise On Idolatry, at the very beginning of ch. 6. The author of the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] holds that פיתום Pithom is so called as if פי־תהום pi-thehom, mouth of the abyss. Rather, פיתום pithom and Πύθων [Pythōn] are the same, and both are formed from פתן pethen, col. 384 or פיתון pethun, as we have said. ¶

Obiter nota, ventriloquum, ut Graecis Πύθωνα et Εὐρυκλέα, ita Babyloniis Σακχούραν fuisse dictum. Sic testatur Photius Auctore 94. ex Iamblicho De amoribus Rhodanis et Sinonidis, Καὶ ἐγγαστρίμυθον, ὃν καί φησιν, ὥσπερ Ἕλ-ληνες μὲν Εὐρυκλέα λέγουσι, Βαβυλώνιοι δὲ Σακχούραν ἀποκαλοῦσι· Sacchuras fortasse est Arabum ساحر sachir, vel سحار sacchar, quomodo magum illi vocant. Pro mago ساحر sachir occurrit Actor. VIII, 9. et XIII, 8. et Deut. XVIII, 10. et Plurale سحرة sachara, Act. XIX, 19. Et a Singulari سحار sacchar Plurale سحارون saccharuna Gen. XLI, 8. 24. Et Foemininum سحارة sacchara pro venefica, Exod. XXII, 18. Et سحر sichr pro magia et veneficio, Act. VIII, 9. et Galat. V, 20. in Pentateucho et Novo Testamento Erpenianae editionis. Et Verbum سحر sachara, vel in secunda Conjugatione sacchara, incantare, in Nubiensi Geographo Clim. 1. part. 7. يسحرون الحيوان الضار Incantant noxia animalia. Vel, quod mihi sit verosimilius, sacchuras est זכורו zacchuru, vel zechuru, quomodo a Chaldaeis et Syris ariolum appellari notius est, quam ut probatione egeat. Exempla vide in Chaldaeis et Syris paraphrasibus, Levit. XIX, 31. Deut. XVIII, 11. et 1 Sam. XXVIII, 3. etc. Et in Sanhedrin fol. 65. 2. et 105. 1. et in Vajekra Rabba Sect. 26. etc. Sic ex zacchuru fieret sacchuras, Z et S permutatis, ut in Sagunto pro Ζακύνθῳ (ita enim vocat Stephanus): et in smaragdo pro زمرجد zmargiad.

Note in passing that a ventriloquist, as among the Greeks he was called Πύθων [Pythōn] and Εὐρυκλῆς [Eurykleēs], so among the Babylonians was called Σακχούρας [Sakchouras]. So Photius testifies, Author 94, from Iamblichus, On the Loves of Rhodanes and Sinonis: Καὶ ἐγγαστρίμυθον, ὃν καί φησιν, ὥσπερ Ἕλληνες μὲν Εὐρυκλέα λέγουσι, Βαβυλώνιοι δὲ Σακχούραν ἀποκαλοῦσι· [Kai engastrimython, hon kai phēsin, hōsper Hellēnes men Eurylea legousi, Babylōnioi de Sakchouran apokalousi]. Sacchuras is perhaps the Arabic ساحر [sāḥir] sachir, or سحار [saḥḥār] sacchar, which is what they call a magus. For magus, ساحر [sāḥir] sachir occurs in Actor. VIII, 9. and XIII, 8. and Deut 18:10, and the plural سحرة [saḥara] sachara in Acts 19:19. And from the singular سحار [saḥḥār] sacchar the plural سحارون [saḥḥārūn] saccharuna, Gen 41:8 24. And the feminine سحارة [saḥḥāra] sacchara for venefica [sorceress], Exod 22:18. And سحر [siḥr] sichr for magia [magic] and veneficium [sorcery], Acts 8:9 and Galat. V, 20., in the Pentateuch and New Testament of the Erpenian edition. And the verb سحر [saḥara] sachara, or in the second conjugation sacchara, "to enchant", in the Nubian Geographer, Clim. [=climate (section) of al-Idrīsī's geography, in the Latin Geographia Nubiensis] 1, section 7: يسحرون الحيوان الضار [yasḥarūn al-ḥayawān al-ḍārr] They enchant harmful animals. Or, which seems to me more likely, sacchuras is זכורו zacchuru, or zechuru, as the Chaldeans and Syrians call a soothsayer, which is too well known to need proof. For examples see the Chaldee and Syriac paraphrases, Lev 19:31. Deut 18:11 and 1 Sam 28:3 etc. And in Sanhedrin fol. 65. 2. and 105. 1., and in Vajekra Rabba Sect. 26, etc. Thus from zacchuru would come sacchuras, with Z and S interchanged, as in Saguntum for Ζάκυνθος [Zakynthos] (for so Stephanus calls it), and in smaragdus for زمرجد [zumurjud] zmargiad. ¶
