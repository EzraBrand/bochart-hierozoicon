II, 779 col. 192 CHAPTER VIII. ¶

Probatur, איה aja esse Accipitris speciem, quam veteres aesalonem, Germani Smirle appellant, nos esmerillon.

It is proved that איה aja is a species of hawk, which the ancients call the aesalon, the Germans Smirle, and we esmerillon [merlin]. ¶

Post tractatum De Aquila tres avium appellationes explicare conati sumus, nempe פרס peres, עזניה oznija, et דאה daa, vel ראה raa: quae extra immundarum catalogum nusquam reperiuntur. Itaque cum in harum interpretatione tam veteres, quam novi interpretes maxime dissentiant, aliunde exsculpi non potuit vera significatio, quam ex sola vocum origine. Nunc quatuor subsequentur, איה aja nimirum, et דיה daja, et עורב oreb, et בת־יענה bath-jaana: quae cum alibi reperiantur, in harum explicatione plus affulgebit lucis. Tamen de prima valde ambigitur. Graeci modo vulturem, modo milvum explicant. col. 193 Prius sequuntur Hieronymus, Munsterus, Pagninus, Castalio, Anglica et Itali- ca versio: posterius Arabs in Iobo, Schindlerus et Genevenses. Ionathan reddit דייתא אוכמתא, vulturem nigrum. Ierosolymitanus, אבו ibbu, quod puto idem esse. Onkelos, טרפיתא, rapacem avem, nomine generico, quia ignoravit speciem. Arabes in Mose الصدا bubonem. Kimchius, יגא״וא, picam. Iunius et Buxtorfius, picam, aut cornicem. Arias, variam avem. Pomarius, vultu- rem, aut accipitrem. Aben Ezra, avem insularem, tanquam ab אי, id est, ab insula facto nomine. Alii Hebraeorum, ut R. Levi, Mardochaeus, Aquinas, caeteris prudentiores, solum dicunt esse שם עוף, nomen avis.

After the treatise On the Eagle we tried to explain three names of birds, namely פרס peres, עזניה oznija, and דאה daa, or ראה raa, which are found nowhere outside the catalogue of unclean birds. Since in interpreting these the ancient and modern interpreters disagree greatly, their true meaning could be extracted from no other source than the origin of the words alone. Now four follow, namely איה aja, דיה daja, עורב oreb, and בת־יענה bath-jaana; since these are found elsewhere, more light will shine on their explanation. Yet there is much doubt about the first. The Greeks explain it sometimes as vulture, sometimes as kite. col. 193 The former is followed by Jerome, Munster, Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], and the English and Italian versions; the latter by the Arabic translator in Job, Schindler, and the Genevans. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] renders it דייתא אוכמתא, black vulture; the Jerusalem Targum, אבו ibbu, which I think is the same. Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] has טרפיתא, bird of prey, a generic name, because he did not know the species. The Arabs in Moses have الصدا [al-ṣadā] owl. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] has יגא״וא, magpie. Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], magpie or crow. Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], variegated bird. Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], vulture or hawk. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra], island bird, as if the name were formed from אי, that is, from island. Other Hebrews, such as R. Levi, Mordecai, and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)], more prudent than the rest, say only that it is שם עוף, the name of a bird. ¶

Nobis Hebraeorum האיה haaja avis eadem, quae Arabum اليويو aljuju. Avis est a clamore sic dicta. Nam ut aquilarum clangere est, milvorum jugere vel lipire, accipitrum pipare, et crocitare corvorum: ita hujus avis proprium est يايا jaja, secundum Arabes, et clamor ipse dicitur اليايا aljajao. Inde igitur ipsa avis Hebraice dicta videtur איה aja, ut Arabice يويو juju, et in plurali يايي jaji. Neque novum id videri debet, ut avis nomen a voce sumatur, si vere Varro libro quarto De lingua Latina, Pleraeque (aves) a suis vocibus, ut hae, upupa, cuculus, corvus, hirundo, ulula, bubo: item hae: pavo, anser, gallina, columba. Est porro اليويو aljuju in accipitrum speciebus, quorum ut Aristoteles decem, Plinius sedecim censet genera: ita Arabes multa numerant. Atque in his est صقر sacer, vernaculo sermone sacre, qui rursus in tres species subdividitur, nempe in sacrum proprie dictum II, 780 (1), كونج chauneg, et يويو juju, de quo sic Damir: الصنف الثالث من الصغور اليويو وتسميه اهل مصر والشام الجلم لخغة جناحه وسرعته ولان الجلم هو الذي منجز به وهو المقص وهو طاير صغير قصير الذنب ومزاجه بالنسبة الي الباشق بارد رطب لانه اصبر منه نغسا واثغل حركة ويشرب الما شربا ضروريا كما يشربه الباسق الا انه ابخر ومزاجه بالنسبة الي الصغر حار يابس واذلك هو اشجع منه ويغال ان اول من ضراه واصطاد به بهرام جور وذلك انه شاهد يويو يطارد قبرة ويراوغها ويترفع وينخغض معها وما تركها الي ان صادها فاعجبه ذلك وامر به فادب وصاد به col. 194 Tertia SACRORUM species est aljuju, quem Aegyptii et Syri vocant algelem, propter alarum levitatem et celeritatem. Nempe algelem secula, seu falce instructum sonat (quasi aërem alis tanquam falce dividat). Est autem avis parva, brevis caudae: cujus temperamentum si conferas cum albasik (alio genere accipitris), est frigidum et humidum; quia spiritum diutius continet, et motu gravior est, et bibit aquam, cum necesse est, quomodo bibit albasik; eo tamen est foetidior. At, si conferas cum sacro (proprie dicto), temperamenti calidi et sicci videbitur, ac proinde illo est audacior. Quidam porro, nomine Behram Giur, primus eum ad venationem adhibuisse dicitur. Cum scilicet eo praesente avis juju alaudam insequutus fuisset, et ei colluctatus, cum illa sese efferens, et demittens, neque relinquens donec cepisset: id ille admiratus, avem erudiri jussit, et postmodum ad venationem ea usus est. Haec in Damire, unde constat, eam avem describi, quae Germanice Smirle, Italice smerlo et smeriglio, Gallice dicitur esmerillon. Quippe etiam is censetur inter accipitrum et falconum species. Sed quantitate minimus est (inquit Albertus libri vigesimi tertii capite decimo quarto), nulli tamen animo et audacia cedit pro corporis et virium suarum portione, etc. Itaque Guilielmus Falconarius refert, se harum avium opera aliquando gruem cepisse. Alioqui enim alaudas et similes aves tantum II, 781 vincere potest, etc. Alas habet longissimas respectu sui corporis, caudam mensuratam (id est, mediocrem), crura et pedes planos, etc. Sunt celerrimi, et insidiosi, et in capiendo ascendunt, ac descendunt feriendo, etc. Et Crescentiensis, Ismerli sunt quasi falconelli, id est, falcones parvi, quos et corporis specie et colore pennarum referunt, etc. Capiunt praecipue alaudas, quas tanto desiderio et impetu persequuntur, ut vel ad clibanum usque ardentem quandoque, vel in puteum, vel hominum vestes offendant. Alius apud Gesnerum, Smerillo formam habet falconis, minor sperverio, volacior quam ulla avis alia. Aves capit easdem, quas sperverius, praecipue vero parvas, ut passerem, alaudam et similes (2). Vides, le-ctor, ut Arabum aljuju, ita nostrum smirlum, vel smerillonem esse in accipitrum specie, eumque statura parvum, et cauda brevi, et volatu celerem, et audacia insignem, et alaudarum venationi maxime addictum: ut minime sit dubitandum, quin diversis nominibus eadem avis designetur. Sed et aliud est notandum inprimis, quia maxime ad rem nostram facit. Si quis vivaces oculos habet et maxime perspicaces, eum vulgato adagio Galli smerillonis oculos habere dicunt; quia tales habet haec avis. Et Arabum quoque poetae oculos sui juju multum commendant. In his anonymus apud Damirem,

In our view the Hebrew האיה haaja is the same bird as the Arabic اليويو [al-yūyū] aljuju. The bird is so called from its cry. For just as eagles are said to clangere, kites jugere or lipire, hawks pipare, and ravens crocitare, so the proper sound of this bird, according to the Arabs, is يايا [yāyā] jaja, and the cry itself is called اليايا [al-yāyā] aljajao. From this, then, the bird itself seems to have been called in Hebrew איה aja, as in Arabic يويو [yūyū] juju, and in the plural يايي [yāyī] jaji. Nor should it seem strange that a bird's name is taken from its voice, if Varro is right in book 4 of On the Latin Language: Most (birds) are named from their own calls, such as these: upupa, cuculus, corvus, hirundo, ulula, bubo; likewise these: pavo, anser, gallina, columba. Further, اليويو [al-yūyū] aljuju is among the species of hawks; and as Aristotle reckons ten kinds of them and Pliny sixteen, so the Arabs too count many. Among these is the صقر [ṣaqr] sacer, in the vernacular sacre, which is again subdivided into three species, namely the saker properly so called II, 780 R1, the كونج [kawnaj] chauneg, and the يويو [yūyū] juju, of which Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] says: الصنف الثالث من الصغور اليويو وتسميه اهل مصر والشام الجلم لخغة جناحه وسرعته ولان الجلم هو الذي منجز به وهو المقص وهو طاير صغير قصير الذنب ومزاجه بالنسبة الي الباشق بارد رطب لانه اصبر منه نغسا واثغل حركة ويشرب الما شربا ضروريا كما يشربه الباسق الا انه ابخر ومزاجه بالنسبة الي الصغر حار يابس واذلك هو اشجع منه ويغال ان اول من ضراه واصطاد به بهرام جور وذلك انه شاهد يويو يطارد قبرة ويراوغها ويترفع وينخغض معها وما تركها الي ان صادها فاعجبه ذلك وامر به فادب وصاد به [al-ṣinf al-thālith min al-ṣughūr al-yūyū wa-tusammīhi ahl Miṣr wa-l-Shām al-jalam li-khiffat janāḥihi wa-surʿatihi wa-li-anna al-jalam huwa alladhī munjaz bihi wa-huwa al-miqaṣṣ wa-huwa ṭāyir ṣaghīr qaṣīr al-dhanab wa-mizājuhu bi-l-nisba ilā al-bāshiq bārid raṭb li-annahu aṣbar minhu nafasan wa-athqal ḥarakatan wa-yashrab al-mā shurban ḍarūriyyan kamā yashrabuhu al-bāsiq illā annahu abkhar wa-mizājuhu bi-l-nisba ilā al-ṣaqr ḥārr yābis wa-li-dhālika huwa ashjaʿ minhu wa-yuqāl anna awwal man ḍarrāhu wa-iṣṭāda bihi Bahrām Jūr wa-dhālika annahu shāhada yūyū yuṭārid qubbara wa-yurāwighuhā wa-yatarafaʿ wa-yankhafiḍ maʿahā wa-mā tarakahā ilā an ṣādahā fa-aʿjabahu dhālika wa-amara bihi fa-uddiba wa-ṣāda bihi] col. 194 The third species of SAKERS is aljuju, which the Egyptians and Syrians call algelem, because of the lightness and swiftness of its wings. For algelem means a sickle, or one furnished with a sickle (as though it cut the air with its wings as with a sickle). It is a small bird with a short tail. If you compare its temperament with that of albasik (another kind of hawk), it is cold and moist, because it holds its breath longer and is heavier in movement, and it drinks water when it must, as albasik drinks; yet it is more foul-smelling than it. But if you compare it with the saker (properly so called), it will appear to be of a hot and dry temperament, and is accordingly bolder than it. Moreover, a certain man named Behram Giur is said to have been the first to use it for hunting. For when, in his presence, a juju bird had pursued a lark and grappled with it, rising and descending with it, and not leaving it until it had caught it, he marvelled at this, ordered the bird to be trained, and afterwards used it for hunting. So far Damir. From this it is clear that the bird described is the one called in German Smirle, in Italian smerlo and smeriglio, in French esmerillon [merlin]. For it too is reckoned among the species of hawks and falcons. But in size it is the smallest (says Albertus, book 23, chapter 14), yet it yields to none in spirit and daring, in proportion to its body and strength, etc. Thus William the Falconer reports that he once caught a crane by means of these birds. Otherwise it can overcome only larks and similar birds II, 781 etc. Its wings are very long in relation to its body, its tail moderate (that is, of middling size), its legs and feet flat, etc. They are very swift and crafty, and in taking their prey they ascend, and descend as they strike, etc. And Crescentiensis: The Ismerli are like falconelli, that is, little falcons, which they resemble both in shape of body and in colour of plumage, etc. They take chiefly larks, which they pursue with such eagerness and impetus that they sometimes dash even against a burning oven, or into a well, or against men's clothing. Another writer in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium]: The Smerillo has the form of a falcon, is smaller than the sparrowhawk, and flies faster than any other bird. It takes the same birds as the sparrowhawk, but especially small ones, such as the sparrow, the lark and the like R2. You see, reader, that like the Arabs' aljuju, our smirlus or smerillon belongs to the hawk species, is small in stature, short-tailed, swift in flight, remarkable for daring, and especially given to hunting larks; so that there can be no doubt at all that the same bird is designated by different names. But another point must above all be noted, because it bears most on our subject. If someone has lively and very keen eyes, the French say by a common proverb that he has the eyes of a smerillon, because this bird has such eyes. And the Arab poets too greatly praise the eyes of their juju. Among them an anonymous poet in Damir: ¶

يويو مهذب رشيق ۞ كان عينيه لذي التحقيق ۞ فصان مخروطان من عقيق ۞

يويو مهذب رشيق ۞ كان عينيه لذي التحقيق ۞ فصان مخروطان من عقيق ۞ [yūyū muhadhdhab rashīq ۞ ka-anna ʿaynayhi li-dhī al-taḥqīq ۞ faṣṣān makhrūṭān min ʿaqīq ۞] ¶

Iuju (volatu) celer et pernix, cujus oculi sunt vere suaves, et pupillae tanquam torno expolitae plus quam sapphirus. Et Abu Navvas in opere De aucupiis, cui titulus طريدة Tarida, de eadem ave inter caetera haec habet:

The Iuju is swift and nimble (in flight), whose eyes are truly lovely, and its pupils, as if polished on the lathe, more than sapphire. And Abu Nuwas, in his work On Fowling, entitled طريدة [Ṭarīda] Tarida, says of the same bird, among other things: ¶

ازرق لا تكذب عيناه ۞ فلا يري القانص ما يراه ۞

ازرق لا تكذب عيناه ۞ فلا يري القانص ما يراه ۞ [azraq lā takdhib ʿaynāhu ۞ fa-lā yarā al-qāniṣ mā yarāhu ۞] ¶

Quae Latinis verbis pene ad verbum sic expressimus, col. 195

These we have rendered in Latin words almost word for word thus: col. 195 ¶

Caesius, atque oculis nunquam fallentibus usus,
Et quod venator non videt, ille videt.

Grey-eyed, and with eyes that never deceive,

Haec porro ad rem nostram ideo maxime pertinere diximus; quia de ejusdem avis oculo haec leguntur, Iob. XXVIII, 7. Semitam non novit עיט, avis, neque eam vidit עין איה, oculus τοῦ aja. Agit de fodinis, unde hominum industria metalla eruuntur. Quae cum in intimis terrae visceribus lateant, harum semitam, II, 782 inquit sacer scriptor, non novit עיט ajit, id est, avis non quaevis, sed avis rapax, quod genus multo est perspicacius. Haec in priore hemistichio, et in al-tero, huic parallelo, Neque eam vidit oculus τοῦ aja, vel juju, si mavis, id est, smerillonis, licet illa avis sit oculatissima, et tam cernat acutum, quam ut aquila, aut serpens Epidaurius. Smerillonem esse veterum αἰσαλῶνα, aesalonem, multa probant. 1. Quod Aristoteles (Hist. lib. 9. cap. 36.) et Plinius (lib. 10. cap. 8.) aesalonem recensent inter accipitrum species. 2. Quod ab Aeliano (lib. 2. cap. 51.) vocatur ὄρνις ἰσχυρός (3). 3. Et a Plinio (lib. 10. cap. 74.) parva avis. 4. Quod idem Plinius (lib. 10. cap. 8.) aesalonem scribit omni tempore apparere, cum caeteri hyeme abeant. Id enim smerilloni prorsus convenit, ut a Turnero notatum. Verba sunt: Inter minores accipitres sola merlina, ut Angli vocant, sive smerla, ut Germani, semper apparet. Tamen id falsum esse, contendit nobilissimus Dominus de Grentemesnil. Sunt, qui pro aesalone aesaronem scribant. Hesychius, Αἰσάρων· εἶδος ἱέρακος. Utrumque fortasse ab αἶσα, quia est avis αἴσιος, auspicata, et boni ominis, ut accipitrum omne genus. Quod nullo negotio probaverim, si tanti esset.

We have said that these words bear especially on our subject, because the following is read of the eye of this same bird, Iob. XXVIII, 7: The bird עיט knew not the path, nor has עין איה, the eye of the aja, seen it. He is speaking of mines, from which metals are dug out by human industry. Since these lie hidden in the innermost bowels of the earth, their path, II, 782 says the sacred writer, the עיט ajit knew not, that is, not just any bird, but the bird of prey, a kind that is far more keen-sighted. This is in the first hemistich; and in the other, parallel to it: Nor has the eye of the aja, or juju if you prefer, seen it, that is, of the smerillon [merlin], although that bird is most sharp-eyed and sees as keenly as the eagle or the Epidaurian serpent. Many things prove that the smerillon is the αἰσαλών [aisalōn], the aesalon, of the ancients. 1. Aristotle (Hist. book 9, ch. 36) and Pliny (book 10, ch. 8) list the aesalon among the species of hawks. 2. By Aelian (book 2, ch. 51) it is called ὄρνις ἰσχυρός [ornis ischyros] R3. 3. And by Pliny (book 10, ch. 74) a small bird. 4. The same Pliny (book 10, ch. 8) writes that the aesalon appears at every season, whereas the others depart in winter. This fits the smerillon exactly, as Turner [=William Turner, Avium praecipuarum historia (1544)] has noted. His words are: Among the smaller hawks only the merlin, as the English call it, or smerla, as the Germans do, is always seen. Yet the most noble Lord de Grentemesnil contends that this is false. Some write aesaron instead of aesalon. Hesychius: Αἰσάρων· εἶδος ἱέρακος [Aisarōn; eidos hierakos]. Both perhaps come from αἶσα [aisa], because it is a bird αἴσιος [aisios], auspicious and of good omen, like every kind of hawk. This I could prove without any trouble, if it were worth the effort. ¶
