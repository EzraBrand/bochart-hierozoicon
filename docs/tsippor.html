II, 721 col. 145 CAP. XXI.

II, 721 col. 145 Chapter 21. ¶

De voce צפור tsippor, quae et quasvis aves sonat, et speciatim passerem. Hujus species juxta Arabes, et de passere domestico et ejus pipitu.

On the word צפור tsippor, which denotes both birds of any kind and specifically the sparrow. Its kinds according to the Arabs; and on the house sparrow and its chirping. ¶

Inter senilis aetatis incommoda, quae graphice depingit Solomon, hoc quoque recensetur Eccles. XII, 6. quod excitatur, et surgit לקול הצפור, ad vocem τοῦ tsippor, seu avis, id est, galli, ut multi putant. Quos doctissimus Mercerus ita refellit: Qui gallum intelligunt, ad cujus vocem excitetur, vim vocis Hebraicae צפור (tsippor) non intelligunt, quae aviculam significat, etc. nec de majoribus avibus dicitur. Et idem in Thesauro, צפור (tsippor) significat minutiores aves, etc. עוף (oph) autem generaliter est avis, aut volucris. Et Schindlerus, צפור (tsippor) avicula circumvolitans, aut quae in aëre gyratur. Comprehendit aves minores, et harum frequentiorem hominibus passerem, avem communissimam. Ita etiam plerique alii recentiores lexicographi. Et in Philonis libro, Quis rerum divinarum sit haeres, Sepphora est ὀρνίθιον avicula. Tamen apud Hebraeos עוף oph et צפור tsippor, sunt aeque generalia, et omnes omnino volucres complectitur utrumque nomen. Hinc Kimchius et Pomarius in Lexicis suis ad vocem צפור tsippor, שם כלל לכל עוף, omni avi commune nomen est. Et Aquinas, שם כולל לכל מיני עוף שיעופף, Nomen hoc complectitur omnem avis speciem, quae volat. Quod verissimum esse, apparebit conferenti loca Scripturae. Nam Deut. IV, 17. cum Deus coli prohibet תבנית כל צפור כנף אשר תעוף בשמים, similitudinem omnis tsippor alatae, quae per coelum volat, quis solum prohiberi dixerit avicularum cultum, non etiam majorum avium? et Deut. XIV, 11. cum idem Deus concedit esum כל צפור טהורה, omnis tsippor mundae, an solum aviculas concessisse dicetur, non gallinas, et meleagrides, et phasianos? Aut quis unquam ad aviculas restrinxit legem, quae habetur Deut. XXII, 6. Si obviam tibi fiat קן צפור, nidus tsippor in ulla arbore, aut humi, nidus pullorum, aut ovorum, ubi mater incubet pullis, aut ovis, ne accipito matrem cum pullis? Rursus quis negaverit, omnes aves significari, cum Deus homini subjecisse legitur bestias agri, צפור שמים, tsippor coelorum, et pisces maris, Psal. VIII, 9.? Aut cum col. 146 ad celebrandum Deum invitantur ferae, et jumenta omnia, reptilia, וצפור כנף, et tsippor alata, Psal. CXLVIII, 10.? Aut cum in mysticae arboris ramis ha-bitare leguntur כל צפור כל כנף, omnis tsippor, omne alatum, Ezech. XVII, 23.? Et Plurali numero צפרי שמיא, tsippare coelorum, Dan. IV, 9. 18.? Denique rapaces aves, quae fere sunt multo maximae, hoc nomine designari patet, cum Ezech. XXXIX, 4. 17. copias Gog et Magog Deus tradit vorandas לעיט צפור, II, 722 rapaci tsippor alatae. Et Daniel IV, 20. ubi Babylonii regis ungues crevisse dicuntur כצפרין, ut tsipparin, ut rapacium avium ungues, quae ab unguium curvatura et amplitudine Graece dicuntur γαμψώνυχες. Itaque palam est, extendi nomen צפור tsippor etiam ad maximas aves. Rabbi Moses, filius Guersom, aves ita dici contendit; שבעלות השחר מתחילות לרנן ולצפצוף quia ad ortum aurorae canere et minurire incipiunt. Ego non quidem a cantu, qui non est communis omnibus, sed a צפר, id est, mane, dici autumo; quia solenne est avibus, summo mane surgere, sive canorae sint, aut non canorae: cum ferae tum altum dormiant, quia noctem ducunt insomnem, Psal. CIV, 20.

Among the infirmities of old age which Solomon vividly depicts, this too is listed, Eccl 12:6: that one is roused and rises לקול הצפור, at the voice of the tsippor, or bird, that is, of the cock, as many think. The most learned Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] refutes them thus: Those who understand the cock, at whose voice one is roused, do not understand the force of the Hebrew word צפור (tsippor), which means a little bird, etc., and is not said of larger birds. And the same author in his Thesaurus: צפור (tsippor) means smaller birds, etc.; עוף (oph), however, is a bird or winged creature in general. And Schindler: צפור (tsippor) is a little bird flying about, or one that circles in the air. It comprises the smaller birds, and among them the sparrow, the one most frequently found among men, a very common bird. So too most other recent lexicographers. And in Philo's book Who is the Heir of Divine Things, Sepphora is ὀρνίθιον [ornithion], a little bird. Yet among the Hebrews עוף oph and צפור tsippor are equally general, and both names embrace absolutely all winged creatures. Hence Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] in their Lexicons, under the word צפור tsippor: שם כלל לכל עוף, it is a name common to every bird. And Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: שם כולל לכל מיני עוף שיעופף, This name comprises every species of bird that flies. That this is entirely true will be clear to anyone who compares the passages of Scripture. For in Deut 4:17, when God forbids the worship of תבנית כל צפור כנף אשר תעוף בשמים, the likeness of any winged tsippor that flies through the heavens, who would say that only the worship of little birds is forbidden, and not that of larger birds as well? And in Deut 14:11, when the same God permits the eating of כל צפור טהורה, every clean tsippor, will he be said to have permitted only little birds, and not hens, guinea fowl and pheasants? Or who ever restricted to little birds the law found in Deut 22:6: If there come before you קן צפור, a nest of a tsippor, in any tree or on the ground, a nest of young or of eggs, where the mother is sitting on the young or on the eggs, do not take the mother with the young? Again, who would deny that all birds are meant when we read that God subjected to man the beasts of the field, צפור שמים, the tsippor of the heavens, and the fish of the sea, Ps 8:9? Or when col. 146 wild beasts, and all cattle, creeping things, וצפור כנף, and the winged tsippor are invited to praise God, Ps 148:10? Or when we read that in the branches of the mystical tree dwell כל צפור כל כנף, every tsippor, everything winged, Ezek 17:23? And in the plural, צפרי שמיא, the tsippare of the heavens, Dan 4:9 18.? Finally, it is clear that birds of prey, which are generally by far the largest, are designated by this name, when in Ezek 39:4 17. God hands over the hosts of Gog and Magog to be devoured לעיט צפור, II, 722 by the ravenous winged tsippor. And in Daniel IV, 20, where the nails of the Babylonian king are said to have grown כצפרין, like tsipparin, that is, like the talons of birds of prey, which from the curvature and size of their talons are called in Greek γαμψώνυχες [gampsōnyches]. It is therefore evident that the name צפור tsippor extends even to the largest birds. Rabbi Moses, son of Gershom, maintains that birds are so called שבעלות השחר מתחילות לרנן ולצפצוף, because at the rising of the dawn they begin to sing and to warble. I, for my part, hold that they are named not from singing, which is not common to all, but from צפר, that is, morning; because it is customary for birds to rise very early in the morning, whether they are songbirds or not, while wild beasts are then sound asleep, because they spend the night awake, Psal. CIV, 20. ¶

Sed, utut omnibus avibus commune sit nomen צפור tsippor, tamen Hebraei asserunt, quandoque hoc eodem nomine passerem speciatim significari. Kimchius in Psal. LXXXIV, 4. וצפור הוא עוף קטן וקורין לו בלעז פשרא Et tsippor (ibi loci) parva est avis, quam vulgari lingua (id est, Hispanica) vocant paxaro (id est, passerem). Hinc Pomarius, קמחי אומר שלפעמים יוקח בעד עוף קטן שקורין לו בלעז פאסרו, Dixit Kimchius, aliquando sumi pro avicula, quam vernaculo (seu Italico) sermone vocant passaro. Aquinas, הוא שם מין עוף מה, Nomen est certae avis speciei. Et paullo post, והוא עוף קטן וידוע, Est avicula nota, nempe passer. Proinde ut Hieronymus passerem reddit Lev. XIV, 4. ita etiam vetus interpres Psal. X, 1. et LXXXIII, 4. et CI, 8. et CIII, 16. et CXXIII, 7. Graecos sequutus, qui στρουθίον exponunt in iisdem Psalmorum locis, et Eccles. XII, 6. et Thren. III, 52. Et vicissim Graecum στρουθία, id est, passeres, Matth. X, 29. et Luc. XII, 6. Syrus ܨܦܪܝܢ tsipparin interpretatur: pro quo Arabs عصافير atsaphiro. Illis enim عصفور otsphur est passer, voce facta ex צפור tsippor, gutturali praefixa. Quod cum ignoraret Chamza, Grammaticus Arabs non incelebris, putavit عصفور voce composita passerem dici; quia عصي وفر percutit et fugit. Nec Hebraeis peculiare est, quod ea voce passerem vocant, qua etiam majores aves. Graecum enim passeris nomen στρουθὸς de gallina quoque usurpatur. Nicander in Alexipharmacis versu 60.

But although the name צפור tsippor is common to all birds, the Hebrews nevertheless assert that this same name sometimes signifies the sparrow specifically. Kimchi on Psal. LXXXIV, 4: וצפור הוא עוף קטן וקורין לו בלעז פשרא And tsippor (in that place) is a small bird, which in the vernacular (that is, Spanish) they call paxaro (that is, sparrow). Hence Pomarius: קמחי אומר שלפעמים יוקח בעד עוף קטן שקורין לו בלעז פאסרו, Kimchi said that it is sometimes taken for a little bird, which in the vernacular (or Italian) tongue they call passaro. Aquinas: הוא שם מין עוף מה, It is the name of a certain species of bird. And a little later: והוא עוף קטן וידוע, It is a well-known little bird, namely the sparrow. Accordingly, just as Jerome renders it sparrow in Lev. XIV, 4, so too does the old translator in Psal. X, 1, and LXXXIII, 4, and CI, 8, and CIII, 16, and CXXIII, 7, following the Greeks, who render it στρουθίον [strouthion] in those same passages of the Psalms, and in Eccles. XII, 6, and Thren. III, 52. And conversely, the Greek στρουθία [strouthia], that is, sparrows, in Matth. X, 29, and Luc. XII, 6, the Syriac translator renders ܨܦܪܝܢ [צפרין] tsipparin, for which the Arabic has عصافير [ʿaṣāfīr] atsaphiro. For to them عصفور [ʿuṣfūr] otsphur is the sparrow, a word formed from צפור tsippor with a guttural prefixed. Not knowing this, Chamza, a not undistinguished Arab grammarian, thought that the sparrow is called عصفور by a compound word, because عصي وفر [ʿaṣá wa-farra] it strikes and flees. Nor is it peculiar to the Hebrews that they call the sparrow by the same word as they also call larger birds. For the Greek name of the sparrow, στρουθὸς [strouthos], is also used of the hen. Nicander in the Alexipharmaca, line 60: ¶

Ἠέ τι μηελόεντα χαλικρότερον ποτὸν ἴσχοις Ὄρνιθος στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — — —

Ἠέ τι μηελόεντα χαλικρότερον ποτὸν ἴσχοις Ὄρνιθος στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — — — [Ēe ti mēeloenta chalikroteron poton ischois / Ornithos strouthoio katoikados] ¶

Sive aliquod medullosum meracum potabile pharmacum habeas ex ave passere domestica, id est, ex gallina. Et rursus versu 535.

Or may you have some marrowy, undiluted drinkable remedy from the domestic sparrow-bird, that is, from the hen. And again, line 535: ¶

Ἠὲ πάτον στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — —

Ἠὲ πάτον στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — — [Ēe paton strouthoio katoikados] ¶

Aut stercus passeris domestici, id est, gallinae. Unde factum, ut στρουθὸς diceretur gallina illa Libyca, avium longe maxima, uno verbo struthio. Quod imitati II, 723 Romani scriptores struthionem appellant marinum passerem. Plautus in Persa Actu secundo,

Or the dung of the domestic sparrow, that is, of the hen. Hence it came about that the great Libyan hen, by far the largest of birds, was called στρουθὸς [strouthos], in a word the ostrich. Imitating this, the II, 723 Roman writers call the ostrich the sea sparrow. Plautus in the Persa, Act 2: ¶

Vola curriculo. P. Istuc marinus passer per circum solet.

Fly at a run. P. That is what the sea sparrow usually does around the circus. ¶

Ausonius Epistola undecima,

Ausonius, Epistle 11: ¶

Ovum tu coque passeris marini. col. 147

Cook you the egg of the sea sparrow. col. 147 ¶

Festus, Passer marinus, quem vocat vulgus struthiocamelum. Passeris Romana vox putatur ad Hebraeam צפור tsippor alludere, et ab illa esse orta. Sed vir magnus in Solinum observat, passerem etiam passam dici, ut Gallice paisse passerem foeminam. Hinc Glossae, Passa, εἶδος ὀρνέου. Et passa factum ex Aeolico πίζα, pro σπίζα. Nempe σπίζα est fringillus, avis, passeri similis. Hesychius, Σπίζα· ὀρνιθάριον στρουθῷ παρεμφερές. Et quandoque latius sumitur pro avicula in genere. Ut in Timone apud Laërtium de Arcesilao,

Festus: The sea sparrow, which the common people call the struthiocamelus. The Roman word passer is thought to allude to the Hebrew צפור tsippor and to have arisen from it. But a great man, commenting on Solinus, observes that the sparrow was also called passa, as the female sparrow is called paisse in French. Hence the Glosses: Passa, εἶδος ὀρνέου [eidos orneou]. And passa was formed from the Aeolic πίζα [piza], for σπίζα [spiza]. For σπίζα [spiza] is the finch, a bird similar to the sparrow. Hesychius: Σπίζα· ὀρνιθάριον στρουθῷ παρεμφερές [Spiza: ornitharion strouthō paremferes]. And sometimes it is taken more broadly for a little bird in general, as in Timon, quoted by Laertius, on Arcesilaus: ¶

Οἱ δέ μιν ἠΰ τε γλαῦκα περὶ σπίζαι τερατοῦντο,

Οἱ δέ μιν ἠΰ τε γλαῦκα περὶ σπίζαι τερατοῦντο, [Hoi de min ēy te glauka peri spizai teratounto] ¶

Illum autem mirabuntur ut aviculae, quae noctuam circumstant, proinde Hesychius, Σπίζια· ὄρνεα ἅπαντα. Recte, si modo ὄρνεα illi sunt ὀρνίθια, vel ὀρνιθάρια· aviculae, non aves. Sic Arabicum عصفور, quamvis proprie passerem sonat, tamen avicularum multas species complectitur. Damir, وهى انواع منها ما هو مطرب بصوته ومنها ما يعجب بصورته وحسنه والعصفور الصوار هو الذي يجيب اذا ادعي من الصرورة وعصفور الجنة وهو الخطاف والعصفور البيوت فان في طباعه اختلافا Passerum sunt variae species: alii enim placent vocis modulatione; alii specie et forma sunt mirabiles. Et passer suar dictus is est, qui vocatus stridendo respondet. Et passer paradisi est hirundo. Et passer domesticus is, qui natura sua est pugnax, etc. Et infra, ومن انواعه عصفور الشوك واكثر ماواه السياج وزعم ارسطو ان بينه وبين الحمار عداوة E speciebus ejus est passer spinarum, qui in vepretis plerumque degit. Refert Aristoteles, inter eum et asinum mutuum esse odium; palam est, acanthidem intelligi, quam alii carduelem, alii spinum, alii ligurinum explicant. De ea enim Philosophus Hist. libri octavi capite tertio, Τὰ δὲ τοιάδε ἀκανθοφάγα, ἀκανθὶς, θραυπίς· ἔτι ἡ καλουμένη χρυσομήτρις. Ταῦτα γὰρ πάντα ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκανθῶν νέμεται. Quae Gaza sic reddidit: Spinam appetunt illa, spinus, sive ligurinus, carduelis, item quae aurivittis appellatur. Haec enim omnia victitant in spinis. Et libri noni capite primo, Ὄνος δὲ, καὶ ἀκανθίδες πολέμιοι· αἱ μὲν γὰρ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀκανθῶν βοητεύουσιν (legendum II, 724 βιοτεύουσιν), ὁ δὲ ἁπαλὰς οὔσας ἐσθίει τὰς ἀκάνθας· Inimici sunt asinus et acanthides: illis enim victus ex spinis, quas adhuc teneras pascit asinus. Et hanc avem merito Arabes referunt ad passerum genus. Acanthis enim eadem, quae acánthyllis, de qua Hesychius, Ἀκανθυλλίς· στρουθοῦ γένος, Acanthyllis, passeris genus. Gallice dicitur serin. An a Sirenum cantu, ut vult Bellonius Ornithologiae libri septimi capite decimo quarto. Sequitur in Damire, ومن انواعه القبرة والحسون والبلبل والصعوة والحمرة والعندليب والمكاكي والصافر والتنوط والوصع والابوبراقش col. 148 والقبعة Etiam e speciebus ejus sunt kubbara (corydalus), et hassun (alibi non reperio, videtur a pulchritudine dicta) (1), et bulbul (philomela), et savva (avicula rubro vertice), et hamra (alaudae species rubra), et andalib (avis species, philomelae similis,), et mechachi (Plurale a Singulari مكا maccha, avis, a sibilo dicta,), et saphir (quae et ipsa tota nocte sibilat capite inclinato, et pedibus ad arborem applicatis), et thenavvut (avicula, huic non absimilis, a nido appellata, quem ex arbore suspendit,), et vvasa (regulus), et abubarakis (avicula canora, longo collo et pedibus, pennis rubris, flavis, virilibus et coeruleis, quae colores subinde mutant), et kubaa (avicula varia, collum in metu contrahens, unde illi nomen). Hae sunt aves, quas ad passerum genus referunt Arabes. Quomodo etiam apud Hebraeos, auctore Kimchio in Lexico, הוא רב המין לפיכך כלל כל עוף בזה השם, Passer multas habet species: unde est, quod hoc nomen ad omnes aves refertur.

But they will marvel at him like little birds that surround an owl. Accordingly Hesychius: Σπίζια· ὄρνεα ἅπαντα [Spizia; ornea hapanta]. Rightly, provided that his ὄρνεα [ornea] are ὀρνίθια [ornithia] or ὀρνιθάρια [ornitharia], that is, little birds, not birds. Thus the Arabic عصفور [ʿuṣfūr], although properly it means sparrow, nevertheless embraces many species of little birds. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: وهى انواع منها ما هو مطرب بصوته ومنها ما يعجب بصورته وحسنه والعصفور الصوار هو الذي يجيب اذا ادعي من الصرورة وعصفور الجنة وهو الخطاف والعصفور البيوت فان في طباعه اختلافا [wa-hiya anwāʿ minhā mā huwa muṭrib bi-ṣawtihi wa-minhā mā yuʿjib bi-ṣūratihi wa-ḥusnihi wa-l-ʿuṣfūr al-ṣawwār huwa alladhī yujību idhā duʿiya min al-ṣarūra wa-ʿuṣfūr al-janna wa-huwa al-khuṭṭāf wa-l-ʿuṣfūr al-buyūt fa-inna fī ṭibāʿihi ikhtilāfan] There are various species of sparrows: some please by the modulation of their voice; others are admirable for their appearance and form. And the sparrow called suar is the one that, when called, answers by chirping. And the sparrow of paradise is the swallow. And the house sparrow is the one that is quarrelsome by nature, etc. And further on: ومن انواعه عصفور الشوك واكثر ماواه السياج وزعم ارسطو ان بينه وبين الحمار عداوة [wa-min anwāʿihi ʿuṣfūr al-shawk wa-akthar maʾwāhu al-siyāj wa-zaʿama Arisṭū anna baynahu wa-bayna al-ḥimār ʿadāwa] Among its species is the sparrow of thorns, which mostly lives in thickets. Aristotle reports that there is mutual hatred between it and the ass. It is plain that the acanthis is meant, which some explain as the goldfinch, others as the siskin, others as the ligurinus. For of it the Philosopher writes in Hist. book 8, chapter 3: Τὰ δὲ τοιάδε ἀκανθοφάγα, ἀκανθὶς, θραυπίς· ἔτι ἡ καλουμένη χρυσομήτρις. Ταῦτα γὰρ πάντα ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκανθῶν νέμεται [Ta de toiade akanthophaga, akanthis, thraupis; eti hē kaloumenē chrysomētris. Tauta gar panta epi tōn akanthōn nemetai]. Gaza rendered this as follows: These seek thorns: the siskin or ligurinus, the goldfinch, and also the one called aurivittis. For all these live on thorns. And in book 9, chapter 1: Ὄνος δὲ, καὶ ἀκανθίδες πολέμιοι· αἱ μὲν γὰρ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀκανθῶν βοητεύουσιν [Onos de, kai akanthides polemioi; hai men gar apo tōn akanthōn boēteuousin] (read II, 724 βιοτεύουσιν [bioteuousin]), ὁ δὲ ἁπαλὰς οὔσας ἐσθίει τὰς ἀκάνθας [ho de hapalas ousas esthiei tas akanthas]: The ass and the acanthides are enemies: for they get their living from thorns, which the ass eats while they are still tender. And the Arabs rightly assign this bird to the sparrow kind. For the acanthis is the same as the acanthyllis, of which Hesychius says: Ἀκανθυλλίς· στρουθοῦ γένος [Akanthyllis; strouthou genos], Acanthyllis, a kind of sparrow. In French it is called serin; whether from the song of the Sirens, as Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] holds in the Ornithology, book 7, chapter 14. There follows in Damir: ومن انواعه القبرة والحسون والبلبل والصعوة والحمرة والعندليب والمكاكي والصافر والتنوط والوصع والابوبراقش col. 148 والقبعة [wa-min anwāʿihi al-qubbara wa-l-ḥassūn wa-l-bulbul wa-l-ṣaʿwa wa-l-ḥumra wa-l-ʿandalīb wa-l-makākī wa-l-ṣāfir wa-l-tanawwuṭ wa-l-waṣaʿ wa-l-abū barāqish wa-l-qubaʿa] Also among its species are the kubbara (the crested lark), and the hassun (I do not find it elsewhere; it seems to be named from its beauty) R1, and the bulbul (the nightingale), and the savva (a little bird with a red crown), and the hamra (a red species of lark), and the andalib (a species of bird similar to the nightingale), and the mechachi (plural of the singular مكا [mukkā] maccha, a bird named from its whistling), and the saphir (which also whistles all night long with its head bent down and its feet fastened to the tree), and the thenavvut (a little bird not unlike this one, named from the nest that it hangs from a tree), and the vvasa (the wren), and the abubarakis (a songbird with long neck and legs, and red, yellow, green and blue feathers, which repeatedly change their colours), and the kubaa (a variegated little bird that draws in its neck when afraid, whence its name). So too among the Hebrews, according to Kimchi in his Lexicon: הוא רב המין לפיכך כלל כל עוף בזה השם, The sparrow has many species: hence this name is applied to all birds. ¶

Nempe ferinum aliquid illi a natura insitum est. Proinde carne vescitur, et pullos suos non cibat rostro in illorum ora inserto. Sed cum avibus non rapacibus hoc illi commune est, quod non habet ungues, quibus laniet, neque rostrum aduncum, et grana ac olera comedit. Cum in arborem incidit, e quatuor digitis tres in anteriora porrigit, et unum retro. At in reliquis avibus bini digiti sunt anterius, et totidem posterius. Mas a foemina distinguitur nigra barba, quam habet ad instar viri, hirci, aut galli. Nusquam est avis, aut fera, aut jumentum in prolem propensius, quodque amet vehementius. Unde est, quod pullos et nidum suum in locis cultis collocat, et sub aedium tectis metu rapacium. Cum desolatur urbs, ex ea excedunt passeres, et simul cum incolis redeunt. Passer ambulare nescit, nec nisi saltuatim fertur. Avis est tam multi coitus, ut quandoque in hora centies coëat, ac proinde brevis est aevi, et plerumque ultra annum non vivit. Dociles habet pullos supra vulgus avium, ita, ut vocati respondeant. Passerem a parasanga redire, testatur Algiahid. His plura liceat addere Alkazuinio, sed quae toti passerum generi conveniunt, non soli domestico.

For something wild has been implanted in it by nature. Accordingly it feeds on flesh, and does not feed its young by inserting its beak into their mouths. But it has this in common with non-raptorial birds, that it has no talons with which to tear, nor a hooked beak, and it eats grain and vegetables. When it alights on a tree, of its four toes it stretches three forward and one backward, whereas in other birds two toes are in front and as many behind. The male is distinguished from the female by a black beard, which it has like a man, a he-goat, or a cock. Nowhere is there a bird, wild beast, or beast of burden more devoted to its offspring or loving it more ardently. Hence it places its young and its nest in inhabited places and under the roofs of houses, for fear of birds of prey. When a city is deserted, the sparrows leave it, and return together with the inhabitants. The sparrow does not know how to walk, and moves only by hopping. It is a bird of such frequent coition that it sometimes mates a hundred times in an hour, and consequently it is short-lived, and generally does not live beyond a year. Its young are more teachable than the common run of birds, so that when called they answer. Algiahid testifies that a sparrow returns from a parasang away. One could add more from Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt], but these things apply to the whole sparrow genus, not to the domestic sparrow alone. ¶

In Hebrew צפצף tsiphseph means to murmur and to mutter, Isa 8:19 and XXIX, 4. This is taken from the twittering of birds. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Es. XXIX.: ציפצוף הוא לשון קול לחש כקול עופות דקין, tsiphtsuph signifies a muttering voice, like the voice of little birds. This is evident from Isa 38:14, where it is applied to the swallow. Yet more properly צפצוף is the chirping of sparrows. Pomarius: צפצף עשה קול צפורים, tsiphseph, he chirped in the manner of sparrows. That this is true is clear from the Arabs, for whom صفصف [ṣafṣaf] tsophtsoph is عصفور [ʿuṣfūr] sparrow, and صفصفة [ṣafṣafa] tsaphtsapha is صوته [ṣawtuhu] its voice. So it is given in the Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]. Hence in Es. II, 726 X, 14. the Hebrew words ופצה פה ומצפצף, And opening the mouth, and chirping, are rendered in Greek thus: Καὶ ἀνοίγων στόμα, καὶ στρουθίζων [Kai anoigōn stoma, kai strouthizōn]. On which Theodoret says: Ἀπὸ τῆς κρατούσης συνηθείας παρὰ ἀνθρώποις εἴρηται τῇ γραφῇ. εἰώθαμεν γὰρ λέγειν περὶ τῶν λίαν ἀγωνιώντων, Οὐκ ἐτόλμησε γρύξαι· οἱ δὲ λοιποί φασιν, Οὐκ ἐτόλμησε τρύσαι. τὸ οὖν Ἀνοίγων στόμα, καὶ στρουθίζων, ἀντὶ τοῦ Οὐ μόνον οὐδεὶς ἀντιπαρατάξασθαί μοι τολμήσει, ἀλλ᾽ οὐδὲ γρύζειν στρουθοῖς παραπλησίως ἐπιχειρήσει [Apo tēs kratousēs synētheias para anthrōpois eirētai tē graphē. eiōthamen gar legein peri tōn lian agōniōntōn, Ouk etolmēse gryxai; hoi de loipoi phasin, Ouk etolmēse trysai. to oun Anoigōn stoma, kai strouthizōn, anti tou Ou monon oudeis antiparataxasthai moi tolmēsei, all' oude gryzein strouthois paraplēsiōs epicheirēsei]. Scripture has taken this from human usage. For we are accustomed to say of those who are in great distress, He did not dare to mutter. Others, however, say, He did not dare to squeak. Thus "opening the mouth and chirping" (there will be no one) is the same as if he said, Not only will no one dare to oppose me, but no one will even attempt to chirp in the manner of sparrows. For it is a continuation of the metaphor. There the Prophet speaks in the words of the king of Assyria, who boasts like a Thraso and compares the peoples he has conquered with a nest of birds which fear of the fowler so dismays that they do not even dare to chirp.
