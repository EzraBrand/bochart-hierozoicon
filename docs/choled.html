II, 435 CHAPTER 35. col. 1021

Probatur, חולד choled non esse Mustelam, ut volunt; sed Talpam. Alia Talpae nomina.

It is proved that חולד choled is not the weasel, as they hold, but the mole. Other names of the mole.

Levit. XI, 29. Hebraeum חלד choled Graeci γαλῆν, et Hieronymus mustelam col. 1022 reddit, et Ionathan כרכושתא carcoustha; et Syrus ܟܟܘܫܬܐ cacaustho, quod id ipsum est (1). In Hesychio quidem κέρκος est ἀρουραῖος μῦς, mus agrestis. II, 436 Et suspicabar olim, ex κέρκος deductum fuisse כרכושתא carcoustha, eadem significatione. Sed כרכושתא carcoustha esse animal, feli quadamtenus simile, probat proverbium Talmudicum in Sanhedrin cap. II. fol. 85. I. כרכושתא ושונרא עבדו הלולא מחרבא דבישא גדא Carcoustha et felis cum faciunt nuptias, extollit se is, qui fuerat infortunatus. Et mustelam esse, ex eo constat, quod Psal. CXIII, 9. Hebraea verba עקרת הבית, sterilem domus, Elia teste, sic redduntur in nonnullis exemplaribus Chaldaeae Paraphrasis, דיתבא מוריקא לאינשי ביתה היך כרכושתא דיתבא מדורא בעקרי ביתא Eam, quae assidet livida viris domus suae, ut carcoustha, quae statuit mansionem in fundamentis domus. Quasi עקרת eo loco sterilem non significet, sed eam, quae prae moerore habitat בעקרי in fundamentis, id est, in infima parte) aedium, quomodo carcoustha solet. Quae ad mustelae κατοικιδίου descriptionem pertinere, patebit ex sequentibus. Itaque, cum חולד choled in Mose sit mustela, ex tot veterum sententia, videbuntur id merito sequi Hebraei omnes, Baal Aruch, R. Selomo, Kimchius, Pomarius, Aquinas: et recentiores Scripturae interpretes, Pagninus, Arias, Castalio, Iunius, Tremellius, etc. vernaculi etiam, quotquot videre licuit, Galli puta, Itali, Angli, Belgae, Germani. Ut Lexicographos taceam, quorum nullus est, quod quidem sciam, qui חולד choled aliter quam mustelam, aut martem reddat, quae est mustelae species. Et Hebraeum Aristeae paraphrastem, qui Graeca haec verba, Κακοποιητικὸς γὰρ ὁ τρόπος ἐστὶ, καὶ γαλῆς, καὶ μυῶν, Hebraice sic reddit:

Lev 11:29 The Hebrew חלד choled the Greeks render γαλῆν [galēn], and Jerome mustela [weasel] col. 1022; Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] renders it כרכושתא carcoustha, and the Syriac ܟܟܘܫܬܐ [ככושתא] cacaustho, which is the very same thing R1. In Hesychius, indeed, κέρκος [kerkos] is ἀρουραῖος μῦς [arouraios mys], field mouse. II, 436 And I once suspected that כרכושתא carcoustha was derived from κέρκος [kerkos], with the same meaning. But that כרכושתא carcoustha is an animal somewhat like the cat is proved by the Talmudic proverb in Sanhedrin ch. 2, fol. 85. 1: כרכושתא ושונרא עבדו הלולא מחרבא דבישא גדא, "When the carcoustha and the cat make a wedding, he who had been unfortunate lifts himself up." And that it is the weasel is clear from the fact that in Ps 113:9 the Hebrew words עקרת הבית, the barren woman of the house, are rendered thus, on the testimony of Elias, in some copies of the Chaldee Paraphrase: דיתבא מוריקא לאינשי ביתה היך כרכושתא דיתבא מדורא בעקרי ביתא, "She who sits pale before the men of her house, like the carcoustha, which sets its dwelling in the foundations of the house." As if עקרת in that place did not mean barren, but she who out of grief dwells בעקרי in the foundations, that is, in the lowest part of the house, as the carcoustha is wont to do. That this fits the description of the κατοικιδίου [katoikidiou] [domestic] weasel will be evident from what follows. Therefore, since חולד choled in Moses is the weasel, according to the opinion of so many ancients, all the Hebrews will seem to follow this with good reason: Baal Aruch [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh], R. Solomon, Kimchi, Pomarius, Aquinas; and the more recent interpreters of Scripture, Pagninus, Arias, Castellio, Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], Tremellius, etc.; also the vernacular ones, as many as I have been able to see, namely the French, Italian, English, Dutch, German. Not to mention the lexicographers, of whom there is none, as far as I know, who renders חולד choled otherwise than as weasel, or martes [marten], which is a species of weasel. And the Hebrew paraphrast of Aristeas, who renders these Greek words, Κακοποιητικὸς γὰρ ὁ τρόπος ἐστὶ, καὶ γαλῆς, καὶ μυῶν [Kakopoiētikos gar ho tropos esti, kai galēs, kai myōn], into Hebrew thus:

II, 437 כי הנה החולד והעכבר הם בטבעם דורכים אל ההשחתה ומועדים תמיד להרע, Ecce enim choled et achbar, (id est, mustela et mus) natura sua ad deperditionem tendunt, et ad malum sunt semper proclives.

II, 437 כי הנה החולד והעכבר הם בטבעם דורכים אל ההשחתה ומועדים תמיד להרע, "For behold, the choled and the achbar (that is, the weasel and the mouse) by their nature tend toward destruction, and are always inclined to evil."

Tamen habeo causas dubitandi, an haec sit vera versio. Quarum prima est, quod palam inde formatum Syrum ܚܘܠܕܐ chuldo significat talpam, non mustelam. Ita statuunt Iohannes Baptista Ferrarius, et Thomas a Novaria in Le-xicis, aut Nomenclatoribus Syriacis, Romae editis, annis 1622. et 1636. (2). Et confirmat Maronitarum doctissimus Gabriel Sionita, qui verba Syri interpretis Proverb. XXIV, 31. ܘܚܦܝܘ ܐܦܘܗܝ ܚܘܠܕܐ Latine ita reddidit: Et cooperuerunt superficiem ejus talpae. Neque vero locus patitur, ut mustelae intelligantur. Agitur enim de agro pigri hominis, qui ita negligitur, ut illum urticae obtegant. Urticae, id est, חרלים, ita habetur in Hebraeo. Sed Syrus legit חלדים, litera Res sede sua mota, et in Daleth mutata; quia sunt literae similes: ac proinde reddidit ܚܘܠܕܐ (3). Qua voce non mustelas, sed talpas significari, res ipsa docet. Neque enim mustelis scatet ager neglectus, sed talpis scatere potest, ut docet Poeta summus lib. 1. Georgicorum,

Yet I have reasons to doubt whether this is the true rendering. The first of them is that the Syriac ܚܘܠܕܐ [חולדא] chuldo, plainly formed from it, means the mole, not the weasel. So Giovanni Battista Ferrari and Thomas a Novaria determine in their Lexicons, or Syriac Nomenclators, published at Rome in the years 1622 and 1636 R2. And this is confirmed by the most learned of the Maronites, Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)], who rendered the words of the Syriac translator in Proverb. XXIV, 31., ܘܚܦܝܘ ܐܦܘܗܝ ܚܘܠܕܐ [וחפיו אפוהי חולדא], into Latin thus: And moles covered its surface. Nor indeed does the passage allow weasels to be understood. For it concerns the field of a lazy man, which is so neglected that nettles cover it. Nettles, that is, חרלים, is what stands in the Hebrew. But the Syriac read חלדים, the letter Resh being moved from its place and changed into Daleth, because the letters are similar; and accordingly he rendered ܚܘܠܕܐ [חולדא] R3. That this word signifies not weasels but moles the matter itself shows. For a neglected field does not swarm with weasels, but it can swarm with moles, as the greatest Poet teaches in book 1 of the Georgics:

— — — — saepe exiguus mus Sub terris posuitque domos, atque horrea fecit: Aut oculis capti fodere cubilia talpae.

— — — — often the tiny mouse has set up its home beneath the ground and built its granaries; or blinded moles have dug their chambers.

Quae maxima est agri pernicies. Unde Simocatta in Epistolis, Τί δῆτα περὶ τῶν ἀσπαλάκων λέξαιμι, φοβερὸν γὰρ τῷ γεωργῷ τὸ κακὸν, καὶ δυσανταγώνι-στον τὸ πολέμιον. De talpis igitur quid dixerim? Aratori enim terribile hoc ma- col. 1023 lum est, et hostis non facile expugnabilis. Proinde hoc animal finxit Stesimbrotus ὑπὸ τῆς Γῆς τυφλωθῆναι, διὰ τὸ φθείρειν τοὺς καρποὺς, a Tellure fuisse excaecatum, eo quod fruges corrumperet. Ita Suidas et Apostolius in Τυφλότερος ἀσπάλακος.

This is the greatest bane of a field. Hence Simocatta in his Epistles: Τί δῆτα περὶ τῶν ἀσπαλάκων λέξαιμι, φοβερὸν γὰρ τῷ γεωργῷ τὸ κακὸν, καὶ δυσανταγώνιστον τὸ πολέμιον [Ti dēta peri tōn aspalakōn lexaimi, phoberon gar tō geōrgō to kakon, kai dysantagōniston to polemion]. What then shall I say about moles? For to the ploughman this col. 1023 evil is terrible, and the enemy not easily overcome. Accordingly Stesimbrotus fabled that this animal ὑπὸ τῆς Γῆς τυφλωθῆναι, διὰ τὸ φθείρειν τοὺς καρποὺς [hypo tēs Gēs typhlōthēnai, dia to phtheirein tous karpous], had been blinded by Earth, because it ruined the crops. So Suidas and Apostolius under Τυφλότερος ἀσπάλακος [Typhloteros aspalakos].

Praeterea non Syris solum, sed et Arabibus خلد chuld, vel chold, est talpa. Quod, ut caetera taceam, aperte docent ejus descriptiones. Alcamus II, 438 الخلد الغارة العميا Alchold, mus caecus. Liber, cui titulus est الفلاحة الفارسية Agricultura Persica apud Abenbitarem, الخلد دابة عميا تحت الارض تاكل عروق الشجر ويحب رايحة البصل والكراث وتخرج من حجرتها في طلب الرايحتها Alchold, animal coecum, sub terra arborum radices comedens, quodque cepae et porri odore a) ita delectatur, ut eo allectum exeat e latibulo suo (4). Et Giahid, الخلد دويبة عميا صما لا تعرف ما بين يديها الا بالشم تخرج من حجرها وهي تعلم انها لا سمع لها ولا بصر فتنفتح فاها وتقف عند حجرها فياتي الذباب فيسقط علي شدقيها ويمر بين لحييها فيستد خله جوفها بنفسها وهي تعرض لذلك في الساعات التي يكون فيها الذباب اكثر Alchold est animalculum caecum, et surdum, quodque non novit objecta, nisi per odoratum. Cum e latibulo suo exit, auditu et visu se carere non ignarus, aperto ore stat prope latibulum, et venientes muscae in buccam ejus incidunt, et ambulant inter maxillas, et per ejus halitum in ventrem attrahuntur. Ac proinde os dilatat iis horis, quibus muscae sunt frequentiores. Haec ille, quibus الخلد alchold, id est, talpam, non solum visu, sed et auditu privat. At aliter statuit Damir, cujus verba sunt: كل حيوان له عينان الا الخلد وانما خلق كذلك لانه ترابي جعل الله له الارض كالما للسمك وغذاوه في بطنها وليس له في ظاهرها قوة ولا نشاط ولما لم يكن له بصر عوضه الله حدة حاسة الشمع فيدرك الوطي col. 1024 الخفي من مسافة بعيدة فانا احس بذلك جعل يحفر الارض Omne animal habet oculos praeter alchold, qui creatus est sine oculis; quia in terra degit, quam assignavit ipsi Deus, ut aquam piscibus: ita, ut in ventre terrae sit alimentum ejus, et extra eam robore et alacritate careat. II, 439 Sed visus defectum compensavit Deus acumine sensus aurium, quibus etiam e longinquo spatio suspenso gradu incedentes percipit, et, quamprimum percepit, in terram reconditur. Quibus haec Plinii consona: Liquidius audiunt talpae obrutae terra, tam denso atque surdo Naturae elemento. Sed ex nonnullorum sententia duplex est talpae genus, nempe خلد chold, et زباب zebab: quarum illa visu, haec auditu caret. Damir in capite الفار, id est, De mure, ومنها اليرابوع والزباب والخلد فالزباب صم والخلد اعمي Etiam in speciebus murium sunt aljarbuo (seu choerogryllius), et zebab, et chold; quorum zebab est surdus, et chold caecus. Multo plura possem addere: sed ex his patet plus satis, tam Arabibus, quam Syris, chold, vel chuld esse talpam.

Moreover, not only for the Syrians but also for the Arabs خلد [khuld] chuld, or chold, is the mole. This is plainly shown, to say nothing of the rest, by the descriptions of it. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] II, 438: الخلد الغارة العميا [al-khuld al-ghāra al-ʿamyā] Alchold, the blind mouse. The book entitled الفلاحة الفارسية [al-Filāḥa al-Fārisiyya] Persian Agriculture, cited by Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār]: الخلد دابة عميا تحت الارض تاكل عروق الشجر ويحب رايحة البصل والكراث وتخرج من حجرتها في طلب الرايحتها [al-khuld dābba ʿamyā taḥt al-arḍ taʾkul ʿurūq al-shajar wa-yuḥibb rāyiḥat al-baṣal wa-l-kurrāth wa-takhruj min ḥujratihā fī ṭalab al-rāyiḥatihā] Alchold, a blind animal that eats the roots of trees under the ground, and that takes such delight in the smell of onion and leek a) that, lured by it, it comes out of its hiding-place R4. And Giahid: الخلد دويبة عميا صما لا تعرف ما بين يديها الا بالشم تخرج من حجرها وهي تعلم انها لا سمع لها ولا بصر فتنفتح فاها وتقف عند حجرها فياتي الذباب فيسقط علي شدقيها ويمر بين لحييها فيستد خله جوفها بنفسها وهي تعرض لذلك في الساعات التي يكون فيها الذباب اكثر [al-khuld duwaybba ʿamyā ṣammāʾ lā taʿrif mā bayn yadayhā illā bi-l-shamm takhruj min ḥujrihā wa-hiya taʿlam annahā lā samʿ lahā wa-lā baṣar fa-tanfatiḥ fāhā wa-taqif ʿind ḥujrihā fa-yaʾtī al-dhubāb fa-yasquṭ ʿalā shidqayhā wa-yamurr bayn laḥyayhā fa-yastad khalahu jawfahā bi-nafsihā wa-hiya taʿriḍ li-dhālika fī al-sāʿāt allatī yakūn fīhā al-dhubāb akthar] Alchold is a small animal, blind and deaf, which does not recognize what is in front of it except by smell. When it comes out of its hiding-place, well aware that it lacks hearing and sight, it stands with open mouth near its hiding-place; and flies that come fall into its mouth and walk between its jaws, and are drawn by its breath into its belly. And so it opens its mouth wide at those hours when flies are most numerous. So he, who deprives الخلد [al-khuld] alchold, that is, the mole, not only of sight but also of hearing. But Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] judges otherwise; his words are: كل حيوان له عينان الا الخلد وانما خلق كذلك لانه ترابي جعل الله له الارض كالما للسمك وغذاوه في بطنها وليس له في ظاهرها قوة ولا نشاط ولما لم يكن له بصر عوضه الله حدة حاسة الشمع فيدرك الوطي [kull ḥayawān lahu ʿaynān illā al-khuld wa-innamā khuliqa ka-dhālika li-annahu turābī jaʿala Allāh lahu al-arḍ ka-l-māʾ li-l-samak wa-ghidhāʾuhu fī baṭnihā wa-laysa lahu fī ẓāhirihā quwwa wa-lā nashāṭ wa-lammā lam yakun lahu baṣar ʿawwaḍahu Allāh ḥiddat ḥāssat al-shamʿ fa-yudrik al-waṭʾ] col. 1024 الخفي من مسافة بعيدة فانا احس بذلك جعل يحفر الارض [al-khafī min masāfa baʿīda fa-anā aḥass bi-dhālika jaʿala yaḥfur al-arḍ] Every animal has eyes except alchold, which was created without eyes, because it lives in the earth, which God assigned to it as water to the fishes, so that its food is in the belly of the earth, and outside it it lacks strength and vigour. II, 439 But God compensated for the lack of sight by the keenness of the sense of hearing, by which it perceives even from a great distance those who walk with stealthy step; and as soon as it perceives them, it hides itself in the earth. With this agrees the statement of Pliny: Moles, though buried in the earth, so dense and deaf an element of Nature, hear more clearly. But in the opinion of some there are two kinds of mole, namely خلد [khuld] chold and زباب [zabāb] zebab, of which the former lacks sight, the latter hearing. Damir, in the chapter الفار [al-fār], that is, On the mouse: ومنها اليرابوع والزباب والخلد فالزباب صم والخلد اعمي [wa-minhā al-yarābūʿ wa-l-zabāb wa-l-khuld fa-l-zabāb ṣumm wa-l-khuld aʿmā] Among the species of mice there are also aljarbuo (or choerogryllius), zebab, and chold; of which zebab is deaf and chold blind. I could add much more, but from this it is more than sufficiently clear that for the Arabs as well as the Syrians, chold or chuld is the mole.

Tertio, in Talmude Babylonico tractatu Moëd Katon cap. 1. fol. 6. b. in Misnae verbis, צדין את האישות ואת העכברים, Venantur licite talpas et mures, in his (inquam) verbis האישות, id est, talpa, vel talpae (quod omnes agnoscunt, et nos infra pluribus docebimus) in Talmude Hierosolymitano redditur, חולדה chulda. Quod quidem in eo non legi; quia liber non est ad manum: sed ita asserit Glossa ad locum Moëd Katon, quem nuper citavimus, אישות מפירש בירושלמי חולדה Vox אישות in Hierosolymitana Talmude redditur חולדה chulda. Ibi igitur חולדה chulda est talpa, non mustela.

Third, in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Moëd Katon, ch. 1, fol. 6b, in the words of the Mishnah צדין את האישות ואת העכברים, One may lawfully hunt moles and mice, in these words (I say) האישות, that is, the mole or moles (as all acknowledge, and as we shall show at greater length below), is rendered in the Jerusalem Talmud as חולדה chulda. I have not, indeed, read this in it myself, since the book is not at hand; but so the Gloss asserts on the passage of Moëd Katon just cited: אישות מפירש בירושלמי חולדה The word אישות is rendered in the Jerusalem Talmud as חולדה chulda. There, therefore, חולדה chulda is the mole, not the weasel.

Quarto, Levit. XI, 29. חולד choled ordinem ducit in iis animalibus, quae continentur nomine שרץ. Horum porro duo propria sunt. Unum, quod, propter exiguitatem pedum, repere videntur potius, quam ambulare. Alterum, quod in immensum sobolescunt. Id enim propriè significat verbum שרץ, ac proinde piscibus tribuitur Gen. I, 20. et ranis Exod. VIII, 3. et Psal. CV, 30. et καθ' ὑπερβολὴν Israelitis in Aegypto Exod. I, 7. quia in illa servitute Dei be-nedictio eorum numerum auxit, pene dixero, supra omnem fidem. Has autem notas nemo nescit, mustelis minus convenire, quam talpis, quarum crura sunt brevissima, et (alicubi auctore Plinio) tam numerosa est multitudo, ut tota arva subruant.

Fourth, in Lev 11:29 חולד choled heads the list of those animals which are comprised under the name שרץ. Two things, moreover, are characteristic of these. One, that because of the shortness of their feet they seem to creep rather than walk. The other, that they multiply beyond measure. For this is what the verb שרץ properly signifies, and accordingly it is applied to fishes in Gen 1:20, to frogs in Exod 8:3 and Ps 105:30, and, καθ' ὑπερβολὴν [kath' hyperbolēn], to the Israelites in Egypt, Exod 1:7, because in that servitude God's blessing increased their number, I might almost say, beyond all belief. Everyone knows that these marks suit weasels less than moles, whose legs are very short and whose multitude (on the authority of Pliny somewhere) is so great that they undermine whole fields.

Quinto, si חולד choled est talpa, recte iunguntur in Mose חולד choled, et עכבר achbar, id est, talpa et mus: cum talpa sit muri congener, adeoque muris species, si Arabibus credimus: et Hesychio, cui Λὼτ est ἀσπάλαξ, talpa, seu μῦς τυφλὸς ὁ τὴν γῆν τρυπῶν, mus caecus, terram penetrans: et Scholiasti Aristophanis in Acharnan. ubi σκάλοπας definit μύας τινὰς, οὓς φαμεν σπά-λακας, mures quosdam, quos vocamus talpas: et Oecumenio in 2 Petri I, 9. ubi μυωπάζειν vult idem esse, quod τυφλώττειν, caecutire, ἀπὸ τῶν ὑπὸ τὴν γῆν μυῶν τυφλῶν εἰς ἅπαν διατελούντων, a muribus, qui sub terra omnino caeci degunt, II, 440 id est, a talpis. Proinde juxta Scaligerum b) talpa est in genere murium. Aliis talpa solum est animal, muri simile. Suidas, Ἔστι δὲ ὁ ἀσπάλαξ ζῶον μυῒ ἐμφερὲς, ὑπὸ γῆν διάγον, τῷ ὀφθαλμῷ ἐστερημένον· Talpa est animal, muri simile, sub terra degens, oculis privatum. Proinde junguntur in Talmu- col. 1025 dis loco, quem citavimus, צדין אישות ועכברים, Venantur talpas et mures. At mustela plane diversum est animal, mure majus et validius, eique tam infensum, quam felis. Sixth, the verb חלד chalad (from which חולד choled is derived) means to penetrate and to dig under. It is properly said of the earth, as these examples show. The Jerusalem Talmud, in Kilayim, last chapter, as the distinguished Buxtorf cites it: מחליד הקב״ה לפניהם את הארץ God, the Holy One, Blessed be He, digging under the earth before them. And the Babylonian Talmud, in Bava Batra fol. 19b, of vines: מפני שמחלידין את הקרקע ומעלין עפר תיחוח Because they dig under the ground and loosen the soil. And the Syriac translator of the hedgehog in Es. XXXIV, 15.: ܘܢܚܠܕ ܘܢܥܘܠ ܒܛܠܠܗ [ונחלד ונעול בטללה] And it will dig out, and penetrate into its shade. From this it is transferred metaphorically to other things. II, 441 Thus חלד, of metals, is to contract rust; and חלודה, rust, in Jonathan חלדות, Num. XXXI, 22., because it penetrates metals and eats into them to their innermost parts. Likewise gangrene is called ܡܣܟܝܢܘܬܐ [מסכינותא] in the Syriac paraphrase, 2 Tim. To the same belongs the fact that in 2 Tim. For it speaks there of those who, as if making for the depths of houses, search and scrutinize everything most hidden. Accordingly the Arabic version published at Paris rendered it with a not dissimilar metaphor: الذين يغوصون في المنازل [alladhīna yaghūṣūna fī al-manāzil] who plunge themselves into houses. Et Arrianus in Alexandro, Χάλυβες πρῶτοι ἀνθρώπων αἰτίαν ἔχουσι χαλκεύ-σασθαι σίδηρον· Chalybibus crimini datur, quod primi ferrum cuderint. Et haec nomina omnino alludunt ad verbum חלד chalad; quod, cum de terra dicitur, fodere significat, ut pluribus supra c) probatum. Iam quis non videt, eodem commode revocari nomen חולד choled, pro talpa, quae ad id videtur nata et instituta, ut indesinenti labore terram subruat et suffodiat. Ideo talpam nemo definit, qui non hoc ponat in parte definitionis. Sic apud Hesychium in vocibus Λὼτ et Σκάλοψ legitur, esse μῦς τυφλὸς ὁ τὴν γῆν τρυ-πῶν, mus caecus, qui terram perforat; et ζῶον γεώρυχον τυφλὸν, caecum animal, terram fodiens. Epiphanius in Adamitis statim initio, Ἀσπάλακα κα-λοῦσί τι ζῶον ἐν τῇ γῇ κάτω ἐμφωλεῦον, τετράπουν, ὅπερ ὀρύττει τὴν γῆν, etc. Talpam vocant animal quoddam quadrupes, latebras habens in terrae penetralibus, quam effodit, etc. In Hieronymo in Esaiam capite secundo, Est animal absque oculis, quod semper terram fodit, et humum egerit. In R. Selomone ibidem, חפרפרות מיני שרצים שחופרין הארץ שקורין טלפא בלעז, Chaphorpheroth sunt reptilium species, quae terram fodiunt, et vulgari lingua vocantur talpae. Et in Moed Katon fol. 6. b. בריה שאין לה עיניים חופרת בקרקע Creatura est, oculis carens, et terram fodiens. Proinde naturae consulti notant, eo pertinere, quod rostro est acutissimo. Et priorum crurum pars anterior hoc habet peculiare, quod ossibus constat geminis et brevissimis, quaeque in os humeri statim inseruntur, ut sic fortius fodiant. Qua ipsa de causa priores etiam pedes et digiti non deorsum, sed ad latera spectant; et volam habent bene latam, et ungues robustissimos, et ad corporis modulum longe majores, quam in reliquis animalibus. Unde est, quod tam facile tamque expedite terram fodiunt, ut fidem id prope superet: quod rerum nostrarum damno quotidie experimur. Itaque etiam alterum talparum nomen חפרפרות, Es. II, 20. est a fossione sumtum. Nam, ut חלד chalad, ita etiam חפר chaphar, fodere significat: quod nemo nescit. Unde geminatum חפרפרות significationem intendit, ut eo nomine designetur animal, quod in terrae fossione totum est. Sed et eodem revocandum Graecum σπάλαξ, vel ἀσπάλαξ, cujus vera origo doctos latuit hactenus. Nam, qui παρὰ τὸ σπᾶν, id est, a trahendo deducunt, nugas agunt. Quin σπάλαξ, per metathesin factum ex σκάλοψ, quod vetus fuit talpae vocabulum apud Athenienses, ut ex Nicocle scimus: Σκάλοψ, ὁ σπάλαξ λέγεται παρὰ col. 1027 Ἀττικοῖς, ὡς λέγει Νικοκλῆς. Ita Etymologus. Et confirmat Aristoteles in Acharnan. Ubi pro talpis habet σκάλοπας. Ad quem locum Scholiastes, Σκά-λοπας] μύας τινὰς, οὓς φαμεν σπάλακας. Dicitur autem σκάλοψ, non II, 443 quasi κεκαλυμμένους ἔχων τοὺς ὦπας, ut nugatur Etymologus: sed ἀπὸ τοῦ σκάλ-λειν, ἢ σκαλεύειν, a fodiendo; et voce composita σκαλόπεζα, quasi fossorios habens pedes, ut apud Poetam ἀργυρόπεζα Θέτις, argenteis pedibus Thetis. Hesychius, Σκάλοψ· σκαλόπεζα, ἀσπάλαξ. Ita legendum, non σκαλόπετα, quomodo habent nostrae editiones. Rursus, παρὰ τοῦ σκάλοπος, a talpa fit σκαλοπιὰ pro talparum latibulo, vel terrae cumulo, a talpis excitato. Et sic omnino sumendum in his Theophrasti Historiae libri septimi capite undecimo, de phasgani (seu gladioli) radicibus, Πολλὰς δὲ εὑρίσκουσι παρὰ ταῖς σκαλοπιαῖς· χαίρει γὰρ, καὶ συλλέγεται τὸ ζῶον. Multas reperiunt prope scalopias, seu talparum foramina; illas enim appetit et colligit hoc animal. Pessime igitur interpretes, Permultas juxta eam scolopendras reperiunt. Neque verum, quod alii volunt, τὴν σκαλοπιὰν idem esse, quod τὴν σκάλοπα. Ac si quis τὴν μυρμη-κιὰν, formicarum latibulum idem esse vellet, quod τὴν μύρμηκα, formicam. Quod prope omiseram, etiam in quadam Galliae parte talpa vocatur foyon, ut fovir Gallis est fodere, quod observat Capellus ὁ μακαρίτης Sacrae Criticae pagina 382. Et Germanica talpae plura nomina d), eodem pertinentia, refert Gesnerus in Talpa, pagina 932. Itaque id commune est Hebraeis, Graecis, Germanis et nonnullis Gallorum, ut a fodiendo talpam nominent.

And Arrian in the Alexander: Χάλυβες πρῶτοι ἀνθρώπων αἰτίαν ἔχουσι χαλκεύσασθαι σίδηρον [Chalybes prōtoi anthrōpōn aitian echousi chalkeusasthai sidēron]; the Chalybes are charged with having been the first to forge iron. And Ammianus, book 22: the Chalybes, by whom iron was first dug out and worked. Yet others attribute the same invention to the Cyclopes. Pliny, book 7, chapter 56: Some think that the Chalybes, others that the Cyclopes II, 442 first showed the craft of working bronze. However that may be, from the frequent digging of metals the land of the Chalybes could be called Chaldia, and its inhabitants Chaldi, Chaldii and Chaldaei. For that the ancients wrote it in all these ways is attested by Eustathius in his Notes on Dionysius Periegetes, page 101 b. And these names clearly allude to the verb חלד chalad, which, when said of the earth, means to dig, as was proved at length above c). Now who does not see that the name חולד choled can fittingly be referred to the same root, for the mole, which seems born and designed for this very purpose: to undermine and dig beneath the earth with unceasing labour? That is why no one defines the mole without making this part of the definition. Thus in Hesychius, under the words Λὼτ [Lōt] and Σκάλοψ [Skalops], we read that it is μῦς τυφλὸς ὁ τὴν γῆν τρυπῶν [mys typhlos ho tēn gēn trypōn], a blind mouse that bores through the earth; and ζῶον γεώρυχον τυφλὸν [zōon geōrychon typhlon], a blind animal that digs the earth. Epiphanius, on the Adamites, right at the beginning: Ἀσπάλακα καλοῦσί τι ζῶον ἐν τῇ γῇ κάτω ἐμφωλεῦον, τετράπουν, ὅπερ ὀρύττει τὴν γῆν [Aspalaka kalousi ti zōon en tē gē katō emphōleuon, tetrapoun, hoper oryttei tēn gēn], etc. They call mole a certain four-footed animal that has its lairs in the depths of the earth, which it digs out, etc. In Jerome on Isaiah, chapter 2: It is an animal without eyes, which is always digging the earth and throwing up the soil. In R. Solomon on the same passage: חפרפרות מיני שרצים שחופרין הארץ שקורין טלפא בלעז, Chaphorpheroth are kinds of creeping things that dig the earth, and in the vernacular are called talpae [moles]. And in Moed Katan fol. 6b: בריה שאין לה עיניים חופרת בקרקע It is a creature that lacks eyes and digs in the earth. Accordingly, naturalists note that its very sharp snout serves this purpose. And the forward part of its forelegs has this peculiarity, that it consists of paired and very short bones, which are inserted directly into the humerus, so that they may dig more powerfully. For the same reason the forefeet and toes point not downward but sideways; and they have a quite broad palm and very strong claws, far larger in proportion to the size of the body than in other animals. Hence they dig the earth so easily and so swiftly as almost to pass belief, as we experience daily to the damage of our property. Therefore the other name for moles, חפרפרות, Isa 2:20, is also taken from digging. For, like חלד chalad, חפר chaphar also means to dig, as everyone knows. Hence the reduplicated חפרפרות intensifies the meaning, so that the name designates an animal wholly given over to digging the earth. But the Greek σπάλαξ [spalax] or ἀσπάλαξ [aspalax] must also be referred to the same idea; its true origin has so far escaped the learned. For those who derive it παρὰ τὸ σπᾶν [para to span], that is, from drawing, talk nonsense. Rather, σπάλαξ [spalax] was formed by metathesis from σκάλοψ [skalops], which was the old word for the mole among the Athenians, as we know from Nicocles: Σκάλοψ, ὁ σπάλαξ λέγεται παρὰ col. 1027 Ἀττικοῖς, ὡς λέγει Νικοκλῆς [Skalops, ho spalax legetai para Attikois, hōs legei Nikoklēs]. So the Etymologicum. And Aristotle confirms it in the Acharnians, where he has σκάλοπας [skalopas] for moles. On this passage the Scholiast: Σκάλοπας] μύας τινὰς, οὓς φαμεν σπάλακας [Skalopas] myas tinas, hous phamen spalakas]. It is called σκάλοψ [skalops], however, not II, 443 as if κεκαλυμμένους ἔχων τοὺς ὦπας [kekalymmenous echōn tous ōpas], as the Etymologicum idly claims, but ἀπὸ τοῦ σκάλλειν, ἢ σκαλεύειν [apo tou skallein, ē skaleuein], from digging; and by the compound word σκαλόπεζα [skalopeza], as if having digging feet, as in the Poet ἀργυρόπεζα Θέτις [argyropeza Thetis], silver-footed Thetis. Hesychius: Σκάλοψ· σκαλόπεζα, ἀσπάλαξ [Skalops; skalopeza, aspalax]. So it should be read, not σκαλόπετα [skalopeta], as our editions have it. Again, from σκάλοψ [skalops], mole, comes σκαλοπιὰ [skalopia] for a mole's lair, or a heap of earth thrown up by moles. And it must certainly be taken so in these words of Theophrastus, History, book 7, chapter 11, on the roots of the phasganon (or gladiolus): Πολλὰς δὲ εὑρίσκουσι παρὰ ταῖς σκαλοπιαῖς· χαίρει γὰρ, καὶ συλλέγεται τὸ ζῶον [Pollas de heuriskousi para tais skalopiais; chairei gar, kai syllegetai to zōon]. They find many near the scalopiae, or mole-holes; for this animal seeks them and gathers them. Very badly, then, do the translators render it: They find a great many scolopendras near it. Nor is it true, as others hold, that τὴν σκαλοπιὰν [tēn skalopian] is the same as τὴν σκάλοπα [tēn skalopa]. As if someone held that τὴν μυρμηκιὰν [tēn myrmēkian], the ants' nest, were the same as τὴν μύρμηκα [tēn myrmēka], the ant. What I had nearly omitted: in a certain part of France too the mole is called foyon, as fovir is French for to dig, as the late Cappel (ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs]) observes in the Critica Sacra, page 382. And Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], under Talpa, page 932, records several German names for the mole d) that belong to the same idea. Thus it is common to the Hebrews, the Greeks, the Germans and some of the French to name the mole from digging.

Cum ex iis, quae dicta sunt, liquido constare putem, חולד choled Mosi fuisse talpam, aeque ac Syrum ܚܘܠܕܐ chulda, et Arabicum خلد chuld, vel chold: non tamen dissimulandum, apud Talmudicos doctores חולדה chulda plerumque esse mustelam. Quod haec loca palam faciunt. In tractatu Cholin ubi supra, חלדה הר בחלדה הדרה בעיקרי בתים דמכסי הכא Cultri immersio in mactatione Chalada dicitur, phrasi mutuata ex chulda, quae degit in fundamentis aedium, ut ibi lateat. Non quadrat in talpam, quae non nisi in agro degit, sed in mustelam κατοικίδιον. Cui passim opponitur חולדת סנאים, aut חולדת הסניים, mustela ruborum, id est, sylvestris: ut in Kilaim cap. 8. fol. 32. a. b. הקופד וחולדת הסניים, hericius et mustela ruborum. Ubi Glossa Maimonidis, וחולדת הסניים חיה דומה לחולדה ממיני השועלים, id est, Mustela ruborum fera est similis vulgari mustelae inter vulpium species. Et in Bava kama cap. 4. fol. 80. 1. מגדלין כלבין כופרין וחתולין וקופין וחולדות סנאים מפני שעושין לנקראת הבית, Nutrire lices canes villaticos, et feles, et simias, et mustelas ruborum (quae vocantur חולדות), quia factae sunt ad perfodiendas aedes. Et paullo post, אמר ר׳ יהודה שרצא חרצא ואיכא דאמר חרוא דקטני שקי דרעיא ביני וורדיני, Dixit R. Iuda, Est reptile (CHARTSA) exscindens. Et sunt, qui dicunt (CHAR-ZA) perforans, quod gracilia habet crura, et pascit inter dumeta. Sic apud Plinium II, 444 libri vigesimi noni capite quarto, Mustelarum duo genera: alterum sylvestre. Distant magnitudine. Graeci vocant ictidas, etc. Haec autem, quae in domibus nostris oberrat, etc. Id ipsum, quod diximus, חולדה chulda, Talmudicis fere mustelam esse, non talpam, sequitur ex eo, quod hoc animal pueros strangulare volunt, quod de talpa nemo dixerit. In quam rem affertur locus ex tractatu Thaanith cap. 1. fol. 8. 1. בא וראה כמה גדולים בעלי אמנה מניין מחולדה ובור ומה המאמין בחולדה ובור כך המאמין בהקב״ה על אחת כמה וכמה, Veni, et vide, quomodo in magnis fidei testibus censeantur chulda et puteus. Et quis chul- col. 1028 dae et puteo fidit, qui Deo Sancto Benedicto non fidat multo magis? Ad quem locum hanc fabellam refert Glossator Talmudicus: מחולדה ובור שהמיתו שני בני אדם מצוי הוא באגדה מעשה בבחור אחד שנתן אמונתו לריבה אחת שישאנה אמרה מי העיד והיה שם בור א׳ וחולדה אמר הבחור בור וחולדה עדים בדבר לימים עבר על אמונתו ונשא אחרת והוליד שני בנים אחד נפל לבור ומת ואחד נשכתו חולדה ומת אמרה לו אשתו מה מעשה הוא זה שבנינו מתים במיתה משונה ואמר לה כך וכך היה מעשה, Chulda et puteus, a quibus duo homines letho dati. Historia habetur in Aggada de juvene quodam, qui, cum puellae cui- dam fidem suam obstringeret, fore, ut eam duceret in uxorem, Quis erit testis, inquit illa? Ibi autem erant puteus et chulda (mustela). Et dixit juvenis, Puteus et chulda rei gestae testes erunt. Sed post aliquot dies is idem, violata fide, aliam duxit, ex qua cum peperisset duos filios: horum unus in puteum delapsus exspira- vit; alter quoque a chulda, seu mustela, morsus obiit. Tum ab uxore interro- gatus, quamobrem ambo filii violenta morte obiissent, narravit illi, quomodo res se haberet. Ejusdem historiae multo plures circumstantias refert Baal Aruch, et in his marem fuisse puerum, et jam trimestrem a chulda fuisse strangulatum. Verba sunt: וילדה בן זכר כשהגיע לג׳ חרשים חנקתו חולדה Et illa peperit fili- um marem, quem chulda strangulavit, cum tertium mensem jam attigisset. Ita de الزبزب zebzeb, mustelae majoris, seu viverrae specie, Bagdadi capta anno Hegirae 304. suprae) narraverunt Arabes انه ياكل اطفالهم eam infantes suos vorasse, et virorum manus, et mulierum mammas momordisse. His addenda, quae Talmudici afferunt in Collatione vocum חולדה chulda, vel חולד choled, et חלד cheled: quarum haec orbem significat Ps. XLIX, 2. ubi homines vocantur יושבי חלד, incolae cheled, id est, terreni orbis. Et doctores inter se disputant, quorsum terra dicatur חלד cheled, et quaerunt, quid commune habeat cum חולד choled, animali. Et in Talmude Babylonico tractatu Cholin cap. 9. fol. 127. a. quidam respondet, כל שיש ביבשה יש בים חוץ מן החולדה, Quidquid animalium est in terra, esse etiam in mari, sola chulda excepta. Quod ne putetur II, 445 dici gratis, en bellam historiam, quam ex Rabbinorum scriptis profert doctissimus Buxtorfius in Lexico Talmudico ad vocem חלד! Comitia fuerunt principum mundi de administratione recta. Princeps maris conquestus est, se non habere subditos sufficientes. Permissum ipsi proinde fuit, ut ex terra peteret plures, modo de alimento provideret. Conjecit omnia animalia terrestria in mare, cum mandato, ut illic quoque suam speciem propagarent. Factum est: unde dicitur, omnia animalia terrestria esse quoque in mari, nisi quod quaedam in monstrosam formam conversa sunt. Ad mustelam cum pervenisset, ea, in littore stans, dixit principi maris: Quorsum me denuo in mare projicies? Nunquid vides, me in mari jam esse? Id autem dixit, effigiem suam in mari ostendens. Princeps contentus mustelam dimisit. Hinc dicunt Talmudici, omnia terrestria in mari esse praeter mustelam, quae astutia sua evasit. Et terra dicitur חלד cheled, quod propria remanserit mustelae, quae vocatur חולדה chulda, vel חולד choled. In Talmude Hierosolymitano, initio capitis De octo reptilibus, tractatur eadem quaestio, et a nonnullis respondetur id ipsum, quod jam diximus, terram et terrenos homines cum chulda comparari; לפי שכל מה שיש ביבשה יש בים והרבה מינין יש col. 1029 בים מה שאין כן ביבשה ואין חולדה בים eo quod, cum omnia, quae sunt in terra, sint etiam in mari, et multae sint in mari species, quae non sunt in terra, tamen chulda non est in mari. Altera responsio haec est: חולדה זו גוררת ומנחת ואינה וודעת למי מנחת כך כל באי העולם גוררין ומניחין ואינן יודעין למי מניחין כמו שנאמר יצבור ולא ידע מי אספם, Chulda multa attrahit et relinquit; ignara, cui relinquat. Ita quoque mundani omnes multa attrahunt, et relinquunt; nescientes, cui ea sint relicturi. Quomodo dicitur Psal. CXXXIX, 2. „Congregabit, et nesciet, quis ea sit collecturus“. Ita haec citat Aruch, quae, utut nugacissima, tamen ad scopum nostrum faciunt. Docent enim, voce chulda a plerisque Talmudicis non significari talpam. Neque enim talpa, quae caeca est, imaginem suam in mari vidisse diceretur, et aliis ostendisse. Neque in latibulis suis quidquam reponere legitur et recondere. Sed mustelae hoc ingenium est, de qua scribit ubi supra Abdollatiph Bagdadensis, في طبعه انه يسرق ما وجد من فضة او ذهب كما يفعل الغار E natura ejus est, ut suffuretur, quidquid argenti (aut auri) reperit, quomodo mures solent. Itaque de mustela ibidem haec historia narratur: رجل صاد فرخا منها وحبسه في قفص بحيث تراه امه فلما اراته ذهبت ثم جات وفي فمها دينار فالقته بين يديه كانها تفدي ولدها فلم يتركه لها فذهبت وعادت بدينار اخر حتي بلغت من العدد خمسا فلما II, 446 ان لا يطلق لها ذهبت وعادت بخرقة كانها تشير الي فراغ حاصلها فلم يكترث بها فلما رات ذلك عادت الي دينار فاخذته منه فخشي الرجل من ذلك فاطلق لها ولدها Quidam mustelae catulum cepit, et cavea inclusit, ita, ut videret mater. Quae, cum illum vidisset, abiit, et rediit cum denario in ore, quem coram eo projecit, tanquam catulum redemptura. Sed, cum is non dimisisset, profecta rursus alios attulit denarios, donec ad quinarium numerum pervenisset. Et, cum vidit, catulum suum nondum dimitti, pannum etiam attulit (in quo erant nummi), ut doceret, se totum peculium exhausisse. Cumque se non respici animadvertit, ad nummos rediit, ut illos reciperet. Sed vir ille, metu perculsus, restituit ei catulum. Patet igitur, a Talmudicis voce חולדה chulda plerumque significari mustelam. Sed illorum auctoritas hac in parte nos parum movet, cum ipsi sibi non constent, et apud Arabes et Syros chuld, col. 1030 vel chulda sit talpa; et tam origo vocis, quam Mosaici textus circumstantiae palam doceant, vocem ita esse sumendam.

Since I think it is clear from what has been said that the חולד choled of Moses was the mole, just like the Syriac ܚܘܠܕܐ [חולדא] chulda and the Arabic خلد [khuld] chuld or chold, it must nevertheless not be concealed that among the Talmudic doctors חולדה chulda is for the most part the weasel. The following passages make this plain. In the tractate Cholin, cited above: חלדה הר בחלדה הדרה בעיקרי בתים דמכסי הכא, "The plunging of the knife in slaughtering is called Chalada, a phrase borrowed from the chulda, which lives in the foundations of houses so as to lie hidden there." This does not fit the mole, which lives only in the fields, but rather the domestic weasel, κατοικίδιον [katoikidion]. To it is everywhere opposed חולדת סנאים or חולדת הסניים, the weasel of the brambles, that is, the wild one: as in Kilaim ch. 8, fol. 32a b, הקופד וחולדת הסניים, "the hedgehog and the weasel of the brambles." There the Gloss of Maimonides says: וחולדת הסניים חיה דומה לחולדה ממיני השועלים, that is, "The weasel of the brambles is a wild animal similar to the common weasel, among the species of foxes." And in Bava kama ch. 4, fol. 80. 1: מגדלין כלבין כופרין וחתולין וקופין וחולדות סנאים מפני שעושין לנקראת הבית, "It is permitted to keep farm dogs, and cats, and apes, and weasels of the brambles" (which are called חולדות), "because they are made for boring through houses." And a little later: אמר ר׳ יהודה שרצא חרצא ואיכא דאמר חרוא דקטני שקי דרעיא ביני וורדיני, "R. Judah said: It is a cutting reptile (CHARTSA). And there are those who say a boring one (CHARZA), because it has slender legs and feeds among the thickets." Thus in Pliny, II, 444 book 29, chapter 4: "There are two kinds of weasels: one is wild. They differ in size. The Greeks call them ictides, etc. But this one, which roams about our houses, etc." The very point we have made, that חולדה chulda among the Talmudists is generally the weasel and not the mole, follows from the fact that they hold that this animal strangles children, which no one would say of the mole. To this end a passage is adduced from the tractate Thaanith ch. 1, fol. 8. 1: בא וראה כמה גדולים בעלי אמנה מניין מחולדה ובור ומה המאמין בחולדה ובור כך המאמין בהקב״ה על אחת כמה וכמה, "Come and see how the chulda and the well are reckoned among the great witnesses of faith. And who trusts the chulda col. 1028 and the well, and would not much more trust God, the Holy One, Blessed be He?" On this passage the Talmudic Glossator relates this little tale: מחולדה ובור שהמיתו שני בני אדם מצוי הוא באגדה מעשה בבחור אחד שנתן אמונתו לריבה אחת שישאנה אמרה מי העיד והיה שם בור א׳ וחולדה אמר הבחור בור וחולדה עדים בדבר לימים עבר על אמונתו ונשא אחרת והוליד שני בנים אחד נפל לבור ומת ואחד נשכתו חולדה ומת אמרה לו אשתו מה מעשה הוא זה שבנינו מתים במיתה משונה ואמר לה כך וכך היה מעשה, "The chulda and the well, by which two people were put to death. The story is found in the Aggada about a certain young man who pledged his faith to a girl that he would marry her. 'Who will be witness?' she said. Now there were a well and a chulda (weasel) there. And the young man said, 'The well and the chulda will be witnesses of the matter.' But some days later the same man broke his faith and married another woman, and when she had borne him two sons, one of them fell into the well and died; the other also died, bitten by the chulda, or weasel. Then, asked by his wife why both sons had died a violent death, he told her how the matter stood." Baal Aruch reports many more circumstances of the same story, among them that the child was a boy, and was strangled by the chulda when already three months old. His words are: וילדה בן זכר כשהגיע לג׳ חרשים חנקתו חולדה, "And she bore a male son, whom the chulda strangled when he had already reached his third month." Likewise, concerning the الزبزب [al-zabzab] zebzeb, a species of larger weasel or ferret, caught at Baghdad in the year of the Hijra 304, the Arabs related abovee) انه ياكل اطفالهم [annahu yaʾkulu aṭfālahum] that "it devoured their infants," and bit men's hands and women's breasts. To these must be added what the Talmudists adduce in the comparison of the words חולדה chulda, or חולד choled, and חלד cheled; the latter signifies the world in Ps. XLIX, 2., where men are called יושבי חלד, "inhabitants of cheled, that is, of the terrestrial world." And the doctors dispute among themselves why the earth is called חלד cheled, and ask what it has in common with חולד choled, the animal. And in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Cholin ch. 9, fol. 127a, someone answers: כל שיש ביבשה יש בים חוץ מן החולדה, "Whatever animal is on land is also in the sea, the chulda alone excepted." Lest this be thought II, 445 said without grounds, here is a pretty story which the most learned Buxtorf brings forward from the writings of the Rabbis in his Lexicon Talmudicum under the word חלד: "There was an assembly of the princes of the world about proper government. The prince of the sea complained that he did not have enough subjects. He was therefore permitted to seek more from the land, provided he made provision for their food. He cast all land animals into the sea, with the command that they should propagate their kind there too. It was done: hence it is said that all land animals are also in the sea, except that some were changed into a monstrous form. When he came to the weasel, she, standing on the shore, said to the prince of the sea: 'Why will you throw me into the sea again? Do you not see that I am already in the sea?' She said this showing her reflection in the sea. The prince, satisfied, let the weasel go. Hence the Talmudists say that all land creatures are in the sea except the weasel, which escaped by its cunning. And the earth is called חלד cheled because it remained the property of the weasel, which is called חולדה chulda or חולד choled." In the Jerusalem Talmud, at the beginning of the chapter On the eight reptiles, the same question is treated, and some answer exactly what we have just said, that the earth and earthly men are compared with the chulda: לפי שכל מה שיש ביבשה יש בים והרבה מינין יש col. 1029 בים מה שאין כן ביבשה ואין חולדה בים, "because, although everything that is on land is also in the sea, and there are many species in the sea which are not on land, yet the chulda is not in the sea." The other answer is this: חולדה זו גוררת ומנחת ואינה וודעת למי מנחת כך כל באי העולם גוררין ומניחין ואינן יודעין למי מניחין כמו שנאמר יצבור ולא ידע מי אספם, "The chulda drags many things together and leaves them behind, not knowing for whom she leaves them. So too all the people of the world drag many things together and leave them behind, not knowing for whom they will leave them. As it is said, Ps 139:2, „He will heap up, and will not know who will gather them“." So the Aruch cites these things, which, however utterly trifling, nevertheless serve our purpose. For they show that by the word chulda most Talmudists do not mean the mole. For the mole, which is blind, would not be said to have seen its image in the sea and shown it to others. Nor do we read that it stores and hides anything in its burrows. But this is the nature of the weasel, of which Abdollatiph of Baghdad writes in the place cited above: في طبعه انه يسرق ما وجد من فضة او ذهب كما يفعل الغار [fī ṭabʿihi annahu yasriqu mā wajada min fiḍḍa aw dhahab kamā yafʿalu al-ghār] "It is of its nature to steal whatever silver (or gold) it finds, as mice do." Accordingly this story is told there of the weasel: رجل صاد فرخا منها وحبسه في قفص بحيث تراه امه فلما اراته ذهبت ثم جات وفي فمها دينار فالقته بين يديه كانها تفدي ولدها فلم يتركه لها فذهبت وعادت بدينار اخر حتي بلغت من العدد خمسا فلما II, 446 ان لا يطلق لها ذهبت وعادت بخرقة كانها تشير الي فراغ حاصلها فلم يكترث بها فلما رات ذلك عادت الي دينار فاخذته منه فخشي الرجل من ذلك فاطلق لها ولدها [rajul ṣāda farkhan minhā wa-ḥabasahu fī qafaṣ bi-ḥaythu tarāhu ummuhu fa-lammā arathu dhahabat thumma jāt wa-fī famihā dīnār fa-alqathu bayna yadayhi kaʾannahā tafdī waladahā fa-lam yatrukhu lahā fa-dhahabat wa-ʿādat bi-dīnār ākhar ḥattī balaghat min al-ʿadad khamsan fa-lammā an lā yuṭlaq lahā dhahabat wa-ʿādat bi-khirqa kaʾannahā tushīr ilī farāgh ḥāṣilihā fa-lam yaktarith bihā fa-lammā raʾat dhālika ʿādat ilā dīnār fa-akhadhathu minhu fa-khashiya al-rajul min dhālika fa-aṭlaqa lahā waladahā] "A certain man caught a young weasel and shut it in a cage, so that the mother could see it. When she had seen it, she went away and came back with a denarius in her mouth, which she threw down before him, as if to ransom her young. But when he did not release it, she went off again and brought other denarii, until she had reached the number five. And when she saw that her young was still not released, she also brought a rag (in which the coins had been), to show that she had exhausted her entire hoard. And when she noticed that she was paid no heed, she went back to the coins to take them back. But the man, struck with fear, returned her young to her." It is clear, therefore, that by the word חולדה chulda the Talmudists for the most part mean the weasel. But their authority in this matter moves us little, since they are not consistent with themselves, and among the Arabs and Syrians chuld col. 1030 or chulda is the mole; and both the origin of the word and the circumstances of the Mosaic text plainly show that the word must be taken in this sense.

Obiter nota, non solum Hebraeos, sed et nostrorum doctissimos in eo laborare, ut concilient voces חולד choled, et חלד cheled: quarum illa mustelam, ut volunt; haec terram, aut aevum sonat, et doceant, qui factum sit, ut tam diversae vocis notiones ab una origine deductae sint. Et sunt, qui voce חלד cheled nonnisi breve tempus intelligant, ut inde mustela dicatur חולד choled, tanquam animal βραχύβιον. Ita Mercerus. Aliis חלד verbum est sensim abire et irrepere: ac proinde, ut quidem volunt, non solum mustela חולד choled dicitur a furtivo progressu; sed tempus quoque חלד cheled, quia tacite decurrit, neque sentitur ejus motus.

Note in passing that not only the Hebrews but also the most learned of our own scholars labour to reconcile the words חולד choled and חלד cheled, of which the former means, as they would have it, weasel, the latter earth or age, and to show how it came about that the senses of such different words were derived from one origin. There are some who understand by the word חלד cheled nothing but a short time, so that the weasel would hence be called חולד choled as a βραχύβιον [brachybion] animal. So Mercerus. For others חלד is a verb meaning to pass away gradually and to creep in; and accordingly, as they would have it, not only is the weasel called חולד choled from its stealthy movement, but time too is called חלד cheled, because it runs by silently and its motion is not perceived.

Tempora labuntur, tacitisque senescimus annis.

The times glide by, and we grow old with the silent years.

Quae Schindleri sententia est. At Fullero ut חלד chalad, verbum est καταδῦ-ναι, id est, in profunda loca penetrare: ita mustela חולד choled vocatur, tanquam ζῶον καταδυτικὸν, et tam terra ipsa, quam tempus, quo in terra vivimus, חלד cheled, quasi κατάδυσις, ut ab Aquila redditur, id est, latibulum, aut demersio: quia densae caliginis hic plena sunt omnia, neque conferenda cum luce rerum supercoelestium. Quae quidem speciose dicuntur, sed minus solide, ut quidem puto. Certum enim est, verbum חלד chalad duo habere significata diversissima: quorum unum apud Syros; alterum apud Arabes invaluit. Syris nimirum ܚܠܕ chalad proprie est profunda loca fodere et penetrare, ut abunde supra probatum est. At Arabibus خلد chalada plane aliud sonat, nempe manere, durare, perennare, aeternum esse. Atque, ut ex priore significatu factum חולד II, 447 choled, pro talpa: ita ex posteriore חלד cheled, quod primo durationem sonat: ut Ps. XXXIX, 6. וחלדי, Et duratio mea tanquam nihil est coram te; et Ps. LXXXIX, 48. זכור אני מה חלד, Memento, qualis ego sim durationis, id est, quam brevis aevi. Deinde חלד cheled etiam mundum sonat: ut Ps. XLIX, 2. Audite, omnes incolae חלד, mundi: quia mundus, et res, quae in eo sunt, certa duratione constant. Sic עולם olam, huic synonymum, primo saeculum et aeternitatem, deinde etiam mundum significat; quod omnes sciunt.

This is the opinion of Schindler. But for Fuller, just as חלד chalad is a verb meaning καταδῦναι [katadynai], that is, to penetrate into deep places, so the weasel is called חולד choled as a ζῶον καταδυτικὸν [zōon katadytikon], and both the earth itself and the time during which we live on earth are called חלד cheled, as it were κατάδυσις [katadysis], as Aquila renders it, that is, a hiding-place or a submersion: because here all things are full of thick darkness and are not to be compared with the light of supercelestial things. These things are indeed attractively said, but with too little solidity, as I at least think. For it is certain that the verb חלד chalad has two very different meanings, of which one prevailed among the Syrians, the other among the Arabs. For the Syrians, namely, ܚܠܕ [חלד] chalad properly means to dig deep places and to penetrate, as was amply proved above. But for the Arabs خلد [khalada] chalada means something quite different, namely to remain, to endure, to last for ever, to be eternal. And just as from the former meaning came חולד II, 447 choled for the mole, so from the latter came חלד cheled, which first means duration, as in Ps 39:6 וחלדי, And my duration is as nothing before thee; and Ps 89:48 זכור אני מה חלד, Remember of what duration I am, that is, of how brief a span. Then חלד cheled also means the world, as in Ps 49:2 Hear, all ye inhabitants of חלד, the world: because the world and the things in it consist of a fixed duration. So also עולם olam, its synonym, first means age and eternity, and then also the world, as everyone knows.

They also hold that אשת eseth is the mole in these words of David, Ps 58:9, כפל אשת בל חזו שמש, which many of the Hebrews render thus: Like the abortive and the mole, they have not seen the sun. Among them are R. Selomo, R. Obadias Gaon on the Psalms, Pomarius in his Lexicon, and Elias in the Methurgeman, having no doubt been so taught by their forebears. For in the very Mishnah of the Talmud, than which the Jews have nothing, after the Bible, either more sacred or more ancient, the mole is called אישות esuth, a word derived from אשת eseth. So in the tractate Kelim, ch. 11, fol. 34a: מצודת האשות טהורה The net for the mole is clean. And in Moëd Katon, cited above: צדין את האשות ואת העכברים משדה האילן ומשדה הלבן כדרכו במועד ובשביעית It is permitted to hunt the mole and mice, both in a planted field and in an arable field, during the minor festival and in the seventh year. col. 1031 And there the Gemara: מאי אישות אמר רב יהודה בריה שאין לה עיניים What is esuth? Rabbi Judah said: It is an animal lacking eyes. And a little later: מאי קרא כמו שבלול תמס יהלך כפל אשת בל חזו שמש, Why is it so called? And in the same place, in the Gloss, אשות esuth is defined as בריה שאין לה עיניים וחופרת בקרקע, an animal deprived of sight, and digging in the earth. Accordingly the Chaldee translator paraphrases [παραφράζει, paraphrazei] the passage of the Psalm thus: היך נפול ואישותא די סמין ולא חמין שמשא Like an abortive fetus, and the esutha (mole), which are blind and do not see the sun. II, 448 It is certain, however, that this version has nothing sound in it. For the Hebrew words נפל אשת, which they render the abortive and the mole, mean nothing other than ἔκτρωμα γυναικὸς [ektrōma gynaikos], the miscarriage of a woman, as among the ancients Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion and Jerome explained them very well. נפל nephel, for an abortive, takes its name from falling, like the Arabic سقط [saqṭ] sikt, or sukt. Nor does it occur only here, but also in Iob. III, 16. and Eccles. VI, 3. And אשת eseth, for woman, occurs more often in the construct state, but also in the absolute. So Deut 21:11 And if you see among the captives אשת יפת תאר an eseth (that is, a woman) beautiful in form. And 1 Sam 28:7 Seek me אשת בעלת אוב an eseth (or woman), a Pythoness. And Ierem. XIII, 21. Pains will seize you כמו אשת לדה, as labour seizes an eseth (or woman). So here נפל אשת is the abortive fetus of an eseth (or woman). Thus in these words there is nothing difficult, nothing ambiguous, nothing that could be drawn in different directions. And the sense too is perfectly plain. Those who are soon to lose the light of life are compared with the abortive fetus of a woman, whose enjoyment of life was so brief, and in so dark a place, that it never saw the sun. So there was no need to imagine a mole here, especially since it is customary in Scripture to assert of abortive fetuses the very thing the Psalmist asserts here, namely that they have not seen the light. So Iob. III, 16. Like a hidden abortive, I would no longer be; like infants who have not seen the light, which is added by way of explanation [ἐπεξηγητικῶς, epexēgētikōs]. And Eccles. VI, 3. 4. 5. The abortive is happier than he, though it came in vain, and went into darkness, and its name was covered with darkness, and it neither saw the sun nor knew, etc. Accordingly there seems to be an antithesis [ἀντίθετον, antitheton] in the words of Paul, 1 Cor. XV, 8. Ὡσπερεὶ τῷ ἐκτρώματι ὤφθη κἀμοί· [Hōsperei tō ektrōmati ōphthē kamoi], as if he were saying: “Christ was seen even by me, though in matters pertaining to salvation I was once as blind as an abortive is to the light of the sun.” Yet granted, though not conceded, that אשת eseth in the passage of the Psalm means the mole, even so they would not have what they want. For the words נפל אשת would mean the abortive of a mole, not the abortive and the mole, as they render it, supplying the conjunction AND, which does not appear in the text. Yet lest they seem to do this without precedent, R. Selomo here cites a similar passage from Ierem. XI, 19., where he would have the Hebrew words ככבש אלוף rendered like a lamb and an ox, likewise adding the conjunction. But we have shown in its place f) that nothing is to be supplied there, and that in Hebrew כבש אלוף is a tame lamb, and that الوف [alūf] aluph is still taken in this sense among the Arabs today.

There is more reason in those who hold that חפרפרות chapharpheroth are moles in this passage, Isa 2:20 In that day a man shall cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold, which he had made for himself to worship, לחפרפרות ולעטלפים, to the chaphorpheroth (or moles) and the bats. But much must be noted about this word. Let the first point II, 449 be this: although it is a single word, it is nevertheless written separately, לחפר פרות, as if there were two words, as happens with some others. Thus at 2 Par. XXXIV, 6. in most col. 1032 editions בחר בתיהם is written for בחרבותיהם, with their hammers. Ierem. XLVI, 20. יפה פיה for יפהפיה, most beautiful. Thren. IV, 3. כי ענים for כיענים, like ostriches; Ezech. XXVII, 6. בת אשורים for בתאשורים, of boxwood. The same happened with this word, so that it was split in two. Hence some explain it as though the words were separate: for instance, to the digger of fruits, as if it were a kind of bird so called because it pierces and bores through the fruits of trees; or to the digging of pits, as if פירות stood for חפירות, which is what the Hebrews call pits. But it is certain that the ancient interpreters, namely the Septuagint, the Elders, Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, Jerome, the Chaldee, the Syriac and the Arabic, read לחפרפרות as one word, since they rendered it by one word. Iarchius and Aben Ezra also hold that it is a single word. But this single word is explained in various ways. The Elders render τὰ μάταια [ta mataia], vain things; Aquila ὄρυγας [orygas], diggings; Symmachus ἄκαρπα [akarpa], fruitless things; Theodotion put the Hebrew word itself, ἀφαρφερώθ [apharpherōth]; the Chaldee טעותא, idols; the Syriac ܣܪܝܩܘܬܐ [סריקותא], vanities; Aben Ezra thinks it is שם עוף יעוף בלילה כעטלף ומביא עצמו ביום במקום סתר, the name of a bird which flies by night like a bat and by day withdraws into a hidden place. To weigh each of these is not for our present leisure R6. It will suffice to point out that, deservedly, all of these are surpassed by Jerome among our writers, and among the Hebrews by R. Selomo, who understand moles here. For from the verb חפר chaphar, which means to dig, animals that are supremely ὀρυκτικὰ [oryktika], and that spend their whole life digging, are most fittingly named. And, as we already touched on in passing above g), in חפרפרות chaphorpheroth the radicals are doubled so that the meaning may be intensified. So too אדמדם adamdem for intensely red; ירקרק jerakrak for intensely yellow; שחרחר secharchor for very dark; עקלקל akalkal for exceedingly winding; הפכפך haphachphach for exceedingly changeable; פתלתל pethalthol for exceedingly twisted; יפהפיה jephephija for exceedingly beautiful. Moreover, this same rendering joins moles with bats, which are animals alike in many respects, although the one creeps beneath the earth and the other rises into the air in flight. First, both are lowly and unclean little animals. Second, both are in some way referred to the mouse kind. This has already been proved of the mole h), and in the case of the bat it is known to everyone. Hence in Nonius, Varro says in the Agathon: I have become a bat; I am neither wholly among the mice nor among the birds. In Aesop a bat, caught by a weasel as a bird, II, 450 in order to escape ἔλεγεν, ὀυκ ὄρνις, ἀλλὰ μῦς εἶναι [elegen, ouk ornis, alla mys einai], said that it was not a bird but a mouse. Scaliger maintains that it has a nature midway between the shrew and the swallow. Accordingly in Eustathius τρισμὸς [trismos] is common to the mouse and the bat. And Victorinus in his book De orthographia calls the bat sorex avis [bird-shrew]; Kimchi calls it העטלף בעל כנפים, a winged mouse; the Lombards ratto penago, that is, feathered mouse; the Spaniards morcego, that is, blind mouse; the French chauve-souris, that is, bald shrew. Third, the mole is blind, and the bat sees little. Hence in Arabic it is خغاش [khughāsh] chuphas. For خغش [khaghash] chaphas among them denotes smallness and weakness of the eyes, and اخغش [akhghash] achphaso is μύωψ [myōps]. Accordingly, as moles live beneath the earth, so bats spend the day in caves and dark places.

This is very much to the point. For idols are said to be cast to the moles and the bats when they are thrust down, as it were into darkness, from the lofty place in which they stood out, so that they may no longer deceive superstitious people with their splendour. And among the Hebrews there seems col. 1033 to have been a proverbial expression by which something utterly despised and held in the greatest contempt was said to be cast to the moles and the bats. Accordingly Kimchi rightly holds that this is said דרך בזיון, that is, by way of contempt. Although the Patriarch Jacob also fulfilled it literally when he buried the idols of his household under the terebinth, Gen. XXXV, 4. Jerome renders it otherwise: In that day a man shall cast away the idols, etc. which he had made for himself to worship, moles and bats. So also, among the Hebrews, Rabbi Solomon. This pleases me less, although the Hebrew could bear it, because I have nowhere read that moles and bats were worshipped as gods by the ancients.

The mole is deservedly counted among the unclean animals in the Law, since it neither divides the hoof nor chews the cud. Moreover, by its blindness it is a type of those whose minds the god of this world has blinded, lest the splendour of the Gospel should dawn upon them, 2 Cor. IV, 4. And while it is continually busy digging in the earth, it represents those men who care for nothing but earthly things, Philipp. III, 19. and whose wisdom is earthly, sensual, devilish, Iacobi III, 15. But the weasel (mustela), which the interpreters have hitherto wanted to be designated by the word חולד choled, signifies, as Cyril taught us above (as emended by us), τὰ δειλὰ, καὶ ἄνανδρα, καὶ ψοφοδεῆ τῶν κλεπτῶν γένη [ta deila, kai ananda, kai psophodeē tōn kleptōn genē]. Of the same sort is that in Aristeas: Κακοποιητικὸς ὁ τρόπος ἐστὶ καὶ γαλῆς, καὶ μυῶν [Kakopoiētikos ho tropos esti kai galēs, kai myōn]; Mischievous are the habits both of the weasel and of mice. And a little later: Τότε τῆς γαλῆς γένος ἰδιάζον ἐστί. χωρὶς γὰρ τοῦ προειρημένου, ἔχει λυμαντικὸν κατάστημα· διὰ γὰρ τῶν ὤτων συλλαμβάνει, τεκνοποιεῖ δὲ τῷ στόματι [Tote tēs galēs genos idiazon esti. chōris gar tou proeirēmenou, echei lymantikon katastēma; dia gar tōn ōtōn syllambanei, teknopoiei de tō stomati]. Which in the Hebrew runs thus: הנה עוד בסגולת טבעו התועבה הזאת שהנקבה תהר מאזנה ותלד מפיה, Behold, moreover, among the peculiarities of its nature is this abomination, that the female conceives through the ear and gives birth through the mouth. And Algiahid, inverting this, pretends that Aristotle taught, in the books On the Nature and Properties of Animals, ان الانثي من بنات عرس تلقح من افواهها وتلد من اذانها [inna al-unthī min banāt ʿirs tulqaḥ min afwāhihā wa-talid min ādhānihā] that the females among weasels conceive through the mouth and give birth through the ear. Yet nothing similar occurs in the writings of Aristotle. Indeed, those who idly claim that the weasel gives birth through the mouth he not only refutes but also reveals the origin of the tale, in book 3 On the Generation of Animals, chapter 6. There, after saying that the womb of the weasel is arranged in the same way as that of other quadrupeds, and that the foetus cannot pass from it to the mouth, he adds that this opinion arose διὰ τὸ τίκτειν πάμπαν τὴν γαλῆν σμικρὰ, τῷ δὲ στόματι πολλάκις μεταφέρειν τοὺς νεοττοὺς [dia to tiktein pampan tēn galēn smikra, tō de stomati pollakis metapherein tous neottous], from the fact that the weasel bears very small young, II, 452 and often carries them about in its mouth. But another explanation presents itself to me, which seems closer to the truth. I think that what the ancients observed in the fish called mustelus [dogfish] was transferred to the weasel. For ὁ γαλεὸς [ho galeos], or the fish mustelus, is also believed to give birth through the mouth. Aelian, History, book 2, chapter 55: Ὁ γαλεὸς ὠδίνει διὰ τοῦ στόματος ἐν τῇ θαλάττῃ [Ho galeos ōdinei dia tou stomatos en tē thalattē], The mustelus gives birth in the sea through the mouth. col. 1034 And in book 9, chapter 65: Οἱ μυούμενοι τοῖν Θεοῖν οὐκ ἂν πάσαιντο γαλεοῦ φασιν· οὐ γὰρ εἶναι αὐτὸν καθαρὸν ὄψον ἐπεὶ τῷ στόματι τίκτει [Hoi myoumenoi toin Theoin ouk an pasainto galeou phasin; ou gar einai auton katharon opson epei tō stomati tiktei]. They say that the priests of the Goddesses (namely Ceres and Proserpina) never eat the mustelus fish, for it is not clean food, since it gives birth through the mouth. And it is believed to give birth through the mouth because it sometimes takes its little ones into its mouth, and those who see them coming out imagine this to be a birth. The Philosopher himself touches on this in passing when he says of the mustelus or galeus fishes, in History, book 6, chapter 10: Ἐξαφιᾶσι καὶ δέχονται εἰς ἑαυτοὺς τοὺς νεοττούς [Exaphiasi kai dechontai eis heautous tous neottous]; They take their young back into themselves after letting them out. But others say it much more explicitly. Antigonus, History 25: Τῶν δὲ γαλεῶν τὰ ἔμβρυα, ἐξιόντα ἐκ τῆς κοιλίας, νέμεται· καὶ πάλιν ἐκδύεται (read εἰσδύεται [eisdyetai]) κατὰ τὸ στόμα [Tōn de galeōn ta embrya, exionta ek tēs koilias, nemetai; kai palin ekdyetai … kata to stoma]. The foetuses of the galei, coming out of the womb, feed, and enter again through the mouth. Athenaeus, book 7, chapter 12: Τίκτει δὲ ὁ γαλεὸς τὰ πλεῖστα τρία, καὶ εἰσδέχεται τὰ γεννηθέντα εἰς τὸ στόμα,. καὶ πάλιν ἀφίησι [Tiktei de ho galeos ta pleista tria, kai eisdechetai ta gennēthenta eis to stoma, kai palin aphiēsi]; The mustelus usually bears three young, and takes them into its mouth, and lets them out again. Plutarch says that the young are formed from an egg within the mother's womb itself, and then let out through the mouth as if by a second generation. The words are in the book Utra anim.: Τίκτουσι μὲν γὰρ ὠὸν, εἶτα ζῶον, οὐκ ἐκτὸς, ἀλλὰ ἐντὸς ἐν ἑαυτοῖς. καὶ τρέφουσιν οὕτως, καὶ φέρουσιν, ὥσπερ ἐκ δευτέρας γενέσεως [Tiktousi men gar ōon, eita zōon, ouk ektos, alla entos en heautois. kai trephousin houtōs, kai pherousin, hōsper ek deuteras geneseōs]. And in Περὶ Φιλοστοργίας [Peri Philostorgias] he says that they take back those they have let out, and wrap them, as they sleep, in their own entrails: Οἱ γαλεοὶ ζωογονοῦσι μὲν ἐν ἑαυτοῖς, ἐκβαίνειν δὲ παρέχουσιν ἐκτὸς, καὶ νέμεσθαι τοῖς σκύμνοις, εἶτα πάλιν ἀναλαμβάνουσι, καὶ περιπτύσσουσιν ἐγκοιμώμενοι τοῖς σπλάγχνοις [Hoi galeoi zōogonousi men en heautois, ekbainein de parechousin ektos, kai nemesthai tois skymnois, eita palin analambanousi, kai periptyssousin enkoimōmenoi tois splanchnois]. Aelian too, book 2, chapter 55: Πάλιν τε εἰσδέχεται τὰ βρέφη, κἀνεμεῖ ταῖς αὐταῖς ὁδοῖς ζῶντα, καὶ ἀπαθῆ [Palin te eisdechetai ta brephē, kanemei tais autais hodois zōnta, kai apathē]; It takes its young back (through the mouth) and by the same way vomits them up again alive and unharmed. That is, it protects them in this way in sudden fear. The same Aelian, book 9, chapter 65: Οὐ τίκτειν δὲ αὐτὸν ἔνιοι λέγουσιν· ἀλλὰ, δείσαντά τι τῶν ἐπιβουλευόντων, τὰ σκυλάκια καταπίνειν, καὶ ἀποκρύπτειν. εἶτα τοῦ φοβήσαντος παραδραμόντος, ζῶντα αὖθις ἀνεμεῖν [Ou tiktein de auton enioi legousin; alla, deisanta ti tōn epibouleuontōn, ta skylakia katapinein, kai apokryptein. eita tou phobēsantos paradramontos, zōnta authis anemein]. Some say that it does not give birth through the mouth, but that, struck with fear of some attacker, it swallows its young and hides them; then, when the fear is removed, it vomits them up again alive. Many report the same of the glaucus fish, as the same Aelian, book 1, chapter 16; Oppian, Halieutica, book 1, line 749; Phile, chapter 69. Tzetzes too, Chiliad 4, History 126, writes that the glaucus, the dogfish and the galeus hide their young in fear, the glaucus and the dogfish in the mouth, the galeus in the belly, καὶ πάλιν ταῦτα γεννᾷν, τοῦ φόβου [kai palin tauta gennan, tou phobou] II, 453 παρελθόντος [parelthontos], and bring them forth again when the fear has passed. Thus from this cloud of witnesses it appears that it is not τὴν γαλῆν [tēn galēn], that is, the four-footed weasel, but τὸν γαλεὸν [ton galeon], that is, the mustelus fish, that emits its young from the mouth; and that this was believed either to be a true birth or to be like a birth. Rightly, therefore, Isidore, Etymologies, book 12, chapter 3, refutes those who say that the weasel conceives through the ear and pours forth its offspring through the mouth (so it must be read, not, as commonly, conceives through the mouth and through the ear, etc., although the Arabs follow the latter). And many indeed said that the weasel gives birth through the mouth, as we know from the Philosopher. But whether anyone said that it conceives through the ear before Aristeas, I do not know; he seems to have embellished the tale in this way so that the weasel might be a type of informers and slanderers. For these, he says, entangle others in evils, ὅσα διὰ ἀκοῆς λαβόντες, ταῦτα τῷ λόγῳ σωματοποιήσαντες [hosa dia akoēs labontes, tauta tō logō sōmatopoiēsantes], as it were embodying in words what they had received through their ears, that is, exaggerating and magnifying them: which is a new and wholly unusual sense of the verb σωματοποιεῖν [sōmatopoiein]. Barnabas too is mistaken when, in order to fasten on the weasel the horrible crime of fellators, he says not that it gives birth through the mouth but κύειν [kyein], conceives. Ἀλλὰ, he says, καὶ τὴν γαλῆν ἐμίσησε καλῶς. οὐ μὴ γάρ φησιν, ὁμοιωθήσῃ τοῖς τοιούτοις, οἵους ἀκούομεν ἀνομίαν ποιοῦντας τῷ στόματι, διὰ ἀκαθαρσίαν, etc. Τὸ γὰρ ζῶον τοῦτο τῷ στόματι κύει [Alla … kai tēn galēn emisēse kalōs. ou mē gar phēsin, homoiōthēsē tois toioutois, hoious akouomen anomian poiountas tō stomati, dia akatharsian … To gar zōon touto tō stomati kyei]. Therefore we do not think anything should be changed in that passage; nor in this respect do we agree col. 1035 with the opinion of a most excellent and most learned man, and a very dear friend of ours, who in his Notes on this Epistle (how learned they are!) holds that this passage is corrupt and must be restored from Aristeas. The Egyptians also wanted the weasels to be an example of the lewdness of tribades, as Horapollo teaches, Hieroglyphica, book 2, chapter 34; but others, more decent, in Plutarch's Isis, hold that τὴν γαλῆν κατὰ τὸ οὖς ὀχευομένην, τῷ δὲ στόματι τίκτουσαν, εἴκασμα τῆς τοῦ λόγου γενέσεως εἶναι [tēn galēn kata to ous ocheuomenēn, tō de stomati tiktousan, eikasma tēs tou logou geneseōs einai], the weasel, since it is covered through the ear and gives birth through the mouth, represents the generation of speech. Although I do not deny that weasels also hunt mice, everyone will think of the cat as soon as the question arises which kind of animal, when lacking, lets mice multiply." So Kirsch. Against this, however, Lorsbach rightly objected, in the book entitled Archiv für die morgenländische Litteratur, vol. 1, p. 204, first, the corresponding Chaldee word כרכושתא, which Bochart has amply proved on this very page to mean weasel, not cat. The name was aptly given to the weasel from its agile and swift movement, for כרכש, كركس [karkas], means he moved, drove to and fro. As to the point on which Kirsch's view chiefly rests, that mice multiplied when the animals called ܟܪܟܘܫܬܐ [כרכושתא] were lacking, Lorsbach answers well that weasels hunt mice no less than cats do, indeed better, because the slenderness of their bodies lets them get into mouse-holes more easily.
