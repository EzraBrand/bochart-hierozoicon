III, 107 CHAPTER 31. col. 343 ¶

Probatur, דוכיפת dukiphath esse Upupam.

Proof that דוכיפת dukiphath is the Hoopoe. ¶

Nomen avis דוכיפת dukiphath magistri Iudaeorum compositum esse recte as-serunt. Sed eos latet ratio compositionis. Si Pagnino credimus, R. Selomo in Commentario exponit gallum sylvestrem, δίλοφον, seu duplicatam cristam habentem: et a duplici crista דוכיפת dukiphath appellari vult, quasi habentem דוכיפות dukephoth, id est (ait) duas cristas. Cujusmodi compositiones in Rabbinorum scriptis non infrequenter occurrunt. Ita caverna Macpela, seu duplex, ab illis vocatur דיוטרון, quasi duplex antrum, voce conflata ex δύο et ἄντρον. Primus homo דיופרצוף διπρόσωπος, quasi Deus illum bifrontem creaverit. Siphon דיופי, quasi duo haberet ora. Arbor bifera דיופרא, quasi δίφορος. Pera duplex דיוסקיא, quasi δίσακκος, ut Gallis bissac, et besace. Duumviri praefecti urbis, seu consules, דיופולין, ex δύο et πόλις. Gladius anceps דיוסטר, propter duo latera. Illis enim סטר est latus. Imago, ex altera expressa, דיוקון, ex δύο et εἰκών. Duae columnae דיומדין, ex דיו et עומדין. Et דיוזגי, δίζυ-γος, bijugus. Et דוכסוסטוס, quasi δύξυστος, bis rasus: ut παλίμψηστος est pergameni certa species, vel bis, vel utrimque rasi et expoliti. Itaque, quasi Prophetarum verba similes compositiones admittant, Iosephus Kimchius, Davidis pater, Psalmi quinquagesimi versu vigesimo, verbum דופי dophi, id est, probrum, vituperium, compositum esse contendit ex דו do, duo, et פי pi, vel phi, os; quia obtrectator aliter, te praesente, aliter, te absente, loquitur, quasi col. 344 duo haberet ora. Quod est valde absurdum, ut vox, a Davide usurpata, pro semi-Latina habeatur, aut saltem pro semi-Graeca. Sed de Mose multo absurdius, cujus est vox דוכיפת dukiphath, in qua versamur. Verum, ut dicam, quod res est, R. Selomo non scribit, quod illi affingit Pagninus, avem דוכיפת dukiphath nomen habere ex דוכפות du-kephoth, sive duabus cristis. Neque enim ulla est avis δίλοφος. Et crista Hebraice non dicitur כיפה kepha, vel כיפת kephath; sed כרבלה kirbela, ut Britonibus cribel. Denique plane aliud sonant verba Solomonis, a Pagnino citata, ולמה נקרא שמו דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזו היא כרבלתו. Quae sic vertenda sunt, Et cur vocatur nomen ejus dukiphath? Quia eminentia ejus alligata est, id est, crista ejus. Nam הוד hod decus, dignitatem, majestatem, eminentiam sonat. Et Verbum כפת kephath Chaldaeis ligare et vincire est. Vult igitur, ex duabus vocibus הודו כפות hodo kaphuth, id est, eminentia (seu crista) ejus alligata est (supple capiti), per aphaeresin factum esse dukiphath; quia cristatae avis nomen est. Neque haec tam sunt R. Selomonis, quam Talmudicorum in Cholin cap. 3. fol. 63. a. ubi dicit R. Iuda, דוכיפת dukiphath ideo appellari, שהודו כפות, quia eminentiam suam habet alligatam. Et III, 108 Glossa in margine, כרבלתו עבה ורומה כמו שכפולה לתוך הראש וכפותה שם Crista ei densa est, et quasi in medio capite vincta, et ibi ligata. Nam pro שכפולה, quod geminatam sonat, legendum esse שכבולה, id est, vinctam, docet sensus, et vox synonyma כפותה, quae sequitur. Simile est mendum in Aruch in his verbis, דוכיפת זה שהודו כפוף. Legendum enim כפות, ut in Talmude et Selomone, non כפוף. Interim haec originatio priore non multo melior est. Nam, utut הוד hod, decoris, roboris, gloriae, dignitatis et majestatis ὑπεροχὴν atque eminentiam sonet, tamen pro crista nusquam sumitur. Neque cristata avis recte diceretur cristam habere alligatam, cum in cristatis avibus crista innata sit, non alligata. Constat igitur, hoc ἔτυμον esse valde coactum et fidiculis extortum.

The masters of the Jews rightly assert that the name of the bird דוכיפת dukiphath is a compound. But the reason for the compounding escapes them. If we believe Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in his Commentary explains it as the wild cock, δίλοφον [dilophon], that is, having a double crest; and he holds that it is called דוכיפת dukiphath from its double crest, as though having דוכיפות dukephoth, that is (he says), two crests. Compounds of this kind occur not infrequently in the writings of the Rabbis. Thus the cave of Machpelah, or the double cave, is called by them דיוטרון, as if double cavern, a word fused from δύο [dyo] and ἄντρον [antron]. The first man is דיופרצוף, διπρόσωπος [diprosōpos], as though God had created him two-faced. A siphon is דיופי, as though it had two mouths. A tree bearing twice a year is דיופרא, as if δίφορος [diphoros]. A double bag is דיוסקיא, as if δίσακκος [disakkos], like the French bissac and besace. The two magistrates governing a city, or consuls, are דיופולין, from δύο [dyo] and πόλις [polis]. A two-edged sword is דיוסטר, because of its two sides; for to them סטר is side. An image copied from another is דיוקון, from δύο [dyo] and εἰκών [eikōn]. Two columns are דיומדין, from דיו and עומדין. And דיוזגי, δίζυγος [dizygos], bijugus [two-yoked]. And דוכסוסטוס, as if δύξυστος [dyxystos], scraped twice: just as παλίμψηστος [palimpsēstos] is a certain kind of parchment, scraped and polished either twice or on both sides. Accordingly, as though the words of the Prophets admitted similar compounds, Joseph Kimchi, the father of David, on Psalm 50, verse 20, contends that the word דופי dophi, that is, reproach, censure, is compounded of דו do, two, and פי pi or phi, mouth; because a slanderer speaks one way in your presence and another in your absence, as though he col. 344 had two mouths. It is quite absurd that a word used by David should be taken for half-Latin, or at least half-Greek. But it is far more absurd in the case of Moses, to whom belongs the word דוכיפת dukiphath that we are dealing with. In truth, to tell the matter as it is, R. Selomo does not write what Pagninus attributes to him, that the bird דוכיפת dukiphath takes its name from דוכפות du-kephoth, or two crests. For there is no bird that is δίλοφος [dilophos]. And a crest in Hebrew is not called כיפה kepha or כיפת kephath, but כרבלה kirbela, as the Bretons say cribel. Finally, the words of Solomon cited by Pagninus mean something quite different: ולמה נקרא שמו דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזו היא כרבלתו. They are to be rendered thus: And why is its name called dukiphath? Because its eminence is bound, that is, its crest. For הוד hod means splendour, dignity, majesty, eminence. And the verb כפת kephath in Chaldee is to bind and to tie. He means, then, that from the two words הודו כפות hodo kaphuth, that is, its eminence (or crest) is bound (supply to its head), dukiphath was formed by aphaeresis, because it is the name of a crested bird. Nor is this so much R. Selomo's as the Talmudists', in Chullin ch. 3, fol. 63a, where R. Judah says that the דוכיפת dukiphath is so called שהודו כפות, because it has its eminence bound. And III, 108 the Gloss in the margin: כרבלתו עבה ורומה כמו שכפולה לתוך הראש וכפותה שם, Its crest is thick, and as it were tied in the middle of the head and bound there. For in place of שכפולה, which means doubled, one must read שכבולה, that is, tied, as the sense shows, together with the synonymous word כפותה that follows. There is a similar error in the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] in these words: דוכיפת זה שהודו כפוף. For one must read כפות, as in the Talmud and Selomo, not כפוף. Meanwhile this derivation is not much better than the former. For although הוד hod signifies the ὑπεροχὴν [hyperochēn] and eminence of splendour, strength, glory, dignity and majesty, it is nowhere taken for a crest. Nor would a crested bird rightly be said to have its crest bound, since in crested birds the crest is innate, not bound on. It is clear, therefore, that this ἔτυμον [etymon] is very forced and wrung out as if on the rack. ¶

Sed aliud venit in mentem, de quo velim, ut docti judicent. Arabibus ديك dik est gallus, et ديكة dika est gallina. Unde conjectura non levis Hebraeis quoque, aut Syris, דיך dik, vel דוך duk, gallum significasse, et דוך כפא duk kepha, gallum rupis. Quibus vocibus κατὰ σύνθεσιν in unam coalescentibus, ex duk-kepha levi mutatione factum suspicor דוכיפת dukiphath; quorum Caph uno ablato, cujus absentia non potuit suppleri per punctum Daghes propter longam Vocalem praecedentem. Hebraeorum doctores, qui de tali compositione non poterant cogitare, quia nesciebant, duk, vel dik esse gallum, conjecturam tamen nostram valde confirmant, cum dukiphath gallum agrestem, aut sylvestrem, aut montanum explicant. Ita autem explicant fere omnes recentiores. Kimchius et Aquinas reddunt תרנגולא ברא, gallum agrestem. R. Serira Gaon, גאלו דמונ״טייא, gallum montium. Pomarius תרנגול הבר, gallum agri, et Italice gallo, o gallina salvatica. Elias Levita Germanice אוירהאן awerhan, quod sylvestris galli genus est. Syrus quoque hic habet ܬܪܢܓܠܐ ܕܒܪܐ gallum agrestem. Et Chaldaei tres, Hierosolymitanus, Ionathan et Onkelos נגר טורא, gallum montanum. Gallus porro agrestis, sive montanus, idem (credo) sunt cum gallo rupicola. Maxime, cum mons et rupes ita sint synonyma, ut Seniores promiscue usurpent: hinc est, quod Hebraeum צור alibi πέτραν, petram reddunt, alibi ὄρος, montem.

But something else comes to mind, on which I should like the learned to judge. Among the Arabs ديك [dīk] dik is a cock, and ديكة [dīka] dika is a hen. Hence there is a not insubstantial conjecture that among the Hebrews too, or the Syrians, דיך dik, or דוך duk, signified a cock, and דוך כפא duk kepha a cock of the rock. When these words coalesced into one κατὰ σύνθεσιν [kata synthesin], I suspect that from duk-kepha, by a slight change, דוכיפת dukiphath was formed, one of the two Caphs being dropped; its absence could not be supplied by the point Daghes because of the long vowel preceding. The teachers of the Hebrews, who could not have thought of such a composition, since they did not know that duk or dik is a cock, nevertheless strongly confirm our conjecture when they explain dukiphath as the field cock, or the wild cock, or the mountain cock. And almost all the more recent writers explain it so. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] render it תרנגולא ברא, field cock. R. Serira Gaon, גאלו דמונ״טייא, cock of the mountains. Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], תרנגול הבר, cock of the field, and in Italian gallo, o gallina salvatica. Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] Levita in German אוירהאן awerhan, which is a kind of wild cock. The Syriac too has here ܬܪܢܓܠܐ ܕܒܪܐ [תרנגלא דברא], field cock. And the three Chaldees, the Jerusalem, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], נגר טורא, mountain cock. The field cock, moreover, or mountain cock, is (I believe) the same as the rock-dwelling cock; especially since mountain and rock are so far synonymous that the Elders [=the translators of the Septuagint] use them interchangeably: hence they render the Hebrew צור in some places πέτραν [petran], rock, in others ὄρος [oros], mountain. ¶

Quamvis veram hanc esse putem vocabuli originem, tamen etiam alia pot- col. 345 est hujus compositionis ratio assignari, non parum verisimilis. Nempe, si vox דוכיפת divellatur in דוך, et יפת: quarum prior sit gallus, altera portendere. Nam inde sit מופת, signum, non qualecunque, inquit Kimchius, sed quo futurum aliquid portenditur. Quomodo si sumatur דוך־יפת, erit gallus portentosus, aut etiam gallus ominosus, vel auguralis (1).

Although I think this is the true origin of the word, nevertheless another explanation of this composition can also be given col. 345, and a fairly probable one. Namely, if the word דוכיפת is split into דוך and יפת, the former being cock, the latter to portend. For from it comes מופת, a sign, not of any kind, says Kimchi, but one by which something future is portended. Taken in this way as דוך־יפת, it will be the portentous cock, or even the ominous cock, or the augural cock R1. ¶

III, 109 Iam videndum, cui potissimum avi haec nomina tribui possint. Quadruplex est interpretatio. Una Sadducaeorum, qui, cum nomine דוכיפת vulgarem gallinam intelligerent, ab ejus usu abstinuerunt, tanquam ab ave immunda et in Lege prohibita. Quo referendus est in illos Aben Ezrae sarcasmus, אמרו הצדוקים שהיא תרנגולת ואלה טפשי עולם כי מי הגיד להם, Dixerunt Sadducaei, dukiphath esse gallinam. Sed hi sunt stulti mundi (id est, mortalium stultissimi). Quis enim hoc illis indicavit? Altera est interpretatio Eliae in Methurgeman, ubi נגר טורא Germanice vocari dicit אוריארה״אן awerhan, vel orhan. Illud est galli sylvestris genus, vulgari duplo majoris. Gesnerus putat esse Plinii tetraonem. Ipse post Albertum appellat urogallum, Latino nomine ficto ad instar Germanici. Ab uris sic dici putant; quia vulgares gallos magnitudine tantum superat, quantum uri vulgares boves. Tertia interpretatio est Iunii et Tremellii, quibus דוכיפת est attagen; avis, quae maxime fuit in veterum deliciis. Horatius Epoda secundo,

III, 109 Now we must see to which bird above all these names can be assigned. There are four interpretations. One is that of the Sadducees, who, understanding by the name דוכיפת the common hen, abstained from eating it as an unclean bird forbidden in the Law. To this belongs Aben Ezra's [=Abraham ibn Ezra] sarcasm against them: אמרו הצדוקים שהיא תרנגולת ואלה טפשי עולם כי מי הגיד להם, The Sadducees said that dukiphath is the hen. But these are the fools of the world (that is, the most foolish of mortals). For who told them this? The second is the interpretation of Elias in the Methurgeman, where he says that נגר טורא is called in German אוריארה״אן awerhan, or orhan. That is a kind of wild cock, twice as large as the common one. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] thinks it is Pliny's tetrao. He himself, following Albertus, calls it urogallus, a Latin name coined on the model of the German. They think it is so called from the uri [aurochs], because it exceeds common cocks in size as much as the aurochs exceed common oxen. The third interpretation is that of Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], for whom דוכיפת is the attagen, a bird that was among the chief delicacies of the ancients. Sed avis tam delicatae descriptio tam est neglecta, ut hodie docti disputent, an attagen sit gallinago, vel rusticula, vel coturnix, vel Italorum francolinus. A Iunio videtur sumi pro rusticula, quae dicitur woodcock, id est, gallus sylvestris, et alicubi a Gallis gelinotte de bois, id est, sylvestris gallinula.

But the description of so delicate a bird has been so neglected that today the learned dispute whether the attagen is the gallinago [snipe], or the rusticula [woodcock], or the coturnix [quail], or the francolinus of the Italians. By Junius it seems to be taken for the rusticula, which is called woodcock, that is, wild cock, and in some places by the French gelinotte de bois, that is, little wild hen. ¶

Superest, ut dicamus de LXX. Senioribus et Vulgato interprete, qui דוכיפת dukiphath, ἔποπα, upupam reddunt. Quomodo etiam Arabes quatuor interpretes, editi duo, et manuscripti totidem, quos inter se conferre mihi contigit. In his enim omnibus الهدهد alhudhud reperias, id est, upupam. Quod merito sequuntur multi recentiorum. Nam in ea sententia sum, ex multis interpretationibus praeferendam esse antiquissimam; nisi causae sint gravissimae, cur ab ea discedamus, quales hic nullas video. Quidni enim upupam Moses gallum saxatilem, Chaldaei gallum montanum, Rabbini gallum sylvestrem appellaverint, cum et a Graecis videam idem esse factitatum? Sic ἔποπα Hesychius explicat ἀλεκτρυόνα ἄγριον, Aeschylus πετραῖον ὄρνιν, avem (aut etiam gallinam) saxatilem. Nam, pro gallinaceo genere specialius ὄρνιν, vel ὄρνιθα sumi, docuimus suo loco b). Locus Aeschyli, quem innuimus, sic citatur a Philosopho Historiae libri noni capite quadragesimo nono,

It remains to speak of the LXX Elders and the Vulgate translator, who render דוכיפת dukiphath as ἔποπα [epopa], upupa [hoopoe]. So also four Arabic translators, two printed and as many in manuscript, which I happened to compare with one another. For in all of them you will find الهدهد [al-hudhud] alhudhud, that is, the hoopoe. Many of the more recent scholars rightly follow this. For I hold the view that among many interpretations the most ancient is to be preferred, unless there are very weighty reasons for departing from it, and I see none such here. For why should Moses not have called the hoopoe the rock cock, the Chaldeans the mountain cock, the Rabbis the wild cock, when I see that the Greeks did the same? Thus Hesychius explains ἔποπα [epopa] as ἀλεκτρυόνα ἄγριον [alektryona agrion], and Aeschylus calls it πετραῖον ὄρνιν [petraion ornin], a rock bird (or even a rock hen). For we have shown in its place b) that ὄρνις [ornis] or ὄρνιθα [ornitha] is taken more specifically for the gallinaceous kind. The passage of Aeschylus to which we alluded is cited thus by the Philosopher, History, book 9, chapter 49: ¶

Τοῦτον δ᾽ ἐπόπτην ἔποπα τῶν αὑτοῦ κακῶν
Πεποικίλωκε, κ᾽ ἀποδηλώσας ἔχει
Θρασὺν πετραῖον ὄρνιν ἐν παντευχίᾳ.

Τοῦτον δ᾽ ἐπόπτην ἔποπα τῶν αὑτοῦ κακῶν Πεποικίλωκε, κ᾽ ἀποδηλώσας ἔχει Θρασὺν πετραῖον ὄρνιν ἐν παντευχίᾳ. [Touton d᾽ epoptēn epopa tōn hautou kakōn pepoikilōke, k᾽ apodēlōsas echei thrasyn petraion ornin en panteuchia.] ¶

Quae vir magnus ita reddit,

Which a great man renders thus: ¶

Hunc fecit upupam, qui suorum facinorum
Sit ipse testis, variegato corpore
Insessor ales rupium, ornatu ferox. col. 346

He made him a hoopoe, to be himself the witness of his own crimes, with variegated body, a winged dweller of the rocks, fierce in his array. col. 346 ¶

Agitur de Tereo, quem suorum malorum ἐπόπτην, seu inspectorem, vocat, ad ἔποπος, seu upupae nomen alludens, in quam mutatus Tereus factus est πε-τραῖος ὄρνις, saxatilis avis, aut gallina, si mavis. Quid, quod hodieque upupam Graecorum vulgus vocat ἀγριοκόκορον, gallum sylvestrem? Gallus dicitur ratione cristae, quamvis in gallo crista ex cartilagine constet, in upupa ex plumis. Cur porro montanus vocetur, et sylvestris, et saxatilis, non est obscurum: cum III, 111 in montium jugis, et praeruptis rupium, et sylvarum recessibus commorari soleat. The subject is Tereus, whom he calls the ἐπόπτην [epoptēn], or overseer, of his own evils, alluding to the name ἔποψ [epops], or hoopoe, into which Tereus was changed and became a πετραῖος ὄρνις [petraios ornis], a rock bird, or hen if you prefer. What of the fact that even today the common people of the Greeks call the hoopoe ἀγριοκόκορον [agriokokoron], wild cock? It is called a cock on account of its crest, although in the cock the crest consists of cartilage, in the hoopoe of feathers. Further, why it is called mountain, wild and rock bird is not obscure, since III, 111 it usually dwells on mountain ridges, steep cliffs and the recesses of forests. The Philosopher, History, book 1, chapter 1: Τὰ δ᾽ ὄρεια (ζῶα), ὥσπερ ἔποψ [Ta d᾽ oreia (zōa), hōsper epops]: Some animals are mountain-dwellers, like the hoopoe. I pass over the fact that some write that the hoopoe, in the ancient superstition of magicians and augurs, was among the foremost of the birds of omen. The Arabic Pandectarius tells the fable that the stone Quirin betrays secrets and stirs up fantasies if it is placed on the breast of a sleeper, and that it is found in the nest of the hoopoe, which, he says, is wholly a bird of sorcery and augury, as magicians and augurs report. According to Isidore, Origins, book 12, chapter 7: If anyone going to sleep anoints himself with its blood, III, 112 he will see demons choking him. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] has much of this sort in the chapter On the properties of the hoopoe: that by fumigation with this or that part of it harmful things are driven from a dovecote; demons are cast out of the body; a man who has been bound by a spell against his marriage and devotatus [bewitched], to use Apuleius' word, is released; that eating its roasted heart with rue sharpens the wit and stirs the memory; that by hanging up its feathers one wins a lawsuit. He also prescribes various love-potions from the same bird, and likewise drugs by which enemies may be removed, places made desolate, eyes knocked out, and other such superstitious, vain, impious and — ἄῤῥητ᾽ ἀμείνω [arrhēt᾽ ameinō] things. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] treats this same subject in such a way that he scarcely yields to Damir. From this it is clear that the magicians held the hoopoe in the highest wonder as a prodigious animal. ¶

Iudaei quoque narrant, דוכיפת dukiphat Solomoni obtulisse שמיר samir, id est, vermiculum, cujus virtute fissi fuerint templi lapides durissimi. Ita habetur in Cholin ubi supra, דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזהו שהביא שמיר לבית המקדש col. 347 Dukiphat, avis, cui crista alligata est; ea est, quae vermiculum Samir attulit ad domum sanctuarii. De quo prolixam fabulam legat, qui potest, in tractatu Gittin cap. 7. fol. 68. a. b. Summa est, Solomonem, auctore Asmodaeo, rege daemonum, misisse ad hujus avis nidum, quem habet in montibus inter petras durissimas, et repertos ibi pullos, quos in vitro pellucido servi Solomonis incluserint. Ad quos accessus cum matri non pateret amplius, eam attulisse vermem שמיר samir, cujus attactu vitrum statim dissiliit et confractum est. Nam τοῦ samir, inquiunt, ea virtus est, ut non vitrum solum, sed et durissima quaeque diffindat. Itaque eo usus est Moses ad gemmas τοῦ Ephod scalpendas et insculpendas. Et Solomon ad dedolandos templi lapides absque ullo ferramento. Quod ideo duxi observandum, quia etiam in Graecis scriptoribus upupa legitur, non vermis quidem virtute, sed vi herbae cujusdam omnia obstantia perfringere. Aelianus Historiae libri tertii capite vigesimo sexto, Ἔτυχε δὲ ἐν τῷ τοῦ τείχους ἐρημοτέρῳ ὅδε ὁ ὄρνις παιδοποιησάμενος, ἔν τινι ῥήγματι λίθου ὑπὸ τοῦ χρόνου διαστάντι, ἐκεῖν ὁ τοῦ τείχους μελεδωνὸς, ἰδὼν ἔνδον τὰ βρέφη, κατήλειψε τὸν χηραμὸν τῷ πηλῷ· καὶ ὑποστρέψας ὁ ἔποψ, ὡς εἶδεν αὐτὸν ἀποκλεισθέντα, πόαν ἐνόμιζε, καὶ προσήνεγκε τῷ πηλῷ. ὁ δὲ κατεῤῥύη, καὶ προσῆλθε πρὸς τὰ αὐτοῦ ἐκεῖνος τέκνα. εἶτα ἐπὶ νομὴν ἦξεν, αὖθις οὖν ὁ αὐτὸς ἐπήλειψεν ἄν-θρωπος· καὶ ἡ ὄρνις τῇ αὐτῇ πόᾳ ἀνέῳξε τὸν χηραμὸν καὶ τὸ τρίτον ἐπράχθη τὰ αὐτά. Cum in parietis parte maxime deserta peperisset haec avis, in lapidis rima vetustate acta, et parietis curator, pullis intus observatis, luto rimam obduxisset: upupa post reditum, murum obstructum videns, herbam attulit, et luto ad-movit. Quo statim dissoluto, illa ad pullos suos accessit, et inde pastum rediit. Et, cum idem ille vir parietem rursus obduxisset, avis eum hac ipsa herba denuo reseravit: atque id tertio factum est. Vides utrobique hominis industria pullos III, 113 inclusos, et matrem upupam occulta vi haec claustra reserantem, ut illis subveniat. Ut valde sit verisimile, fabulam unam ex altera esse natam.

The Jews also relate that the דוכיפת dukiphat brought Solomon the שמיר samir, that is, a little worm by whose power the hardest stones of the temple were split. So it is found in Cholin, in the place cited above: דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזהו שהביא שמיר לבית המקדש col. 347 Dukiphat, the bird whose crest is bound up; it is the one that brought the little worm Samir to the house of the sanctuary. Whoever can may read a long tale about this in the tractate Gittin ch. 7, fol. 68a b. The gist is that Solomon, on the advice of Asmodaeus, king of the demons, sent to the nest of this bird, which it keeps in the mountains among the hardest rocks; and that the chicks found there were shut up by Solomon's servants in transparent glass. Since the mother no longer had access to them, she brought the worm שמיר samir, at whose touch the glass at once burst apart and was shattered. For the samir, they say, has such power that it splits not only glass but even the hardest of all things. Therefore Moses used it to cut and engrave the gems of the Ephod, and Solomon to dress the stones of the temple without any iron tool. I have thought this worth noting because in Greek writers too the hoopoe is said to break through all obstacles, not indeed by the power of a worm, but by the force of a certain herb. Aelian, History, book 3, chapter 26: Ἔτυχε δὲ ἐν τῷ τοῦ τείχους ἐρημοτέρῳ ὅδε ὁ ὄρνις παιδοποιησάμενος, ἔν τινι ῥήγματι λίθου ὑπὸ τοῦ χρόνου διαστάντι, ἐκεῖν ὁ τοῦ τείχους μελεδωνὸς, ἰδὼν ἔνδον τὰ βρέφη, κατήλειψε τὸν χηραμὸν τῷ πηλῷ· καὶ ὑποστρέψας ὁ ἔποψ, ὡς εἶδεν αὐτὸν ἀποκλεισθέντα, πόαν ἐνόμιζε, καὶ προσήνεγκε τῷ πηλῷ. ὁ δὲ κατεῤῥύη, καὶ προσῆλθε πρὸς τὰ αὐτοῦ ἐκεῖνος τέκνα. εἶτα ἐπὶ νομὴν ἦξεν, αὖθις οὖν ὁ αὐτὸς ἐπήλειψεν ἄνθρωπος· καὶ ἡ ὄρνις τῇ αὐτῇ πόᾳ ἀνέῳξε τὸν χηραμὸν καὶ τὸ τρίτον ἐπράχθη τὰ αὐτά. [Etyche de en tō tou teichous erēmoterō hode ho ornis paidopoiēsamenos, en tini rhēgmati lithou hypo tou chronou diastanti, ekein ho tou teichous meledōnos, idōn endon ta brephē, katēleipse ton chēramon tō pēlō; kai hypostrepsas ho epops, hōs eiden auton apokleisthenta, poan enomize, kai prosēnenke tō pēlō. ho de katerrhyē, kai prosēlthe pros ta autou ekeinos tekna. eita epi nomēn ēxen, authis oun ho autos epēleipsen anthrōpos; kai hē ornis tē autē poa aneōxe ton chēramon kai to triton epratchthē ta auta.] When this bird had hatched its young in the most deserted part of a wall, in a crack of a stone opened by age, and the keeper of the wall, having noticed the chicks inside, had plastered over the crack with mud, the hoopoe on its return, seeing the wall blocked, brought a herb and applied it to the mud. This at once dissolved, and she went in to her chicks, and then went back out to feed. And when the same man had plastered the wall over again, the bird opened it once more with this same herb; and this happened a third time. You see in both accounts chicks shut in by human contrivance III, 113, and the mother hoopoe opening these barriers by a hidden power in order to help them. So it is very likely that the one fable arose from the other. ¶

Thus, just as the Hebrews do with the bird dukiphath, so the Arabs insert many trifles about the hoopoe into the history of Solomon. ¶

I add that there is no apparent reason why the hen, or the attagen, or the urogallus col. 348 or tetraon should be counted among the unclean birds. Indeed, everyone knows that the use of the hen was permitted to the Jews. And you may read the same about the attagen in Aristeas, a spurious author, to be sure, but nevertheless a Jewish one and very old, since he is cited by Josephus as a genuine writer. His words are: Τῶν γὰρ πτηνῶν οἷς χρώμεθα πάντα ἥμερα καθέστηκε. διαφέρει καθαρότητι, πυροῖς καὶ ὀσπρίοις χρώμενα πρὸς τὴν τροφήν· οἷον περιστεραὶ, τρυγόνες, ἀτταγαὶ, πέρδικες, ἔτι χῆνες, καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ὅσα τοιαῦτα. [Tōn gar ptēnōn hois chrōmetha panta hēmera kathestēke. diapherei katharotēti, pyrois kai ospriois chrōmena pros tēn trophēn; hoion peristerai, trygones, attagai, perdikes, eti chēnes, kai ta alla hosa toiauta.] The birds that we use are all tame; they excel in cleanness and are fed on wheat and pulses, such as doves, turtledoves, attagens, partridges, geese too, and others of that kind. (Instead of ἀτταγαὶ [attagai], attagens, ἀττακοὶ [attakoi] is wrongly read, which is a kind of locust, Levit. XI, 22.) The hen and the attagen, therefore, were permitted to the Jews, and there appears to be no reason why they should have been forbidden to them. But in the case of the hoopoe the matter is clear, since it is a most filthy bird, which builds its nest out of dung and feeds on the most fetid things. Aristotle, History, book 9, chapter 15: Ὁ δὲ ἔποψ τὴν νεοττίαν μάλιστα ποιεῖται ἐκ τῆς ἀνθρωπίνης κόπρου. Aelian, History, book 3, III, 114 chapter 26: Οἱ δὲ ἀντὶ τοῦ πηλοῦ χρίουσι τὴν καλιὰν, ἀποπάτημα ἀνθρώπου περιβαλόντες, τῇ δυσωδίᾳ τε καὶ κακοσμίᾳ ἀνείργοντες καὶ ἀναστέλλοντες τὸ ζῶον τὸ ἑαυτοῖς πολέμιον· [Hoi de anti tou pēlou chriousi tēn kalian, apopatēma anthrōpou peribalontes, tē dysōdia te kai kakosmia aneirgontes kai anastellontes to zōon to heautois polemion;] Instead of mud, they plaster their nest with human excrement, warding off and repelling by the stench and foul smell any animal hostile to them. Pliny, book 10, chapter 29: A bird otherwise filthy in its feeding. Cyril, book 3, On Adoration: Εἶδος δὲ τοῦτο στρουθίου τὰ τῶν τελμάτων ἀεὶ δυσωδέστατα περιθέοντος, καὶ τὰ ἐκ βορβόρου καὶ ἀκαθαρσίας συλλέγοντος εἰς τροφήν· [Eidos de touto strouthiou ta tōn telmatōn aei dysōdestata peritheontos, kai ta ek borborou kai akatharsias syllegontos eis trophēn;] It is a kind of little bird that constantly flies about the most fetid parts of marshes and seeks its food from mire and filth. And on Zechariah, volume 3: Φιλοβόρβορον (ἐστὶ) καὶ κοπροβόρον καὶ ταῖς σκωλήκων ἀγέλαις ἀγρίως ἐπιμαινόμενον, καὶ τροφὴν ἀεὶ ποιεῖσθαι κατειθισμένον τὴν ἐσχάτην ἀκαθαρσίαν· [Philoborboron (esti) kai koproboron kai tais skōlēkōn agelais agriōs epimainomenon, kai trophēn aei poieisthai kateithismenon tēn eschatēn akatharsian;] It is a bird that delights in mire, feeds on dung, rages fiercely against swarms of worms, and is accustomed to take all the most unclean things as food. And in Homily 10 On the Paschal Feasts: Ἀκάθαρτον γὰρ τὸ στρουθίον, φημὶ δὴ τὸν ἔποπα, σκωλήκων τε καὶ περισσευμάτων τῶν ἀπὸ γαστρὸς ὅτι μάλιστα βορόν· [Akatharton gar to strouthion, phēmi dē ton epopa, skōlēkōn te kai perisseumatōn tōn apo gastros hoti malista boron;] The hoopoe, I say, is an unclean little bird, which delights above all in feeding on worms and the excrements of the belly. Jerome on Zechariah ch. 5: They call it Ἔποπα [Epopa] from the fact that it settles on human excrement. They say it is a most filthy bird, always dwelling in tombs, always in human dung. Finally, it is also said to make its nest from it, and to feed its young on the little worms of rotting dung. Hence Isidore, Etymologies, book 12, chapter 7: The Greeks call it upupa, because it settles on human excrement and feeds on stinking dung, a most filthy bird R2. Yet I by no means approve this derivation of the Greek name. I prefer to agree with Varro, who derives the name upupa from its call. For it seems constantly to repeat epop or upup. Hence it is told in the myths that Tereus, changed into a hoopoe, cried ποῦ; ποῦ; [pou? pou?] where? where?, as pursuers do, when he was pursuing Procne and Philomela. And the Arabic name of the hoopoe, هدهد [hudhud] hudhud, seems to belong to the same origin. For the calls of birds, since they are not articulate, can be rendered in various ways. ¶

Ad Hebraeum דוכיפת dukiphath accedit Aegyptium KUKUPHA, quo upupa significatur. Hinc in Lexico Coptico ΚΟΥΚΟΥΦᾺ Arabice redditur الهدهد alhudhud, id est, upupa. Syrum quoque ܩܝܩܘܦܐ kikupha, III, 115 in Syro Ferrarii Lexico sic explicatur. Et cucuphae nomine upupam intelligi, constat in his Hori Apollinis libri primi capite quinquagesimo secundo, Εὐχα-ριστίαν γράφοντες (Αἰγύπτιοι) κουκουφὰν ζωγραφοῦσι, διότι τοῦτο μόνον τῶν ἀλόγων ζώων, ἐπειδὰν ὑπὸ τῶν γονέων ἐκτραφῇ, γηράσασιν αὐτοῖς, τὴν col. 349 αὐτὴν ἀνταποδίδωσι χάριν. Gratum indicantes animum (Aegyptii) cucupham pingunt, propterea quod solum hoc ex mutis animantibus, postquam a parentibus educatum est, iisdem, senio confectis, parem refert gratiam. Nempe upupas Aegyptii in honore habent, Aeliano teste, Historiae libri decimi capite decimo sexto; quia sunt πρὸς τοὺς γειναμένους εὐσεβεῖς, erga parentes piae.

Close to the Hebrew דוכיפת dukiphath is the Egyptian KUKUPHA, which means the hoopoe. Hence in the Coptic Lexicon ΚΟΥΚΟΥΦᾺ [koukoupha] is rendered in Arabic by الهدهد [al-hudhud] alhudhud, that is, the hoopoe. The Syriac ܩܝܩܘܦܐ [קיקופא] kikupha too III, 115 is so explained in Ferrarius's Syriac Lexicon. And that the hoopoe is meant by the name cucupha is clear from these words of Horapollo, book 1, chapter 52: Εὐχαριστίαν γράφοντες (Αἰγύπτιοι) κουκουφὰν ζωγραφοῦσι, διότι τοῦτο μόνον τῶν ἀλόγων ζώων, ἐπειδὰν ὑπὸ τῶν γονέων ἐκτραφῇ, γηράσασιν αὐτοῖς, τὴν col. 349 αὐτὴν ἀνταποδίδωσι χάριν. [Eucharistian graphontes (Aigyptioi) koukouphan zōgraphousi, dioti touto monon tōn alogōn zōōn, epeidan hypo tōn goneōn ektraphē, gērasasin autois, tēn autēn antapodidōsi charin.] When they signify gratitude, they (the Egyptians) paint a cucupha, because this alone of the dumb animals, after it has been reared by its parents, repays them the same kindness when they are worn out with age. For the Egyptians hold hoopoes in honour, as Aelian testifies, History, book 10, chapter 16, because they are πρὸς τοὺς γειναμένους εὐσεβεῖς [pros tous geinamenous eusebeis], dutiful towards their parents. ¶

Iam vide, lector, praeclaram metamorphosim upupae in alaudam, cujus auctor Pandectarius: Alauda (inquit) Arabice est upupa avis. Imo Arabice alauda vox est nihili. Sed is ex alhudhud fecit alaudam. Sic Athenienses de alauda fabulari, quod de upupa Brachmanes, observat Aelianus Historiae libri decimi sexti capite quinto.

Now see, reader, a remarkable metamorphosis of the hoopoe into a lark, whose author is the Pandectarius: Alauda [lark], he says, is in Arabic the bird hoopoe. On the contrary, alauda in Arabic is a meaningless word. But he made alauda out of alhudhud. Thus Aelian observes, History book 16, chapter 5, that the Athenians tell of the lark the same story that the Brahmins tell of the hoopoe. ¶
