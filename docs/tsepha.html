III, 182 CAP. IX. col. 399

III, 182 Chapter 9 col. 399 ¶

Probatur, צפע tsepha, vel צפעני tsiphoni, esse Regulum, seu Basiliscum.

It is proved that צפע tsepha, or צפעני tsiphoni, is the regulus, or basilisk. ¶

De serpente צפע tsepha, vel צפעני tsiphoni, legitur Prov. XXIII, 32. Tandem mordebit ut serpens, atque ut tsiphoni punget. Et Esa. XI, 8. Oblectabit se lactens infans super foramen aspidis: et super cavernam tsiphoni, qui nuper ablactatus est, manum suam mittet. Et Es. XIV, 29. E radice serpentis prodibit tsepha. Et Esa. LIX, 5. Ova tsiphoni excludunt, etc. Qui comedit de ovis eorum, morietur: et, si quis ea conterat, erumpet vipera. Et Ierem. VIII, 17. Immittam in eos serpentes tsiphonim, in quos nulla est incantatio; et mordebunt vos, ait Dominus. Ex quibus locis hos constat satis immanes esse serpentes. Sed speciem definire non est obvium. Graeci alibi κεράστας reddunt, alibi ἀσπίδας et ἔγγονα ἀσπίδων, alibi ὄφεις θανατοῦντας· Chaldaeus constanter חורמנין hurmanim; ut Syrus plerumque ܚܘܪܡܢܝܢ hormanin, semel tamen ܐܣܦܣ, aspidem, et semel ܓܪܣܐ, amphisbaenam: Aquila βασιλίσκους, Hieronymus regulos, Munsterus hydros, Iunius haemorrhoos, Castalio viperas. Kimchius solum dicit serpentem esse הקשה והרע, durum et malum: et Aquinas, קשה ורע, durum et dirum.

Of the serpent צפע tsepha, or צפעני tsiphoni, we read in Prov 23:32. At the last it will bite like a serpent, and sting like tsiphoni. And Isa 11:8. The sucking child shall play over the hole of the asp, and the weaned child shall put his hand on the den of tsiphoni. And Isa 14:29. Out of the serpent's root shall come forth tsepha. And Isa 59:5. They hatch the eggs of tsiphoni, etc. He who eats of their eggs shall die; and if one crushes them, a viper will break out. And Jer 8:17. I will send among them serpents, tsiphonim, against which there is no charm; and they shall bite you, says the Lord. From these passages it is clear enough that these are very savage serpents. But to determine the species is not easy. The Greeks in some places render κεράστας [kerastas], in others ἀσπίδας [aspidas] and ἔγγονα ἀσπίδων [engona aspidōn], in others ὄφεις θανατοῦντας [opheis thanatountas]; the Chaldee consistently חורמנין hurmanim, as the Syriac mostly has ܚܘܪܡܢܝܢ [חורמנין] hormanin, but once ܐܣܦܣ [אספס], asp, and once ܓܪܣܐ [גרסא], amphisbaena. Aquila has βασιλίσκους [basiliskous], Jerome regulos, Münster hydros, Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] haemorrhoos, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] viperas. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] says only that the serpent is הקשה והרע, harsh and evil, and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] קשה ורע, harsh and dire. ¶

Nos Hieronymi versionem his omnibus praeferimus. 1. Quia in veteribus plures habet suffragatores. Praeiverat enim Aquila, omnium interpretum longe accuratissimus. Accedit Chaldaei paraphrastis auctoritas, qui חורמן hurman constanter reddit, ut Syrus plerumque ܚܘܪܡܢ horman, id est, regulum: ut Arabicum حرمانا harmene, qua voce vel regulum, vel serpentem, regulo simillimum, significari, constat. Nam immedicabilem esse utriusque morsum, et utrumque non solum morsu, sed visu etiam et sibilo nocere, tradit Avicenna. Nempe, ut de basilisco dixerat, تقتل بصفيرها الي علوة ومن وقع عليه بصرها من بعيد مات Sibilo suo necat col. 400 usque ad sagittae iactum (interpres inepte, si exaltat ipsum). Et moritur is, in quem eminus incidit visus ejus: ita de harmene scribit capite sequenti, قد ذكر حرمانا في صفات قريبة من صفات الملكة من انها لا تسوي وليس انما تقتل باللسع فقط بل باللحظ وباسماع الصفير Utique memoratur, harmene attributa habere proxima basilisci attributis: quia non solo morsu laedit, aut vero interficit, III, 183 sed visu etiam atque auditu sibili. Unde Pandectarius: Harmene, basi- liscus. Item, Karmene (perperam pro Harmene) est serpens, non interficiens morsu tantum imo visu et auditu, sive sibilo. Tantum eos vult Avicenna quantitate differre, cum basilisci longitudo dicatur esse شبران الي ثلثة e duo- bus palmis ad tres: harmenes autem, من ذراع الي ذراع ونصف ab uno cubito ad cubitum et semi. Sed illum hic fugit ratio mensurarum. Nam شبر est palmus major, Graece σπιθαμὴ, seu mensura duodecim digitorum, quae duplicata facit cubitum. Itaque longitudo a duobus ad tres palmos plane eadem est, quae ab uno cubito ad unum cubitum et semi. Et hae duae mensurae differunt verbo tenus, re consentiunt. Sed alii Arabes rectius basilisco solum شبر tribuunt, id est, unam σπιθαμὴν longitudinis. Alkazuinius in capite حية, seu De Serpentibus, ومنها الحية تقال لها الملكة طولها شبرا واكثر Etiam in iis serpens Almelecha (seu basiliscus), cujus longitudo est palmi (majoris) et amplius. Ita etiam Damir in capite الملكة Almelecha. Quibus consona tradunt plerique veterum. Nicander Theriacῶn versu 398.

We prefer Jerome's version to all of these. 1. Because among the ancients it has more supporters. For Aquila, by far the most accurate of all the translators, had led the way. To this is added the authority of the Chaldee paraphrast, who consistently renders חורמן hurman, as the Syriac mostly has ܚܘܪܡܢ [חורמן] horman, that is, the regulus; like the Arabic حرمانا [ḥrmānā] harmene, by which word, it is agreed, either the regulus or a serpent very similar to the regulus is meant. For Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] relates that the bite of both is incurable, and that both harm not only by their bite but also by their gaze and hiss. Thus, as he had said of the basilisk, تقتل بصفيرها الي علوة ومن وقع عليه بصرها من بعيد مات [taqtul bi-ṣafīrihā ilā ghalwa wa-man waqaʿa ʿalayhi baṣaruhā min baʿīd māt] It kills with its hiss col. 400 as far as a bowshot (the translator ineptly has if it raises itself). And he on whom its gaze falls from afar dies; so he writes of the harmene in the following chapter: قد ذكر حرمانا في صفات قريبة من صفات الملكة من انها لا تسوي وليس انما تقتل باللسع فقط بل باللحظ وباسماع الصفير [qad dhukira ḥrmānā fī ṣifāt qarība min ṣifāt al-malika min annahā lā tusawwī wa-laysa innamā taqtul bi-l-lasʿ faqaṭ bal bi-l-laḥẓ wa-bi-ismāʿ al-ṣafīr] It is indeed recorded that the harmene has attributes very close to the attributes of the basilisk, since it injures, or indeed kills, not by its bite alone, III, 183 but also by its gaze and by the hearing of its hiss. Hence the Pandectarius: Harmene, basilisk. Likewise: Karmene (wrongly for Harmene) is a serpent that kills not only by its bite but by its gaze and by being heard, that is, by its hiss. Avicenna holds that they differ only in size, since the length of the basilisk is said to be شبران الي ثلثة [shibrān ilā thalātha] from two spans to three, but that of the harmene من ذراع الي ذراع ونصف [min dhirāʿ ilā dhirāʿ wa-niṣf] from one cubit to a cubit and a half. But here he has overlooked the relation of the measures. For شبر [shibr] is the greater span, in Greek σπιθαμὴ [spithamē], a measure of twelve fingers, which doubled makes a cubit. So a length of two to three spans is exactly the same as one of one to one and a half cubits. These two measures differ in words but agree in substance. Other Arabs, however, more correctly assign the basilisk only one شبر [shibr], that is, one σπιθαμὴν [spithamēn] of length. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] in the chapter حية [ḥayya], or On Serpents: ومنها الحية تقال لها الملكة طولها شبرا واكثر [wa-minhā al-ḥayya tuqāl lahā al-malika ṭūluhā shibran wa-akthar] Among them also is the serpent Almelecha (or basilisk), whose length is a span (the greater) and more. So also Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] in the chapter الملكة [al-malika] Almelecha. Most of the ancients report what agrees with this. Nicander, Theriaca, verse 398. ¶

Ξανθὸς, ἐπὶ τρία δῶρα φέρων μῆκός τε, καὶ ἰθὺν.

Ξανθὸς, ἐπὶ τρία δῶρα φέρων μῆκός τε, καὶ ἰθὺν. [Xanthos, epi tria dōra pherōn mēkos te, kai ithyn.] ¶

Flavus est, ad tres palmos (minores) habens longitudinem, et (crescendi) impetum. Hi tres palmi minores majorem unum faciunt, id est, unam σπιθαμήν. Sunt enim singuli quatuor digitorum. Aelianus Historiae lib. 2. cap. 5. Σπιθαμὴ δὲ τοῦ βασιλίσκου τὸ μῆκός ἐστι· Basilisci longitudo est unius spithamae, seu palmi majoris. Plinius lib. 8. cap. 21. Cyrenaica hunc generat provincia, duodecim non amplius digitorum magnitudine. Aëtius Tetrab. 4. Serm. 1. cap. 33. Hoc ani- mal magnitudine est trium palmorum. Graece, nisi fallor, τριῶν παλαιστῶν. Haec vera basilisci magnitudo est, quam in triplum auxerunt, qui cubitum cum semisse, aut tres σπιθαμὰς ei assignarunt, majores palmos cum minoribus confundentes; quod frequenter fieri monent scriptores. Porro cur a Chaldaeis vocetur חורמן hurman, his verbis aperit Rabbi Selomo in Genes. XLIX, 17. חורמן שם מין נחש שאין רפואה לנשיכתו והוא צפעני וקרוי חורמן על שם שעושה הכל חרם, Hurman vocatur serpentis species, cujus morsus est insanabilis. Is est Hebraeis TSIPHONI, et Chaldaice dicitur HURMAN; quia omnia facit חרם, va- stationem: id est, quia omnia perdit, et vastat, et ad internecionem destruit.

It is yellow, about three (lesser) palms in length, and has a (growing) impetus. These three lesser palms make one greater palm, that is, one σπιθαμή [spithamē]; for each is of four fingers. Aelian, History book 2, ch. 5: Σπιθαμὴ δὲ τοῦ βασιλίσκου τὸ μῆκός ἐστι [Spithamē de tou basiliskou to mēkos esti]; The length of the basilisk is one spithame, or greater palm. Pliny book 8, ch. 21: The province of Cyrenaica produces it, no more than twelve fingers in size. Aëtius, Tetrab. 4, Serm. 1, ch. 33: This animal is three palms in size; in Greek, if I am not mistaken, τριῶν παλαιστῶν [triōn palaistōn]. This is the true size of the basilisk, which was tripled by those who assigned it a cubit and a half, or three σπιθαμάς [spithamas], confusing the greater palms with the lesser; writers warn that this happens frequently. Further, why it is called חורמן hurman by the Chaldeans, R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] explains in these words on Genes. XLIX, 17.: חורמן שם מין נחש שאין רפואה לנשיכתו והוא צפעני וקרוי חורמן על שם שעושה הכל חרם, Hurman is the name of a species of serpent whose bite is incurable. It is TSIPHONI to the Hebrews, and in Chaldee is called HURMAN, because it makes everything חרם, a devastation: that is, because it destroys and lays waste all things and annihilates them. ¶

Secundo, Hebraeum nomen צפע tsepha, vel צפעני tsiphoni, basilisco appri- col. 401 me convenit; quia ipso sono videtur horrorem sibili exprimere, quo caetera animalia vel fugat, vel interficit. Solum fugare dixerant vetustiores. Nicander ubi supra, III, 184 Οὐκ ἄρα δὴ κείνου σπειραχθέα κνώδαλα γαίης Ἰϋγὴν μίμνουσιν, etc.

Second, the Hebrew name צפע tsepha, or צפעני tsiphoni, fits the basilisk col. 401 exceedingly well, because by its very sound it seems to express the horror of the hiss with which it either puts the other animals to flight or kills them. The older writers had said only that it puts them to flight. Nicander, in the place cited above, III, 184 Οὐκ ἄρα δὴ κείνου σπειραχθέα κνώδαλα γαίης Ἰϋγὴν μίμνουσιν [Ouk ara dē keinou speirachthea knōdala gaiēs Iygēn mimnousin], etc. ¶

Ejus sibilum non exspectant spira (caudae) onusta (id est, majora) terrae reptilia. Archelaus in Aeliano, libri secundi capite septimo; Ἐπὰν δὲ βασιλίσκου συρίγματος ἀκούσῃ, (τὰ τῶν ὄφεων φῦλα) τὰ μὲν ὑπὸ τοῖς ἰλεοῖς, καὶ τῇ ψάμμῳ, ἀφανίζεσθαι τὴν ταχίστην· Cum reguli sibilum audiverunt (anguium genera) statim in cavernis, aut sub arena sese abdere, etc. Lucanus libro nono,

The reptiles of the earth, laden with coils (of the tail) (that is, the larger ones), do not await its hiss. Archelaus in Aelian, book 2, chapter 7: Ἐπὰν δὲ βασιλίσκου συρίγματος ἀκούσῃ, (τὰ τῶν ὄφεων φῦλα) τὰ μὲν ὑπὸ τοῖς ἰλεοῖς, καὶ τῇ ψάμμῳ, ἀφανίζεσθαι τὴν ταχίστην [Epan de basiliskou syrigmatos akousē, (ta tōn opheōn phyla) ta men hypo tois ileois, kai tē psammō, aphanizesthai tēn tachistēn]; When they have heard the hiss of the regulus (the kinds of snakes) at once hide themselves in holes or under the sand, etc. Lucan, book 9: ¶

Sibilaque effundens omnes terrentia pestes.

And pouring forth hisses that terrify all the pests. ¶

Plinius, Sibilo omnes fugat serpentes. Solinus, Sibilum ejus etiam serpentes alii perhorrescunt. Sed qui sequuntur basilisci sibilum lethiferum esse asserunt. Galenus, capite octavo Ad Pisonem, Ὁρασθεὶς μόνον, καὶ συρίττων ἀκουσθεὶς, ἀναιρεῖ τοὺς ἀκούσαντας, καὶ τοὺς ἰδόντας αὐτόν· Visus solum, atque auditus cum sibilat, audientes et videntes se necat. Aëtius, Ajunt, quod sibilans auditus, eos qui ipsum audierunt tollat. Damir, ubi De Serpentum speciebus: ونوع اخر اذا سمع الانسان صغيره مات Est alia serpentis species, cujus sibilum quisquis audit, moritur. Et Alkazuinius, اذا صغرت تموت من صغيرها كل حيوان سمع ذلك بعد ما ينفخ ويسيل منه الصديد, Cum sibilat, ejus sibilo audito, moriuntur omnia animalia, quamprimum flavit, et venenum a se dimisit. His adde, quae ex Avicenna jam citata sunt. Sed notetur inprimis hic Isidori locus; SIBILUS idem est, qui et regulus; sibilo enim occidit, antequam vel mordeat, vel exurat. Itaque ut Latine sibilus a sibilo: ita Hebraice TSEPHA, vel TSIPHONI, dici potuit a sono, quem edit inter sibilandum.

Pliny: With its hiss it puts all serpents to flight. Solinus: Even other serpents shudder at its hiss. But later writers assert that the basilisk's hiss is deadly. Galen, chapter 8 of To Piso: Ὁρασθεὶς μόνον, καὶ συρίττων ἀκουσθεὶς, ἀναιρεῖ τοὺς ἀκούσαντας, καὶ τοὺς ἰδόντας αὐτόν [Horastheis monon, kai syrittōn akoustheis, anairei tous akousantas, kai tous idontas auton]; Merely by being seen, and heard when it hisses, it kills those who hear and see it. Aëtius: They say that, heard hissing, it carries off those who have heard it. Damir, where he treats Of the species of serpents: ونوع اخر اذا سمع الانسان صغيره مات [wa-nawʿ ākhar idhā samiʿa al-insān ṣaghīrahu māta] There is another species of serpent: whoever hears its hiss dies. And Alkazuinius: اذا صغرت تموت من صغيرها كل حيوان سمع ذلك بعد ما ينفخ ويسيل منه الصديد [idhā ṣaghirat tamūtu min ṣaghīrihā kull ḥayawān samiʿa dhālika baʿda mā yanfukhu wa-yasīlu minhu al-ṣadīd], When it hisses, on hearing its hiss all animals die, as soon as it has blown and released its poison. Add to these what has already been cited from Avicenna. But note especially this passage of Isidore: The SIBILUS is the same as the regulus; for it kills by its hiss before it either bites or burns. Thus, just as in Latin sibilus comes from sibilo [to hiss], so in Hebrew it could be called TSEPHA or TSIPHONI from the sound it makes while hissing. ¶

Tertio ad Hebraeum צפע tsepha videtur alludere Verbum Arabicum سفع saphaa, quod flatu adurere significat. Unde καύσωνες, venti adurentes dicuntur سوافع (1). Id nempe basilisci proprium, quod flatu adurit. Horus libri III, 185 primi Hieroglyphicῶn capite primo, Προσφυσῆσαν ἑτέρῳ παντὶ ζώῳ (τοῦτο γέ-νος,) δίχα καὶ τοῦ δακεῖν ἀναιρεῖ· Hoc genus, si aliud quodvis animal afflaverit, etiam sine morsu interimit. Et libri secundi capite quinquagesimo septimo, Τοὺς πλησιάζοντας τῷ ἑαυτοῦ φησήματι φονεύει· Accedentes afflatu suo perimit. Aelianus Historiae libri secundi capite quinto, Θεασάμενος ὁ τῶν ὄφεων μήκιστος αὐτὸν, οὐκ εἰς ἀναβολὰς, ἀλλ᾽ ἤδη ἐκ τῆς τοῦ φυσήματος προσβολῆς αὐτός ἐστιν (lege καυτὸς, vel καυστός ἐστιν). Eum intuitus serpens unus e maximis, non post moram aliquam, sed statim flatus ipsius vi exuritur. Plinius libri octavi capite vigesimo primo, Necat frutices non contactos modo, verum et afflatos, exurit herbas, rumpit saxa, talis vis malo est. Solinus, Exstinguit herbas, necat arbores, ipsas etiam corrumpit auras. Itaque, ut basiliscus צפע tsepha dicitur, ita سفع saphaa, de vento, est urere, et torrere quomodo basiliscus, in cujus ha-litu est vis caustica. Illud quidem per Tsade, hoc vero per Sin scribitur. Sed apud Arabes literae Sin, et Sad, vel Sade, sunt ita permutabiles, ut innumera vocabula et per hanc et per illam scribant. Sic سلط, vel صلط praefecit. اسلنطح, vel اصلنطح, latus fuit: سوق vel صوق, platea: col. 402 سراط vel صراط, via, si cum Kesre; et gladius longus, si cum Damma scribatur: سقاب vel صقاب, vitta cruenta, quam gestant Arabicae mulieres, cum in grave aliquod infortunium inciderunt: سنط vel صنط, acacia: سعطر vel صعطر, origanum: اسفنط vel اصفنط, species potus ex succo uvarum, aromatibus condito: سعوط vel صعوط, ἔῤῥινον purgando capiti: مسمرط vel مصمرط, oblongum de capite, etc. (2).

Third, the Arabic verb سفع [safaʿa] saphaa, which means to scorch with a blast, seems to allude to the Hebrew צפע tsepha. Hence καύσωνες [kausōnes], scorching winds, are called سوافع [sawāfiʿ] R1. For this is precisely the property of the basilisk: it scorches with its breath. Horapollo, book 1 III, 185 of the Hieroglyphica, chapter 1: Προσφυσῆσαν ἑτέρῳ παντὶ ζώῳ (τοῦτο γένος,) δίχα καὶ τοῦ δακεῖν ἀναιρεῖ [Prosphysēsan heterō panti zōō (touto genos,) dicha kai tou dakein anairei]; This kind, if it has breathed upon any other animal, kills it even without a bite. And book 2, chapter 57: Τοὺς πλησιάζοντας τῷ ἑαυτοῦ φησήματι φονεύει [Tous plēsiazontas tō heautou phēsēmati phoneuei]; It kills those who approach with its breath. Aelian, Historia book 2, ch. 5: Θεασάμενος ὁ τῶν ὄφεων μήκιστος αὐτὸν, οὐκ εἰς ἀναβολὰς, ἀλλ᾽ ἤδη ἐκ τῆς τοῦ φυσήματος προσβολῆς αὐτός ἐστιν [Theasamenos ho tōn opheōn mēkistos auton, ouk eis anabolas, all᾽ ēdē ek tēs tou physēmatos prosbolēs autos estin] (read καυτὸς [kautos], or καυστός ἐστιν [kaustos estin]). A serpent, one of the very largest, on seeing it, is burnt up not after some delay but at once by the force of its breath. Pliny, book 8, chapter 21: It kills shrubs, not only those it touches but also those it breathes upon; it scorches grasses, it bursts rocks: such is the power of this evil creature. Solinus: It extinguishes grasses, kills trees, and corrupts even the very air. Therefore, just as the basilisk is called צפע tsepha, so سفع [safaʿa] saphaa, said of the wind, means to burn and to scorch, as the basilisk does, in whose breath there is a caustic power. The former, indeed, is written with Tsade, the latter with Sin. But among the Arabs the letters Sin and Sad or Sade are so interchangeable that they write countless words with either. Thus سلط [sallaṭa] or صلط [ṣallaṭa], he set in authority; اسلنطح [islanṭaḥa] or اصلنطح [iṣlanṭaḥa], it was broad; سوق [sūq] or صوق [ṣūq], street; col. 402 سراط [sirāṭ] or صراط [ṣirāṭ], way, if written with Kesre, and long sword, if with Damma; سقاب [siqāb] or صقاب [ṣiqāb], a bloodied fillet, which Arab women wear when they have met with some grievous misfortune; سنط [sanṭ] or صنط [ṣanṭ], acacia; سعطر [saʿtar] or صعطر [ṣaʿtar], origanum; اسفنط [isfanṭ] or اصفنط [iṣfanṭ], a kind of drink made from grape juice seasoned with spices; سعوط [saʿūṭ] or صعوط [ṣaʿūṭ], an ἔῤῥινον [errhinon] for purging the head; مسمرط [musamraṭ] or مصمرط [muṣamraṭ], oblong of head, etc. R2. ¶

III, 186 Quarto, etsi basiliscus in Iudaea nullus fuerit, Iudaeis tamen potuit esse notus per Aegyptios. In Aegypto enim erat frequens, et in Libya et Cyrenaica, quae Aegypto vicinae sunt. Ammianus libro vigesimo secundo, Serpentes quoque Aegyptus alit innumeros, ultra omnem perniciem saevientes, basiliscos et amphisbaenas, etc. Plinius libri octavi capite vigesimo primo, Cyrenaica hunc generat provincia. Solinus, Cyrenis a tergo barbarorum variae nationes, et solitudo inculta, squallens et inaccessa, quae basiliscum creat. Aelianus, libri tertii capite trigesimo primo, Libyam vocat τὴν τῶν τοιούτων τροφὸν, talium altricem terram. Et Avicenna abundare dicit ببلاد الترك ولوبية in regione Turciae et Libyae, non Arorch et Nubiae, ut habent interpretes. Itaque basiliscus in Aegyptiis fuit notissimus, qui sua lingua ΟΥΒΑΙ͂ΟΝ vocabant (ut hodieque ʼΟΒΙῸΝ vocant serpentem quemvis) et deorum capitibus basiliscum aureum circumponebant. Et in Symbolis Sycophantam per basiliscum designabant, qui solo afflatu perimit: ut aevum basilisci imagine, caudam sub reliquo corpore abscondentis. Vide Horum libri primi capite primo, et libri secundi capite quinquagesimo septimo.

III, 186 Fourth, even if there was no basilisk in Judaea, it could nevertheless have been known to the Jews through the Egyptians. For it was common in Egypt, and in Libya and Cyrenaica, which border on Egypt. Ammianus, book 22: Egypt also nourishes innumerable serpents, raging beyond all destructiveness: basilisks and amphisbaenas, etc. Pliny, book 8, chapter 21: The province of Cyrenaica produces it. Solinus: Behind Cyrene lie various barbarian nations, and an uncultivated wilderness, squalid and inaccessible, which breeds the basilisk. Aelian, book 3, chapter 31, calls Libya τὴν τῶν τοιούτων τροφὸν [tēn tōn toioutōn trophon], the land that nurtures such creatures. And Avicenna says it abounds ببلاد الترك ولوبية [bi-bilād al-Turk wa-Lūbiya] in the region of Turkey and Libya, not of Arorch and Nubia, as the translators have it. Thus the basilisk was very well known to the Egyptians, who in their language called it ΟΥΒΑΙ͂ΟΝ [ouBAION] (just as even today they call any serpent ʼΟΒΙῸΝ [obion]), and they placed a golden basilisk around the heads of the gods. And in their symbols they signified a sycophant by the basilisk, which kills by its breath alone, just as they signified eternity by the image of a basilisk hiding its tail beneath the rest of its body. See Horapollo, book 1, chapter 1, and book 2, chapter 57. ¶
