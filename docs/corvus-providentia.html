II, 794 col. 203 CHAPTER XI. ¶

Dei providentia circa Corvos.

The providence of God concerning ravens. ¶

Cum corvum appareat hactenus vile et abjectum esse animal, et hominibus odiosum, nonnulli mirantur, cur corvos Deo esse curae sacra scriptura toties et tam diserte testetur. Sic Iob. XXXIX, 3. (al. XXXVIII, 41.) Quis corvo praeparat escam suam, cum pulli eius ad Deum clamant, errantes inopia cibi? Et Psal. CXLVII, (al. CXLVI,) 9. Qui dat jumentis escam suam, et pullis corvorum, qui clamant. Et Luc. XII, 24. Considerate corvos, qui neque serunt, neque metunt, neque cellam, neque horreum habent, et tamen Deus alit illos. Nec patiuntur multi veteres haec ad literam intelligi. Deum invocare (inquit Hilarius in Psalmum CXLVI.) proprium hominis officium est. Corvorum vero pulli quomodo Deum invocant? Et cur pulli corvorum potius, quam corvi etc.? Aut quae esca praebetur? Nunquid ut foenum jumentis, et ut herbam hominibus, sic corruptorum cadaverum foetidas sordes Deus orantibus se corvorum pullis, et pecudum mortes ad cibum vitae praesentis impertiet? Haec ita intelligere, non dicam erroris, sed irreligiositatis extremae est. Et Augustinus, Hoc forte putabimus, quia corvi Deum invocant, ut det illis escam. Ne hoc cogitetis, irrationalem animam invocare Deum etc. In figura accipite dictum. Et auctor Commentariorum in Psalmos sub Hieronymi nomine, Responde, Iudaee, responde, qui Iudaicam literam tantum sequeris. Dicis istos corvos vere corvos esse? Si ergo de pullis corvorum scriptum est, „Et pullis corvorum invocantibus eum;“ Et scriptum est Ioel. II. a). Omnis, qui invocaverit nomen Domini, salvus erit: Ergo et pulli corvorum salvi erunt in resurrectione. Itaque ad allegorias, tanquam ad sacram anchoram, confugiunt sacri Doctores. Et per corvos intelligunt gentes; per pullos corvorum, Christianos, qui, utut orti ex gentibus, ta- col. 204 men verum Deum invocant. At, quod illorum pace dixero, Allegoriae literalem sensum non destruunt, sed praesupponunt. Illis igitur indulgere nolim, antequam de literali sensu constet. Quem in his locis si rejicimus, nihil erit in scriptura certum, et quod non facile possit alio detorqueri. Iobum enim quidni fateamur tam de veris corvis egisse capite XXXIX. quam de veris ibicibus, leonibus, onagris, equis, aquilis, struthionibus etc. in iis, quae hoc eodem capite vel sequuntur, vel antecedunt? In tot animalium descriptionibus cum vltro fateamur, reliquas ad literam sumi, cur haec sola nihil habebit, quod non sit allegoricum? Ita enim in his Davidis, Quae dat jumentis escam suam, et pullis II, 795 corvorum, qui clamant, cum versiculi pars prior vera jumenta, quidni posterior quoque veros cervos significabit? Et in Christi verbis, Considerate corvos, qui neque serunt, neque metunt etc. aves intelligi, non homines, aperte docet 1. hujus loci collatio cum parallelo Matth. VI, 26. Intuemini volucres coeli, quae non serunt, neque metunt, etc. 2. Quod negantur serere, et metere, et horrea habere, in quae proventus suos cogant. 3. Quod in fine versus Christus addit, Quanto vos praestatis volucribus? ut in Luca: aut, Nonne vos illis longe praestatis? ut in Matthaeo habetur. 4. Denique si corvi, aut aves allegoricae intelligantur, lilia quoque in sequentibus non erunt vera lilia, sed quidquid τοῖς ἀλ-ληγορηταῖς libuerit confingere. Hoc igitur certum maneat, in locis, quae citavimus, veros corvos significari. Nec est, quod quemquam moveat, quod ex Psalmo objicitur, τοῖς νεοσσοῖς τῶν κοράκων τοῖς ἐπικαλουμένοις αὐτὸν, pullis corvorum invocantibus eum, cum Hebraea solum habeant, Deum escam suam dare לבני עורב אשר יקראו, filiis corvi, qui clamant. Quod sequuntur Chaldaea, Syra, et Hieronymiana versio. Sed, post Graecos, Vulgatus interpres, Aethiops, et Arabes duo, Pronomen addiderunt; quasi Propheta expresse dixerit, corvorum pullos Deum invocare, aut clamare ad Deum. Tamen si, post verba Davidis, pullis corvorum, qui clamant, aliquis supplendum censeat ad Deum, non vehementer pugnabo, cum in Iobo diserte legantur pulli corvorum ad Deum clamare: sed quo sensu dicuntur leunculi rugire ad praedam, et ut quaerant a Deo escam suam, Psal. CIV, 21. Et omnium animalium oculi ad Deum spectare, qui dat illis escam tempore suo, Psal. CXLV, 15. Et bestiae agri ad eum glocitare, eo quod exaruerunt alvei aquarum, Ioel. I, 20. Recte igitur Theodoretus, Οὐ λογικὴ τῶν κοράκων ἡ φύσις, οὐδ᾽ ὅτι λογικῶς ἐπικαλεῖται τὸν ποιητήν· ἀλλ᾽ ἡ διὰ τὴν χρείαν κραυγὴ μιμεῖται τὴν αἴτησιν. Non est rationalis corvorum natura, neque rationis ductu opificem invocat: sed clamor, ob necessitatem emissus, petitionem imitatur. Quae Elias Cretensis fere totidem verbis exscripsit in Notis ad Gregorii Orationem LI. Et metaphora ubique elegans, cum de corvis agitur, ideo elegantior est, quod, cum aves pleraeque Deum celebrare cantu suo, et hymnos εὐχαριστικοὺς in illius laudes canere videantur, corvus vocem clamosam habet, et obstreperam, tanquam importuni flagitatoris. Et re vera crocitatu suo nonnunquam stipem rogare creditur. Proinde (ut narrat Aelianus Historiae lib. 2. cap. 48.) Κόρακες Αἰγύπτιοι, ὅσοι τῷ Νείλῳ προσδιαιτῶνται, τῶν πλεόντων ἐοίκασιν ἱκέται εἶναι, λαβεῖν τι αἰτοῦντες, corvi Aegyptii, Nilum accolentes, nauigantibus quasi supplices esse videntur, sibi quidpiam dari efflagitantes. Et in Hesychio, Κοράξαι, ἄγαν προσλιπαρῆσαι· πεποίηται παρὰ τοὺς κόρακας (malim παρὰ τοῦ κόρακος.). Quibus verbis vult τὸ κοράξαι idem esse, quod obnixe rogare, et vocem esse a corvis sumptam. Et rationem addit Suidas in Κοράττειν, addens, sumptam esse ἀπὸ τῶν περὶ τὰς θύρας κοράκων περιπετομένων, II, 796 καὶ μὴ ἀποχωρούντων, a corvis, qui (aliquid petentes) aedium fores circumvolitant, neque inde discedunt.

Since it appears from what has been said so far that the raven is a base and abject animal, and hateful to men, some wonder why sacred Scripture so often and so explicitly testifies that ravens are a care to God. Thus Job 39:3 (var. XXXVIII, 41.): Who prepares for the raven its food, when its young cry to God, wandering for lack of food? And Psal. CXLVII, (var. CXLVI,) 9. Who gives to the beasts their food, and to the young of the ravens that cry. And Luc. XII, 24: Consider the ravens, which neither sow nor reap, which have neither storeroom nor barn, and yet God feeds them. Nor do many of the ancients allow these passages to be understood literally. To call upon God (says Hilary on Psalm 146) is the proper office of man. But how do the young of ravens call upon God? And why the young of ravens rather than the ravens etc.? Or what food is supplied? Will God, as he gives hay to the beasts and grass to men, so bestow the stinking filth of rotting carcasses and the deaths of cattle as food for the present life on the young ravens that pray to him? To understand these things so is, I will not say an error, but the height of irreligion. And Augustine: Shall we perhaps think this, that ravens call upon God to give them food? Do not imagine this, that an irrational soul calls upon God etc. Take what is said as a figure. And the author of the Commentaries on the Psalms under the name of Jerome: Answer, Jew, answer, you who follow only the Jewish letter. Do you say these ravens are truly ravens? If then it is written of the young of ravens, „And to the young of ravens calling upon him;“ and it is written Ioel. II. a), Whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved: then the young of ravens too will be saved in the resurrection. And so the sacred Doctors take refuge in allegories as in a sacred anchor. By ravens they understand the gentiles; by the young of ravens, Christians, who, though sprung from the gentiles, col. 204 nevertheless call upon the true God. But, if I may say so with their leave, allegories do not destroy the literal sense but presuppose it. I would therefore not indulge in them before the literal sense is established. If we reject it in these passages, nothing in Scripture will be certain, nothing that cannot easily be twisted elsewhere. For why should we not admit that Job in chapter 39 treated of real ravens just as of real ibexes, lions, wild asses, horses, eagles, ostriches etc. in what follows or precedes in this same chapter? Since in so many descriptions of animals we freely admit that the rest are taken literally, why should this one alone contain nothing that is not allegorical? So too in these words of David, Who gives to the beasts their food, and to the young II, 795 of the ravens that cry: since the first part of the verse signifies real beasts, why should the latter not also signify real ravens? And in Christ's words, Consider the ravens, which neither sow nor reap etc., that birds are meant, not men, is plainly shown 1. by comparing this passage with the parallel Matt 6:26. Behold the birds of the air, which do not sow, nor reap, etc. 2. Because they are denied to sow, reap and have barns into which to gather their produce. 3. Because at the end of the verse Christ adds, How much more are you worth than the birds? as in Luke; or, Are you not of much more value than they? as in Matthew. 4. Finally, if ravens or birds are understood allegorically, the lilies in what follows will not be real lilies either, but whatever it pleases τοῖς ἀλληγορηταῖς [tois allēgorētais] to invent. Let this therefore stand certain, that in the passages we have cited real ravens are meant. Nor should anyone be moved by the objection from the Psalm, τοῖς νεοσσοῖς τῶν κοράκων τοῖς ἐπικαλουμένοις αὐτὸν [tois neossois tōn korakōn tois epikaloumenois auton], to the young of ravens calling upon him, since the Hebrew has only that God gives his food לבני עורב אשר יקראו, to the sons of the raven, which cry. This is followed by the Chaldee, the Syriac, and Jerome's version. But, after the Greeks, the Vulgate translator, the Ethiopic, and the two Arabic versions added the pronoun, as if the Prophet had expressly said that the young of ravens call upon God, or cry to God. Yet if anyone thinks that after David's words, to the young of ravens that cry, one should supply to God, I will not strongly contest it, since in Job the young of ravens are explicitly said to cry to God: but in the sense in which young lions are said to roar after their prey, and to seek their food from God, Ps 104:21; and the eyes of all animals to look to God, who gives them their food in its season, Ps 145:15; and the beasts of the field to cry out to him, because the watercourses are dried up, Joel 1:20. Rightly therefore Theodoret: Οὐ λογικὴ τῶν κοράκων ἡ φύσις, οὐδ᾽ ὅτι λογικῶς ἐπικαλεῖται τὸν ποιητήν· ἀλλ᾽ ἡ διὰ τὴν χρείαν κραυγὴ μιμεῖται τὴν αἴτησιν [Ou logikē tōn korakōn hē physis, oud᾽ hoti logikōs epikaleitai ton poiētēn; all᾽ hē dia tēn chreian kraugē mimeitai tēn aitēsin]. The nature of ravens is not rational, nor does it call upon its maker by the guidance of reason: but the cry uttered from need imitates a petition. Elias of Crete copied this almost word for word in his Notes on Gregory's Oration 51. And the metaphor, elegant everywhere, is the more elegant where ravens are concerned, because, while most birds seem to celebrate God with their song and to sing hymns εὐχαριστικοὺς [eucharistikous] in his praise, the raven has a clamorous and noisy voice, like an importunate beggar. And in fact it is believed sometimes to ask for alms with its croaking. Hence (as Aelian relates, History book 2, ch. 48) Κόρακες Αἰγύπτιοι, ὅσοι τῷ Νείλῳ προσδιαιτῶνται, τῶν πλεόντων ἐοίκασιν ἱκέται εἶναι, λαβεῖν τι αἰτοῦντες [Korakes Aigyptioi, hosoi tō Neilō prosdiaitōntai, tōn pleontōn eoikasin hiketai einai, labein ti aitountes], the Egyptian ravens that live along the Nile seem to be suppliants, as it were, of those sailing by, begging that something be given them. And in Hesychius: Κοράξαι, ἄγαν προσλιπαρῆσαι· πεποίηται παρὰ τοὺς κόρακας [Koraxai, agan prosliparēsai; pepoiētai para tous korakas] (I would prefer παρὰ τοῦ κόρακος [para tou korakos]). By these words he means that τὸ κοράξαι [to koraxai] is the same as to ask insistently, and that the word is taken from ravens. And Suidas under Κοράττειν [Korattein] adds the reason, saying it was taken ἀπὸ τῶν περὶ τὰς θύρας κοράκων περιπετομένων, II, 796 καὶ μὴ ἀποχωρούντων [apo tōn peri tas thyras korakōn peripetomenōn, kai mē apochōrountōn], from ravens which (seeking something) fly around the doors of houses and do not depart from there. ¶

At, inquiunt, cur a Iobo et Davide non ipsis corvis, sed corvorum pullis col. 205 tribuitur haec escae flagitatio? Cui quaestioni ut satisfiat, Hebraei et Arabes multa comminiscuntur, ut patebit ex sequentibus. Rabbi Eliezer in Capitulis fere in fine cap. 21. Cum corvi genuerunt pullos suos, videntes, eos esse albos, aufugiunt ab illis, existimantes, illos esse pullos serpentis: tum Deus Sanctus Benedictus illis largitur alimentum absque penuria. Rabbi Selomo in Iobum, יתעו מבלי אכל שאביהם שונאם והקב״ה מזמן להם יתושים הנבראים מצואתן ונכנסין לתוך פיהם, Vagantur absque cibo; quia patri suo sunt exosi: sed Deus Sanctus Benedictus culices ipsis praeparat, ex proprio ipsorum stercore procreatos, qui in os eorum ingrediantur, Aben Ezra in Psalmum CXLVII. חכמי הגויים אומרים כי אבותם יעזבום בהולדם, Philosophi gentilium asserunt, eos a parentibus deseri, quam primum nati sunt. Et Kimchius, לפי שהם לבנים בהולדם ואמותיהם מניחים אותם ולא יביאו להם טרפם כי יחשבו כי אינם בניהם לפי שהם לבנים והם צווחים כאלו יקראו לאל בעד טרפם והקב״ה מזמין להם יתושין ואוכלים אותם, quia nascuntur albi, matres eos deserunt, neque iis cibum afferunt: neque enim pro suis agnoscunt, cum albi sint. Proinde clamant, quasi a Deo cibum suum peterent. Et Deus ipsis culices praeparat, quibus vescantur. Haec Hebraei: jam audi Arabes. In his Haririus poeta sic canit in مقامة Mekama, seu stantia 13.

But, they ask, why do Job and David attribute this demand for food not to the ravens themselves, but to the chicks of the ravens? col. 205 To answer this question the Hebrews and Arabs devise many things, as will be clear from what follows. Rabbi Eliezer in the Capitula, near the end of ch. 21: When the ravens have hatched their chicks and see that they are white, they flee from them, thinking that they are the young of a serpent; then God, the Holy One, Blessed be He, grants them food without want. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Job: יתעו מבלי אכל שאביהם שונאם והקב״ה מזמן להם יתושים הנבראים מצואתן ונכנסין לתוך פיהם, They wander without food, because they are hateful to their father; but God, the Holy One, Blessed be He, prepares for them gnats, generated from their own dung, which enter into their mouth. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] on Psalm CXLVII.: חכמי הגויים אומרים כי אבותם יעזבום בהולדם, The philosophers of the gentiles assert that they are deserted by their parents as soon as they are born. And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]: לפי שהם לבנים בהולדם ואמותיהם מניחים אותם ולא יביאו להם טרפם כי יחשבו כי אינם בניהם לפי שהם לבנים והם צווחים כאלו יקראו לאל בעד טרפם והקב״ה מזמין להם יתושין ואוכלים אותם, because they are born white, their mothers desert them and do not bring them food; for they do not recognize them as their own, since they are white. Therefore they cry out, as if they were asking God for their food. And God prepares gnats for them to feed on. So much for the Hebrews; now hear the Arabs. Among them the poet Hariri sings thus in مقامة [maqāma] Mekama, or stantia, 13. ¶

يا رازق النعاب في عشه وجابر العظم الكسير المهيض

يا رازق النعاب في عشه [yā rāziq al-naʿʿāb fī ʿushshihi] وجابر العظم الكسير المهيض [wa-jābir al-ʿaẓm al-kasīr al-mahīḍ] ¶

O qui corvum alis in nido suo, et onagri os fractum et contritum reparas. Cujus distichi primam partem Davidi tribuit Scholiastes, et sic exponit: الغراب اذا فقس عن فراخه خرجت بيضا فاذا راها ابواها كذلك نفرا عنها فتفتح افواها فيرسل الله تبارك وتعالي لها ذبابا فيدخل في افواها فيركون لها غذا حتي تسود فاذا اسودت عاد ابواها اليها فغذياها ويرفع الله الذباب عنها Corvus cum pullos excludit, albi prodeunt. Quales cum vident parentes, fugam capessunt. Illi autem ora aperiunt. Et Deus Benedictus et excelsus, muscas iis mittit, quae, in ora ingredientes, sunt ipsis pro cibo, donec nigrescant. Et tum demum parentes redeuntes eos alunt. Deus autem ab illis muscas amovet. Quae eadem docet Alkazuinius verbis parum diversis. Et Damir aliquanto uberius, اذا خرجت الفراخ II, 797 الغراخ من البيض طردتها لانها تخرج قبيحة المنظر حدا انا تكون صغار الاجرام عظيم الروس والمناقر جرد اللون الاجساد متفواتا الاعضا فالابوان col. 206 ينظران الفرخ كذلك فيتركانه فيجعل الله تعالي قوته في الذباب والبعوض الكاين في عشه الي ان يقوي وينبت ريشه فيعود اليه ابواه فيرزقانه Cum pulli ex ovo exeunt, pellunt eos (parentes); quia exeunt admodum foedi visu. Quippe sunt parvo corpore, magno capite, et rostro, glabra specie, corporis membris divulsis et male cohaerentibus. Parentes igitur pullum deserunt, cum eum talem vident. Sed summus Deus dat ei pro cibo muscas et culices: qui cibus in nido est, donec viribus confirmetur (pullus), et plumae crescant, et ad eum redeant parentes, a quibus alatur. Quod ipsum, ut confirmet alius Arabs, Chalpholachmar nomine, pullorum corvi foetiditatem ita describit: رايت فرخ الغراب فلم ار صورة اقبح منه ولا اسمج الا اقذر ولا اشر رايت راسا كبيرا ومنقارا طويلا وذلك مع صغر البدن وصغر الجناح وهو امرط نتن الريح Vidi pullum corvi, quo nihil unquam foedius, deformius, impurius et nequius a me conspectum est. Vidi magnum caput, et longum rostrum, cum parvo corpore et parvis alis. Etiam glaber is erat et foetentis halitus, etc. Plura ex Arabum scriptis describere non vacat, et haec abunde sufficiunt. Itaque hoc solum addam, etiam e veteribus ecclesiae doctoribus fuisse, qui hanc fabulam non multo aliter adornaverint. Chrysostomus in Sermone De Elia, Κεχηνότες εἰς ἀέρα τῶν κοράκων οἱ νεοσσοὶ, καὶ παρὰ τῶν γονέων μὴ τρεφόμενοι, ἔχουσιν αὐτοὶ ζῶα μικρά τινα τὴν θείαν ἐπιπέμπουσαν πρόνοιαν διακονούμενα· καὶ οὕτως ἐκ τοῦ ἀέρος ἐκεῖνα δεχόμενοι πρὸς αὔξησιν τρέφονται· Ad aërem hiantes corvorum pulli, cum a parentibus non alantur, habent animalcula quaedam, quae Dei providentiae, a quo mittuntur, inserviunt; et iis, ex aëre acceptis, nutriuntur, donec adolescant. Olympiodorus in Iobum, Λέγεται ὁ κόραξ μὴ ἐκτρέφειν τὰ ἔκγονα ὑπὸ λαι-μαργίας, ἀλλ᾽ ἀτημελήτους ἐᾶν τοὺς νεοσσούς· τοὺς δὲ, ὑπὸ τῆς ἐνδείας πιεζο-μένους, φυσικῇ τινι κεχρῆσθαι πρὸς Θεὸν τῇ βοῇ, καὶ ὑπὸ μικρῶν τινων ζωϋ-φίων, γινομένων ἐν ταῖς νεοσσιαῖς, ἐκτρέφεσθαι. Dicitur corvus prae ingluvie foetum suum non alere, sed pullos neglectos deserere: illos vero, urgente fame, clamore quodam naturali ad Deum uti, et parvis quibusdam bestiolis, quae in nidis nascuntur, nutriri. Gregorius in Iobum lib. 30. Editis pullis (corvus), ut fertur, escam praebere dissimulat, priusquam plumescendo nigrescant, eosque inedia II, 798 affici patitur, quoad usque in illis per pennarum nigredinem sua similitudo videatur. Qui huc illucque vagantur in nido, et ciborum expetunt aperto ore subsidium. At, cum nigrescere coeperint, tanto illis praebenda alimenta ardentius requirit, quanto illos alere diutius distulit. Isidorus, Originum lib. 12. cap. 7. Fertur haec col. 207 avis, quod editis pullis escam plene non praebeat, priusquam in iis pennarum nigredinem, similitudinem proprii coloris, agnoscat. Postquam vero tetros (malim atros) plumis aspexerit, in toto agnitos abundantius pascit. Cassiodorus in Psalmos, et qui sub Hieronymi nomine circumfertur, pullos, ajunt, ita neglectos, rore coelesti pasci, qui cicadarum victus est, non corvorum. Muscas vero et culices quis in corvi nido credat nasci tanta copia, ut pluribus pullis, iisque avidissimis, alendis sufficiat? Et statim natos culices in pullorum os devorandos ultro se ingerere? Aut parentes pullos suos ideo odisse, quod nascantur ex parte implumes, cum reliquis avibus id ipsum accidat? Aut ad eos, semel desertos, iterum se conferre, quam primum plumis vestiti sunt, vel cum eae sunt nigriores; ac si ab aliquo moneantur, jam pullos suos melius se habere, nec causam esse amplius, cur ab iis abhorreant? Uno verbo, hoc certum est et jam in ore vulgi, avi nulli nidum suum sordere; et nullum esse animal, cui sibi similium forma non placeat. Hinc Epicharmus apud Laertium in Platone,

O you who nourish the raven in its nest, and repair the broken and crushed jaw of the wild ass. Cassiodorus on the Psalms, and the author who circulates under the name of Jerome, say that the chicks, thus neglected, are fed by heavenly dew, which is the food of cicadas, not of ravens. But who would believe that flies and gnats are born in a raven's nest in such abundance as to suffice to feed several chicks, and very greedy ones at that? And that the gnats, as soon as they are born, of their own accord thrust themselves into the chicks' mouths to be devoured? Or that the parents hate their chicks because they are born partly featherless, when the very same thing happens to other birds? Or that, having once deserted them, they return to them as soon as they are clothed with feathers, or when these are blacker, as if someone had told them that their chicks are now in better shape and there is no longer reason to shrink from them? In a word, this is certain and already a common saying: to no bird is its own nest foul; and there is no animal that is not pleased by the form of its like. For to a dog a dog seems the most beautiful thing, and an ox to an ox, an ass to an ass, a pig to a pig. And Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods book 1: Do you think there is any beast on land or sea that does not take the greatest delight in a beast of its own kind? If it were not so, why would a bull not long to touch a mare, or a stallion a cow? Or do you suppose that an eagle, or a lion, or a dolphin prefers any shape to its own? So the Hebrews and the Arabs talk nonsense when they keep saying that the raven shrinks from its newly hatched chicks because they are wonderfully ugly. For if τὰ κορακίδια [ta korakidia] look vile to us, it does not follow that they look vile also to their parents, who view them with other eyes. And indeed, although these fables were invented solely to explain Scripture, they in no way agree with the words of Scripture. For Job does not say that the chicks of the ravens cry to God in the nest, but when they wander and stray about for lack of food. The subject, therefore, is chicks that have already left the nest. For that chicks should wander here and there within the nest itself, as Gregory says, cannot be conceived; the narrowness of a nest does not allow such wandering of the chicks. Nor in fact does the raven neglect its young while they are in the nest; rather, once they are fully reared and able to fly, it drives them from the nest, so that thereafter they may provide for themselves. The Philosopher, Hist. book 9, ch. 31: Οἱ δὲ κόρακες τοὺς ἑαυτῶν νεοττοὺς, ὅταν οἷοί τ᾽ ὦσιν ἤδη πέτεσθαι, τὸ μὲν πρῶτον ἐκβάλλουσιν, II, 799 ὕστερον δὲ καὶ ἐκ τοῦ τόπου ἐκδιώκουσι· [Hoi de korakes tous heautōn neottous, hotan hoioi t᾽ ōsin ēdē petesthai, to men prōton ekballousin, hysteron de kai ek tou topou ekdiōkousi] But ravens, when their chicks are now able to fly, first cast them out of the nest, then drive them out of the whole district. Pliny, book 10, ch. 12, on the crow: This bird alone even feeds its chicks for some time after they can fly. All the others of the same kind drive their chicks from the nests and force them to fly, as do the ravens too, which drive each of their young, once strong, farther away. Aelian, On Animals book 2, ch. 49: Τούς γε μὴν νεοττοὺς τοὺς ἐκτραφέντας διώκουσι, καὶ τῆς ἑαυτῶν ἕκαστος καλιᾶς φυγάδας ἀποφαίνουσιν ὑπὲρ ὅτου τροφὰς μαστεύουσι. [Tous ge mēn neottous tous ektraphentas diōkousi, kai tēs heautōn hekastos kalias phygadas apophainousin hyper hotou trophas masteuousi.] Their chicks, indeed, once reared, they chase away; and each drives them from its nest, for which reason the chicks thereafter seek their own food. It is only when the chicks have been cast out in this way, then, that there happens col. 208 what the sacred writer relates: that, wandering for lack of food, they as it were cry to God. For since they are not yet at full strength, nor in the perfect vigour of their age, and have not yet grown used to procuring food for themselves by their own effort and industry, they are utterly at a loss, croak loudly, and fill the air with plaintive cries, as though complaining to God of their parents' ἀστοργία [astorgia]. And in these straits God comes to their aid, not by miraculously producing flies and gnats that thrust themselves of their own accord into their mouths, but by suggesting to them what must be done and how to provide against pressing hunger, so that they do not perish of starvation. Thus the same thing happens here to the chicks of ravens as to the whelps of lions, which, deserted by their mother, lack and suffer hunger, Ps 34:11, and roar after their prey, as though seeking their food from God, Ps 104:21. And God satisfies them, Job 39:1 (var. XXXVIII, 39). Yet one need not trouble much over why this is attributed to the chicks of ravens rather than to the ravens themselves, since Christ said it of ravens too, Luke 12:24: Consider the ravens, which neither sow etc. and yet God feeds them. Nor was there any reason for the Arabic translator to render ravens as فراخا الغربان [firākhā al-ghirbān] chicks of ravens. For a translator may not change anything in the words of the sacred text unless necessity compels him, and here no such necessity appears, since Christ says nothing of ravens that does not suit the whole species. Consider, he says, the ravens, which neither sow nor reap, which have neither storeroom nor barn, and yet God feeds them. This is as true of the parents as of the chicks: neither sows or reaps; neither has storerooms or barns. Finally, God provides for both alike. Indeed, Christ applies these same words to all birds, Matt 6:26: Consider, he says, the birds of the air, which neither sow nor reap etc. But what he asserts there of birds in general he narrows, Matt 10:29, to sparrows, which, though very cheap and sold two for a farthing, God nevertheless does not judge unworthy of his care. And in Luke 12:24 he extends the same to ravens too, which, ugly birds though they are, harsh of voice and of ill omen, he nevertheless does not disdain to feed. So nothing peculiar is attributed here to ravens or to the chicks of ravens. Rather, they, like the sparrows, are expressly mentioned among the other birds, to make clear that God's providence not only cares for birds in general, but extends even to the cheapest and most despised of them, II, 800 so that men, and faithful men above all, may conclude the more surely that they are in God's care: what he so kindly grants to little creatures of the lowest order, he will not deny to those by whom he is worshipped and invoked. Having explained these things thus, I will not conceal that in Servius, in his Notes on book 1 of the Georgics, one reads this: Pliny in his Natural History says that ravens are forgetful and for the most part do not return to their nests at all; but by a certain provision of nature they gather at their nests things that can produce worms, by which their abandoned chicks are sometimes fed. Hence it may seem that the fable we have refuted can be confirmed by the authority of Pliny. But there is no reason for this to trouble anyone. For Servius either deceives or is deceived, since nothing of the kind is read either in Pliny or in any of the philosophers who preceded the age of Servius. So this is a mere dream from the ivory gate, of the sort that learned men have already observed slipped from him in large numbers. ¶
