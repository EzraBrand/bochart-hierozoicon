II, 838 CHAPTER 16. col. 238 ¶

Explicatur de Struthione locus obscurissimus Iob. XXXIX, 16. al. 13.

The very obscure passage on the ostrich, Job 39:16 var. 13., is explained ¶

Iobi XXXIX, 16. (al. 13.) struthio-camelus sex versibus describitur, quorum quinque posteriores utcunque explicant interpretes. Sed in primo ita haerent, ut vix ullus sit Scripturae locus, qui minus intelligatur. Neque uspiam inter illos majus est dissidium, quod ex collatione patebit, quam instituere hic libet, ut lectori res sit clarior. Illum igitur versum, quem dico, Septuaginta sic explicant, Πτέρυξ τερπομένων νεέλασσα, ἐὰν συλλάβῃ ἀσίδα, καὶ νέσσα· Ala laetantium neelassa, si conceperit asida et nessa. Et Aquila, Πτερύγιον αἰ-νέντων συναναπλέκει . . . . ἐρώδιος, καὶ ἱέραξ· Alam laudantium simul implicat . . . . ardea et accipiter. Et Symmachus, Πτερὸν ἀγλαισμοῦ περιφύεται, εἰ ἐναγκαλίσεται ὁ κύκνος πτίλους αὐτοῦ· Ala nitoris circumnascitur, si ulnis complectatur cygnus pennas suas. Et Hieronymus, Penna struthionis similis est pennis erodii et accipitris. Et Syrus, quem Arabs fere sequitur, ܒܓܦ ܡܫܒܚܢܐ ܡܬܪܘܡܐ ܘܦܪܚܐ ܘܐܬܝܐ ܘܡܩܢܢܐ, Alis laudum extollitur, et volat, et venit, et nidificat. Et Chaldaeus, גדפא דתרנגול דמשבחא ומקלסא ואין אברא דחוריתא ונוציציתא, Ala galli, qui laudat et celebrat: et an ala ciconiae et accipitris? Quae veterum versiones non solum inter se dissident δὶς διὰ πασῶν, sed et pleraeque absurdae sunt et inconcinnae, et sacro scriptori ea affingunt, quae nullum habent sensum commodum. Neque convenit magis inter quosdam recentiorum. Ita enim Castalio, Utrum generosiores sunt alae struthio-cameli, an pennae plumaeque ciconiae? Et Arias plane aliter, et nullo sensu: Ala exultantium laeta, an penna ciconiae, et pluma? Et Iunius rursus aliter, Alamne pavonum exultandam, an pennam ciconiae; aut struthio-camelo dares? Aliter etiam vernaculae, Gallicae, inquam, Anglicae, Belgicae et Italicae: An dedisti pavonibus, aut, An opera tua pavones habent alas egregias, aut, alas, quibus exultant? An struthio-cameli dedisti pennas et plumas? Quae singula revocare ad examen quia non vacat, sufficiet, ut de vulgari nostra agamus, quae nec ipsa videtur esse accurata satis. Nam duo sunt potissimum, quae in ea desideramus: unum, quod nimis multa supplet, quae suppleri non solent, nempe statim initio versus אם נתת, An dedisti? et literam Lamed, Dativi notam, ante voces רננים et חסידה. Alterum, quod רננים renanim, pavones; et חסידה chasida struthionem explicat: quorum neutri possum assentiri. Dicam igitur de utriusque vocis significatione, quid sentiam, ut vera totius versiculi significatio inde emergat.

In Iobi XXXIX, 16. (var. 13.) the ostrich is described in six verses, of which the interpreters explain the last five after a fashion. But in the first they are so stuck that there is scarcely any passage of Scripture that is less understood. Nowhere is there greater disagreement among them, as will be clear from a comparison, which I am pleased to set out here so that the matter may be clearer to the reader. That verse, then, of which I speak, the Septuagint renders thus: Πτέρυξ τερπομένων νεέλασσα, ἐὰν συλλάβῃ ἀσίδα, καὶ νέσσα· [Pteryx terpomenōn neelassa, ean syllabē asida, kai nessa] The wing of those that rejoice is neelassa, if it has conceived asida and nessa. And Aquila: Πτερύγιον αἰνέντων συναναπλέκει . . . . ἐρώδιος, καὶ ἱέραξ· [Pterygion ainentōn synanaplekei . . . . erōdios, kai hierax] The heron and the hawk together entwine . . . . the wing of those that praise. And Symmachus: Πτερὸν ἀγλαισμοῦ περιφύεται, εἰ ἐναγκαλίσεται ὁ κύκνος πτίλους αὐτοῦ· [Pteron aglaismou periphyetai, ei enankalisetai ho kyknos ptilous autou] A wing of splendour grows round about, if the swan should clasp its feathers in its arms. And Jerome: The feather of the ostrich is like the feathers of the heron and the hawk. And the Syriac, which the Arabic mostly follows: ܒܓܦ ܡܫܒܚܢܐ ܡܬܪܘܡܐ ܘܦܪܚܐ ܘܐܬܝܐ ܘܡܩܢܢܐ [בגף משבחנא מתרומא ופרחא ואתיא ומקננא], With wings of praises it is lifted up, and flies, and comes, and nests. And the Chaldee: גדפא דתרנגול דמשבחא ומקלסא ואין אברא דחוריתא ונוציציתא, The wing of the cock, which praises and celebrates: and is it the wing of the stork and the hawk? These versions of the ancients not only differ from one another δὶς διὰ πασῶν [dis dia pasōn], but most of them are also absurd and incongruous, and attribute to the sacred writer things that yield no suitable sense. Nor is there more agreement among some of the moderns. For Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] has: Are the wings of the ostrich nobler, or the feathers and plumage of the stork? And Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], quite differently and with no sense: The wing of those that exult is glad; or the feather of the stork, and plumage? And Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], differently again: Would you give the wing to the peacocks to exult in, or the feather of the stork, or to the ostrich? Differently also the vernacular versions, I mean the French, English, Dutch and Italian: Have you given to the peacocks, or Do the peacocks have splendid wings through your work, or wings in which they exult? Have you given feathers and plumage to the ostrich? Since there is no leisure to examine each of these, it will suffice to deal with our vulgar version, which itself also does not seem accurate enough. For there are chiefly two things we find wanting in it. One, that it supplies too much that is not usually supplied, namely at the very beginning of the verse אם נתת, Have you given?, and the letter Lamed, the mark of the dative, before the words רננים and חסידה. The other, that it explains רננים renanim as peacocks, and חסידה chasida as ostrich; with neither of which can I agree. I shall therefore say what I think about the meaning of each word, so that the true meaning of the whole verse may emerge from it. ¶

II, 839 Prior nempe רננים renanim, dum Vocales aberant, legi potuit ronenim, id est, cantantes, laudantes, exultantes, etc. Unde natae illae versiones, ala laudantium, ala laetantium, etc. in Senioribus, Aquila et aliis. II, 839 The former, namely רננים renanim, could, while the vowels were absent, be read ronenim, that is, singing, praising, exulting, etc. Hence arose those versions, wing of the praising, wing of the rejoicing, etc., in the Elders [=the translators of the Septuagint], Aquila and others. But the Chaldee and Jerome, even though the points were lacking, nevertheless rightly gathered from the sequence of the words that רננים is something else, namely the name of a bird. And all the Hebrews embrace the same opinion, although they cannot determine which birds are called רננים renanim. The Chaldee renders it cock, not the common one, but תרנגול דמשבחא ומקלסא, the cock which praises and celebrates God. Of which the same translator speaks on the words of Job cursing his day, Job 3:7. Let no song come into it: לא תעול רננא דתרנגול ברא למקלסה בה, Let not the song of the wild cock come to praise in it. And on Iobi XXXVIII, 36. מי שוי בכלין חוכמתא או יהב לתרנגול ברא בינתא למקלסה למריה, Who has put wisdom in the reins? or given the wild cock understanding to praise his Lord? And on Ps 50:11. גלין קדמי כל מיני עופא דפרחין באויר שמיא ותרנגול ברא דקרסולי שרין בארעא ורישיה מטי בשמיא מרנן קדמי, Known to me are all the kinds of birds that fly in the air of the heavens; and the wild cock, whose feet stand on the earth and whose head reaches to heaven, sings before me. Of this same cock Mahomet speaks thus in the Chronica Espahan: لله ديك ابيض جناحاه موشيا بالزبرجد والياقوت واللولو له جناح بالمشرق وجناح بالمغرب وراسه تحت العرش وقوايمه في الهوي يوذن في كل سحر فيسمع اهل السموات والارض الا الثقلين الجن والانس فعند ذلك تجيبه ديوك الارض فاذا دنا يوم القيمة يقال الله تعالي له ضم جناحيك وغض صوتك فيعلم اهل السموات والارض الا الثقلين ان الساعة قد اقتربت [li-llāh dīk abyaḍ janāḥāhu mawshiyyā bi-l-zabarjad wa-l-yāqūt wa-l-luʾluʾ lahu janāḥ bi-l-mashriq wa-janāḥ bi-l-maghrib wa-raʾsuhu taḥt al-ʿarsh wa-qawāʾimuhu fī al-hawā yuʾadhdhin fī kull saḥar fa-yasmaʿ ahl al-samawāt wa-l-arḍ illā al-thaqalayn al-jinn wa-l-ins fa-ʿinda dhālika tujībuhu duyūk al-arḍ fa-idhā danā yawm al-qiyāma yaqūl Allāh taʿālā lahu ḍumma janāḥayka wa-ghuḍḍ ṣawtaka fa-yaʿlam ahl al-samawāt wa-l-arḍ illā al-thaqalayn anna al-sāʿa qad iqtarabat] God has a white cock, whose wings are adorned with emeralds, carbuncles and pearls. One of them is spread towards the east, the other towards the west; its head is beneath the throne of glory, and its feet in the air. Every morning it calls to prayer, and all the inhabitants of heaven and earth hear it, except men and col. 240 demons. Therefore the cocks of the earth answer it. And when the day of judgement is at hand, God Most High will say to it: Draw in your wings and restrain your voice, so that all the inhabitants of heaven and earth, except men and demons, may know that the hour is at hand. Because this fabulous cock is imagined to praise God at fixed hours, whence it is called מרנן merannen, praising, Ps 50:11, and its song רננא renana, Job 3:7, the circumcised doctors would have the same cock signified by the word רננים renanim in the passage with which we are dealing. But it is strange that that cock should be called by a plural word, since it is unique. The same objection could be raised against R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)]. For to him רננים renanim is that bird which in the II, 840 Mishnah is called בר יוכני bor juchne, or son of the nest, of which we spoke above a); and it too is singular and unique in its kind. ¶

Sed, his nugis valere jussis, alios audiamus. R. Levi dubitat, an utraque vox כנף רננים canaph renanim, aut solum ultima pertineat ad hujus avis nomen. Aben Ezra dici vult renanim a verbo רנן ranan, quod cantare est, בעבור נועם קולו, propter elegantiam vocis suae. Et in Pomario quidam pronuntiant avem esse, שמתפאר בקולו הערב רוסיניולו בלעז, quae suavitatem vocis ostentat, Italice dictam rossignuolo, id est, lusciniam. Tamen ipse mavult pavonem intelligi, שמתפאר בראיית כנפיו, qui pennarum suarum ostentatione gloriatur. Et inter utramque sententiam Kimchius ita est ambiguus, ut in posteriorem magis inclinet. Fortasse, inquit, est avis canora, et a cantus modulatione sic dicta: או מרנן ומתעלס בכנפיו שהם יפות כמו הטווס ואולי הוא הוא, aut avis est, quae laetatur, et exultat propter pennarum pulchritudinem, ut pavo, et fortasse is ipse est. Quae sententia, licet nova, nec nisi cunctanter prolata, tamen a nostris accepta est cum summo applausu, tanquam iis verior, quae ad Sagram. Itaque Pagninus hic habet, Alis pavo exultat. Ita etiam Mercerus. Et Iunius, alam pavonum exultandam. Et omnes omnium linguarum vernaculae versiones, in quas incidere licuit, idem sequuntur. Nempe quotquot pavonem, vel ex li-bris, vel ex αὐτοψίᾳ noverunt, eum sciunt pennas cùm pompa et fastu ostentare, illarumque adeo pulchritudine exultare et superbire. But, bidding farewell to these trifles, let us hear others. R. Levi doubts whether both words, כנף רננים canaph renanim, or only the latter, belong to the name of this bird. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] holds that it is called renanim from the verb רנן ranan, which means to sing, בעבור נועם קולו, on account of the elegance of its voice. And in Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] some declare it to be a bird שמתפאר בקולו הערב רוסיניולו בלעז, which displays the sweetness of its voice, called in Italian rossignuolo, that is, nightingale. Yet he himself prefers the peacock to be understood, שמתפאר בראיית כנפיו, which glories in the display of its feathers. And between the two opinions Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] is so undecided that he leans rather to the latter. Perhaps, he says, it is a songbird, and so called from the modulation of its song: או מרנן ומתעלס בכנפיו שהם יפות כמו הטווס ואולי הוא הוא, or it is a bird which rejoices and exults because of the beauty of its feathers, like the peacock, and perhaps it is the peacock itself. This opinion, although new and put forward only hesitantly, has nevertheless been received by our scholars with the greatest applause, as being truer than what happened at the Sagra [the proverb for a thing beyond doubt]. Thus Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae] here has The peacock exults in its wings. So also Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs]. And Junius, the wing of peacocks is to be exulted in. And all the vernacular versions in all languages that I have been able to come across follow the same. For all who have known the peacock, whether from books or from αὐτοψίᾳ [autopsia], know that it displays its feathers with pomp and pride, and so exults and prides itself in their beauty. Hence Ovid in book 1 of the Art [of Love]: ¶

Laudatas ostendit avis Iunonia pennas:
Si tacitus spectes, illa recondit opes.

The bird of Juno displays its feathers when praised:
If you watch in silence, it hides away its riches. ¶

Et Plinii libri decimi capite vigesimo, Gemmantes laudatus expandit colores ad-verso maxime sole, quia sic fulgentius radiant. Sed Graeci potissimum hoc argumento sibi placent, et eloquentiae suae vela tam late explicant, II, 841 quam late pavo pennas expandit. In quibus ut vetustior, ita simplicior Moschus hoc solum habet Idyll. 2.

To this belong these words of Columella, book 9, chapter 11: He covers himself, as if admiring himself, with the jewelled feathers of his tail, and when he does this, he is said to "wheel." And those of Pliny, book 10, chapter 20: When praised he spreads out his jewelled colours, especially facing the sun, because thus they shine more brilliantly. And a little later: And he gathers into a heap all the eyes of his feathers, which he delights to have looked at. But it is above all the Greeks who take pleasure in this theme, and spread the sails of their eloquence as widely II, 841 as the peacock spreads his feathers. Among them Moschus, being older, is also simpler, and has only this, Idyll. 2: ¶

Ὄρνις ἀγαλλόμενος πτερύγων πολυανθέϊ χροιῇ,

Ὄρνις ἀγαλλόμενος πτερύγων πολυανθέϊ χροιῇ, [Ornis agallomenos pterygōn polyantheï chroiē,] ¶

Avis pennarum florido colore exultans. Sed longe plura Dio in Olympico sermone, ubi pavonem describit ἐπαφόμενον, καὶ ἐπιδεικνύοντα τὸ κάλλος τῶν πτε-ρῶν, ὅταν ἁβρύνεται πρὸς τὴν θήλειαν, ἀνακλάσας τὴν οὐρὰν, καὶ περιστήσας αὐτῷ πανταχόθεν, ὥσπερ εὐειδὲς θέατρον, ἤ τινι γραφῇ μιμηθέντα οὐρανὸν ποικίλον ἄστροις· Se ipsum extollentem, et pennarum pulchritudinem ostentantem, cum foeminae delicias facit, caudam infringens, et sibi circumquaque statuens, tanquam speciosum theatrum, aut pictura quadam coelum, sideribus distinctum, imitan- col. 241 tem. Et infra, Ἐν μέσῳ δὲ μάλα ἥσυχον καὶ ἀτρεμοῦντα παρέχει ἑαυτὸν θεάσασθαι, ὥσπερ ἐν πομπῇ περιστρεφόμενον· In medio sese quietum et immobilem praebet spectandum, quasi in pompa circumactum. In priore Dionis loco, pro ἀνακλάσας τὴν οὐρὰν, Nobilissimus Dominus de Grentemesnil legendum monet ἀνακλίσας aperiens, vel expandens; quod mihi maxime arridet. Aelianus Historiae animalium libri quinti capite vigesimo primo, Ὁ ταὼς οἶδεν ὀρνί-θων ὡραιότατος ὢν, καὶ ἔνθα οἱ τὸ κάλλος κάθηται, καὶ τοῦτο οἶδε, καὶ ἐπ' αὐτῷ κομᾷ, καὶ σοβαρός ἐστι, καὶ θαῤῥεῖ τοῖς πτεροῖς, ἅπερ οὖν αὐτῷ καὶ κόσμον παρατίθησι, καὶ πρὸς τοὺς ἔξωθεν φόβον ἀποστέλλει. Pavo scit, se omnium avium esse formosissimum, et, in quo forma sita sit, apprime novit, atque eo sese effert, et superbus est, et plumis suis confidit, quae et ipsi ornamentum afferunt, et alios pavore quodam percellunt. Et infra, Ὅπως ἔχει τῆς εἰς τὴν ἐπί-δειξιν ἀφθονίας παρίστησιν. ἐᾷ γὰρ ἐμπλησθῆναι τῆς θέας τὸν παρεστῶτα, καὶ ἑαυτὸν περιάγει, δεικνὺς φιλοπόνως τὸ τῆς πτερώσεως πολύμορφον. Quanto sui ostentandi desiderio teneatur, demonstrat. Spectatori enim expleri permittit conspectu suo, et quaquaversum obvertitur, pennarum varietatem magno studio ostendens. Why then, by the same reasoning, should not the ostrich also be placed in the first part of the verse? Especially since the descriptions that follow, of the horse, the hawk, the eagle, and of Behemoth and Leviathan, always keep the same rule. Here, then, I shall believe the matter stands in the same way, and that in the first part of the verse is read the name of the animal whose vivid description follows, until someone produces a reason for a difference, and here none presents itself. 5. Since it is agreed that the following description pertains to the col. 247 ostrich, its name had to be put first, just as with the ten other animals which are described by Job. But if רננים renanim are not ostriches, it is certain that the name of the ostrich appears nowhere in this verse. For it is certain that חסידה chasida, which they explain as ostrich, is not the ostrich; since it is a migratory bird, Ierem. VIII, 7, and flies aloft, II, 850 Zech 5:9, and nests in fir trees, Ps 104:17, and has its name from piety: חסידה chasida literally means pious. This name in no way suits the ostrich, since in Lam 4:3 and in this very passage the ostrich is described as a bird impious and pitiless towards its young. Unless they would have it called חסידה κατ᾽ ἀντίφρασιν [kat᾽ antiphrasin], in the way, namely, that they call the Furies Eumenides. Therefore the best interpreters, and even those very ones who would have chasida here be the ostrich, consistently render it elsewhere as stork. That this rendering is most true I shall prove at length in its place. Among the recent interpreters there is one learned and pious man to whom these things were by no means unknown. He, then, although carried away by the common error he takes רננים renanim for peacocks, nevertheless explains חסידה chasida as stork. But because the name of the ostrich could not be absent from this verse without sacrilege, he would have the last word נוצה be not only a bird, with R. Selomo, but the ostrich. But if נוצה is the name of a bird, since it is formed from flying (for the verb נצה means to fly), it will not fit the ostrich, which alone of birds lacks flight and never raises itself from the ground. What of the fact that נוצה is not even a bird? For נוצה signifies nothing other than feather or plumage. This is clear from Ezek 17:3, where the eagle is described as מלא הנוצה, full of plumage, and רב נוצה, of much plumage, or abounding in plumage, as he himself renders it. Why then should a word which there means plumage be taken here for a bird, and indeed for the ostrich? Why should a new and unheard-of meaning be attached to this word, without witness, without example, without any shadow of reason, a meaning which the very origin of the word plainly refutes? From this it follows that the ostrich is not named in the last hemistich, and must therefore be sought in the first, namely in the word רננים renanim. Otherwise it would be entirely absent from this verse R1. And when it is said in the verse immediately following, She leaves her eggs on the ground, etc. (which it is certain are said of the ostrich), these things would be said II, 851 of a bird that has nowhere been named before, and the discourse would be left hanging, nor would these words cohere in any way with what precedes. ¶

Ad primum respondeo, struthionem avem esse non avem, quae in multis accedit ad naturam quadrupedis. Quod pridem observavit Philosophus libro quarto De partibus fere in fine. Verba sunt, Quaedam habet avis, quaedam quadrupedis propria. Non esse quadrupedem, suadet, quod pennas habet, et non esse avem, quod non volat in sublime, nec pennas ad volatum commodas, sed pilosas habet. Insuper tanquam quadrupes superiora habet cilia, et capite glaber est, et parte colli superiore; adeoque cilia habet pilosiora. Sed, ut avis, inferiora habet plumis operta. Et bipes etiam est, ut avis; sed bisulcus ut quadrupes. Neque enim ha-bet digitos, sed bifidam ungulam: cuius causa est magnitudo, quam non habet avis, sed quadrupedis. So much for the words. As for the sense, we have already explained the first hemistich fully and shown in what sense the wing of ostriches is said to exult, or to be glad. But concerning the second hemistich, truly the wing of the stork, and plumage, or truly a wing as of a stork, and plumage, two questions can be asked. 1. What is the point of that affirmation, TRULY a wing, and plumage? 2. Why is it called the wing and plumage of a stork, or as of a stork? To the first I answer that the ostrich is a bird that is not a bird, which in many respects approaches the nature of a quadruped. This was long ago observed by the Philosopher in book 4 of On the Parts, near the end. His words are: It has some features proper to a bird and some proper to a quadruped. That it is not a quadruped is suggested by its having feathers; that it is not a bird, by its not flying aloft and having feathers not suited to flight but hair-like. Moreover, like a quadruped it has upper eyelashes, and it is bare on the head and on the upper part of the neck, so that its eyelashes are rather hairy. But, like a bird, its lower parts are covered with plumage. And it is two-footed like a bird, but cloven-hoofed like a quadruped. For it does not have toes but a divided hoof; the cause of this is its size, which is not that of a bird but of a quadruped. Hence Diodorus, on Arabia, in book 2: Φέρει δὲ καὶ ζῶα διφυῆ, καὶ μεμιγμένα ταῖς ἰδέαις, ὧν αἱ μὲν ὀνομαζόμεναι στρουθοκάμηλοι περιειλήφασι τοῖς τύποις μίγματα χηνῶν, καὶ καμήλων· [Pherei de kai zōa diphyē, kai memigmena tais ideais, hōn hai men onomazomenai strouthokamēloi perieilēphasi tois typois migmata chēnōn, kai kamēlōn] It also produces animals of a double nature and of two forms, among which those called ostriches have an appearance mixed of geese and camels. For χηνῶν [chēnōn] read πτηνῶν [ptēnōn], because, having proved this part by part, he concludes that the ostrich χερσαῖον ἅμα φαίνεσθαι, καὶ πτηνὸν [chersaion hama phainesthai, kai ptēnon], seems at once a land animal and a winged one. Hence Herodotus calls them στρουθὸς καταγαίους [strouthous katagaious], that is, ground sparrows. And Pliny writes that they are almost of the genus of beasts. And Tertullian: A beast of some kind rather than a bird. And Alkazuinius: حيوان مركب من خلقة الطير والجمل [ḥayawān murakkab min khilqat al-ṭayr wa-l-jamal] It is an animal composed of the nature of a bird and of a camel. Hence there is a proverb of the Arabs in Damir, about one col. 249 who is inconsistent and does not distinguish good from bad: النعامة لا طير ولا جمل [al-naʿāma lā ṭayr wa-lā jamal] The ostrich is neither bird nor camel. Accordingly, in Fable 28 of Gabrias II, 853, when the wild beasts and the birds were disputing with each other, Λίβυσσα στρουθὸς [Libyssa strouthos], the Libyan ostrich, deceived both sides, since it seemed ¶

Avis quidem esse, ex parte autem fera, volucribus caput, pedes feris ostendens. Cum igitur hoc animal quadrupedis tam multa habeat, hic a sacro scriptore describitur cum feris quadrupedibus. Et quia res παράδοξος videri queat, tale animal pennas et plumas habere, ut veras aves: ideo proponitur cum hac asseveratione, Vere penna, etc. et pluma, supple illis est. Neque obscurum est, cur non solum pennas habere dicatur, sed et ciconiae pennas. Struthio colore pennarum plane ciconiam refert. Marmolius ubi supra, En las alas y en la cola tiene grandes plumas negras y blancas, come las de la cigueña. In alis et in cauda magnas ha-bet pennas, nigras et albas, ut ciconiae pennas. Hinc Scaliger i), Cardanum arguens, quod struthioni tribuat pennas coeruleas, candidas, nigras, rubras, virides, quibus ornantur militum galeae, Risum, inquit, movent colores, quos ab infectorum officinis in apothecas naturae transtulisti. Quis enim te non rideat, illa venditantem, hosce colores, quos in galeis infigunt, nativos esse struthio-camelis? Omnes enim fusco et albo picti sunt, ut mihi dixerunt multi mercatores Africani. Quod ut melius intelligerem, ciconiae colori comparabant. Versus iste, sic expositus, sequentibus lucem inferet (3).

To be a bird indeed, but in part a wild beast, showing its head to the birds and its feet to the beasts. Since, then, this animal has so much of the quadruped, it is described here by the sacred writer together with the wild quadrupeds. And because it might seem a παράδοξος [paradoxos] thing that such an animal should have feathers and plumage like true birds, it is therefore set forth with this affirmation: Truly a wing, etc., and plumage; supply they have. Nor is it obscure why it is said to have not only feathers but the feathers of a stork. In the colour of its feathers the ostrich plainly resembles the stork. Marmol, in the place cited above: En las alas y en la cola tiene grandes plumas negras y blancas, come las de la cigueña. In its wings and tail it has large feathers, black and white, like the feathers of the stork. Hence Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] i), criticizing Cardano for attributing to the ostrich blue, white, black, red and green feathers, with which soldiers' helmets are adorned, says: The colours provoke laughter which you have transferred from the dyers' workshops into the storehouses of nature. For who would not laugh at you, peddling the claim that these colours which they fix on helmets are native to ostriches? For all are marked with dusky and white, as many African merchants told me. That I might understand it better, they compared it to the colour of the stork. This verse, thus explained, will shed light on those that follow R3. ¶
