II, 572 Chapter VII.

Probatur, חריונים, vel דביונים, 2 Reg. VI, 25. esse ciceris genus, non stercus columbarum, ut hactenus creditum.

It is proved that חריונים, or דביונים, 2 Reg. VI, 25, is a kind of chickpea, not dove's dung, as has hitherto been believed.

Narratur 2 Reg. VI, 25. Samariae famis tempore רובע הקב חריונים, quartam partem cabi stercoris columbarum venditam fuisse quinque argenteis. Nummos argenteos intellige semunciales, quorum singuli fere valent triginta sex as-ses Turonicos, ut summa fuerit praeter propter novem librarum nostrae monetae. Porro cabum Hebraei omnes summo consensu tradunt capacem fuisse duntaxat ovorum viginti quatuor. Ergo cabi quarta pars fuerit logus, id est, mensura sex ovorum. Haec probare non aggrediar, ne videar actum agere. Tantum conabor satisfacere quaestioni difficillimae, quamque nemo solvit hactenus, cur mensura tantula rei tam vilis, nempe columbini stercoris, tanti aestimata fuerit, et in quem usum comparata.

It is related in 2 Reg. VI, 25 that in the time of the famine at Samaria רובע הקב חריונים, the fourth part of a cab of dove's dung, was sold for five pieces of silver. Understand by these silver coins half-ounce pieces, each worth about thirty-six Tours sous, so that the total came to roughly nine livres of our money. Further, all the Hebrews with complete agreement report that the cab held only twenty-four eggs. So the fourth part of a cab would be a log, that is, a measure of six eggs. I shall not undertake to prove these things, lest I seem to do what has already been done. I shall only try to answer a most difficult question, which no one has solved so far: why so tiny a measure of so worthless a thing, namely dove's dung, was valued so highly, and for what use it was bought.

Verum haec verba Rabsakis aliud non sunt, quam jactabundi Thrasonis inanis insultatio, qua futurum significabat, ut Hierosolymitani summa rerum omnium inopia premerentur. I. There are those who say that the Samaritans, pressed by hunger, used this dung as food. Among them is Eutychius, patriarch of Alexandria, in his Annals, where he speaks of the famine of Samaria, p. 213: واكلوا زبل الحمام [wa-akalū zibl al-ḥamām] And they ate dove's dung. In the same way Rabshakeh, about to besiege Jerusalem, threatened that he would make the inhabitants eat their own dung and drink their own urine, 2 Reg. XVIII, 27, and Es. XXXVI, 12. But these words of Rabshakeh are nothing but the empty taunt of a boastful Thraso, by which he meant that the people of Jerusalem would be oppressed by the utmost want of all things. And the Hebrews rightly interpret the col. 39 saying על דרך גוזמא, by way of hyperbole; for it is a thing without precedent that anyone should, like swine, have swallowed the dung of others, let alone his own. Nature utterly recoils from so monstrous a food. Or if, when famine struck, necessity bound with iron spikes drove someone to it, he would still do it in vain, because nothing remains in excrement that can be digested. This is far more true of dove's dung than of human dung, because it is much hotter. This is taught both by experience itself and by the books of the physicians and of all who have written on manuring. Varro, De re rust. book 1, ch. 38: Cassius writes that the best dung (for manuring fields) is that of birds, etc. Of these, that of doves is the best, because it is the hottest. Columella book 2, ch. 15: There are, then, three chief kinds of dung: that from birds, that from men, and that from cattle. Of birds' dung the first is held to be that which is cleared out of dovecotes. Pliny book 17, ch. 9: Marcus Varro gives first place to the dung of thrushes from aviaries. Columella puts that from dovecotes next, II, 573 then that from henhouses. Some prefer that from dovecotes. Palladius book 2, ch. 33: That of doves (dung) is the most fiery, and that of other birds is quite useful. Quintilius book 2 of the Geopon.: Ἔστι δὲ κόπρος μὲν καλὴ ἡ τῶν πτηνῶν ἁπάντων, πλὴν τῶν χηνῶν, καὶ τῶν ἐνύδρων ὀρνέων, διὰ τὴν ὑγρότητα [esti de kopros men kalē hē tōn ptēnōn hapantōn, plēn tōn chēnōn, kai tōn enydrōn orneōn, dia tēn hygrotēta], etc. Πολὺ δὲ καλλίων ἡ τῶν περιστερῶν, πολὺ τὸ θερμὸν ἔχουσα [poly de kalliōn hē tōn peristerōn, poly to thermon echousa]· The dung of all birds is good, except that of geese and water-birds, because of its moisture, etc. but that of doves is far superior, because it is the most fiery. Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] book 2 of the Canon, in the chapter زبل [zibl], or On dung: زبل الحمام اسخان الازبال المستعمل [zibl al-ḥamām askhan al-azbāl al-mustaʿmal] Dove's dung is the hottest of all those that have any use. The cause is clear from Aristotle, who lists the dove first among those that have θερμὴν κοιλίαν [thermēn koilian], a hotter stomach, De part. anim. book 2, ch. 7. Accordingly some of the moderns write that the dove, like the ostrich, swallows and digests small stones. And the Arabic name of the dove, حمام [ḥamām] himam, is formed from heat. These things, I think, show abundantly that nothing remains in dove's dung that a human being could digest R1.

II, 574 II. Driven from this position, R. Jonah [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim] and R. David devise something else, namely that the Israelites used the dung in place of wood, since during the siege of Samaria they could not go out to gather firewood. This is a little less absurd, since dove's dung can serve in place of wood. For Galen reports as an established fact that dove's dung catches fire so easily that somewhere in Mysia a neighbouring house burned down because dove's dung had ignited spontaneously from the sun's rays. Near that house, namely, κόπρος ἀπέκειτο περιστερῶν, ἤδη σεσηπυῖα, καὶ τεθερμαμένη, καὶ ἀτμὸν ἀναπέμπουσα, καὶ ἁπτομένοις ἱκανῶς θερμή. ταύτης δὲ πλησίον ἦν, ὡς καὶ ψαύειν ἤδη θυρὶς ἔχουσα ξύλα νεωστὶ καταληλιμμένα ῥητίνῃ πολλῇ, θέρους οὖν μέσου λαῦρος ἥλιος προσβαλὼν ἐξῆψε τὴν ῥητίνην τε, καὶ τὰ ξύλα [kopros apekeito peristerōn, ēdē sesēpuia, kai tethermamenē, kai atmon anapempousa, kai haptomenois hikanōs thermē. tautēs de plēsion ēn, hōs kai psauein ēdē thyris echousa xyla neōsti katalēlimmena rhētinē pollē, therous oun mesou lauros hēlios prosbalōn exēpse tēn rhētinēn te, kai ta xyla], there lay dove's dung, already putrid and hot, giving off steam, and quite hot to the touch, and so close to that house, that it touched a window, whose wood had recently been smeared with much resin: so in midsummer the blazing sun, striking from above, set the wood and the resin alight. So Galen, book 3 De temperam. Similar is what one reads in the same author, book 1 De different. febr. and in the book De morborum causis: that the dung of wild doves catches fire more slowly but holds it longer. So no one should think this material wholly unfit for feeding a fire. But truly, who would believe that in the city of Samaria, which, being the royal seat, we may fairly suppose was very far removed from rustic ways, there was so much dove's dung that fires could be built from it? Or that there was anyone who would not rather go without fire for a time than buy so tiny a quantity of a substance most foul-smelling in the flames at so great a price? col. 40 Moreover, these things do not hang together: that there was a great famine in Samaria because there was nothing to feed a fire with except dove's dung, bought at a high price.

III. The third opinion is that of those who hold that the Israelites bought this dung in order to manure better the fields enclosed within the city, and so provide against famine in the coming year. As if it were very likely that in a most populous city there was so much farmland; or that those who lacked the means to live from day to day had the means to sow; or that the citizens of Samaria were so anxious about the future when they had nothing for the present; or so fastidious in choosing dung that, when other dung, only a little less useful, was available in great abundance, they bought dove's dung at so high a price.

IV. The fourth opinion, equally absurd, is that of Josephus: that dove's dung served them ἀντὶ ἁλῶν [anti halōn], in place of salt. For although I grant that it does not lack salt, yet if they had to use salt from excrement, everyone knows that there is II, 575 much more salt in urine, and that it is extracted more easily and with less disgust. So there would have been no need to buy at so high a price what everyone had at home. Besides, what would be the point of that salt in so great a famine? If one cared to play the rhetorician, I know that much could be gathered in praise of salt, and that after bread hardly anything is more useful for life: without it a great part of foods is flat and tasteless, can that which is unsavoury be eaten without salt? Job 6:6. Thus, under the Law no sacrifice was without salt, Lev 2:13 and Mark 9:49. And even among the heathen, on the authority of Pliny, no rites were performed without salted meal. So too there is hardly any table of some refinement that lacks salt. But salt also seems to have been the food of the poor. For people of slender means used πρὸς ἅλα δειπνεῖν, καὶ κύμινον [pros hala deipnein, kai kyminon], to dine on salt and cumin, as can be gathered from Plutarch. Hence Diogenes, in Laertius book 6, answered Craterus when he invited him: Ἀλλὰ βούλομαι ἐν Ἀθήναις ἅλα λείχειν, ἢ παρὰ Κρατερῷ τῆς πολυτελοῦς τραπέζης ἀπολαύειν [Alla boulomai en Athēnais hala leichein, ē para Kraterō tēs polytelous trapezēs apolauein]· But I would rather lick salt at Athens than enjoy a sumptuous table at the house of Craterus. Unde est, quod Sirach. XXXIX, 31. quaedam laudantur tanquam II, 576 ἀρχὴ πάσης χρείας εἰς ζωὴν ἀνθρώπου, principium (id est, praecipua) omnium eorum, quae ad vitam necessaria sunt. Varro also reports that the ancients used (salt) as a relish: for they ate salt with bread and cheese, as the proverb shows. So Pliny, book 31, ch. 7, where shortly before the same author says: It is also involved in honours and in military service: hence salaries are so called, with great authority among the ancients. Likewise, Ancus Martius, the king, gave the people six thousand modii of salt as a largess. The kings of Assyria also gave salt as pay. Accordingly, in Ezra 4:14 the governors of Samaria write this to King Artaxerxes: Because די־מלח היכלא מלחנא, we are salted with the salt of the palace. That is the literal rendering; that is, as Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] rightly puts it, we receive a salary from the king's palace. But no one praises salt more fully than Plutarch, Sympos. book 5, Probl. 10, where, on the question why Homer a) calls salt divine, he asserts among other things that salt is τῶν ἄλλων ὄψων ὄψον εἶναι, καὶ ἥδυσμα [tōn allōn opsōn opson einai, kai hēdysma], the relish and seasoning of all other relishes. Likewise, Θρίγκωμα τῆς τροφῆς γινόμενον εἰς τὸ σῶμα, καὶ παρέχον ἐναρμοσίαν αὐτῇ προς τὴν ὄρεξιν [thrinkōma tēs trophēs ginomenon eis to sōma, kai parechon enarmosian autē pros tēn orexin], that which becomes a safeguard of food in the body, making it agreeable and suited to our palate. Hence in Sirach. XXXIX, 31. certain things are praised as II, 576 ἀρχὴ πάσης χρείας εἰς ζωὴν ἀνθρώπου [archē pasēs chreias eis zōēn anthrōpou], the beginning (that is, the chief) of all things necessary for life. And among these salt comes before all food. But whatever may be said of salt, this much is certain: salt is nothing but col. 41 a seasoning, and seasonings are not usually bought at a great price in a severe famine, when food is lacking. For hunger itself is the best seasoning, and one does not then seek what stimulates the palate, but what satisfies.

V. The fifth opinion is that of the Talmudists. To understand it, the reader must first be told that there is this Talmudic axiom in the tractate Megilla, ch. 3: כל המקראות הכתובין בתורה לגנאי קורין אותן לשבח, All words written indecently in the Law are read decently. Therefore, wherever words occur in Scripture that are less decent in common usage, the teachers of the Hebrews, out of regard for decency, wanted them read otherwise than they are written. So in place of the word found in the text they substitute in the margin another, more decent one, which it is still the custom today to read in its place in the synagogues. Hence for שגלb), to have intercourse, they read שכב, to lie with; for עפוליםc), diseases of the anus, טחורים, haemorrhoids; for טחוראותd), latrines, properly places of dung, מוצאות, privies; for חורים and שיניםe), dung and urine, צואה and מימי רגלים, excrement and water of the feet. In the same way, in this passage, for חריונים, dove's dung, they want דביונים to be read, the flux of doves, a word compounded of דב, flux, and יונים, doves. But the Talmudists interpret דביונים differently, as if it were compounded of the Chaldee pronoun ד, which corresponds to the Hebrew אשר, who, which, that, the preposition ב, in, and the noun יונים, doves. For them, then, דביונים means that which (supply: is or was) in the doves. For they hold that at Samaria doves had been trained to go out foraging in the fields, so to speak; and that when they returned stuffed and swollen, their owners shook out their crops, extracted the grain and sold it at a high price. So, in their view, the words of the sacred text run thus: The famine in Samaria was so great that a quarter of a kab of the grains that were in the doves, and were extracted from their crops, sold for five shekels. Hence Comestor in the Historia Scholastica, book 4 of Kings, ch. 10: Some relate that by the word dung is meant the little bladder of the dove, in which were found grains brought from outside. I do not think anything more absurd than this fiction could have been devised. Not that I deny that doves, through love of their young, can be so trained that, released from a besieged city, they return home; for we have shown in the immediately preceding chapter that ancient and more recent people often used such messengers. But who would believe that at Samaria there were so many such docile doves? Or that in fields laid waste far and wide by the enemy they found crops everywhere at hand? Or that, returning with full crops, II, 577 they so easily disgorged whatever they had swallowed in the field? And that by such arts even one kab of grain could have been gathered in the whole time of the siege? Or, if God had sustained this people for some time in so miraculous a way, that Scripture would have passed over so unheard-of a thing so lightly? But we argue in vain, since the word חריונים, which is in the text, can in no way be drawn to this sense. Indeed, hardly, or not even hardly, can the marginal word דביונים be rendered that which is in the doves, unless we suppose that a Chaldee pronoun was prefixed to a Hebrew noun, and so a hybrid, half-barbarous word was formed.

Junius, thinking he could avoid these Symplegades by a new interpretation, rendered this passage as follows: There arose a great famine in Samaria, etc. so that the fourth part, the belly itself, which is in the hollow of doves, was of five shekels of silver. But at first reading it is immediately apparent that this exceedingly learned man would not have rendered it thus, almost, I would say, without any sense, had he not felt himself to be in great straits. For what would the fourth part, the belly itself of doves be? And what point was there in specifying that the belly is in the hollow? Although the hollow is rather in the belly than the belly in the hollow. col. 42 Again, how could it have entered the mind of that man, who was most perspicacious, that the belly of doves was sold separately? Nor can what is found in his version, the fourth part, the belly itself, which is in the hollow of doves, be extracted by any torture from the Hebrew words רובע הקב חריונים. For if רבע, the fourth part, and הקב, the belly, as the distinguished Junius renders it, were in apposition, each noun would have the He hajedia, or article, prefixed, as one would say in Greek τὸ τέταρτον, ἡ κοιλία [to tetarton, hē koilia]. I add that in this version קב kab is wrongly confused with קבה keba. Of these, קבה keba means belly or crop; but קב kab is something quite different, namely κάβος [kabos], cabus, a kind of measure. So it is explained among the Hebrews by the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], Elias and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] in their Lexicons, by Mardochaeus in his Concordance, by Alphes in the tractate On Passover, ch. 5, fol. 176, and by the Gloss on the Talmud passim. Hence the compound תרקב tharkab means to some two cabs, as if contracted from תרי קבין there kabin, two cabs; to others, more correctly, three cabs, as if it were תרי וקב, two and a cab, one more. So R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in various places of the Talmud. And κάβος [kabos] is also a measure in Hesychius, Philoxenus, the Suda, the Etymologicum, Phavorinus and others. Finally, none of the ancients renders חריונים otherwise than as dove's dung. Let Josephus and the Greek, Chaldee, Syriac, Arabic and Latin translators be consulted, and, among the Hebrews, Iarchius, Kimchi and R. Levi on this passage. Among them a remarkable agreement will be found, which rarely happens in the interpretation of an obscure passage. Indeed, of the more recent writers no one dissents except Junius and Fuller, about whom now a few words.

For he tries to prove that חריונים are the belly and entrails of doves as follows: "The Hebrew חור first means white; 2. a hole, from the brightness of the light that is usually seen through it; 3. τρώγλην [trōglēn] a cavity, which is a kind of hole; 4. the belly, in Greek κοιλίαν [koilian], which is a notable cavity of the human body; whence alvus [belly] is named as if alveus [hollow], κοιλία ἀπὸ τοῦ κοίλου [koilia apo tou koilou]; 5. the excrement enclosed in the belly, by a metonymy of the contents for the container. This is Fuller's opinion, in which (if I may say so with all respect to a most careful writer) I seem to find not a few things that have not even the appearance of truth. Thus, that the entrails of doves should be called דביונים in Hebrew has never occurred to anyone even in a dream. Rather, all explain it as excrement, so named from flowing, just as in Greek ῥεῦμα γαστρὸς [rheuma gastros] and διάῤῥοια [diarrhoia], and in Latin alvi profluvium [flux of the belly], are said of this kind of flux. And in Celsus the febres fluentes [flowing fevers], in Greek ῥοώδεις πυρετοὶ [rhoōdeis pyretoi], are those which loosen the bowels. Again, in what the same Fuller asserts, that the word חור means 1. white, 2. a hole, 3. a cavity, 4. the belly, crop, entrails, 5. excrement, most is fabricated. חור for white is not so much Hebrew as Chaldee and Arabic. But for a hole or a cavity it is Hebrew, and from a different root. For the former is from חור chavar, to grow white; the latter from the geminate חרר charar, to perforate. Accordingly you may read in the Talmud, tractate Succa, fol. 20b, חור שחררוהו מים, a hole which water has bored. And for the same Talmudists חריר is the eye of a needle, as ܚܪܘܪܐ [חרורא] is for the Syrians in Matt 19:24. Further, that this חור also means belly, or crop, or entrails, col. 43 is a mere fabrication, whose author is not cited, nor indeed can be cited, since no one has ever used it so. Finally, חר, or rather חרי, or חרא, for excrement, is an altogether different word, whose root is neither חור nor חרר, but חרה or חרא. Hence the Talmudists consistently write חרי or חריא. And in Arabic خري [kharī] charia, with hamza at the end, means to void excrement, خرء [khurʾ] excrement, مخرأة [makhraʾa] latrine. Indeed the Hebrews too, when they write in full, insert Aleph, as in Isa 36:12. לאכול את חראיהם, to eat their own dung. And 2 Reg. X, 27. They destroyed the house of Baal and made it למחראות, into latrines. Nor is the Aleph there adventitious, as they think, but radical, and to be supplied where it is missing, as is clear from Arabic usage. These points observed, it appears that the eminent Fuller did not know the etymology of the word or its true spelling, and indiscriminately took three words of different origin, which led him astray. For since whatever he adduces rests solely II, 579 on the etymologies of words, if these etymologies are false, whatever is built upon them must also be false. And truly most futile is what he ascribes to the sacred author, that a cab of the bellies and entrails of doves was sold in Samaria for five shekels. For it has never been heard of that the crop and entrails of doves were sold separately. I admit that one reads this of hens, and that in Arabic تبودك [tabūdak] thabudach is the name for one who sells the entrails of a hen, namely the heart, gizzard, etc. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] explains it as من يبيع ما في بطون الدجاج من القلب والقانصة [man yabīʿu mā fī buṭūn al-dajāj min al-qalb wa-l-qāniṣa] But of doves' entrails nothing of the kind occurs anywhere, so far as I know. Nor is it fitting that doves' intestines should have been measured by cabs. For the cab is a measure not of entrails but of grains, like the seah, ephah and cor. Six cabs make a seah, three seahs an ephah, ten ephahs a cor, the largest dry measure. Accordingly in the Talmud and in the Aruch you read everywhere קב חטין, a cab of wheat. And it is defined by Elias as מדת היובש, a measure of dry things; by Pomarius as מרת של חטום, a grain measure; by Aquinas as מדת למדות דבר היבש, a measure for measuring a dry thing. So in Pollux, Κάβασον δὲ ἐκάλουν τὸν περὶ τὴν τροφὴν ἄπληστον, etc. Κάβος γὰρ σίτου μέτρον [Kabason de ekaloun ton peri tēn trophēn aplēston, etc. Kabos gar sitou metron]. To establish it, we must first lay down as most certain that by dove's dung some kind of food is meant. The first is of those called produce (grain crops, σιτώδη [sitōdē]). Of these there are five kinds: wheat, spelt, barley, fox's ear (oats, whose ear resembles a fox's tail), rye. The second is of those they call pulses, that is, all seeds eaten by man, such as beans, peas, millet, rice, sesame, poppy, chickling and the like. Where men gather by hand, without a sickle, pulses and other things of that kind, in the half-green field. Once these points are noted, it will at once be clear what Josephus means when he says that dove's dung served the Samaritans in place of salt. Theophrastus (De causis plantarum, book 4, chapter 9), inquiring into the reasons why the chickpea ripens sooner than other pulses, gives this one among others: Ἡ ἅλμη πρὸς τὴν τελείωσιν συμβάλλεται [Hē halmē pros tēn teleiōsin symballetai], The saltiness of the chickpea contributes to that quick ripening. And in book 5, chapter 11: Ὁ ἐρέβινθος ἀπόλλυται σφακελίας, ὅταν ἀνθοῦσιν ὕδωρ ἐπιγινόμενον ἐπικλύσῃ τὴν ἄλμην· τὸ γὰρ ἔφαλμα οἷον σφακελίζειν ἐστί [Ho erebinthos apollytai sphakelias, hotan anthousin hydōr epiginomenon epiklysē tēn halmēn; to gar ephalma hoion sphakelizein esti]. The chickpea perishes of blight when rain washes off its saltiness while it is in flower: for the loss of its saltiness is to it a kind of blight. Cato too says of the chickpea, in De re rustica, chapter 37: If you sow chickpea in crumbly soil, since it is pulled up, it is salty and harmful. And Columella, book 2, chapter 14, says that Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] states that the soil is harmed most II, 589 by the poison of chickpea and flax: the one because it is of a salty nature, the other because it is of a hot nature. And Pliny, book 18, chapter 12: It is the nature of the chickpea to grow with saltiness; therefore it burns the soil. And in book 17: A defect common to all crops is blight, the chickpea's too, when the rain, washing off its saltiness, makes it sweeter. Galen, in book 6 of De simplic., calls this bitterness, when he says of chickpeas: Μετέχουσι δέ τι καὶ τῆς πικρότητος [Metechousi de ti kai tēs pikrotētos]; They also have something of bitterness. And of the wild chickpea: Δριμύτερός τ᾽ ἐστι, καὶ πικρότερος [Drimyteros t' esti, kai pikroteros], It is sharper and more bitter. But he put bitter for salty, as when the Poet says of the sea,

Doris amara suam non intermisceat undam.

let bitter Doris not mingle her wave.

Proinde ex ciceris quodam genere, ut notavimus, Arabes celeberrimum illud sal eliciunt, quod inde vocatur sal alkali, id est, sal adusti ciceris. Ad locum igitur, in quo versamur, cum monuissent Hebraei vetustiores, stercus columbarum id esse, unde elicitur sal: id mala manu acceptum a Iosepho, qui scripsit, columbarum stercus Samariae fuisse ἀντὶ ἁλῶν, pro sale. Ex quo Theodoretus in 4. Reg. Quaest. 21. Ἡ τῆς πολιορκίας ἀνάγκη, καὶ τοῦ λίμου τὸ μέγεθος ἠνάγκασεν αὐτοὺς ἀντὶ ἁλῶν κεχρῆσθαι τῷ κόπρῳ. Necessitas obsidionis et famis magnitudo cogebat eos stercore uti pro sale (9).

Accordingly, as we have noted, the Arabs extract from a certain kind of chickpea that very famous salt which is therefore called sal alkali, that is, salt of burnt chickpea. So, on the passage with which we are dealing, when the older Hebrews had remarked that it was dove's dung, from which salt is extracted, this was misunderstood by Josephus, who wrote that at Samaria dove's dung was used ἀντὶ ἁλῶν [anti halōn], in place of salt. Hence Theodoret on 4 Kings, Question 21: Ἡ τῆς πολιορκίας ἀνάγκη, καὶ τοῦ λίμου τὸ μέγεθος ἠνάγκασεν αὐτοὺς ἀντὶ ἁλῶν κεχρῆσθαι τῷ κόπρῳ [Hē tēs poliorkias anankē, kai tou limou to megethos ēnankasen autous anti halōn kechrēsthai tō koprō]. The necessity of the siege and the severity of the famine compelled them to use dung in place of salt R9.
