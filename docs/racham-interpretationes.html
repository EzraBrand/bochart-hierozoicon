III, 48 col. 297 CHAPTER XXV. ¶

Variae interpretationes vocis רחם racham; ubi multa de avibus שרקרק serakrak, Cygno, Pelicano et Merope.

Various interpretations of the word רחם racham; with much on the birds שרקרק serakrak, the Swan, the Pelican and the Bee-eater. ¶

Etiam anxie disputatur de ave רחם racham, vel רחמה rachama, ut scribitur Deut. XIV, 17.

There is also anxious dispute about the bird רחם racham, or רחמה rachama, as it is written in Deut. XIV, 17. ¶

I. Ionathan et Syrus explicant ܣܪܩܪܩܐ serakreka; et Onkelos ירקרקא jerakreka. Quod idem esse puto, quamvis illud a sibilo dicatur, hoc a viridi colore. Sed et Talmudici שרקרק serakrak reddunt. Locus est in tractatu Cholin fol. 63. a. רחם זו שרקרק אמר רבי יוחנן למה נקרא שמו רחם כיון שבא רחם באו רחמים לעולם, „Racham avis eadem, quae serakrak. Dixit Rabbi „Iohannes, Cur vocatur racham? (Id est, cur illi nomen a misericordia inditum, „quae Hebraeis est רחם)? Cum venit racham, veniunt misericordiae in mun-„dum.“ Et ibi Glossa, רחמים מטר, Misericordiae, id est, pluvia, quam Deus III, 49 pro benignitate sua nobis indulget, juxta illud Lucae Act. XIV, 16. Non se reliquit sine testimonio ἀγαθοποιῶν benefaciens, coelitus dans nobis pluvias. Ibidem docent, שרקרק serakrak, a clamore suo dici. Sequitur enim, אמר רב בבי בר אביי והוא דיתיב אמידי ועביד שרקרק, Dixit Rabbi Bibi, filius Abii, Avis est, quae ubicunque sedeat, facit serakrak, id est, sibilo suo videtur id efferre. Ad-dunt, דאי יתיב בארעא ושריק אתי משיחא שנאמר אשרקה להם ואקבצם, Cum in terra sedebit sibilans, veniet Messias, prout dicitur Zachar. X, 8. „Sibi- col. 298 labo ad eos, et congregabo eos. “ Creditur nempe avis esse fausti ominis: atque ut habet Glossator, cum non stat, sed sedet et sibilat, סימן בשורה הוא, signum est boni nuncii. Inprimis si in terra sedeat, שאין דרכו, quod praeter morem facit. Illa igitur sic sedente et sibilante, aut Deo ipso per illam avem, si recutitis doctoribus credimus, venturus est Iudaicus Messias et Iudaei, toto terrarum orbe dispersi, congregabuntur juxta vaticinium, quod modo citavimus, Sibilabo ad eos, et congregabo eos. Itaque eorum multum interest, ut sciant, quaenam sit avis, quod tamen prorsus ignorant. Nam Iudaei Germaniae, Munstero et Fagio testibus, picam interpretantur. Quod sequitur in Latina versione Munsterus, et Deodatius in Italica. Et videri potest verisimile, cum apud Gesnerum dicat Gratarolus, picam a veteribus dictam fuisse pluviae avem; quia amplius solito stridens certo imbrem indicat. Et recens picae nomen καρακάξα, quo utuntur hodie Graeci, accedit ad serakraka. Tamen a pica differre docent Arabum descriptiones, qui eandem avem vocant شرقراق sirikrak, et per methathesim شقراق sikirrak, et شرشاق sirisak: et in Alcamus definiunt طاير مرقط بخضرة وحمرة وبياض ويكون بارض الاحرام Avem viridi, rubro et albo distinctam, quae est in terra Alachram, id est, circa Meccham et Medinam (1). Et in Alkazuinio, طاير يقال له بالفارسية III, 50 كاسينة اخضر اللون احمر المنقار وقد يكون اصفر عدو النحل ياكل منها ويقتل ما لا ياكل Avem, quae Persice CASINA dicitur, plumarum colore viridi, rostro rubro, quandoque etiam flavo; apibus inimicam, quas deglutit, quin et eas perimentem, quas non comedit. Et in Damire, طاير صغير يسمي الاخيل والعرب تتشام به وهو اخضر مليح بقدر الحمامة خضرته حسنة مشبعة وفي اجنحته سواد وله مشتا ومصيف يكثر في البلاد الروم والشام وخراسان ونواجد وفي طبعه شرة وشراسة وسرقة فراخ غيره وهو لا يزال الا متباعدا من الناس ويالف الروابي وروس الجبال لكنه يحضن بيضه في العمران العوالي الذي لا تناله الايدي وعشه شديد النتن Avem parvam alio nomine achial dictam (id est, maculosam, quam- col. 299 vis aliis achial sit avis صرد surad, quae vulgo lanius minor). Arabes inauspicatam putant. Est viridis, elegans, magnitudinis columbae. Viride ejus pulchrum et saturum. Aliis nigra est. Tam hyberna, quam aestiva est. Abundat in terris Graecorum, Syriae, Chorasan et Negid (in Arabia Felice). Pravae naturae est et malae indolis. Suffuratur pullos non suos. Est ab omni hominum consortio remotissima. In collibus habitat, et in vertice montium. Et ova sua fovet in locis celsissimis, ad quae manus non attingunt. Nidum habet summe foetidum. De eadem ave dicit Algiahid, انه نوع من الغربان وفي طبعه السرقة والعفة من السفاد وهو كثير الاستغاثة اذا طار بقربه طاير ضربه وصاحْ كانه المضروب, Esse corvi speciem, et furacis esse naturae, et a coitu multum abstinere, querulamque et clamosam esse, ita, ut, si alia avis prope advolet, ea verberata clamet, quasi vapulasset ipsa. Esum alii Arabum concedunt, alii vetant لاستخباثه propter foeditatem ejus. Tamen his verbis commendat Avicenna Canonis libro secundo, capite De carnibus, لحم الشقراق كاسر III, 51 الرياح وابعد اللحمان من تعفن اقله شحما وايبسه جوهرا Caro sikirrak pellit flatus, et, cum parum sit pinguis substantiaeque siccissimae, a corruptione maxime distat. In Paroemiographis hoc proverbium de illa ave occurrit: اشام من الاخيل وهو الشقراق Inauspicatius ave achial, id est, ave sikirrak. Proinde Damir supra dixerat, eam avem ab Arabibus pro inauspicata haberi. Talmudici tamen contra statuunt, avem esse fausti ominis: sed quis miretur in iis, quae ex opinione pendent, varia esse hominum judicia. صاحب الحيل, id est, auctor artium, felli ejus vim tribuit, quam hic intactam praeterire non possum. اذا كان الذهب ناقص العيار يذاب ويفرغ عليه من مرارته فانه يتحجر ويبردان عياره كما لو افرغ عليه مرارة الثعلب فانه ينقص عياره Si in auro pondus deficit, liquefit, et fel istius avis ipsi affunditur: et, cum aurum coaluit, gravius fit pondere. Ut contra fit levius, si vulpis fel affundatur. Minus mirum quod sequitur, اذا اتخذ من مرارته خضاب سود الشعر tincturam ex eo felle sumptam denigrare capillos. In Tartariae campestribus circa Caucasum et Caucasias portas, quas hodie Derbend, id est, angustias et Temir capi, id est, ferream portam, vocant, harum avium est copia, de quibus notatu dignum est, quod refert Haitho Armenus in libro De Tartaris capite quadragesimo septimo. In quadam planitie, quae vocatur Mon- col. 300 ga (lege Mongal) stant hyeme quaedam aves magnitudinis phasianorum, quae pulcherrimas habent plumas, et vocantur seicerach (شقراق) unde, quando aliquae gentes ingrediuntur illam planitiem, statim fugiunt illae aves, et transeunt per illas custodias, et fossata (ubi Derbend et Porta ferrea, de quibus paullo ante egerat), et qui sunt deputati ad custodiam illius loci per illas aves, manifeste cognoscunt inimicorum adventum, et ad defensionem continuo sunt parati. Ex his omnibus multa colligo: Primo has aves non esse picas, ut Hebraei sentiunt, et fortasse etiam ex Arabibus Algiahid; cum mole corporis, pennarum et rostri colore, victu, foetore, nido, quem sibi struunt in remotissimis montibus, prorsus a pica differant. Secundo, non esse picos virides, ut statuit vir doctissimus et mihi amicissimus, cum huic avi cum pico vix quidquam commune sit praeter colorem, qui in utroque est viridis, sed in pico dilutior. Tertio, quamvis non possum definire, quae avis sit شرقراق sirikrak; quia in Europaeis avibus nulla est huic similis; constat tamen, illi cum Hebraeo רחם racham nihil esse commune; et graviter falli Talmudicos, Chaldaeos, Syros et Hebraeos recentiores, qui racham et serakraka eandem avem esse definiunt. Sed jam alii sunt audiendi.

I. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and the Syriac explain it as ܣܪܩܪܩܐ [סרקרקא] serakreka; and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] as ירקרקא jerakreka. I think these are the same, although the former is named from hissing, the latter from green colour. The Talmudists too render it שרקרק serakrak. The passage is in the tractate Cholin fol. 63a רחם זו שרקרק אמר רבי יוחנן למה נקרא שמו רחם כיון שבא רחם באו רחמים לעולם, "Racham is the same bird as serakrak. Rabbi Johanan said: Why is it called racham? (That is, why was it given a name from mercy, which in Hebrew is רחם?) When the racham comes, mercies come into the world." And there the Gloss says: רחמים מטר, Mercies, that is, rain, which God III, 49 in his kindness grants us, according to the words of Luke in Acts 14:16: He did not leave himself without witness, ἀγαθοποιῶν [agathopoiōn] doing good, giving us rains from heaven. In the same place they teach that שרקרק serakrak is named from its cry. For there follows: אמר רב בבי בר אביי והוא דיתיב אמידי ועביד שרקרק, Rabbi Bibi, son of Abii, said: It is a bird which, wherever it sits, makes serakrak, that is, it seems to utter this with its hissing. They add: דאי יתיב בארעא ושריק אתי משיחא שנאמר אשרקה להם ואקבצם, When it sits on the ground hissing, the Messiah will come, as it is said Zech 10:8: "I will hiss col. 298 for them, and gather them." The bird, namely, is believed to be of good omen; and, as the Glossator has it, when it does not stand but sits and hisses, סימן בשורה הוא, it is a sign of good tidings. Especially if it sits on the ground, שאין דרכו, which it does contrary to its habit. When it sits and hisses thus, therefore, or when God himself hisses through that bird, if we believe the circumcised doctors, the Jewish Messiah will come, and the Jews, scattered over the whole world, will be gathered according to the prophecy just cited, I will hiss for them, and gather them. So it matters greatly to them to know what bird this is; yet they are wholly ignorant of it. For the Jews of Germany, as Münster and Fagius testify, interpret it as the magpie. Münster follows this in his Latin version, and Diodati in his Italian. And it may seem probable, since in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] Gratarolus says that the magpie was called by the ancients the bird of rain, because when it chatters more than usual it surely signals a shower. And the recent name of the magpie, καρακάξα [karakaxa], which the Greeks use today, comes close to serakraka. Yet the descriptions of the Arabs show that it differs from the magpie. They call the same bird شرقراق [shirqirrāq] sirikrak, and by metathesis شقراق [shiqirrāq] sikirrak, and شرشاق [shirshāq] sirisak; and in the Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] they define it: طاير مرقط بخضرة وحمرة وبياض ويكون بارض الاحرام [ṭāyir muraqqaṭ bi-khuḍra wa-ḥumra wa-bayāḍ wa-yakūn bi-arḍ al-aḥrām] A bird spotted with green, red and white, which is found in the land of Alachram, that is, around Mecca and Medina R1. And in Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: طاير يقال له بالفارسية III, 50 كاسينة اخضر اللون احمر المنقار وقد يكون اصفر عدو النحل ياكل منها ويقتل ما لا ياكل [ṭāyir yuqāl lahu bi-l-fārisiyya kāsīna akhḍar al-lawn aḥmar al-minqār wa-qad yakūn aṣfar ʿaduww al-naḥl yaʾkul minhā wa-yaqtul mā lā yaʾkul] A bird which in Persian is called CASINA, green in the colour of its feathers, with a red beak, sometimes also yellow; hostile to bees, which it swallows, and indeed it also kills those which it does not eat. And in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: طاير صغير يسمي الاخيل والعرب تتشام به وهو اخضر مليح بقدر الحمامة خضرته حسنة مشبعة وفي اجنحته سواد وله مشتا ومصيف يكثر في البلاد الروم والشام وخراسان ونواجد وفي طبعه شرة وشراسة وسرقة فراخ غيره وهو لا يزال الا متباعدا من الناس ويالف الروابي وروس الجبال لكنه يحضن بيضه في العمران العوالي الذي لا تناله الايدي وعشه شديد النتن [ṭāyir ṣaghīr yusammī al-akhyal wa-l-ʿarab tatashāʾam bihi wa-huwa akhḍar malīḥ bi-qadr al-ḥamāma khuḍratuhu ḥasana mushbaʿa wa-fī ajniḥatihi sawād wa-lahu mashtā wa-maṣīf yakthur fī al-bilād al-rūm wa-l-shām wa-khurāsān wa-nawājid wa-fī ṭabʿihi shirra wa-sharāsa wa-sariqat firākh ghayrihi wa-huwa lā yazāl illā mutabāʿidan min al-nās wa-yaʾlaf al-rawābī wa-rūs al-jibāl lākinnahu yaḥḍun bayḍahu fī al-ʿumrān al-ʿawālī alladhī lā tanāluhu al-aydī wa-ʿushshuhu shadīd al-natn] A small bird, by another name called achial (that is, spotted, col. 299 although for others achial is the bird صرد [ṣurad] surad, commonly the lesser shrike). The Arabs consider it ill-omened. It is green, elegant, of the size of a dove. Its green is beautiful and deep. Its wings have black. It has both winter and summer quarters. It abounds in the lands of the Greeks, Syria, Chorasan and Negid (in Arabia Felix). It is of a wicked nature and evil disposition. It steals chicks not its own. It keeps as far as possible from all company of men. It lives on hills and on the tops of mountains. And it broods its eggs in the highest places, which hands cannot reach. Its nest is extremely foul-smelling. Of the same bird Algiahid says: انه نوع من الغربان وفي طبعه السرقة والعفة من السفاد وهو كثير الاستغاثة اذا طار بقربه طاير ضربه وصاحْ كانه المضروب [annahu nawʿ min al-ghirbān wa-fī ṭabʿihi al-sariqa wa-l-ʿiffa min al-sifād wa-huwa kathīr al-istighātha idhā ṭār bi-qurbihi ṭāyir ḍarabahu wa-ṣāḥ kaʾannahu al-maḍrūb], that it is a species of raven, of thievish nature, much abstaining from coition, querulous and clamorous, so that, if another bird flies near, it strikes it and cries out as if it had itself been beaten. Some of the Arabs permit eating it, others forbid it لاستخباثه [li-stikhbāthihi] because of its foulness. Yet Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] commends it in these words in book 2 of the Canon, the chapter On meats: لحم الشقراق كاسر III, 51 الرياح وابعد اللحمان من تعفن اقله شحما وايبسه جوهرا [laḥm al-shiqirrāq kāsir al-riyāḥ wa-abʿad al-luḥmān min taʿaffun aqalluhu shaḥman wa-aybasuhu jawharan] The flesh of the sikirrak dispels flatulence, and, since it is little fatty and of very dry substance, it is the furthest from corruption. In the Paroemiographers this proverb about that bird occurs: اشام من الاخيل وهو الشقراق [ashʾam min al-akhyal wa-huwa al-shiqirrāq] More ill-omened than the bird achial, that is, the bird sikirrak. Accordingly Damir had said above that this bird is held ill-omened by the Arabs. The Talmudists, however, hold on the contrary that it is a bird of good omen; but who would wonder that in matters which depend on opinion men's judgements vary? صاحب الحيل [ṣāḥib al-ḥiyal], that is, the author of the arts, attributes a power to its gall which I cannot pass over here untouched: اذا كان الذهب ناقص العيار يذاب ويفرغ عليه من مرارته فانه يتحجر ويبردان عياره كما لو افرغ عليه مرارة الثعلب فانه ينقص عياره [idhā kān al-dhahab nāqiṣ al-ʿiyār yudhāb wa-yufragh ʿalayhi min marāratihi fa-innahu yataḥajjar wa-yabrudān ʿiyāruhu kamā law afragh ʿalayhi marārat al-thaʿlab fa-innahu yanquṣ ʿiyāruhu] If gold is deficient in weight, it is melted and the gall of this bird is poured on it; and when the gold has solidified, it becomes heavier in weight. Just as, on the contrary, it becomes lighter if the gall of a fox is poured on it. Less wonderful is what follows: اذا اتخذ من مرارته خضاب سود الشعر [idhā ittakhadh min marāratihi khiḍāb sawwad al-shaʿr] a dye made from that gall blackens the hair. In the plains of Tartary around the Caucasus and the Caucasian gates, which today they call Derbend, that is, the narrows, and Temir capi, that is, the iron gate, there is an abundance of these birds, about which what Hayton the Armenian relates in his book On the Tartars, chapter 47, is worth noting: In a certain plain, which is called Mon- col. 300 ga (read Mongal), there stay in winter certain birds of the size of pheasants, which have very beautiful feathers and are called seicerach (شقراق [shiqirrāq]); hence, when any peoples enter that plain, those birds immediately flee and pass through those guard-posts and ditches (where Derbend and the Iron Gate are, which he had treated a little before), and those who are appointed to guard that place clearly recognize by those birds the approach of enemies, and are at once prepared for defence. From all this I gather many things. First, that these birds are not magpies, as the Hebrews think, and perhaps also, among the Arabs, Algiahid; since they differ entirely from the magpie in bodily size, in the colour of feathers and beak, in diet, in stench, and in the nest they build in the most remote mountains. Second, that they are not green woodpeckers, as a most learned man and very dear friend of mine holds, since this bird has hardly anything in common with the woodpecker except colour, which in both is green, but paler in the woodpecker. Third, although I cannot determine what bird the شرقراق [shirqirrāq] sirikrak is, because among European birds none is like it, it is nevertheless certain that it has nothing in common with the Hebrew רחם racham; and that the Talmudists, the Chaldees, the Syrians and the more recent Hebrews, who determine that racham and serakraka are the same bird, are gravely mistaken. But now others must be heard. ¶

III, 52 II. In Samaritana paraphrasi רחם racham est ࠍࠏࠌࠉࠕࠀ naamitha, struthio: quo nihil absurdius fingi potuit. Nam struthionem versu decimo sexto jam prohibuerat Moses, ut abunde a) probatum est. Itaque causa nulla est, cur versu decimo octavo eundem rursus prohibeat. Praeterea cum struthio sit avis immisericors et crudelis in suos, quorsum nomen habeat a misericordia factum, quae Hebraeis est רחם rechem.

III, 52 II. In the Samaritan paraphrase רחם racham is ࠍࠏࠌࠉࠕࠀ [נעמיתא] naamitha, ostrich; nothing more absurd could have been invented. For Moses had already forbidden the ostrich in verse 16, as has been amply a) proved. So there is no reason why he should forbid the same bird again in verse 18. Besides, since the ostrich is a bird without mercy and cruel to its own young, why should it have a name derived from mercy, which in Hebrew is רחם rechem? ¶

III. Idem in Graecos potest objici, quibus רחם racham est κύκνος, cygnus. Utut enim cygnos Graeci poëtae summis laudibus evehant, tamen quam sint immites, docent haec Philosophi Historiae libri noni capite primo, Εἰσὶ δὲ οἱ κύκνοι ἀλληλοφάγοι μάλιστα τῶν ὀρνέων. Unde Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo tertio, Iidem mutua carne vescuntur inter se. Is igitur in Aristotele legerat, cygnos ἀλληλοφαγεῖν, mutuo se vorare, nec simpliciter ἀλληλοκτονεῖν, se mutuo interimere, quomodo hunc locum citant Athenaeus libro nono, et Aelianus in Variis libri primi capite decimo quarto, ut diritatem facti emolliant. Unde est, quod in fabulis homines duri et immites in cygnos mutari narrantur. Antonius Liberalis de Cygno, Apollinis filio, qui a patre factus est cygnus: Οὗτος ἦν εὐσχήμων τὴν ὄψιν· τὸ δὲ ἦθος ἄχαρις καὶ ἄγροικός. Facie quidem erat formosus; sed moribus rudis et agrestis. Sunt et alii duo Cygni, quibus idem fatum affingunt b): Ὁ μὲν Ἄρεως υἱὸς, ὃς ἐν Θετταλίᾳ τοὺς παριόντας λοχῶν, καὶ ἀποκτείνων, καὶ βουλόμενος ταῖς κεφαλαῖς ναὸν ἀνεγεῖραι τῷ πατρὶ, ὃν Ἡρακλῆς ἀπέκτεινεν· ὁ δὲ Ποσειδῶνος, etc. ὃν Ἀχιλλεὺς ἀπέκτεινεν. Unus Martis filius, qui in Thessalia transeuntes insidiis appetens perimebat, ut ex eorum capitibus patri templum excitaret, quem interfecit Hercules: alius Neptuni filius, etc. quem occidit Achilles. Atque hic quoque narratur Philonomen uxorem propria manu necasse, et filium Tennem, arca inclusum, in mare conjecisse. Neque vero satis certum est, κύκνον hic haberi pro רחם racham: cum in Hieronymo, qui Graecos fere sequitur, alius sit ordo vocum; ita, ut cygnus τῷ תנשמת thinsemeth, et porphyrio τῷ racham respondeat. Quod videri queat minus alienum; quia רחם racham verbum est diligere: porphyrionem autem φιλοοίκειον εἶναί φασι, καὶ τὴν συντροφίαν τῶν συννόμων ἀγαπᾷν. Quod multis probat Aelianus Historiae libri quinti capite vigesimo octavo porphyrionis cujusdam exemplo, qui, convictore gallo privatus, inedia sibi mortem conscivit.

III. The same objection can be made against the Greeks, for whom רחם racham is κύκνος [kyknos], swan. For however highly the Greek poets extol swans, how savage they are is taught by these words of the Philosopher, History, book 9, chapter 1: Εἰσὶ δὲ οἱ κύκνοι ἀλληλοφάγοι μάλιστα τῶν ὀρνέων [eisi de hoi kyknoi allēlophagoi malista tōn orneōn]. Hence Pliny, book 10, chapter 23: They also feed on one another's flesh. He had therefore read in Aristotle that swans ἀλληλοφαγεῖν [allēlophagein], devour one another, and not simply ἀλληλοκτονεῖν [allēloktonein], kill one another, as Athenaeus, book 9, and Aelian in the Varia, book 1, chapter 14, cite this passage, so as to soften the horror of the act. This is why in fables hard and savage men are said to be changed into swans. Antoninus Liberalis, on Cycnus, son of Apollo, who was made a swan by his father: Οὗτος ἦν εὐσχήμων τὴν ὄψιν· τὸ δὲ ἦθος ἄχαρις καὶ ἄγροικός [houtos ēn euschēmōn tēn opsin; to de ēthos acharis kai agroikos]. He was handsome in appearance, but rude and boorish in character. There are also two other Cycni to whom the same fate is ascribed b): Ὁ μὲν Ἄρεως υἱὸς, ὃς ἐν Θετταλίᾳ τοὺς παριόντας λοχῶν, καὶ ἀποκτείνων, καὶ βουλόμενος ταῖς κεφαλαῖς ναὸν ἀνεγεῖραι τῷ πατρὶ, ὃν Ἡρακλῆς ἀπέκτεινεν· ὁ δὲ Ποσειδῶνος, etc. ὃν Ἀχιλλεὺς ἀπέκτεινεν [ho men Areōs huios, hos en Thettalia tous pariontas lochōn, kai apokteinōn, kai boulomenos tais kephalais naon anegeirai tō patri, hon Hēraklēs apekteinen; ho de Poseidōnos, etc. hon Achilleus apekteinen]. One, the son of Mars, who in Thessaly ambushed and killed passers-by, in order to build a temple to his father from their heads, and whom Hercules slew; the other, the son of Neptune, etc., whom Achilles killed. This latter is also said to have killed his wife Philonome with his own hand and to have thrown his son Tennes, shut in a chest, into the sea. Yet it is not quite certain that κύκνος [kyknos] here stands for רחם racham, since in Jerome, who generally follows the Greeks, the order of the words is different, so that swan corresponds to תנשמת thinsemeth, and porphyrio to racham. This might seem less inappropriate, because רחם racham is the verb to love; and the porphyrio, they say, φιλοοίκειον εἶναί φασι, καὶ τὴν συντροφίαν τῶν συννόμων ἀγαπᾷν [philooikeion einai phasi, kai tēn syntrophian tōn synnomōn agapan]. Aelian, History, book 5, chapter 28, proves this at length by the example of a certain porphyrio which, deprived of the cock that was its companion, starved itself to death. ¶

III, 55 However that may be, whatever is adduced about the love and gratitude of bee-eaters toward their parents does not meet the point. For in that case רחם racham would not be a φιλότεκνος [philoteknos] bird, as the Hebrews hold, but a φιλοπάτωρ [philopatōr] one. And sometimes it is attributed to a father: as in Ps 103:13. כרחם אב על בנים, As a father is merciful toward his children. And Isa 63:15–16. There is a further species, described by Hasselquist [=Fredrik Hasselquist, Iter Palaestinum (1757)] in his Travels, p. 302 f., under the name merops galilaeus, whose Arabic name he says is Varvar; this very word appears in Golius' [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)] Lex. Arab., p. 2650, under the root وَرْوَرَ [warwara], he sharpened: "وَرْوَارٌ [warwārun] merops [bee-eater], from its sharp bill." Compare what Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde] has on this III, 50 bird, Fasc. 1, Chapter 5, p. 73–75. He thinks it is the same as the Corvus Aegyptius, green and spotted, with the middle of the back blue, described by Hasselquist, p. 296 f. But when Oedmann asserts that the Arabs write that the شقراق [shqrāq] is a harbinger of rain, that very learned man is mistaken. Certainly the Arabs whom Bochart excerpted in this place say nothing of the kind.
