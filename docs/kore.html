Hebraeo nomine קורא kore, non significari perdicem, ut hactenus creditum est, sed rusticulae, vel gallinaginis genus.

That the Hebrew name קורא kore does not signify the partridge, as has hitherto been believed, but a kind of woodcock (rusticula) or gallinago. ¶

Quae avis Hebraice קורא kore, eadem Chaldaice dicitur קוראה koraa, vel קוריה korea, et Arabice قارية karia. Hujus Sacri Scriptores duobus locis me- col. 81 minerunt. In priore David fugitivus Saulem ita compellat 1 Sam. XXVI, 20. Egressus est rex Israelis ad quaerendum pulicem unum, כאשר ירדוף קורא בהרים, ac si quis persequeretur avem kore in montibus. Alter locus sic habet Ierem. XVII, 11. Kore collegit, et non peperit: ita qui congerit opes, sed non in justitia, in medio dierum suarum derelinquet eas, et tandem erit insipiens. Ex quibus locis discere est, קורא kore avem esse montanam, non ita magni pretii, et in cujus aucupio male operam insumat auceps; atque ex illarum numero, quae alienis ovis incubant. Id enim sibi volunt haec Ieremiae verba, קורא דגר ולא ילד, Kore collegit, et non peperit, id est, aliena collegit ova, non a se parta, quae umbra sua foveret. Hinc Es. XXXIV, 15. de serpente קפוז kippoz, id est, acontia, seu jaculo, ודגרה בצלה, et collegit in umbra sua, id est, incubavit ovis. Et Iob. XXXIX, 14. pro תחמם, calefaciet, de struthionis ovis, Chaldaeus habet תדגר, colliget. Quod monere necesse fuit, quia colligendi verbum in Ieremia perperam multi referunt ad pullorum ἐπισύναξιν. In his Hippolytus, Chrysosto- mus, Olympiodorus, et ex Hebraeis R. Selomo (1). Haec ex Scriptura hactenus.

The bird called קורא kore in Hebrew is called קוראה koraa or קוריה korea in Chaldee, and قارية [qāriya] karia in Arabic. The sacred writers mention it in two places. col. 81 In the first, David, a fugitive, addresses Saul thus, 1 Sam 26:20: the king of Israel has come out to seek a single flea, כאשר ירדוף קורא בהרים, as if one were pursuing the bird kore in the mountains. The other passage reads thus, Jer 17:11: The kore gathered, and did not bring forth: so he who amasses riches, but not by justice, will leave them in the midst of his days, and at his end will be a fool. From these passages we learn that the קורא kore is a mountain bird, not of great value, in the catching of which the fowler spends his effort badly; and that it is one of those birds which sit on the eggs of others. For this is what these words of Jeremiah mean, קורא דגר ולא ילד, the kore gathered, and did not bring forth, that is, it gathered the eggs of others, not laid by itself, to brood them under its shadow. Hence Isa 34:15, of the serpent קפוז kippoz, that is, the acontias or dart-snake, ודגרה בצלה, and it gathered in its shadow, that is, it brooded its eggs. And at Job 39:14, for תחמם, will warm, said of the ostrich's eggs, the Chaldee has תדגר, will gather. This needed to be pointed out, because many wrongly refer the verb to gather in Jeremiah to the ἐπισύναξιν [episynaxin] of the chicks. Among them are Hippolytus, Chrysostom, Olympiodorus, and among the Hebrews R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] R1. So much from Scripture. ¶

Non est tamen, quod quis colligat ex Hebraeae fabulae cum Graeca et Romana collatione, avem קורא kore eandem esse cum grue. Nam, ut Hebraei lupo leonem, II, 634 ita grui potuerunt aliam avem substituere. At saltem hoc habemus, avi kore longum esse rostrum. Quod idem asserunt Hebraei omnes. Aruch in קר quinto עוף הוא ופיו שמנקר בו ארוך ביותר, Avis est, cujus os, id est, ro- strum, valde longum est. Ita etiam R. Aquinas, et David de Pomis, קורא צפור שיש לו מקרא ארוך, Kore, avis longum habens rostrum.

Yet there is no reason for anyone to conclude, from comparing the Hebrew fable with the Greek and Roman, that the bird קורא kore is the same as the crane. For just as the Hebrews substituted the lion for the wolf, II, 634 so they could substitute another bird for the crane. But at least we gather this: that the bird kore has a long beak. All the Hebrews assert the same. The Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], under קר, fifth entry: עוף הוא ופיו שמנקר בו ארוך ביותר, It is a bird whose mouth, that is, beak, is very long. So also R. Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] and David de Pomis [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary]: קורא צפור שיש לו מקרא ארוך, Kore, a bird having a long beak. ¶

VI, 41.) avis est, quae pedes habet breves, longum rostrum, viridem colorem (aliud exemplar habet اخضر الظهر dorsum viride) eam amant Arabes, ut qui habeant pro ave auspicata, et hominem liberalem illi conferunt. Et Giauhari, القارية طير خضر تحبها الاعراب ويشهبون بها الرجل السخي وذلك لانها تنذر بالمطر Karia, avis viridis, quam amant Arabes, et liberali homini comparant; quia pluviam significat. Et Alcamus القارية طاير اذا راوه استبشروا بالمطر كانه رسول الغيث او مقدمة السحاب Karia avis, quam qui vident propter futuram pluviam laetantur, ac si imbris esset praenuntia, ac nubium anteambulo. Rursus Damir in titulo De ejus usu, الحل لان العرب كانت تاكلها Esus ejus concessus est; quia comedunt Arabes. Et liber, cui titulus الحج Alchag, seu Peregrinatio, الحمام يغدي بشاه وما دونه من القواري وغيرها يغدي بالقيمة Colum- ba (quam quis peregrinantium ad pseudo-prophetae sepulchrum dono daturum se vovit) ove redimitur: sed quidquid est infra columbam (puta kariae aves, aut aliae similes) ejus pretio dato redimitur. Inde duo collegit Damir, primo, edulem esse avem, secundo, a columba differre. Quamvis Ibno Sachith, in libro De puritate sermonis, القواري id est, karias aves definiat طير خضر لها ترجيع aves virides, quae vocem in gutture reciprocant, ad instar gementis columbae, quod alioqui iis avibus proprium est, quae aquam uno haustu sorbent. Id Arabice dicitur عب abba.

But the Arabs teach us much more about the bird قارية [qāriya] karia, which Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] describes thus: القارية كسارية طاير قصير الرجلين طويل المنقار اخضر اللون تحبه الاعراب وتتيبن به ويشهبون [al-qāriya ka-sāriya ṭāyir qaṣīr al-rijlayn ṭawīl al-minqār akhḍar al-lawn tuḥibbuhu al-aʿrāb wa-tatayabbanu bihi wa-yushahbūn] col. 82 به الرجل السخي [bihi al-rajul al-sakhī] Karia, a noun of the same form as saria (that is, beam, Luke 6:41), is a bird which has short feet, a long beak, a green colour (another copy has اخضر الظهر [akhḍar al-ẓahr] a green back); the Arabs love it, since they hold it a bird of good omen, and they compare a generous man to it. And Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ]: القارية طير خضر تحبها الاعراب ويشهبون بها الرجل السخي وذلك لانها تنذر بالمطر [al-qāriya ṭayr khuḍr tuḥibbuhā al-aʿrāb wa-yushahbūn bihā al-rajul al-sakhī wa-dhālika li-annahā tundhiru bi-l-maṭar] Karia, a green bird, which the Arabs love and to which they compare a generous man, because it signals rain. And Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: القارية طاير اذا راوه استبشروا بالمطر كانه رسول الغيث او مقدمة السحاب [al-qāriya ṭāyir idhā raʾawhu istabsharū bi-l-maṭar ka-annahu rasūl al-ghayth aw muqaddimat al-saḥāb] Karia, a bird at the sight of which people rejoice because of rain to come, as though it were the herald of the shower and the forerunner of the clouds. Again Damir, under the heading On its use: الحل لان العرب كانت تاكلها [al-ḥill li-anna al-ʿarab kānat taʾkuluhā] Eating it is permitted, because the Arabs eat it. And the book entitled الحج [al-ḥajj] Alchag, or Pilgrimage: الحمام يغدي بشاه وما دونه من القواري وغيرها يغدي بالقيمة [al-ḥamām yughdī bi-shāh wa-mā dūnahu min al-qawārī wa-ghayrihā yughdī bi-l-qīma] A dove (which one of the pilgrims has vowed to give as an offering at the tomb of the false prophet) is redeemed with a sheep; but whatever is below a dove (for example karia birds, or others like them) is redeemed by paying its price. From this Damir inferred two things: first, that the bird is edible; second, that it differs from the dove. However, Ibno Sachith, in the book On the Purity of Speech, defines القواري [al-qawārī], that is, karia birds, as طير خضر لها ترجيع [ṭayr khuḍr lahā tarjīʿ] green birds which make their voice reverberate in the throat, like a cooing dove, which is otherwise peculiar to those birds that drink water in a single draught. This is called عب [ʿabb] abba in Arabic. ¶

His ita explicatis sequitur, avem קרא kore, vel قارية karia, si mavis, prout Arabice vocatur, nobis esse ignotam; quia constat, apud nos nullam haberi II, 635 avem, cui conveniant omnes hae notae. Tamen omnium, quas novimus, huic proxime accedit Aristotelis ὁ σκολόπαξ, vel ἀσκολόπαξ. Gaza vocat gallinaginem; Plinius et Martialis rusticulam et rusticam perdicem; Nemesianus scolopacem; Glossae veteres acceiam, unde Thusci hodieque acceigam: Germani sneppam; et Galli beccassam a magnitudine rostri. Quo eodem refero Hebraeum קורא kore. Neque enim a clamando factum, ut putant, sed a nomine קורה kora, quod primo trabem et tignum sonat, deinde etiam palum acutum, ex quo fiunt sepes. Unde est, quod verbum קרה kera, inde deductum, non solum contignare et contabulare significat, ut in Nehemia passim: sed et obsepire, vel sepem instaurare, ut in tractatu Talmudico Moed Katon cap. 1. fol. 7. b. ומקרין את הפריצה במועד כיצד מקרין רב יוסף אמר בהוצא ודפני, Etiam sepis rupturam reparare licet die festo. At quomodo reparatur? Rabbi Ioseph dixit, palis ex palma et lauro. Itaque, ut σκολόπαξ Graece dicitur, quod rostrum longum et rectum habeat instar σκόλοπος, id est, pali: ita Hebraice קורא kore, quia Hebraeis קורה kora non solum est δόκος, sed et σκόλοψ. Ita, ut par sit ratio. Finale Aleph est pro He, ut in נקרא 2 Sam. I, 6. Et in nomine קראה col. 83 pro occursu, quod fit a verbo קרה, occurrere. These things having been thus explained, it follows that the bird קרא kore, or قارية [qāriya] karia if you prefer, as it is called in Arabic, is unknown to us, since it is clear that among us there is no II, 635 bird to which all these marks apply. Yet of all the birds we know, the closest to it is Aristotle's ὁ σκολόπαξ [ho skolopax], or ἀσκολόπαξ [askolopax]. Gaza calls it gallinago; Pliny and Martial call it woodcock (rusticula) and rustica perdix [rustic partridge]; Nemesianus scolopax; the old Glosses acceia, whence the Tuscans to this day say acceiga; the Germans sneppa; and the French beccassa, from the size of its beak. To the same bird I refer the Hebrew קורא kore. For it is not formed from crying out, as people think, but from the noun קורה kora, which first means beam and timber, and then also a sharpened stake, from which fences are made. Hence the verb קרה kera, derived from it, means not only to lay beams and to floor with planks, as passim in Nehemiah, but also to fence in, or to repair a fence, as in the Talmudic tractate Moed Katon, ch. 1, fol. 7b: ומקרין את הפריצה במועד כיצד מקרין רב יוסף אמר בהוצא ודפני, One may also repair a breach in a fence on a festival day. But how is it repaired? Rabbi Joseph said: with stakes of palm and laurel. Thus, just as the σκολόπαξ [skolopax] is so called in Greek because it has a long, straight beak like a σκόλοψ [skolops], that is, a stake, so in Hebrew it is called קורא kore, because for the Hebrews קורה kora is not only δόκος [dokos] but also σκόλοψ [skolops]. So the reasoning is parallel. The final Aleph stands for He, as in נקרא 2 Sam 1:6, and in the noun קראה col. 83 for meeting, which comes from the verb קרה, to meet. Besides the size of the beak, the bird kore has this in common with the scolopax: both have short feet, both are edible, both live in the mountains. For just as David says that τὸν [ton] kore is hunted over the mountains, so Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] writes that the scolopax spends the summer in the Alps and the Pyrenees, and in the highest mountains of Switzerland, Savoy and Auvergne. Neither exceeds the bulk of a dove. For just as the قارية [qāriya] karia is defined by the Arabs as دون الحمام [dūn al-ḥamām] below a dove, so Nemesianus says of the scolopax: ¶

— — praeda est facilis, et amoena scolopax: Corpore non Paphiis avibus majore videbis.

— — the lovely scolopax is an easy prey: you will see it no larger in body than the Paphian birds. ¶

Paphiis avibus, id est, columbis. Quin ut Arabum karia, ita scolopax quoque, seu rusticula, pluviae est praenuntia. Gesnerus in Avibus pag. 504. Rusticulae minoris (Galli beccassinam vocant) vocem auditam pluviae prognosticon quidam faciunt. Sequitur, talem esse sonum vocis, qualis est cultri, per serram ducti. Quod velle videntur Arabes, cum kariae tribuunt ترجيع id est, vocem reciprocam. Itaque hactenus omnia sunt similia satis. Tamen color dissimilis, qui in karia viridis, saltem in dorso: in rusticula ita varius, ut a viridi plurimum distet. Praeterea, quamvis edulis sit utraque avis, tamen a Davide קורא kore non videtur magni fieri; et Arabes kariam non alia de causa celebrant, quam quia est inter pluviae prognostica: at nostra rusticula mensarum deliciis est maxime nobilis, neque perdici plurimum cedit. Hinc Martialis libri decimi tertii Epigrammate septuagesimo sexto, sub lemmate Rusticula, II, 636 Rustica sim, an perdix, quid refert, si sapor idem est? Carior est perdix, sic sapit illa magis.

to the Paphian birds, that is, to doves. Indeed, like the karia of the Arabs, the scolopax too, or woodcock (rusticula), is a harbinger of rain. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] in his Birds, p. 504: Some make the call of the lesser woodcock (the French call it beccassine), when heard, a prognostic of rain. He adds that the sound of its call is like that of a knife drawn across a saw. This seems to be what the Arabs mean when they attribute to the karia ترجيع [tarjīʿ], that is, a reciprocating call. So far, then, everything is quite similar. The colour, however, is different: in the karia it is green, at least on the back, while in the woodcock it is so mottled that it is very far from green. Moreover, although both birds are edible, David does not seem to have held the קורא kore in great esteem; and the Arabs celebrate the karia for no other reason than that it is among the prognostics of rain. Our woodcock, by contrast, is most renowned among the delicacies of the table, and yields little to the partridge. Hence Martial, book 13, Epigram 76, under the heading Rusticula: II, 636 Whether I am a woodcock or a partridge, what does it matter, if the flavour is the same? The partridge is dearer; that is why it tastes better. ¶

Denique de rusticula nemo scripsit, quod de קורא kore Ieremias, Kore collegit (seu incubavit) et non peperit. Concludo igitur, τὸν kore avem esse, illis locis peculiarem, a rusticula ita diversam, ut ab ea non multum differat (2). Iam dicendum, quomodo hanc vocem explicent veteres et novi interpretes.

Finally, no one has written of the woodcock what Jeremiah wrote of the קורא kore: the kore gathered (or sat on eggs) and did not bring forth. I conclude, therefore, that the kore is a bird peculiar to those regions, distinct from the woodcock, yet not differing from it greatly R2. We must now state how the ancient and modern interpreters explain this word. ¶

Kore קרא redditur a Graecis νυκτικόραξ nycticorax 1 Sam. XXVI, 20. sed haud dubie prava lectione deceptis. Legerant nimirum קדא pro קרא, Daleth pro Res, quae literae sunt inter se simillimae. Vox קדא apud Hebraeos desiit esse in usu: sed inde Chaldaei formarunt קדיא, et Syri ܥܕܝܢ, permutatis Koph et Ajin; ut cum ܥܛܪ, vaporem dicunt pro קטר, et ܥܪܝܐ, frigus pro II, 637 קריא. Veram esse hanc conjecturam, ex eo patet, quod alibi νυκτικόραξ in Graeca versione respondet Hebraeo כוס, pro quo Chaldaeus Onkelos habet קריא Levit. XI, 17. Et Syrus interpres ܒܣܢ Psal. CII, 7. Ergo כוס, et קרא, unde קריא et ܒܣܢ sunt synonyma. Itaque, si כוס est νυκτικόραξ, quomodo Graeci reddunt, ita etiam קרא. Simile mendum est in tractatu Talmudico Nidda fol. 23. a. ubi pro קדיא per Daleth scribitur octies קריא per Res, ac si esset avis kore, cum sit avis nocturna. Illi enim jungitur קפופא kiphupha, id est, noctua. Et dicuntur aves esse דעיניהן הולכות לפניהן כשלאדם, quarum oculi non sunt a latere, ut reliquarum avium, sed in anteriore parte, ut hominum. Et in Glossa utraque describitur עוף צועק בלילה ופניו דומות לחתול ועיניו לפניו, Avis, noctu clamans, et faciem habens ad instar felis, et oculos in anteriore parte. Rursus in Berachoth cap. 9. fol. 57. b. כל מינין עופית יפין לחלום חוץ מן קריא וקפופא וקורפראי, Omnes avium species in somniis faustae sunt, praeter nycticoracem, noctuam et vespertilionem. In his, inquam, verbis rursus קריא per Res scribitur, quod mutandum in Daleth. Ita denique in Chaldaea col. 84 paraphrasi Es. XXXIV, 15. pro וקורידהון ינצפון בטולה rescribe וקודידהון, Et nycticoraces eorum ululabunt in umbra ejus. Ibi enim Res pro Daleth. Unde est, quod Latinus interpres Chaldaei sic reddidit, Et perdices garrient, etc. Quod est prorsus absurdum. Agitur enim de Idumaeae vastatione, ad quam non pertinet, ut perdices in ea garriant, sed ut nycticoraces et bubones horrendum in modum ululent.

Kore קרא is rendered by the Greeks νυκτικόραξ [nyktikorax], nycticorax, 1 Sam 26:20, but they were doubtless misled by a faulty reading. They had evidently read קדא for קרא, Daleth for Resh, letters that are very similar to each other. The word קדא ceased to be in use among the Hebrews; but from it the Chaldeans formed קדיא, and the Syrians ܥܕܝܢ [עדין], with Koph and Ayin interchanged, as when they say ܥܛܪ [עטר], vapour, for קטר, and ܥܪܝܐ [עריא], cold, for II, 637 קריא. That this conjecture is correct is evident from the fact that elsewhere in the Greek version νυκτικόραξ [nyktikorax] corresponds to the Hebrew כוס, for which the Chaldee Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] has קריא, Lev 11:17, and the Syriac translator ܒܣܢ [בסן], Ps 102:7. Therefore כוס and קרא, whence קריא and ܒܣܢ [בסן], are synonyms. So if כוס is the νυκτικόραξ [nyktikorax], as the Greeks render it, so too is קרא. There is a similar error in the Talmudic tractate Niddah, fol. 23a, where instead of קדיא with Daleth, קריא with Resh is written eight times, as if it were the bird kore, whereas it is a night bird. For joined to it is קפופא kiphupha, that is, the owl. And they are said to be birds דעיניהן הולכות לפניהן כשלאדם, whose eyes are not at the side, as in other birds, but in front, as in humans. And in the Gloss both are described as עוף צועק בלילה ופניו דומות לחתול ועיניו לפניו, a bird that cries at night, with a face like a cat's and eyes in front. Again in Berakhot, ch. 9, fol. 57b: כל מינין עופית יפין לחלום חוץ מן קריא וקפופא וקורפראי, All species of birds are auspicious in dreams, except the nycticorax, the owl and the bat. In these words too, I say, קריא is written with Resh, which must be changed to Daleth. So finally in the Chaldee col. 84 paraphrase of Isa 34:15, for וקורידהון ינצפון בטולה read וקודידהון, And their nycticoraces shall howl in its shade. For there too Resh stands for Daleth. This is why the Latin translator of the Chaldee rendered it thus: And the partridges shall chatter, etc. This is utterly absurd. For the passage concerns the devastation of Edom, to which it is not fitting that partridges should chatter in it, but that nycticoraces and eagle-owls should howl in a horrifying manner. ¶

Alter de kore locus Ierem. XVII, 11. קרא דגר ולא ילך Graece sic explicatur, Ἐφώνησεν πέρδιξ, συνήγαγεν ἃ οὐκ ἔτεκεν, Clamavit perdix, congregavit quae non peperit. Et verba ἐφώνησεν πέρδιξ sunt duplex versio vocis קרא, quae ab aliis kara, ab aliis kore lecta est, et ab illis ἐφώνησεν, ab his πέρδιξ reddita (3). Quod postremum sequuntur Hieronymus, Syrus, Arabs, Hebraei et recentiores omnes, quasi ad vulgarem perdicem haec referantur. Et varie id explicatur. Sunt enim, qui perdicem asserunt aliena ova suffurari, et fovere, et pullos, ex iis natos, sibi vindicare tanquam suos. Epiphanius in Physiologo capite nono, Οὐκ ἀρκεῖ αὐτῇ τῆς ἰδίας γαστρὸς ὁ τόκος, ἀλλὰ κλέπτουσα τὰ ὠὰ τῶν ἀλλοτρίων φέρει αὐτὰ ἐπὶ τὴν νεοττίαν αὐτῆς· Non sufficit illi proprii ventris foetus, sed aliarum ova diripiens in suum nidum defert. Eustathius in Hexaëmeron pag. 29. Ἀλλότρια δὲ κλέπτων ὠὰ νεοσσοποιεῖ· Aliena ova surrepta II, 638 educit. Ambrosius in Hexaëmeron libri sexti capite tertio, Perdicem astutam novimus, quae aliena ova diripiat, hoc est, perdicis alterius, et corpore foveat suo. Et in Epistola ad Sabinum, ubi Ieremiae locum multis enarrat; Quidam naturae perdicis etiam istud aptandum putarunt, eo quod aliena diripiat ova, et foveat suo corpore, atque hac sua fraude partus alienos studeat acquirere. Philastrius, De Haeresibus in Praefatione, Inimicum humani generis perdici fuisse ab Ieremia pro- pheta non immerito comparatum (oportet) agnoscere, qui, cum infoecunditatem patiatur, saepius aliorum furtim foetus incubans, quasi suos jam fovet ac vindicat filios. Cassiodorus, Variorum libri secundi Epistola decima quarta, Perdicibus etiam mos est, ova perdita per alterius matris damna sarcire, ut adoptione alienae sobolis incommoda suae reparent orbitatis. Isidorus Orig. lib. 12. cap. 7. Perdix adeo est fraudulenta, ut alteri ova diripiens foveat. Damir capite الحجل seu De perdice, والانثي اذا اصيب بيضها قصدت عش غيرها وغلتها علي بيضها وتسرقه وتحضنه Et foemina, cum corruptum est ovum ejus, alius perdicis nidum invadit, et per fraudem surre- ptum ovo suo substituit et fovet. Haec ille, quae fateor me non capere satis. Neque enim video, quomodo queat perdix aliena ova suffurari, cum neque rostro id possit praestare, neque habeat digitos ad id accommodatos. Itaque magnus in Solinum auctor pagina centesima quinquagesima Plinio dicam scribit non immerito, eo quod perdices ova sua alio transferre tradiderit, cum id non ferat earum natura, et ex Aristotele contrarium possit adstrui.

The other passage on the kore, Jer 17:11. קרא דגר ולא ילך, is rendered thus in Greek: Ἐφώνησεν πέρδιξ, συνήγαγεν ἃ οὐκ ἔτεκεν [Ephōnēsen perdix, synēgagen ha ouk eteken], "The partridge cried out, it gathered what it did not bring forth." The words ἐφώνησεν πέρδιξ [ephōnēsen perdix] are a double rendering of the word קרא, which some read as kara and others as kore; the former rendered it ἐφώνησεν [ephōnēsen], the latter πέρδιξ [perdix] R3. The latter rendering is followed by Jerome, the Syriac, the Arabic, the Hebrews and all the more recent writers, as though these words referred to the common partridge. And this is explained in various ways. For there are those who assert that the partridge steals the eggs of others, incubates them, and claims the chicks hatched from them as its own. Epiphanius, in the Physiologus, chapter 9: Οὐκ ἀρκεῖ αὐτῇ τῆς ἰδίας γαστρὸς ὁ τόκος, ἀλλὰ κλέπτουσα τὰ ὠὰ τῶν ἀλλοτρίων φέρει αὐτὰ ἐπὶ τὴν νεοττίαν αὐτῆς [Ouk arkei autē tēs idias gastros ho tokos, alla kleptousa ta ōa tōn allotriōn pherei auta epi tēn neottian autēs]; "The offspring of her own womb does not suffice her, but she snatches the eggs of others and carries them to her own nest." Eustathius, on the Hexaemeron, p. 29: Ἀλλότρια δὲ κλέπτων ὠὰ νεοσσοποιεῖ [Allotria de kleptōn ōa neossopoiei]; "It steals the eggs of others and hatches II, 638 them." Ambrose, in the Hexaemeron, book 6, chapter 3: "We know the partridge to be crafty, for it snatches the eggs of another, that is, of another partridge, and warms them with its own body." And in the Letter to Sabinus, where he expounds the passage of Jeremiah at length: "Some have thought that this too should be applied to the nature of the partridge, because it snatches the eggs of others and warms them with its own body, and by this fraud seeks to acquire the offspring of others." Philastrius, On Heresies, in the Preface: "We must recognize that the enemy of the human race was not undeservedly compared by the prophet Jeremiah to the partridge, which, since it suffers barrenness, often secretly sits on the young of others and then warms and claims them as its own children." Cassiodorus, Variae, book 2, Letter 14: "Partridges too have the custom of making good their lost eggs at the loss of another mother, so as to repair the hardship of their own bereavement by adopting another's brood." Isidore, Orig. book 12, ch. 7: "The partridge is so deceitful that it snatches another's eggs and warms them." Damir, in the chapter الحجل [al-ḥajal], or On the partridge: والانثي اذا اصيب بيضها قصدت عش غيرها وغلتها علي بيضها وتسرقه وتحضنه [wa-l-unthā idhā uṣība bayḍuhā qaṣadat ʿushsha ghayrihā wa-ghallathā ʿalā bayḍihā wa-tasriquhu wa-taḥḍunuhu] "And the female, when her egg has been spoiled, invades the nest of another partridge, and substitutes for her own egg one stolen by fraud, and warms it." So he writes; I confess I do not sufficiently understand it. For I do not see how a partridge could steal the eggs of others, since it cannot do this with its beak, nor does it have toes suited to the purpose. Therefore the great author on Solinus, on page 150, not undeservedly brings a charge against Pliny for having reported that partridges carry their eggs elsewhere, since their nature does not allow this, and the contrary can be established from Aristotle. ¶

Proinde videbuntur Hebraei cautius scripsisse. Neque enim perdicem tradunt aliarum ova transferre, sed in alieno nido incubare. R. Selomo in 1 Sam. XXVI, 20. הקורא עוף ששמו קורא ובלעז פרד״יז וכן קורא דגר ולא ילד ומרדף אחר קיני שאר עופות ויושב על ביציהם Kore avis est, quae Hebraice sic voca- tur, et vernacula lingua perdix, de qua in Ieremia dicitur: „Kore collegit, et non peperit:“ quia aliarum avium nidos consectatur, ut earum ova incubet. Et Porta coeli fol. 29. d. הוא רובץ על ביצי העוף אחר ומחמם אותם Alius avis ova incubat atque illa fovet. Ita etiam Kimchius in Ieremiam. Sed idem in col. 85 Lexico perdicem ova aliena primo colligere asserit, ac postmodum iis incubare tanquam suis, הוא מאסף ביצי עוף אחר ורובץ עליהם כאלו הם שלו. Utut sit, illud etiam parum verissimile est, perdicem, etiamsi maxime vellet, alieno nido incubare, quia ovorum vera mater id nequaquam esset passura. Haec igitur sunt valde paradoxa.

Accordingly, the Hebrews will seem to have written more cautiously. For they do not say that the partridge carries off the eggs of others, but that it sits in another's nest. R. Selomo on 1 Sam. XXVI, 20: הקורא עוף ששמו קורא ובלעז פרד״יז וכן קורא דגר ולא ילד ומרדף אחר קיני שאר עופות ויושב על ביציהם "Kore is a bird which is so called in Hebrew, and in the vernacular perdix [partridge], of which it is said in Jeremiah: „The kore gathered, and did not bring forth“; because it pursues the nests of other birds in order to sit on their eggs." And Porta coeli, fol. 29d: הוא רובץ על ביצי העוף אחר ומחמם אותם "It sits on the eggs of another bird and warms them." So too Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] on Jeremiah. But the same author, in his col. 85 Lexicon, asserts that the partridge first gathers the eggs of others and afterwards sits on them as if they were its own: הוא מאסף ביצי עוף אחר ורובץ עליהם כאלו הם שלו. However that may be, this too is hardly very likely, that a partridge, however much it wished, should sit in another's nest, since the true mother of the eggs would by no means allow it. These things, then, are highly paradoxical. ¶

Tamen aliud sequitur longe παραδοξότερον, nempe pullos, ab aliena matre sic exclusos, eam aliquanto post relinquere, ut ad veram matrem redeant, quam nempe ex voce agnoscant. Eustathius ubi supra, Αὐξηθέντες οἱ νεοττοὶ, II, 639 ἕκαστος, κατὰ γένος, πρὸς τοὺς οἰκείους ἀνίπταται, καὶ ἔρημόν αὐτὴν κατα-λιμπάνουσι· Cum pulli adoleverunt, unusquisque, juxta genus suum, ad proprios parentes revolant, eamque solam relinquunt. Ambrosius in Hexaëmeron, Cum eduxit pullos suos, amittit eos: quia, ubi vocem ejus audierunt, quae ova generavit, relicta ea, ad illam se naturali quodam amore et munere conferunt, quam veram sibi matrem ovorum generatione cognoverint, significantes, hanc nutricis fungi officio, illam parentis. Et in Epistola ad Sabinum, Cum labor omnis impensus nutrimentorum educantem exhauserit, atque adolescere pulli coeperint, tunc vocem emittit (vera mater), et quadam pietatis tuba prolem advocat. Quae naturali quodam auditu excitata, agnoscit parentem, et simulantem deserit. Ita, cum vult congregare, quae non peperit, amittit, quos nutriendos putavit. Philastrius, cumque iis jam tempus aetatis discernens adcreverit, tunc, admissis (lege dimissis) falsis parentibus, jura parentum sensu proprio recordantes, palamque oculis intuentes, sequi eos amplius non demorantur. Cassiodorus, Sed mox ut nati fiduciam habere coeperint ambulandi, ad campos exeunt cum nutrice: qui, ut fuerint materna voce commoniti, ovorum suorum potius genitricem petunt, quamvis ab aliis furtivis foetibus educentur. Isidorus, Dum pulli propriae vocem genetricis audierint, naturali quodam instinctu hanc, quae eos fovit, relinquunt; et ad eandem, quae genuit, revertuntur. Damir, في طبع الحجل ان ياتي اعشاش نظاريه فياخذ بيضها فيحضنه فانا اطارت الفراخ لحقت بامهاتها التي باضتها Ex natura perdicis est, ut veniat ad nidos avium, sibi similium, et capiat ova earum, et foveat: sed pulli, cum evolant, adhaerent matribus suis, quae eos pepererunt. Porta coeli, והביצים ההם כשיבקעו ויצאו האפרוחים לא יהיו כמו בניו שילכו אחריו אלא יעזבוהו ולא יכירוהו ולא ילכו אלא אחרי מי שהוא להם, Sed, postquam exclusa sunt ova, et pulli prodierunt, ii non se gerunt ut pulli ejus, ita, ut illam sequantur: sed eam deserunt, neque amplius norunt, neque ullam aliam sequuntur praeter veram marem. Pro שהוא להם legendum שהוא להם אם ut in Kimchio, qui in Ieremiam haec eadem habet. Quibus lector asseri videt rem parum credibilem, nempe perdicum pullos naturaliter scire, ex qua matre prodierit ovum, unde exclusi sunt, et illam, nunquam antea auditam et visam, ex voce statim agnoscere. Cujusmodi sensum quis credat oviparis potius esse, quam viviparis, quae perfectius generant? Aut eo bruta pollere potius, quam hominem, quem naturalem parentum notitiam nullam habere planissimum est. Alioqui Oedipus non occidisset patrem incautus, nec cum matre concubuisset. Proinde apud Poëtam Telemachus, interrogatus, an Ulyssis esset filius, ita respondit a):

Yet there follows something far more παραδοξότερον [paradoxoteron], namely that the chicks, hatched in this way by a foreign mother, leave her some time later in order to return to their true mother, whom they recognize by her voice. Eustathius, in the place cited above: Αὐξηθέντες οἱ νεοττοὶ, II, 639 ἕκαστος, κατὰ γένος, πρὸς τοὺς οἰκείους ἀνίπταται, καὶ ἔρημόν αὐτὴν καταλιμπάνουσι· [Auxēthentes hoi neottoi, hekastos, kata genos, pros tous oikeious aniptatai, kai erēmon autēn kataleimpanousi] When the chicks have grown up, each one, according to its kind, flies back to its own parents, and they leave her alone. Ambrose, in the Hexaëmeron: When she has brought out her chicks, she loses them; for when they have heard the voice of the one who produced the eggs, they leave her and betake themselves, by a kind of natural love and duty, to the one whom they have recognized as their true mother by the generation of the eggs, showing that this one performs the office of a nurse, that one of a parent. And in the Letter to Sabinus: When all the labour spent on nourishment has exhausted the one rearing them, and the chicks have begun to grow up, then she (the true mother) utters her voice, and calls her offspring with a kind of trumpet of devotion. The offspring, roused by a kind of natural hearing, recognizes its parent and deserts the pretender. Thus, when she wishes to gather what she did not bear, she loses those whom she thought to rear. Philastrius: and when the time of discerning age has grown upon them, then, having let go (read dimissis [having dismissed]) their false parents, remembering by their own sense the rights of parents, and seeing them openly with their eyes, they no longer delay to follow them. Cassiodorus: But as soon as the young have begun to have confidence in walking, they go out into the fields with their nurse; and once they have been prompted by the maternal voice, they seek rather the mother of their eggs, although they are reared by others as stolen offspring. Isidore: When the chicks have heard the voice of their own mother, by a kind of natural instinct they leave the one who brooded them, and return to the one who bore them. Damir: في طبع الحجل ان ياتي اعشاش نظاريه فياخذ بيضها فيحضنه فانا اطارت الفراخ لحقت بامهاتها التي باضتها [fī ṭabʿ al-ḥajal an yaʾtī aʿshāsh naẓārīh fa-yaʾkhudh bayḍahā fa-yaḥḍunuh fa-anā aṭārat al-firākh laḥiqat bi-ummahātihā allatī bāḍat-hā] It is of the nature of the partridge that it comes to the nests of birds similar to itself, takes their eggs, and broods them; but the chicks, when they fly off, attach themselves to their mothers who laid them. Porta coeli: והביצים ההם כשיבקעו ויצאו האפרוחים לא יהיו כמו בניו שילכו אחריו אלא יעזבוהו ולא יכירוהו ולא ילכו אלא אחרי מי שהוא להם, But after the eggs are hatched and the chicks have come forth, they do not behave as its chicks, so as to follow it; rather they desert it, and no longer know it, nor do they follow any other except the true mother. For שהוא להם read שהוא להם אם, as in Kimchi, who has these same things on Jeremiah. The reader sees that by these writers something scarcely credible is asserted: namely, that partridge chicks know by nature from which mother came the egg out of which they were hatched, and that they at once recognize her by her voice, though they have never before heard or seen her. Who would believe that such a sense belongs to egg-laying creatures rather than to viviparous ones, which generate more perfectly? Or that brutes excel in it rather than man, who, it is perfectly plain, has no natural knowledge of his parents? Otherwise Oedipus would not have unwittingly killed his father, nor lain with his mother. Accordingly, in the Poet, Telemachus, asked whether he was the son of Ulysses, answered thus a): ¶

Se ipsum nemo novit, a quo sit editus, sed suspicamur omnes, aut credimus. Et vel si verum id esset, quod de perdicum pullis tradunt, tamen vix ac ne vix quidem potuisset observari. Pone enim e pluribus pullis, qui matrem perdicem sequuntur, unum, aut alterum eam deserere, ut ad aliam se conferant, quis ausit pro certo affirmare, hoc ideo fieri, quod exclusi fuerint ex ovis alteri illi surreptis? Quam multa supponenda sunt, ut haec observatio pro vera et comperta habeatur? Itaque apud veteres rerum naturalium scriptores nihil tale occurrit. Et memoria fefellit Hieronymum, cum in Ieremiam haec scripsit; Ajunt scriptores naturalis historiae, tam bestiarum et volucrum, quam arborum herbarumque, quorum principes sunt apud Graecos Aristoteles et Theophrastus, apud nos Plinius Secundus, hanc perdicis esse naturam, ut ova alterius perdicis, id est, aliena furetur, et eis incubet foveatque, cumque foetus adoleverit, avolare ab eo et alienum parentem relinquere. Sed et pii viri, qui cum illo hoc commentum in li-teras retulerunt, plus fidei, quam par fuit, τοῖς τερατολόγοις habuerunt, quibus id propositum fuit, ut populum detinerent per haec portenta narrationum. Nam, ut recte Synesius in Calvitii encomio, Τὸ δὲ ῥᾷστον καταγελάσεται ὁ δῆ-μος· δεῖται γὰρ τερατείας. Quod facile est, ridet plebs; eget enim narratione prodigiosa.

No one knows of himself by whom he was begotten; we all suspect, or believe. And even if what they relate of partridge chicks were true, it could still hardly, if at all, have been observed. For suppose that, of the many chicks following a mother partridge, one or two desert her to go to another: who would dare to affirm for certain that this happens because they were hatched from eggs stolen from that other bird? How many things must be presupposed for this observation to be held true and established? And so nothing of the kind occurs among the ancient writers on natural history. Jerome's memory deceived him when he wrote on Jeremiah: The writers of natural history, of beasts and birds as well as of trees and plants, the chief of whom among the Greeks are Aristotle and Theophrastus, and among us Pliny Secundus, say that this is the nature of the partridge: that it steals the eggs of another partridge, that is, another's eggs, and sits on them and broods them; and when the brood has grown up, it flies away from it and leaves the foreign parent. But the pious men too who, with him, committed this fiction to writing placed more faith than was fitting τοῖς τερατολόγοις [tois teratologois], whose aim was to hold the people's attention with these prodigies of storytelling. For, as Synesius rightly says in the Encomium of Baldness, Τὸ δὲ ῥᾷστον καταγελάσεται ὁ δῆμος· δεῖται γὰρ τερατείας. [To de rhaston katagelasetai ho dēmos; deitai gar terateias.] What is easy, the common people laugh at; for it craves marvellous narration. ¶

Some of the Greek Fathers held a different view: that a female partridge, while another partridge is away, approaches its nest and so deceives the chicks by imitating the mother's voice that they leave the nest and follow her, until the true mother returns and they regret their mistake. Chrysostom reports this only in part: Ἔθος φασὶ τοὺς πέρδικας, μετὰ τὸ ἀποτεχθῆναι τῶν ὠῶν, τοὺς νεοττοὺς τῇ φωνῇ προσκαλεῖσθαι, καὶ γυμνοῦν τὴν τεκοῦσαν [Ethos phasi tous perdikas, meta to apotechthēnai tōn ōōn, tous neottous tē phōnē proskaleisthai, kai gymnoun tēn tekousan]; They say it is the habit of partridges, after laying their eggs, to lure the chicks of others with their voice, and so rob the mother of them. But Olympiodorus says it more fully: Ὁ πέρδιξ, ὥς φασιν, ὑπερήφανος ὢν, διὰ τῆς φωνῆς τοὺς ἀλλοτρίους προσκαλεῖται νεοττοὺς, οἵτινες γνόντες ὕστερον ὅτι οὐκ εἰσὶν αὐτοῦ, καταλιμπάνουσιν αὐτόν [Ho perdix, hōs phasin, hyperēphanos ōn, dia tēs phōnēs tous allotrious proskaleitai neottous, hoitines gnontes hysteron hoti ouk eisin autou, katalimpanousin auton]. The partridge, as they say, being proud, entices the chicks of others with its voice; yet it is deserted by them once they have at last recognized that they are not its own. Since the partridge is a proud animal, if it sees nearby the nest of another partridge with chicks in it, while the father has flown off to feed, it lures the chicks to itself by imitating his voice. Taking it for their true father, they at once run to it. And it boasts of the chicks of another as if they were its own. But as soon as the true father has returned and called, the chicks recognize his voice, leave the spurious father, and go over to the genuine one. But I do not think these things truer than what preceded. For some charge partridges with lewdness, others with malice and cunning, but no one, as far as I know, with pride. Nor does it seem that the chicks of partridges could easily be deceived by an imitation of the mother's voice, unless one supposes them so blind that they recognize their mother by hearing alone and not also by sight. Yet even if this were granted, it would not bear on the passage of Jeremiah, in which דגר, as we have shown, does not mean to gather chicks, but eggs, to be col. 87 brooded. ¶

More ingenious is the view of certain Hebrews cited by Kimchi in his Lexicon: that Jeremiah was speaking only of the male partridge, which, whenever it broods eggs (and that it sometimes broods is attested by Aristotle b), Plutarch c) and Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)] d)), broods eggs that it did not itself lay, but the female did. But it is not very likely that the Prophet had this in view. For the male brooding the eggs is not peculiar to the partridge; in most birds, too, male and female sit in turn. So it is stated in the Philosopher: Ἐπωάζει δὲ τὰ πολλὰ τῶν ὀρνίθων διαδεχόμενα τὸν ἄῤῥενα τοῖς θήλεσι [Epōazei de ta polla tōn ornithōn diadechomena ton arrena tois thēlesi]. Moreover, the comparison would limp on every side if Jeremiah compared the greedy man, who seizes the goods of others, which, ill gotten, are likewise ill lost, with a male partridge brooding the eggs of its mate. For although the male broods eggs that it did not itself produce, they still cannot be called another's, since the offspring belongs as much to the father as to the mother. Nor does it do this with evil intent, like the greedy man who gapes after what belongs to others, but, as Philes says, ἐξ εὐσπλαγχνίας [ex eusplanchnias], out of a certain paternal affection and devotion towards its own. And so the matter turns out well and prosperously for it, since in this way it increases and propagates its kind. There is thus no similarity at all. Not to mention that many deny that male partridges brood eggs, according to Ulysses Aldrovandus, a recent writer, but very experienced in these matters. See him, Ornithologia book 13, ch. 17. ¶

His incommodis urgeri cum videret communem interpretationem vir, Hebraice doctissimus (4), putavit, Ieremiae verba sic posse reddi, Perdix collegit (seu II, 642 fovit ova), sed non peperit, id est, non exclusit ova. Quod perdici cur frequentius quam ulli avium accidat, ut ova foveat, quae non excludat, causae plures afferri queant. Prima, quod neque in rupibus, neque in ramis arborum, neque in culmine turrium, sed humi nidificet, loco, non solum aucupibus, sed feris etiam et reptilibus maxime obvio. Altera, quod si perdicis ova, rore cadente, vel pluvia humescant, ἐὰν μὴ πάλιν ἡ τεκοῦσα ἐπιθάλψῃ αὐτὰ ἐπελ-θοῦσα ταχέως, γίνεται ἄγονα, nisi mater statim incubans ea denuo foveat, infoecunda redduntur. Ita Aelianus libro 10. cap. 35. Hist. animal. Tertia est in maribus, qui intemperantia libidinis ova nonnunquam frangunt, ne foeminae diutius incubando detineantur. Quod praeter Aelianum e) tradunt Aristoteles f), Plinius g), Athenaeus h) et Phile i). Sed huic interpretationi, quam diximus, unum obstat, quod pariendi verbum in Ieremia non pertinet ad ἐκλέπισιν, sed ad ὠοτοκίαν, quam ab exclusione plurimum distingui sciunt omnes. Res est apertissima in Esaiae loco, quem citavimus, de serpente acontia, Ibi nidificabit acontias, et pariet, et rumpet, id est, excludet ova, quae in exclusione rumpuntur. In oviparis igitur apud Hebraeos aliud est partus, aliud exclusio. Partus est ὠοτοκία, et significatur verbis ילד et מלט, ut exclusio verbo בקע, quod rumpere est. Itaque Ieremiae locus, kore collegit, et non peperit, accipiendus est ut sonat. Ut intelligatur de ave, quae ova fovet ab altera parta. Quod naturae consulti perdici nusquam tribuunt, sed soli Patres.

Since a man most learned in Hebrew R4 saw that the common interpretation was pressed by these difficulties, he thought that the words of Jeremiah could be rendered thus: The partridge gathered (or II, 642 warmed eggs), but did not bring forth, that is, did not hatch the eggs. Several reasons could be given why it happens to the partridge more often than to any other bird that it warms eggs which it does not hatch. The first is that it nests neither on rocks, nor on the branches of trees, nor on the tops of towers, but on the ground, in a place most exposed not only to fowlers but also to wild beasts and reptiles. The second is that if the partridge's eggs become wet from falling dew or rain, ἐὰν μὴ πάλιν ἡ τεκοῦσα ἐπιθάλψῃ αὐτὰ ἐπελθοῦσα ταχέως, γίνεται ἄγονα [ean mē palin hē tekousa epithalpsē auta epelthousa tacheōs, ginetai agona], unless the mother at once sits on them and warms them again, they are rendered infertile. So Aelian, book 10, ch. 35 of the Hist. animal. The third lies in the males, which through unrestrained lust sometimes break the eggs, so that the females may not be detained too long by incubating. Besides Aelian e), this is reported by Aristotle f), Pliny g), Athenaeus h) and Philes i). But one thing stands against the interpretation we have described: the verb to bring forth in Jeremiah refers not to ἐκλέπισιν [eklepisin], but to ὠοτοκίαν [ōotokian], which everyone knows to be very different from hatching. The matter is perfectly plain in the passage of Isaiah we have cited about the serpent acontias: There the acontias will nest, and lay, and break, that is, hatch the eggs, which are broken in hatching. Among the Hebrews, therefore, in oviparous animals laying is one thing and hatching another. Laying is ὠοτοκία [ōotokia], and is expressed by the verbs ילד and מלט, as hatching is by the verb בקע, which means to break. Thus the passage of Jeremiah, the kore gathered, and did not bring forth, must be taken as it sounds, so that it is understood of a bird that warms eggs laid by another. This is something the naturalists nowhere ascribe to the partridge, but only the Fathers do. ¶

Et Gesnerus lusciniam insuper, et motacillam, et rubeculam, et ruticillam. Ita, ut hae totidem sint aves, quae fovent, quod non pepererunt. Quibus ex Ieremia addi potest avis קרא kore, ab his omnibus diversa, et scolopaci, seu rusticulae propior, ut a nobis probatum fuit. Quod a Graecis et Romanis non potuit observari, quia haec avis illis fuit ignota. Et ideo fugit Arabes; quia ejus descriptionem paucis perstringunt et levi stylo.

The curruca [warbler], in Greek ὑπολαῒς [hypolais], is a small bird in whose nest the cuckoo usually lays. The Philosopher says of the cuckoo, Hist. book 6, ch. 7: τίκτει δὲ ἐπὶ τῇ τῆς ὑπολαΐδος νεοττείᾳ, ἡ δὲ ἐκλέπει, καὶ ἐκτρέφει [tiktei de epi tē tēs hypolaidos neotteia, hē de eklepei, kai ektrephei]. Hence Theophrastus (De caus. plant. book 2, ch. 24) denied that the race of cuckoos could exist εἰ μὴ ἦν ἡ ὑπολαῒς, ἡ καὶ εἰς τὴν νεοττίαν τὰ ὠὰ τίθησιν [ei mē ēn hē hypolais, hē kai eis tēn neottian ta ōa tithēsin], if there were not the warbler, in whose nest it might lay its eggs. This is not entirely true, since besides the warbler there are also other birds in whose nest the cuckoo ὠοτοκεῖ [ōotokei]. Aristotle in the Mirabilia: Τοὺς κόκκυγάς φασι μὴ ποιεῖν νεοττίαν, ἀλλ’ ἐν ταῖς τῶν φαττῶν, καὶ ταῖς τῶν τρυγόνων ἐκτίκτειν [Tous kokkygas phasi mē poiein neottian, all’ en tais tōn phattōn, kai tais tōn trygonōn ektiktein]. Likewise Hist. book 9, ch. 29: Ἐν ἀλλοτρίαις τίκτει νεοττίαις, μάλιστα μὲν ἐν ταῖς τῶν φαβῶν, καὶ ἐν ὑπολαΐδος, καὶ κορύδου χαμαὶ, ἐπὶ δένδρου ἐν τῇ τῆς χλωρίδος καλουμένης νεοττίᾳ [En allotriais tiktei neottiais, malista men en tais tōn phabōn, kai en hypolaidos, kai korydou chamai, epi dendrou en tē tēs chlōridos kaloumenēs neottia]. Antigonus, Mirab. ch. 109: Κόκκυγος δὲ νεοττοὺς οὐδένα ἑωρακέναι. Τίκτειν γὰρ αὐτὸν, οὐκ ἐν τῇ ἰδίᾳ νεοττίᾳ, ἀλλ’ εἰς τὰς τῶν ὀρνιθίων, ἢ εἰς τὰς τῶν φαβῶν, ἢ ὑποδυσιδέων [Kokkygos de neottous oudena heōrakenai. Tiktein gar auton, ouk en tē idia neottia, all’ eis tas tōn ornithiōn, ē eis tas tōn phabōn, ē hypodysideōn] (read ὑπολαΐδων [hypolaidōn]) εἰσπετόμενον [eispetomenon]. Aelian, Hist. book 3, ch. 30: Οὐ πάντων δὲ ὀρνίθων καλίαις ἐπιπηδᾷ οὗτός γε, ἀλλὰ κορύδου, καὶ φάττης, καὶ χλωρίδος, καὶ πάππου [Ou pantōn de ornithōn kaliais epipēda houtos ge, alla korydou, kai phattēs, kai chlōridos, kai pappou]. You see, reader, that besides the warbler, the wood pigeon, the turtle dove, II, 644 the lark, the chloris and the pappus are also counted among the birds whose nests the cuckoo invades. Some add the sparrow. Isidore, book 12, ch. 7, on the cuckoo: It eats the sparrow's eggs found in the nest and puts its own in their place, which the sparrow takes up, incubates and rears. And Gesner adds the nightingale as well, and the wagtail, the robin and the redstart. So these are so many birds that brood what they did not lay. To them may be added from Jeremiah the bird קרא kore, different from all these and closer to the σκολόπαξ [skolopax] or woodcock (rusticula), as we have proved. This could not be observed by the Greeks and Romans, because this bird was unknown to them. And it escaped the Arabs too, because they touch on its description only briefly and lightly. ¶

At, inquies, si קרא kore est σκολόπακος, aut rusticulae species, aut avis huic simillima, quorsum Graeci, Romani, Syri, Arabes, Hebraei interpretes tanto consensu perdicem reddunt? Duo possunt regeri; 1. Graecos, qui his col. 89 omnibus praeiverunt, promiscue sumpsisse קרא kore, per Koph, et כרא cara, per Caph: quorum hoc perdicem sonat hodieque apud Arabes. Ita in Giggeio scribitur, Alii كري malunt, et كروان Alcamus, الكروان الحجل والقبج والانثي الكروانة ويقال للذكر الكري Alcarvvan, perdix mas, et perdix in genere, et Foeminium alcarvana. Sed et mas dicitur alcara (5) Ezech. VII, 25. קפדה־בא, excidium venit a Graecis redditum ἐξιλασμὸς ἥξει, propitiatio veniet, quasi קפדה per Koph et Daleth idem esset, quod כפרה per Caph et Res. Ita sumi potuit קרא per Koph pro כרא. 2. Praeterea scolopacem perdicis speciem esse, nonnulli putant; quia, licet forma et specie a perdice non parum differat, tamen sapore accedit. Inde in Martiali rusticula ita se jactat:

But, you will say, if קרא kore is a species of σκολόπαξ [skolopax] or woodcock, or a bird very similar to it, why do the Greek, Latin, Syriac, Arabic and Hebrew translators render it partridge with such agreement? Two answers can be given. 1. The Greeks, who preceded all these, col. 89 took קרא kore, with Koph, and כרא cara, with Caph, indiscriminately; and the latter means partridge among the Arabs even today. So it is written in Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)]; others prefer كري [karī], and كروان [karawān]. Alcamus: الكروان الحجل والقبج والانثي الكروانة ويقال للذكر الكري [al-karawān al-ḥajal wa-l-qabj wa-l-unthī al-karawāna wa-yuqāl li-l-dhakar al-karī] Alcarvvan, the male partridge, and the partridge in general; and the female alcarvana. But the male is also called alcara R5. In Ezek 7:25. קפדה־בא, destruction comes, was rendered by the Greeks ἐξιλασμὸς ἥξει [exilasmos hēxei], propitiation will come, as if קפדה with Koph and Daleth were the same as כפרה with Caph and Resh. In the same way קרא with Koph could have been taken for כרא. 2. Moreover, some think the σκολόπαξ [skolopax] is a species of partridge, because, although it differs not a little from the partridge in form and appearance, it comes close to it in taste. Hence in Martial the woodcock boasts thus: ¶

Rustica sim, an perdix? Quid refert, si sapor idem est?

Am I a woodcock or a partridge? What does it matter, if the taste is the same? ¶

Atque id ipsum supplent, quotquot scolopacem rusticam, aut rusticulam appellant. Ut Plinius libri 10. cap. 38. Currunt (aliquae aves) ut perdices, rusticulae. Rusticulam enim aut rusticam dicunt pro perdice rustica. Quod ex Graecis sumptum, quibus ἡ ἄγρια πέρδιξ idem est, quod ὁ σκολόπαξ. Ita definit Herodianus. Potuit igitur πέρδιξ a Graecis interpretibus pro scolopace poni. Maxime, cum Graecum σκολόπαξ sit parum in usu, nec occurrat uspiam, quam in Aristotelis duobus locis q), unde ab Hesychio et Herodiano sumptum. Itaque II, 645 si pro ἐφώνησε πέρδιξ Graeci scripsissent ἐφώνησε σκολόπαξ, in hac voce multi haesissent, ejus novitate perculsi (6).

And this is confirmed by all who call the σκολόπαξ [skolopax] rustica or rusticula, as Pliny, book 10, ch. 38: Some (birds) run, like partridges and woodcocks. For they say rusticula or rustica for perdix rustica [wild partridge]. This is taken from the Greeks, for whom ἡ ἄγρια πέρδιξ [hē agria perdix] is the same as ὁ σκολόπαξ [ho skolopax]. So Herodian defines it. Therefore πέρδιξ [perdix] could have been put by the Greek translators for the skolopax; especially since the Greek σκολόπαξ [skolopax] is little used and occurs nowhere except in two passages of Aristotle q), from which it was taken by Hesychius and Herodian. Hence, II, 645 if instead of ἐφώνησε πέρδιξ [ephōnēse perdix] the Greeks had written ἐφώνησε σκολόπαξ [ephōnēse skolopax], many would have stumbled at this word, struck by its novelty R6. ¶
