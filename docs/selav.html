Varia Coturnicum nomina, שלו, سلوي, سماني, ፈርፈርት et variae species juxta Hebraeos, 1. שיכלי, Συκαλὶς, 2. קכלי, Κίχλη. 3. פסיוני, Phasianus. 4. שליו.

The various names of the quail, שלו, سلوي [salwā], سماني [sumānā], ፈርፈርት [farəfarət] and the various kinds according to the Hebrews: 1. שיכלי, Συκαλὶς [Sykalis], 2. קכלי, Κίχλη [Kichlē]. 3. פסיוני, the pheasant. 4. שליו. ¶

De coturnicibus, quas Israelitis, in deserto fame enectis, liberaliter indulsit Deus, ita Moses Exod. XVI, 13. Et vespere contigit, ut adscendentes coturnices castra tegerent. Et Num. XI, 31. Tum ventus profectus a Domino abripuit coturnices a mari, et in castra dispersit, quasi itinere unius diei hinc, et quasi iti- col. 92 nere unius diei illinc circa castra, idque ad duos cubitos in superficie terrae. 32. Surgensque populus toto die illo, et tota nocte illa, et toto die postero collegerunt coturnices. Qui minimum, decem coros collegit, et eas circa castra late expandebant. 33. Sed cum caro adhuc esset inter dentes nondum conscissa, ira Domini in populum accensa est; et populum percussit Dominus plaga valde magna. 34. Quamobrem vocaverunt nomen loci illius kibroth thaava, (id est, sepulchra concupiscentiae) quia ibi sepeliverant populum eorum, qui concupiverant. David etiam Psal. LXXVIII, 26. Impulit Eurum in coelos, et virtute sua austrum induxit. 27. Et carnem depluit super eos tanquam pulverem, et volucres alatas tanquam arenam maris. 28. Quas in media eorum castra conjecit, circa illorum tabernacula. 29. Et ad satietatem usque comederunt, ab illo, quae concupiverant, adepti. 30. Nondum cupiditatem suam deposuerant, et adhuc in ore cibum habebant. 31. Cum eos invadens ira Dei pinguissimos eorum interfecit, et Israelis lectissimos prostravit. Et Psal. CV, 40. Peientibus coturnices invexit, et pane coelorum satiavit eos.

Of the quails which God generously granted to the Israelites when they were perishing of hunger in the desert, Moses writes thus, Exod 16:13: And it came to pass in the evening that quails came up and covered the camp. And Num 11:31: Then a wind going forth from the Lord swept quails from the sea and scattered them over the camp, about a day's journey on this side and about a day's journey on that side, round about the camp, and that to two cubits above the face of the earth. 32. And the people rose up all that day, and all that night, and all the next day, and gathered the quails. He who gathered least gathered ten cors, and they spread them out widely round about the camp. 33. But while the flesh was still between their teeth, not yet chewed, the wrath of the Lord was kindled against the people, and the Lord smote the people with a very great plague. 34. Therefore they called the name of that place kibroth thaava (that is, graves of craving), because there they had buried the people of those who had craved. David also, Ps 78:26: He drove the east wind into the heavens, and by his power he brought in the south wind. 27. And he rained flesh upon them as dust, and winged fowl as the sand of the sea. 28. These he cast into the midst of their camp, round about their tents. 29. And they ate until they were filled, having received from him what they had craved. 30. They had not yet put away their craving, and the food was still in their mouth, 31. when the wrath of God came upon them and slew the fattest of them, and laid low the choicest of Israel. And Ps 105:40: When they asked, he brought quails, and satisfied them with the bread of heaven. ¶

Hebraeum coturnicis nomen est שלו selau; Chaldaicum quoque סליו selau; Syriacum ܣܠܘܝ salvai, et Arabicum سلوي iisdem literis, quamvis efferant selvva. Unde Alcoranus Azoara 17. cui titulus اعراف seu Limbi انزلنا عليهم المن والسلوي Super eos manna demisimus, et coturnices. Nonnulli mel explicant, quia vox Arabica سلوي selvva tam mel significat, quam coturnices. Quomodo si sumeretur, من manna, et سلوي selvva essent synonyma. Manna enim est mel aerium. Unde est, quod a medicorum filiis vocatur ἀερόμελι, et ab Amynta libro 1. τῶν τῆς Ἀσίας σταθμῶν apud Athenaeum lib. 11. Sed melius Alkazuinius et Ibnobitar, qui asserunt انه السماني eam esse coturnicem. Proprie שלה est pacate vivere, deinde etiam abundare. Unde est, quod a Graecis saepe redditur εὐθηνεῖν, et שליה εὐθηνία. Coturnices igitur a copia dici verissimile est, cum in calidis regionibus vix ulla sit fertilior avis, ut patebit ex sequentibus (1). Talmudicis enim פרפרות parperoth, vel pharpheroth, ἀρχαϊκῶς sunt cupediae quaevis ex rebus minutis et delicatis, cujusmodi afferri solebant, vel statim initio, vel in fine convivii. Unde in tractatu Berachoth cap. 6. fol. 42. a. ברך על הפרפרות שלפני המזון פטר את הפרפרות שלאחר המזון, Qui benedictionem dicit super pharpheroth (id est, cupedias), quae afferuntur ante cibum, liberas facit (a benedictione) pharpheroth, quae post cibum afferuntur. Quia utrisque sufficit una benedictio. Et ibi Glossa, ומביאין לפני הם פרפרות להמשיך אכילה כגון פרגיות ודגים ואח״כ מביאין השלחן Et iis offerebant pharpheroth (seu cupedias) ad irritandam orexin, ut aviculas et pisces: deinde mensam afferebant, id est, consuetos cibos.

The Hebrew name of the quail is שלו selau; the Chaldee is likewise סליו selau; the Syriac ܣܠܘܝ [סלוי] salvai; and the Arabic سلوي [salwā] in the same letters, although they pronounce it selvva. Hence the Qur'an, Azoara 17, entitled اعراف [Aʿrāf] or The Limbo: انزلنا عليهم المن والسلوي [anzalnā ʿalayhim al-manna wa-l-salwā] We sent down upon them manna and quails. Some explain it as honey, because the Arabic word سلوي [salwā] selvva signifies honey as well as quails. Taken in that way, من [mann] manna and سلوي [salwā] selvva would be synonyms. For manna is aerial honey. That is why it is called ἀερόμελι [aeromeli] by the sons of the physicians, and by Amyntas in book 1 of τῶν τῆς Ἀσίας σταθμῶν [tōn tēs Asias stathmōn], cited by Athenaeus, book 11. But Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] and Ibnobitar do better, who assert انه السماني [annahu al-sumānā] that it is the quail. And Alachphas: هو طاير الذي انزله الله تعالي علي بني اسرايل علي القول المشهور وغلط [huwa ṭāʾir alladhī anzalahu Allāh taʿālā ʿalā banī Isrāʾīl ʿalā al-qawl al-mashhūr wa-ghalaṭa] II, 649 الهزلي فظنه الغسل [al-Hudhalī fa-ẓannahu al-ghasl] It is the bird which God Most High sent down upon the children of Israel, according to the received opinion. So Alhazelius erred, who thought it was honey. There are some among the Arabs who write that the quail is called سلوي [salwā] لانه يسلي الانسان عن ساير الادام [li-annahu yuslī al-insān ʿan sāʾir al-idām] because it makes a man forget other foods. But the Hebrew is שלו selau, which I think should be referred to the verb שלה sala, although the Masoretes wrote the latter with right-hand Sin and the former with left-hand, διακριτικῶς [diakritikōs]. Properly שלה means to live in peace, and then also to abound. Hence it is often rendered by the Greeks εὐθηνεῖν [euthēnein], and שליה εὐθηνία [euthēnia]. It is therefore most likely that quails are named from abundance, since in warm regions scarcely any bird is more prolific, as will appear from what follows R1. Another name of the quail among the Arabs, سماني [sumānā] II, 650 sumana, is agreed to be derived from fatness, because it is a very fat bird. Hence R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Exod. XVI.: השלו מין עוף ושמן מאד, The quail, a species of bird which is very fat. And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon: הוא שמן מאד, it is very fat. In the Ethiopic Psalter, Ps 104:38, ፈርፈርት፡ [farəfarət] phorphorath is the quail, the name of the genus having been transferred, as it seems, to the species R2. col. 93 For among the Talmudists פרפרות parperoth or pharpheroth, ἀρχαϊκῶς [archaikōs], are any delicacies made of small and dainty things, such as used to be served either right at the beginning or at the end of a banquet. Hence in the tractate Berachoth, ch. 6, fol. 42a: ברך על הפרפרות שלפני המזון פטר את הפרפרות שלאחר המזון, He who says the blessing over the pharpheroth (that is, delicacies) that are served before the meal exempts (from the blessing) the pharpheroth that are served after the meal. For one blessing suffices for both. And there the Gloss: ומביאין לפני הם פרפרות להמשיך אכילה כגון פרגיות ודגים ואח״כ מביאין השלחן And they served them pharpheroth (or delicacies) to whet the appetite, such as small birds and fish; then they brought the table, that is, the usual foods. ¶

Augustine on Exodus, Question 62: To them the ortygometra was given, that is, birds which many Latin translators have rendered quails, although the ortygometra is another kind of bird, though not altogether unlike quails. And long before, the Philosopher, Hist. book 8, ch. 12: Ὅταν δ' ἐντεῦθεν ἀπάρωσιν (οἱ ὄρτυγες) ἥ τε γλωττὶς συναπαίρει, καὶ ἡ ὀρτυγομήτρα, καὶ ὁ ὠτὸς, καὶ ὁ κύγχραμος [Hotan d' enteuthen aparōsin (hoi ortyges) hē te glōttis synapairei, kai hē ortygomētra, kai ho ōtos, kai ho kynchramos]· When they (the quails) depart from here, the lingulaca departs together with them, and the ortygometra, and the otus, and the cynchramus. From these words it is evident that the ortygometra, like the lingulaca, the otus and the cynchramus, is distinguished from the quail. Accordingly, further on in the same place there follows: Ἡ δὲ ὀρτυγομήτρα παραπλήσιος τὴν μορφὴν τοῖς λιμναίοις ἐστὶ [Hē de ortygomētra paraplēsios tēn morphēn tois limnaiois esti]· The ortygometra is similar in form to the marsh birds. And in Athenaeus, book 9, the same bird is described by Alexander of Myndus as τὸ μέγεθος ἡ λίκη τρυγὼν, σκέλη δὲ μακρὰ, δυσθαλπὴς καὶ δειλὴ [to megethos hē likē trygōn, skelē de makra, dysthalpēs kai deilē], of the size of a turtle-dove, with long legs, sensitive to cold and timid. Δυσθαλὴς [Dysthalēs], that is, ill-flourishing, is written there without any sense; but I have emended it to δυσθαλπής [dysthalpēs]. In the opinion of these authors, then, it is plain that the ortygometra differs in species from the quail R3. For Hesychius, however, ὀρτυγομήτρα [ortygomētra] is nothing other than ὄρτυξ ὑπερμεγέθης [ortyx hypermegethēs], a very large quail, as if the mother or matrix of the others. So among cicadas there is the τεττιγομήτρα [tettigomētra], among sea-urchins the ἐχινομήτρα [echinomētra], among worms the ἰουλομήτρα [ioulomētra] (in Hesychius written ἰουλυμήτρα [ioulymētra]), among the species of lotus Pliny's λωτομήτρα [lōtomētra], and among cities and villages μητρόπολις [mētropolis] and μητροκωμία [mētrokōmia]. For Jansenius [=Cornelius Jansen of Ghent (d. 1576), commentary on Wisdom], on the book of Wisdom, ch. 16, gravely errs in holding that the ortygometra is so called as if μέτρον τῆς ὄρτυγος [metron tēs ortygos], as though it were the measure and regulator of the other quails. For the word is not written with ε, ὀρτυγόμετρα [ortygometra], but with η, ὀρτυγομήτρα [ortygomētra], so that it plainly means what we said: mother or matrix of quails. And even from this you may infer that the ortygometra differs from the common quail only in size. Therefore many use ὀρτυγομήτρα [ortygomētra] and coturnix [quail] indiscriminately. Among them is Josephus, who instead of ortygometras has simply ὀρτύγων πλῆθος [ortygōn plēthos], an abundance of quails, book 3, ch. 1. And again in ch. 12: Πίμπλαται τὸ στρατόπεδον ὀρτύγων ἅπαν [Pimplatai to stratopedon ortygōn hapan], The whole camp is filled with quails. Jerome too, who for the word ortygometra, which he retained in the Psalms and in the book of Wisdom, has coturnices [quails] in the Law. And the author of the Mirabilia, among the works of Augustine, book 1, ch. 27, II, 652 plainly treats these as synonyms when he writes of this miracle: A wind, going forth from the Lord, seized quails across the sea and carried them to the places of the camp. There remains the pheasant, which in former times seems to have been the very summit of delicacies. Thus, according to Athenaeus, book 14, Ptolemy Physcon, although he kept pheasants in great numbers in Egypt, οὐδὲ φασιανικοῦ ὄρνιθός ποτε γεύσασθαι ὡμολόγησεν, ἀλλ᾽ ὥσπερ τι κειμήλιον ἀνακείμενον εἶχε τοὺς δὲ τοὺς ὄρνιθας [oude phasianikou ornithos pote geusasthai hōmologēsen, all᾽ hōsper ti keimēlion anakeimenon eiche tous de tous ornithas], declared that he had never tasted a pheasant, but kept these birds as a stored-up treasure. The Jews living in Arabia today also think that the שלוים are locusts, and Niebuhr [=Carsten Niebuhr, Beschreibung von Arabien (1772)] himself agrees with them, Beschreib. von Arab. p. 172. But the strongest objection is that in the Arabic, Syriac and Chaldee dialects this word without any doubt means quails. Nor is שלו found among the names of locusts in Lev 11:22. More has been noted by I. E. Faber [=J. E. Faber, notes to the German edition of Harmer's Observations (Beobachtungen über den Orient, 1772–79)], ad Harmar. 2, p. 427, and Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde] in the Vermischten Sammlungen vol. 4, ch. 6, p. 58. — That the שלוים are flying fish, met with in the Red Sea, which the inhabitants of those lands are also accustomed to eat (cf. Hasselquist [=Fredrik Hasselquist, Iter Palaestinum (1757)] p. 255; Niebuhr Beschreib. von Arab. p. 176; Forsskål [=Peter Forsskål, Descriptiones animalium (1775)] Descriptt. Animal. p. 16), was formerly the conjecture of OL. RUDBECK (Ichthyol. Bibl. Specim. I.), which I. D. Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer] elaborated further in his Commentat. super lege Mosaica Deut 22:6–7. §9, and in the Addition to §9, in Syntagm. Commentatt. vol. 2, p. 104 and 115; afterwards, however, he himself rejected it, and rightly so, since in the Red Sea there is no such abundance of flying fish that many thousands of people could be fed with them daily. Nor can these fish rise more than three or four feet above the sea. In addition, flying fish are not an ordinary food; rather, dried, they serve as a medicine for strengthening the stomach. Cf. Niebuhr p. 177. For other points see Faber and Oedmann, loc. cit. — The Seleucid birds [Turdus Seleucis Forsskål vol. 6 a, p. 5, no. 16, Arabic سمرمر [samarmar], Turdus roseus Linnaeus 2, p. 819, no. 15, 13th ed.; Oedmann 2, ch. 6, p. 103], whose coming (as Pliny reports, Hist. Nat. 10, ch. 26, sect. 39) the inhabitants of Mount Casius obtain by prayer from Jupiter when locusts are ravaging their crops; it has not been discovered where they come from or where they go, and they are never seen except when their protection is needed, were suspected by II, 650 HERMANN VON DER HARDT to be the same as the שלוים; RATHLEF also fought for this opinion in his Append. Acridotheol., but with futile arguments, which I. D. Michaelis long ago refuted in the commentary cited, p. 104 ff. — There is a bird, common in Syria, Arabia and Egypt, of the size of a partridge or turtle-dove, of the grouse family, with hard and dry flesh, which the Arabs, from the call it utters, call قَطًّا [qaṭṭan] Katta (as Hasselquist and Russell [=Alexander Russell, The Natural History of Aleppo (1756)] write it) (see Golius's [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)] Lexic. p. 1934, where a description of this bird is also found); in Linnaeus it is Tetrao supra varius; rectricibus duabus intermediis duplo longioribus subulatis, see Syst. Nat. vol. 1, part 2, p. 754, no. 11, under the heading Alchata, where the synonyms and those who have treated of it are also listed. That this bird is the שלו of the Hebrews is so certain to Hasselquist that in his Travels p. 333, after describing it fairly accurately under the name Tetrao Israelitarum, he uses these words: this, if any, is the bird taken as food by the Israelites in the desert, since it is common in the places through which they wandered. But this very point holds also of the quail, and what is read of the שלוים in Moses does not fit any other bird, as we shall see in the following Chapter. — I. E. Faber, ad Harmar. 2, p. 442, thinks the Arabs' Katta is the same as the quail, though these birds are carefully distinguished by all who have written on the natural history of birds.
