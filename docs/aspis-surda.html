De Aspide surda, quae aurem obturare legitur, ne audiat vocem incantantium, Psal. LVIII, 5. 6.

On the deaf asp, which is said to stop its ear so as not to hear the voice of the charmers, Ps 58:5–6. ¶

Superest, ut de aspidum, adeoque serpentum omnium, incantatione dicamus col. 385 in verba Psaltis πολυθρύλλητα, Psal. LVIII, 5. 6. Venenum est ipsis simile veneno serpentis: sicut aspidis surdae, obturantis aurem suam, ne mussitantium vocem audiat, et divini incantationum periti. In quibus primo quaeritur, quorsum aspis surda dicatur. Respondet Bustamantinus libri tertii De Scripturae animantibus capite undecimo, surdam ideo appellari, quod, ut acutissimi visus, ita sit auditus hebetissimi. Sed consulti naturae plane contra statuunt, aspidem auditu multo plus valere, quam visu. Nicander in Theriacis versu 162.

It remains for us to speak of the charming of asps, and indeed of all serpents, col. 385 on the much-discussed (πολυθρύλλητα [polythryllēta]) words of the Psalmist, Ps 58:5–6. Their poison is like the poison of a serpent: like that of the deaf asp, which stops its ear so as not to hear the voice of the whisperers, and of the diviner skilled in incantations. Here the first question is why the asp is called deaf. Bustamantinus, in book 3, chapter 11 of De Scripturae animantibus, answers that it is called deaf because, as its sight is very sharp, so its hearing is very dull. But those versed in nature hold exactly the opposite: that the asp is much stronger in hearing than in sight. Nicander, in the Theriaca, line 162: ¶

— — — — ὑπναλέοις δὲ
Αἰὲν ἐπιλλίζουσα φαείνεται ἐνδυκὲς ὄσσοις.
Ἀλλ᾽ ὅταν ἢ δοῦπον νέον οὔασιν, ἠέ τιν᾽ αὐδὴν
Ἀθρήσῃ, νωθρὸν μὲν ἀπὸ ῥεθέως βάλεν ὕπνον.

[— — — — hypnaleois de / Aien epillizousa phaenetai endykes ossois. / All᾽ hotan ē doupon neon ousin, ēe tin᾽ audēn / Athrēsē, nōthron men apo rhetheōs balen hypnon.] ¶

— — — — somnoque gravatis

Plinius libri octavi capite vigesimo tertio, Iam primum hebetes oculos huic malo dedit (rerum natura), eosque non in fronte ex adverso cernere, sed in temporibus: itaque excitatur, sed saepius auditu, quam visu. Frustra regerit Bustamantinus, haec de aspide chersaea dici, non de ptya et chelidonia. Nam de toto genere aspidum Nicander et Plinius agunt. Neque vero quisquam alius a ptyis et chelidoniis chersaeas aspides ita distinxit, ut illas visu, has auditu praepollere somniaret. Omnibus enim aspidibus eadem est oculorum hebetudo, et aurium acrimonia. Mercurialis, Omnibus aspidibus haec communia sunt, visus imbecillus, incessus tardus, auditus subtilis (1).

Pliny, book 8, chapter 23: First of all (nature) gave this pest dull eyes, and made them see not forwards from the front but from the temples; and so it is roused more often by hearing than by sight. In vain does Bustamantinus retort that this is said of the land asp, not of the ptyas and the chelidonia. For Nicander and Pliny treat the whole genus of asps. Nor has anyone else distinguished land asps from ptyades and chelidoniae in such a way as to dream that the latter excel in sight and the former in hearing. For all asps have the same dullness of eyes and sharpness of ears. Mercurialis: Common to all asps are these: weak sight, slow gait, keen hearing R1. ¶

III, 162 Itaque alii surdam aspidem dici volunt non natura, sed consilio; quia, ut addit Psaltes, obturat aures, ne vocem incantatoris audiat. Theodoretus, Προσ-φυῶς αὐτὸν ἀσπίδι παρέβαλλεν, οὐ κωφευούσῃ κατὰ φύσιν, ἀλλὰ καὶ βυούσῃ τὰ ὦτα, καὶ τῆς ἐπῳδῆς ἀποθουμένῃ τὴν προσβολήν· Apposite illum aspidi comparavit, non natura surdae, sed aures obstruenti, ut incantationis vim prohibeat. Quomodo etiam Suidas in Κωφῆς. Et Augustinus, „Sicut aspidis sur-„dae] Unde surdae? unde „Et obturantis aures suas?] ideo surdae, quia obturat aures suas. Mediam viam insistit R. Selomo, scribens, aspidem in senectute una aure obsurdescere, alteram opplere pulvere, ne incantatoris vocem audiat, quod merum commentum est. Sed neque id satis certum, quod, ut ex vulgi rumoribus acceptum, referunt Augustinus, Cassiodorus, Beda, Isidorus, alii, aspidem, cum coeperit pati incantatorem, unam aurem ad terram col. 386 applicare, alteram obturare cauda, ne via pateat incantationi.

III, 162 Others therefore hold that the asp is called deaf not by nature but by design, because, as the Psalmist adds, it stops its ears so as not to hear the voice of the charmer. Theodoret: Προσφυῶς αὐτὸν ἀσπίδι παρέβαλλεν, οὐ κωφευούσῃ κατὰ φύσιν, ἀλλὰ καὶ βυούσῃ τὰ ὦτα, καὶ τῆς ἐπῳδῆς ἀποθουμένῃ τὴν προσβολήν [Prosphyōs auton aspidi pareballen, ou kōpheuousē kata physin, alla kai byousē ta ōta, kai tēs epōdēs apothoumenē tēn prosbolēn]; He aptly compared him to an asp, not deaf by nature, but stopping its ears so as to ward off the force of the incantation. So also Suidas under Κωφῆς [Kōphēs]. And Augustine: “Like the deaf asp” Why deaf? Whence “And stopping its ears?” It is deaf because it stops its ears. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] takes a middle course, writing that the asp in old age grows deaf in one ear and fills the other with dust so as not to hear the charmer's voice, which is a pure fiction. But neither is it certain enough what Augustine, Cassiodorus, Bede, Isidore and others report, as taken from popular rumor: that the asp, when it begins to suffer the charmer, presses one ear to the ground col. 386 and stops the other with its tail, so that no way lies open to the incantation. ¶

Verum, ut tota res clarior fiat, necesse habeo pauca praefari de serpentum incantatione, quos censet Augustinus a) ideo moveri magis hominum carminibus, quam ullum aliud genus animantium, ut hoc esset argumento, primum hominem a diabolo fuisse per serpentem in Paradiso seductum. Itaque integras gentes videas hoc fascini genere infames. In his Marsi et Psylli, quod omnes sciunt. Sed et idem praestare Aegyptios μαγείᾳ τινὶ ἐπιχωρίῳ, magica quadam ratione, illi genti propria, in Aeliano legas Historiae libri sexti capite trigesimo tertio. Et de Nigritis Cadamustus, Sunt etiam fascinatores, id est, incantatores eximii. Fascinant praesertim serpentes. Et de Peruanis in Orbe Novo Spitillus in Collectaneis: Puer festi tempore coronatus, altera manu telo instructa, altera vipera viva dependente, positam in loco quodam ornatissimo sedem occupat. Quae ideo fieri quidam dictitant, quia sunt edentuli, et dentes, quos parvos habent, iis facile avelluntur. But to make the whole matter clearer, I must say a few things first about the charming of serpents. Augustine a) holds that serpents are moved by the songs of men more than any other kind of animal, so that this would be a proof that the first man was seduced by the devil through the serpent in Paradise. And so you may see whole nations notorious for this kind of enchantment. Among them are the Marsi and the Psylli, as everyone knows. But you may also read in Aelian, History book 6, chapter 33, that the Egyptians do the same μαγείᾳ τινὶ ἐπιχωρίῳ [mageia tini epichōriō], by a certain magical art peculiar to that nation. And the Nubian Geographer, first Clime, part 7, reports this very thing of the people of Melinde: يزعمون انهم يسحرون الحيوان الضار حتي لا يضر الا لمن ارادوا ضره [yazʿumūn annahum yasḥarūn al-ḥayawān al-ḍārr ḥattā lā yaḍurr illā li-man arādū ḍarrahu] They are said to charm noxious animals so that they harm no one except whom they themselves wish. And Cadamosto [=Alvise Cadamosto, Navigazioni (1455-56)] of the Negroes: There are also enchanters, that is, outstanding charmers. They enchant serpents above all. And Spitillus in his Collectanea of the Peruvians in the New World: At festival time a boy, crowned, with a spear in one hand and a live viper hanging from the other, takes a seat placed in a very richly adorned spot. From this it is easily inferred that these are arts of the demon, since he is not bitten by the viper. And Teixeira [=Pedro Teixeira, Relaciones (1610)] of the Indians, History of Persia book 1, chapter 29: Los vemos comunmente en la India adò suelen los gentiles traher culebras encantadas, algunes muy grandes, y terribles, por las calles, y casas, haziendo las baylar al son de una flauta, y las rebuelven al cuello, y hazen con ellas otros semejantes ademanes, tratando las con las manos sin III, 163 offensa suya. Y aunque algunos los attribuen a no tener dientes, porque dizen se los sacan siendo chiquitas. La experienzia muestra lo contrario, puez siendo algunas vezes ensannadas, o no tan ligadas con el encanto como convenia, se ha visto, que hazian grande estrago en los aspectantes. In Latin this runs: We have often seen this in India, where the heathen are accustomed to lead charmed serpents, some of them very large and terrible, through the streets and houses; they handle them unharmed, make them dance to the sound of a pipe, twist them round the neck, and perform other gestures of this kind. Some keep saying this happens because they are toothless, and that their teeth, which are small, are easily pulled out. But those who think so are refuted by experience, since these same serpents, when provoked and not bound by the charm as firmly as they should be, have sometimes been seen to cause great slaughter among the spectators R2. ¶

To this very kind of magic Plato alludes in these words in book 2 of the Republic: Θρασύμαχος φαίνεται ὑπό σου ὥσπερ ὄφις κηληθῆναι [Thrasymachos phainetai hypo sou hōsper ophis kēlēthēnai]· Thrasymachus seems to have been charmed by you like a serpent. But whoever wants something more explicit should read, at the end of the Mirabilia attributed to Aristotle [ch. 162], the long story of a serpent called sacred, killed by the spells of a Thessalian witch. Of the same kind is the report that the Indian ἐπῳδοὺς [epōdous], charmers, cure the bites of serpents, as Nearchus, the companion of Alexander, relates, and by flute-players, as Theophrastus relates: the former in Strabo, book 15; the latter in Gellius, book 4, chapter 13. Now, to refute the two objections brought: to the first we answer that the hydrus, the asp, the cerastes and the basilisk col. 394 can be restrained by the devil as easily as any other serpent, provided God permits it; but that God does not will that everything in the world be allowed to the devil. For however proudly that impure spirit boasts g) that all things have been delivered to him, yet God has said to him the very thing he said to the sea h): Thus far shalt thou come, and no further. Hence it is that in Egypt the magicians, who had produced blood, serpents and frogs in appearance, are stuck when it came to the lice i), since God did not permit them to go on mocking Pharaoh with their illusions. And in the Gospel k) the demons enter the swine of the Gergesenes only with Christ's leave. To the second we answer that Lucian's Philopseudes is objected in vain, since by its very title it professes to lie. In vain also the Marsi and Psylli: what is reported of them, if true, ought to be attributed to nature rather than to magical power. I see that most of the ancients held this view. Pliny of the Psylli, book 7, ch. 2: In their body there was an innate poison deadly to serpents, so that by its smell they put them to sleep. And a little after: A similar thing persists in Italy in the people of the Marsi, whom they say are descended from a son of Circe, and that for this reason this natural power is in them. And Solinus: That the people of the Marsi is unharmed by serpents is no wonder. They derive their descent from a son of Circe, and know that from their ancestral power the service of poisons is owed to them; therefore they despise poisons. And elsewhere: Beyond the Garamantes were the Psylli, fortified against noxious poison by an incredible firmness of body. They alone did not perish from the bites of snakes, and, though attacked by a lethal tooth, remained in unimpaired health. Around Parium on the Hellespont the same power was inborn in the Ophiogenes, of whom, after Crates of Pergamum, Varro, Strabo, Pliny, Priscian, etc. have written. What of the fact that Celsus, book 5, ch. 27, attributes nothing whatever to the Psylli beyond the common run of men, except boldness, confirmed by practice itself? Praeterea, quamvis Arabes serpentum alios posse incantari scripserint, alios ab incantatione esse prorsus immunes, quos surdos vocant, Davidis tamen, Solomonis et Ieremiae loca eo referre necesse non est. Neque enim Sancti Prophetae serpentes uspiam ita distinguunt. Sed ex illorum verbis hoc solum elicias, saepe magos id frustra conari, sive aspides, sive regulos, sive aliud quodvis serpentis genus cantu suo delinire et demulcere conentur. Itaque, ut his Scripturae locis sua constet veritas, non opus est sibi fingere, quasdam esse serpentum species, in quas magorum carmina nihil possint. Quippe in eadem serpentis specie potest diversus esse successus, prout varie Deus diabolo habenas aut adducit, aut remittit. Nec daemon paredrus, a quo pendet incantationis tota vis, ad magi nutum semper se accommodat. Unde est, quod illius conatus non semper voto respondet.

Moreover, although the Arabs have written that some serpents can be charmed, while others, which they call deaf, are wholly immune to incantation, it is nevertheless not necessary to refer the passages of David, Solomon and Jeremiah to this. For the holy Prophets nowhere distinguish serpents in this way. From their words you may gather only this: that magicians often attempt it in vain, whether they try to soothe and calm with their chant asps, or reguli, or any other kind of serpent. Therefore, for these passages of Scripture to hold true, there is no need to imagine that there are certain species of serpents against which the magicians' spells have no power. For in the same species of serpent the outcome can differ, according as God variously draws in or slackens the reins on the devil. Nor does the familiar demon, on whom the whole power of the incantation depends, always accommodate himself to the magician's nod. Hence it is that the magician's attempt does not always answer to his wish. ¶
