III, 326 CAP. VII.

III, 326 CHAPTER 7. ¶

Iohannem, Baptistam, veras locustas habuisse pro cibo.

That John the Baptist had real locusts for food. ¶

Locustis patuit hactenus vix ullum esse animal humano generi perniciosius. col. 488 Tamen his quoque suus usus est, quem alii multis explicant. Nobis docere sufficiet, locustas non paucis gentibus in cibum cessisse jam olim, et cedere hodieque, in verba de Iohanne, baptista, πολυθρύλλητα Matth. III, 4. Ἡ δὲ τρο-φὴ αὐτοῦ ἦν ἀκρίδες, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον. Et Marc. I, 6. Ἐσθίων ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον. Quae verba cum clare et aperte doceant, locustas et mel sylvestre pro cibo illi fuisse: tamen, quasi non sit ferendum, ut vir sanctus locustis vitam sustinuisse credatur, veterum et recentiorum multi in omnes formas se transformarunt, ut haec alio detorquerent. Agmen ducunt Ebionaei, in quorum Evangelio haec de Iohanne legebantur. Τὸ βρῶμα αὐτοῦ μέλι ἄγριον, οὗ ἡ γεῦ-σις ἦν τοῦ Μάννα, ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν ἐλαίῳ· Cibus ejus erat mel sylvestre, sapore Mannae, tanquam lagani in oleo fricti. Ad quem locum Epiphanius illos asserit hoc sibi proposuisse, ut veritatis sermonem in mendacium mutarent, et ἀντὶ ἀκρίδων ποιήσωσιν ἐγκρίδας ἐν μέλιτι, ex locustis facerent lagana in melle fricta. Occasione haec confingendi ex eo sumta, quod ἀκρίδες et ἐγκρίδες sunt voces simillimae. Atque in Evangelio junguntur ἀκρίδες καὶ μέλι, ita de Manna dicitur Exod. XVI, 31. Ejus autem sapor erat ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν μέλιτι, ut lagani in melle (sive cum melle,) fricti. Quomodo si sapuerint Iohannis in deserto cibi, non magnum id fuerit austerae vitae argumentum. Alii aliter sentiunt, nempe ἀκρίδες hoc loco esse ἀκρόδρυα, vel τῶν φυτῶν ἀκρέμονας, arborum et herbarum extremitates, quibus Iohannes in illo deserto vixerit. Ita Isidorus Pelusiota libri primi Epistola quinta vult, ut monachus, a Iohanne edoctus, contentus sit ἀκρέμοσι βοτανῶν καὶ φυλλῶν πρὸς ὀλίγην τροφήν, herbarum et foliorum extremis ad tenue alimentum. Et Epistola centesima trigesima secunda ita mordicus haeret huic interpretationi, ut iis, qui ἀκρίδων nomine locustas intelligunt, his verbis dicam scribat: Αἱ ἀκρίδες, αἷς Ἰωάννης ἐτρέφετο, οὐ ζῶά εἰσιν, ὥς τινες οἴονται ἀμαθῶς, κανθάροις ἀπεοικότα· μὴ γένοιτο. ἀλλ᾽ ἀκρέμονες βοτα-νῶν, ἢ φυτῶν. Illae acrides, quibus Iohannes vescebatur, non sunt animalia, scarabaeis similia, ut quidam imperite existimant; absit: sed herbarum aut arborum extrema. Ita etiam Paulinus in carmine de Iohanne, baptista,

It has been shown so far that there is scarcely any animal more destructive to the human race than locusts. col. 488 Yet these too have their use, which others explain at length. It will be enough for us to show that locusts served as food for not a few peoples long ago, and still serve so today, with regard to the much-discussed (πολυθρύλλητα [polythryllēta]) words about John the Baptist in Matth. III, 4: Ἡ δὲ τροφὴ αὐτοῦ ἦν ἀκρίδες, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον [Hē de trophē autou ēn akrides, kai meli agrion]. And Mark 1:6: Ἐσθίων ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον [Esthiōn akridas, kai meli agrion]. Although these words teach clearly and openly that locusts and wild honey were his food, nevertheless, as though it were intolerable to believe that the holy man sustained his life on locusts, many ancients and moderns have twisted themselves into every shape in order to bend these words to another sense. The Ebionites lead the column; in their Gospel the following was read about John: Τὸ βρῶμα αὐτοῦ μέλι ἄγριον, οὗ ἡ γεῦσις ἦν τοῦ Μάννα, ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν ἐλαίῳ [To brōma autou meli agrion, hou hē geusis ēn tou Manna, hōs enkris en elaiō]· His food was wild honey, with the taste of Manna, like a cake fried in oil. On this passage Epiphanius asserts that they set themselves this aim, to change the word of truth into a lie, and ἀντὶ ἀκρίδων ποιήσωσιν ἐγκρίδας ἐν μέλιτι [anti akridōn poiēsōsin enkridas en meliti], to make out of locusts cakes fried in honey. The occasion for this fabrication was taken from the fact that ἀκρίδες [akrides] and ἐγκρίδες [enkrides] are very similar words. And just as in the Gospel ἀκρίδες καὶ μέλι [akrides kai meli] are joined, so it is said of the Manna in Exod 16:31: And its taste was ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν μέλιτι [hōs enkris en meliti], like a cake fried in honey (or with honey). If John's food in the desert had tasted like that, it would not have been much proof of an austere life. Others think otherwise, namely that ἀκρίδες [akrides] in this passage are ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya], or τῶν φυτῶν ἀκρέμονας [tōn phytōn akremonas], the tips of trees and plants, on which John lived in that desert. Thus Isidore of Pelusium, in the fifth Letter of book 1, wants the monk, taught by John, to be content with ἀκρέμοσι βοτανῶν καὶ φυλλῶν πρὸς ὀλίγην τροφήν [akremosi botanōn kai phyllōn pros oligēn trophēn], the tips of plants and leaves for slender nourishment. And in Letter 132 he clings so tenaciously to this interpretation that he brings a charge against those who understand locusts by the name ἀκρίδες [akrides], in these words: Αἱ ἀκρίδες, αἷς Ἰωάννης ἐτρέφετο, οὐ ζῶά εἰσιν, ὥς τινες οἴονται ἀμαθῶς, κανθάροις ἀπεοικότα· μὴ γένοιτο. ἀλλ᾽ ἀκρέμονες βοτανῶν, ἢ φυτῶν [Hai akrides, hais Iōannēs etrepheto, ou zōa eisin, hōs tines oiontai amathōs, kantharois apeoikota; mē genoito. all᾽ akremones botanōn, ē phytōn]. Those acrides on which John fed are not animals resembling beetles, as some ignorantly suppose; far from it: but the tips of plants or trees. So also Paulinus, in his poem on John the Baptist: ¶

Praebebant victum facilem sylvestria mella,
Pomaque, et incultis enatae cautibus herbae.

Wild honey furnished easy sustenance, and fruits, and herbs sprung from untilled rocks. ¶

Et Pantaleon, diaconus, in Sermone De luminibus sanctis, Ὁ τοῖς τῶν μελισσῶν κατὰ τὴν ἔρημον πόνοις, καὶ τοῖς τῶν βοτανῶν συντρεφόμενος ἐκ βρέφους ἀκρίσμασι· III, 327 Qui apum in deserto laboribus, et herbarum extremis ab infantia nutritus est. Et Theophylactus, Τινὲς δὲ (λέγουσι) τὰ ἀκρόδρυα, ἤτοι ὀπώρας ἀγρίας (εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας·) Acridas quidam dicunt esse extrema arborum, aut poma sylvestria. Et Euthymius, Locustas vero quidam herbarum esse virgulta dixerunt. Et Nicephorus Callistus libri primi Historiae capite decimo quarto, Ibi teneriori- bus arborum summitatibus, ἄκροις δρυῶν, pro cibo utebatur. Et superiore saeculo Cajetanus, Suspicor, aequivocum esse nomen ad animalia illa, locustas, et ra- dices, vel summitates teneras virgultorum, seu ramusculorum, seu aliquid hujus- modi, quo crudo vescimur. Quo torrente abreptus Baronius ad annum 31. Quid, inquit, de his sentiendum sit, definiendum nobis non est, sed totum relinquimus lectoris arbitrio. Nam constat, Graecam dictionem ἀκρίδας, et locustam, insecti col. 489 genus, infirmas habens alas ad volandum, et summitates arborum significare. Imo constat, ἀκρίδας et ἀκρόδρυα multum differre, nec a scriptoribus vere Graecis usquam confundi haec duo vocabula. Quod tamen illi veterum, qui a locustarum esu abhorrebant, eo fecere libentius, quod ἀκρόδρυα sciebant nonnullis pro cibo fuisse. Sic, in Paedagogo Clementis lib. 2. cap. 1. Matthaeus, apostolus, σπερμάτων, καὶ ἀκροδρύων, καὶ λαχάνων, ἄνευ κρέων, μετελάμ-βανεν, seminibus, et arborum summitatibus, et oleribus sine carnibus vescebatur. Sed ibidem Iohannes eo ipso a Matthaeo distinguitur, quod ὑπερτείνας τὴν ἐγκράτειαν, ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἤσθιεν ἄγριον, continentiam ulterius promovens, locustas et mel agreste comedebat. Ut illi aliud fuisse scias ἀκρίδας, aliud ἀκρό-δρυα. Sic Epiphanii aevo nonnulli in jejuniis suis usque adeo rigidi erant, ut etiam abstinerent ἀκροδρύων, καὶ ἑψημάτων, ab arborum summitatibus, et ole- rum jusculis: quasi hoc nimium fuisset. Haec ille in Expositione fidei, cap. 23. Tertia sententia eorum est, quibus αἱ ἀκρίδες sunt herbae genus. Euthymius, Locustas vero quidam herbarum esse virgulta dixerunt: quidam autem herbam, di- ctam locustam. Theophylactus, Τινὲς λέγουσι βοτάνας εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας, ἃς καὶ μέλαγρα καλοῦσι· Acridas quidam herbas esse dicunt, quas etiam melagra vocant. Ut alludatur ad μέλι ἄγριον. Itaque, ex hac sententia, idem erunt mel et locustae; herbae scilicet genus, aliunde prorsus ignotae, quae tamen Io- hanni pro cibo sola suffecit. Nihil absurdius fingi potuit. Tamen etiam haec sententia suos habuit sectatores: in his Surianum, monachum, a quo Iacobus de Vitriaco cap. 53. Historiae Hierosolymitanae se didicisse profitetur, ἀκρίδας Io- hannis non fuisse locustas, sed herbam quandam, monachis in Syria apponi solitam. Fortasse etiam Dionysium Carthusianum, qui nonnullos per locustas intelligere scribit herbas deserti, cum non videatur probabile, quod Iohannes, qui nec pane vescebatur, carnes animalium, aut aliquid coctum, comederit. Et Hermo- laus in Dioscoridem Corollario 256. eam herbam ait intelligi, quae Arabice dicitur رجل الجراد pes locustae; id est, blitum, ut quidam volunt; aut, ut III, 328 alii, rapunculum. Quarta sit sententia, quae Iohannis ἀκρίδας aviculas esse vult. Christianus Druthmarus, qui in Matthaeum scripsit nono post Christum saeculo, De locustis audivi quendam dicere, quod istae locustae aviculae fuerunt parvae cum rubeis tibiis. Quod ferendum esset, si solum volucres dixisset. Nam e locustis volucribus nonnullae sunt rubris pedibus. Sed cum aviculam dixerit rubris pedibus, quid aliud intelligamus, quam Graecorum ἐρυθρόποδα? Fringillae speciem esse volunt. Druthmarum certe patet veram avem intellexisse, cum hujus sententiae auctorem ita refellit; Quare laudaretur (Iohannes) de abstinentiae rigore, si volatilia ederet, quae desideraverunt Iudaei? Quinta, locustas esse pisces marinos, quos Iohannes ex Iordane habuerit, ut refert idem Druthmarus: quod est perridiculum. Neque enim in fluviis, sed in ipso mari has locustas reperire est. Ut taceam, locustas marinas a Graecis non dici ἀκρίδας, sed καράβους. Denique cum locustae marinae in deliciis habeantur, hic quoque merito objicit Druthmarus, non esse, cur Iohannes ab abstinentia laudetur, si marinas delicias ederit. Quae eadem in illos objici queant, qui ἀκρίδας in κα-ρίδας, id est, squillas, mutant; quibus in toto cancrorum genere nihil est praestantius. Huic sententiae, quae sexta est, septimam addo viri maximi et piissimi, qui conjicit, Evangelistas hic scripsisse ἀχράδες, id est, pira sylvestria, et exigua mutatione hoc nomen deflexum esse in ἀκρίδες (1).

And Pantaleon the deacon, in his sermon On the Holy Lights: Ὁ τοῖς τῶν μελισσῶν κατὰ τὴν ἔρημον πόνοις, καὶ τοῖς τῶν βοτανῶν συντρεφόμενος ἐκ βρέφους ἀκρίσμασι· [Ho tois tōn melissōn kata tēn erēmon ponois, kai tois tōn botanōn syntrephomenos ek brephous akrismasi] III, 327 He who from infancy was nourished on the labours of the bees in the desert and on the tips of herbs. And Theophylact: Τινὲς δὲ (λέγουσι) τὰ ἀκρόδρυα, ἤτοι ὀπώρας ἀγρίας (εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας·) [Tines de (legousi) ta akrodrya, ētoi opōras agrias (einai tas akridas)] Some say that the acridas are the tips of trees, or wild fruits. And Euthymius: But some have said that the locusts were shoots of herbs. And Nicephorus Callistus, History book 1, chapter 14: There he used for food the tenderer tops of trees, ἄκροις δρυῶν [akrois dryōn]. And in the last century Cajetan: I suspect that the name is equivocal, applying both to those animals, locusts, and to roots, or the tender tops of shrubs or twigs, or something of this kind that we eat raw. Swept away by this torrent, Baronius, under the year 31, says: What we should think of these things is not for us to decide; we leave the whole matter to the reader's judgement. For it is agreed that the Greek word ἀκρίδας [akridas] signifies both the locust, a kind of insect col. 489 having weak wings for flying, and the tops of trees. On the contrary, it is agreed that ἀκρίδας [akridas] and ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] differ greatly, and that these two words are nowhere confused by truly Greek writers. Yet those of the ancients who shrank from eating locusts did this all the more readily because they knew that ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] had served some people as food. Thus in the Paedagogus of Clement, book 2, ch. 1, the apostle Matthew σπερμάτων, καὶ ἀκροδρύων, καὶ λαχάνων, ἄνευ κρέων, μετελάμβανεν [spermatōn, kai akrodryōn, kai lachanōn, aneu kreōn, metelambanen], ate seeds, and tops of trees, and vegetables, without meat. But in the same place John is distinguished from Matthew precisely by the fact that ὑπερτείνας τὴν ἐγκράτειαν, ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἤσθιεν ἄγριον [hyperteinas tēn enkrateian, akridas, kai meli ēsthien agrion], carrying abstinence further, he ate locusts and wild honey. So you may know that for him ἀκρίδας [akridas] were one thing and ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] another. Thus in the age of Epiphanius some were so rigid in their fasts that they abstained even from ἀκροδρύων, καὶ ἑψημάτων [akrodryōn, kai hepsēmatōn], from the tops of trees and from vegetable broths, as though this were too much. So he says in the Exposition of the Faith, ch. 23. The third opinion is that of those for whom αἱ ἀκρίδες [hai akrides] are a kind of herb. Euthymius: But some have said that the locusts were shoots of herbs, and others a herb called locust. Theophylact: Τινὲς λέγουσι βοτάνας εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας, ἃς καὶ μέλαγρα καλοῦσι· [Tines legousi botanas einai tas akridas, has kai melagra kalousi] Some say that the acridas are herbs, which they also call melagra, as an allusion to μέλι ἄγριον [meli agrion]. So, on this opinion, the honey and the locusts will be the same thing: namely a kind of herb otherwise entirely unknown, which nevertheless alone sufficed John for food. Nothing more absurd could be imagined. Yet even this opinion had its followers, among them Surianus, a monk, from whom Jacques de Vitry, ch. 53 of the History of Jerusalem, professes to have learned that the ἀκρίδας [akridas] of John were not locusts but a certain herb that used to be served to monks in Syria. Perhaps also Dionysius the Carthusian, who writes that some understand by locusts herbs of the desert, since it does not seem probable that John, who did not even eat bread, ate the flesh of animals or anything cooked. And Hermolaus, on Dioscorides, Corollary 256, says that the herb meant is the one called in Arabic رجل الجراد [rijl al-jarād] locust's foot; that is, blitum [blite], as some hold, or, as III, 328 others, rapunculum [rampion]. Let the fourth opinion be the one that holds the ἀκρίδας [akridas] of John to be little birds. Christian Druthmar, who wrote on Matthew in the ninth century after Christ: About the locusts I have heard someone say that these locusts were small birds with red legs. This would be tolerable if he had said only winged creatures, for among flying locusts some have red feet. But since he said a little bird with red feet, what else are we to understand than the ἐρυθρόποδα [erythropoda] of the Greeks? They take it to be a species of finch. That Druthmar understood a real bird is certainly clear, since he refutes the author of this opinion thus: Why would (John) be praised for the rigour of his abstinence, if he ate the flying creatures that the Jews longed for? The fifth: that the locusts were sea fish, which John obtained from the Jordan, as the same Druthmar reports, which is quite ridiculous. For these locusts are found not in rivers but in the sea itself. Not to mention that sea locusts [lobsters] are called by the Greeks not ἀκρίδας [akridas] but καράβους [karabous]. Finally, since sea locusts are counted a delicacy, here too Druthmar rightly objects that there would be no reason to praise John for abstinence if he ate marine delicacies. The same objection can be raised against those who change ἀκρίδας [akridas] into καρίδας [karidas], that is, squillas [shrimps], than which nothing in the whole crab family is more excellent. To this opinion, which is the sixth, I add a seventh, that of a very great and most pious man, who conjectures that the Evangelists here wrote ἀχράδες [achrades], that is, wild pears, and that by a slight change this word was turned into ἀκρίδες [akrides] R1. ¶

Sed nihil opus est quidquam mutare, cum in receptam lectionem conspirent libri omnes; neque illa quidquam habeat, quo lector eruditus merito pos- col. 490 sit offendi. Certum enim est, locustarum esum in iis, quae vel ad ortum, vel ad austrum sunt, regionibus fuisse receptissimum. Quae Hieronymi verba sunt in Iovinianum lib. 2. cap. 6. Orientales et Libyae populos, quia per desertum et eremi vastitatem locustarum nubes reperiuntur, locustis vesci moris est. Sic, ab Alexandri aevo, inter Aethiopum populos Graecia descripsit Acridophagos, a locustarum esu sic dictos. De his Agatharchides lib. 5. cap. 27. Ὑπὸ δὲ τὴν ἐαρι-τὴν ἰσημερίαν, ὅτε Λίβυες (lege Λίβες) παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς καὶ Ζέφυροι πνέουσι, παμ-μεγεθῶν ἀκρίδων πλῆθος ἀμύθητον ἐκ τῆς ἀνιςορήτου μετὰ τῶν ἀνέμων παρα-γίνεται χώρας. τῇ μὲν δυνάμει τῆς πλήσεως τῶν ὀρνίθων μικρὸν παραλλάττον, III, 329 μακρὸν δὲ τῷ σώματι. ἀπὸ τούτου δὲ τοῦ ζώου τρέφονται πάντα τὸν χρόνον, ἄλλως τε τούτοις καὶ τεταριχευμένοις χρώμενοι. Sub vernum aequinoctium, cum apud eos spirant Africi et Favonii, inenarrabile locustarum praegrandium agmen ex ignotis terris una cum ventis advolat, quae vi quidem volatus parum ab avibus, sed corpore plurimum differt. Hoc autem animalculo quovis tempore vescuntur, sive cum sale, aut aliter condito. Ita etiam Diodorus lib. 3. editionis Steph. pag. 113. Ἐκ τούτου δαψιλεῖς τροφὰς ἔχου-σιν ἅπαντα τὸν βίον: Ex eo uberem victum habent per omnem vitam. Et Artemidorus apud Strabonem lib. 16. Ζῶσι δ' ἀπὸ ἀκρίδων, ἃς οἱ ἐαρινοὶ Λίβες καὶ Ζέφυροι, πνέοντες μεγάλοι, συνελαύνουσιν εἰς τοὺς τόπους τούτους· Locustis vivunt, quas verni, Africi et Favonii, vehementius flantes, in ea loca impellunt. Et Solinus, Confines Mauritaniae certo tempore locustas terrestres legunt, duratasque salsugine in praesidium vitae solas habent. Et Plinius libri 6. cap. 30. Pars quaedam Aethiopum locustis tantum vivit, fumo et sale duratis in annua alimenta. Et lib. 11. cap. 29. Parthis et hae in cibo gratae. Et de Nasamonibus Eustathius in Dionysium, Ἐσθίουσι δὲ ἀττελάβους πρὸς ἥλιον ξηράναντες· εἶδος δὲ ἀκρίδος ὁ ἀττέλαβος· Attelabos, locustae speciem, ad solem exsiccatos, comedunt. Et Dioscorides, ubi de asiraco, locustae specie; Χρῶνται δ' αὐτῇ κατακόρως οἱ κατὰ Λέπτιν Λίβυες· Ea utuntur in cibo usque ad satietatem Libes, qui ad Leptim incolunt. Porphyrius quoque lib. 1. De abstinentia a carne scribit, exercitui, in Libya inedia laboranti, locustarum nubes se obtulisse.

But there is no need to change anything, since all the books agree on the received reading, and it contains nothing at which a learned reader could rightly col. 490 take offence. For it is certain that the eating of locusts was very widely accepted in the regions that lie either to the east or to the south. These are Jerome's words in Against Jovinian book 2, ch. 6: Among the peoples of the East and of Libya, because clouds of locusts are found throughout the desert and the vastness of the wilderness, it is the custom to feed on locusts. Thus, from the age of Alexander, Greece described among the peoples of Ethiopia the Acridophagi, so called from eating locusts. On these Agatharchides, book 5, ch. 27: Ὑπὸ δὲ τὴν ἐαριτὴν ἰσημερίαν, ὅτε Λίβυες (lege Λίβες) παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς καὶ Ζέφυροι πνέουσι, παμμεγεθῶν ἀκρίδων πλῆθος ἀμύθητον ἐκ τῆς ἀνιςορήτου μετὰ τῶν ἀνέμων παραγίνεται χώρας. τῇ μὲν δυνάμει τῆς πλήσεως τῶν ὀρνίθων μικρὸν παραλλάττον, III, 329 μακρὸν δὲ τῷ σώματι. ἀπὸ τούτου δὲ τοῦ ζώου τρέφονται πάντα τὸν χρόνον, ἄλλως τε τούτοις καὶ τεταριχευμένοις χρώμενοι. [Hypo de tēn earitēn isēmerian, hote Libyes (lege Libes) par᾽ autois kai Zephyroi pneousi, pammegethōn akridōn plēthos amythēton ek tēs anistorētou meta tōn anemōn paraginetai chōras. tē men dynamei tēs plēseōs tōn ornithōn mikron parallatton, makron de tō sōmati. apo toutou de tou zōou trephontai panta ton chronon, allōs te toutois kai tetaricheumenois chrōmenoi.] Around the spring equinox, when the south-west winds and west winds blow among them, an indescribable swarm of very large locusts flies in together with the winds from unknown lands; in the force of their flight they differ little from birds, but in body very greatly. On this little creature they feed at every season, either salted or otherwise preserved. But, and this is the heart of the matter, the locust was edible among the Jews too. This is attested by the Mosaic law, Lev 11:21–22., where four species of locust are permitted for eating: arbe, solam, chargol and chagab. So too the Talmudic doctors, who in the tractate Cholin, ch. 3, fol. 65 and 66, argue at length about the marks by which clean locusts are distinguished from unclean, that is, those which may be eaten from those whose eating is forbidden by the Law. Accordingly, at Exod 10:19, where we read that a wind sent by God III, 331 carried off whatever locusts were in Egypt and cast them into the Red Sea, so that not even one remained in all the borders of the Egyptians, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] in his paraphrase makes an exception: מה דמלחין במנייא לצרוך מיכלהון, what they had preserved with salt in their vessels for use as food R2. ¶

The Arabs teach the same. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter On locusts, under the heading الحكم [al-ḥukm], that is, On use: اجمع المسلمون علي اباحة اكل الجراد [ajmaʿa al-muslimūn ʿalā ibāḥat akl al-jarād], "The Muslims agree that the eating of locusts is lawful." They rely, of course, on the authority of Abdalla, whose words are: غزونا مع رسول الله صلي الله عليه وسلم سبع غزوات ناكل الجراد فيها [ghazawnā maʿa rasūl Allāh ṣallā Allāh ʿalayhi wa-sallam sabʿ ghazawāt naʾkul al-jarād fīhā], "We undertook seven military expeditions with the prophet of God, on whom God be gracious, eating the flesh of locusts." Summa eorum, quae diximus, haec est, praeter Acridophagos, omnibus notos, Nasamones etiam, et Leptitanos, et Parthos, et Graecos, et He- braeos locustas olim pro cibo habuisse. Quomodo Arabes quoque, et Nu- midas, et Mauritanos, et novos Palaestinae incolas jam inde a multis saeculis. Itaque, quod locustas edebat Iohannes, novum non fuit, nec inauditum: sed hoc in illo fuit singulare, quod in deserto solum locustis et agresti melle vescebatur, iisque forte oblatis, nec aliunde comparatis; ut virum illum Iudaei scirent extraordinarium habere munus, atque ad ejus conciones essent attentiores. Causa igitur non fuit, cur Isidorus Pelusiota eos accusaret III, 333 imperitiae, qui locustas hic intelligunt. In quo numero multi fuerunt illo longe celebriores: ut Theophilus Antiochenus, Origenes, Hilarius et Chrysostomus in Matthaeum; Ambrosius in Lucam, Clemens Alexandrinus lib. 2. Paedagogi, Hieronymus lib. 2. in Iovinianum, et Epistola 23. ad Marcellam, Augustinus lib. 10. Confessionum cap. 31. The sum of what we have said is this: besides the Acridophagi, known to all, the Nasamones too, and the people of Leptis, and the Parthians, and the Greeks, and the Hebrews once had locusts for food; as have the Arabs also, and the Numidians, and the Mauretanians, and the new inhabitants of Palestine, now for many centuries. Therefore that John ate locusts was nothing new or unheard of. What was singular in him was that in the desert he fed only on locusts and wild honey, and these perhaps as they chanced to come his way, not procured from elsewhere; so that the Jews might know that this man had an extraordinary office, and might be more attentive to his preaching. There was no reason, then, for Isidore of Pelusium to accuse of III, 333 ignorance those who understand locusts here. Among their number were many far more celebrated than he: such as Theophilus of Antioch, Origen, Hilary and Chrysostom on Matthew; Ambrose on Luke; Clement of Alexandria, book 2 of the Paedagogus; Jerome, book 2 Against Jovinian and Letter 23 to Marcella; Augustine, book 10 of the Confessions, ch. 31. R3 Rosenmüller, III, 332 Forsskål [=Peter Forsskål, Descriptiones animalium (1775)] in Descript. Animall. p. 81: "The Bedouin of Egypt roast grylli of this species [جراد [jarād]] alive over coals, and then, throwing away the wings and legs, eat them greedily. In Arabia Felix, between Mocha and Taizz, I saw women and boys intent on hunting these little birds; they strung them, up to 100, on a thread drawn through the thorax, and offered them for the price of ½ Chomas. The whole bundle is roasted. Not only the legs and wings of the gryllus are torn off, but also the intestines, which are laid bare when the head is pulled out a little. Certainly a juiceless food, yet harmless; except that its excessive use causes sluggish blood, or a melancholic temperament, as the Arabs maintain, which they call Souda. For the rest, I heard a ridiculous defence of this food: the gryllus feeds on pure, useful plants, indeed even ones of medicinal power; it therefore cannot be otherwise than that it becomes wholly wholesome itself. Yet the inhabitants consume too little of them to diminish the number of this harmful swarm even slightly." For more on the eating of locusts see Niebuhr, p. 171, Hasselquist [=Fredrik Hasselquist, Iter Palaestinum (1757)], p. 452 ff., Höst, p. 301, and Oedmann, fasc. 2, Chapter 6, p. 105 ff. and fasc. 6, Chapter 8, p. 78.
