Explicantur obscura aliquot Scripturae loca de Locustis.

Some obscure passages of Scripture on Locusts are explained. ¶

Quaeri potest, quorsum in locustarum, aut bruchorum genere ילק jelek dicatur סמר samar, Ierem. LI, 27. id est, φρίττων, horrens, aut ὀρθόθριξ, horripilans, cujus arrectae sunt horrore comae. Ita Iob. IV, 15. Cum spiritus coram me transiret; תסמר שערת בשרי, arrecti sunt crines carnis meae. Et Psal. CXIX, 120. סמר מפחדך בשרי, Horruit prae metu tui caro mea. Ubi Symmachus ὀρθοτριχεῖ, et Syrus ܣܦܣ? Verbo eleganti facto ex Hebraeo Nomine קפוד, quod hericium sonat. Quomodo Gallice dicimus herisser; non ab horrescendo, ut volunt, sed ab hoc ipso animali, quod nobis un herisson. Romani veteres per periphrasim id exprimebant, ut Poeta,

One may ask why, in the class of locusts or bruchi, the ילק jelek is called סמר samar, Jer 51:27, that is, φρίττων [phrittōn], bristling, or ὀρθόθριξ [orthothrix], horripilans [with hair standing on end], one whose hair stands erect with horror. So Job 4:15. When a spirit passed before me; תסמר שערת בשרי, the hairs of my flesh stood up. And Ps 119:120. סמר מפחדך בשרי, My flesh bristled for fear of thee. Here Symmachus has ὀρθοτριχεῖ [orthotrichei], and the Syriac ܣܦܣ [ספס]? — an elegant verb formed from the Hebrew noun קפוד, which means hedgehog. In the same way we say in French herisser, not from horrescere [to bristle], as some would have it, but from this very animal, which we call un herisson. The ancient Romans expressed it by a periphrasis, as the Poet: ¶

Obstupui, steteruntque comae. — —

I was stunned, and my hair stood on end. — — ¶

Et col. 455

And col. 455 ¶

Arrectaeque horrore comae. — —

And hair erect with horror. — — ¶

Sed sequior aetas horripilare et horripilationem dixit. Apuleius, libro tertio de se ipso loquens, in asinum mutato; Aures immodicis horripilant auctibus. Glossae veteres Horripilatur, ὀρθοτριχεῖ. Lexicon Philoxeni, Ὀρθοτριχιῶ, horripilo. Vulgatus interpres Eccesiast. XXVII, 15. Loquela multum jurans (legerim multum jurantis, ut Graece Λαλιὰ πολυόρκου) horripilationem capiti statuet, Graece ὀρθώσει τρίχας. Quae affectio non videtur in locustam aut bruchum posse cadere, cum depiles sint. Itaque Castalio, Petrus Robertus, Angli interpretes, et fortasse plures alii erucam reddunt, animalculum valde hirsutum. Unde Columella in Horto,

But a later age said horripilare and horripilatio. Apuleius, in his third book, speaking of himself when changed into an ass: My ears bristle with immoderate growth. The old Glossaries: Horripilatur, ὀρθοτριχεῖ [orthotrichei]. The lexicon of Philoxenus: Ὀρθοτριχιῶ [orthotrichiō], horripilo. The Vulgate translator, Eccesiast. XXVII, 15.: Speech that swears much (I would read of one who swears much, as the Greek Λαλιὰ πολυόρκου [Lalia polyorkou]) will make the hair of the head stand on end, in Greek ὀρθώσει τρίχας [orthōsei trichas]. This condition does not seem applicable to a locust or a bruchus, since they are hairless. Therefore Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Petrus Robertus, the English translators, and perhaps many others render it eruca [caterpillar], a very hairy little creature. Hence Columella in the Garden: ¶

Nec solum teneras audent erodere frondes
Implicitus conchae limax, hirsutaque campe.

Nor do the slug wrapped in its shell and the hairy campedare to gnaw only the tender leaves. ¶

Campe, id est, eruca, quomodo interpretatur ipse in Prosa De cultu horti circa finem libri duodecimi; Quae a nobis appellantur ERUCAE, Graece autem κάμ-παι nominantur. Sed una nota versioni adstruendae non sufficit, cum reliquae desunt: puta in eruca volatus, qui τῷ jelek tribuitur Nahum III, 16. ילק פשט ויעף, Ielek grassatus est, et avolavit. Eleganter Hieronymus in Amosi caput quartum, Non avolat eruca ut locusta, etc. Sed permanet perituris frugibus, et tardo lapsu, pigrisque morsibus consumit universa. Praeterea quod Ieremias globos equitum, in Babylonios magno impetu irruentes, confert cum jelek horripilante, III, 272 non videtur in erucam posse quadrare, quae nihil habet, quo hostiles turmas referat.

Campe, that is, eruca, as he himself explains in his prose On the Cultivation of the Garden, near the end of book 12: Those which we call ERUCAE are named in Greek κάμπαι [kampai]. But one feature is not enough to establish this rendering when the others are lacking: for instance flight in the caterpillar, which is attributed to the jelek in Nahum III, 16. ילק פשט ויעף, Ielek spread itself out and flew away. Jerome elegantly, on chapter 4 of Amos: The caterpillar does not fly away like the locust, etc., but remains on the crops that are to perish, and with slow movement and sluggish bites consumes everything. Moreover, that Jeremiah compares the masses of horsemen rushing with great force upon the Babylonians to the bristling jelek III, 272 does not seem to fit the caterpillar, which has nothing to resemble hostile bands. ¶

Proinde Iunio et Tremellio ילק jelek neque locusta est, neque eruca, sed melolontha, animalculum, de quo Hesychius: Μηλολόνθη· εἶδος κανθάρων, οὓς τινες χρυσοκανθάρους λέγουσι. Nempe melolontha dicitur, quia pomorum est pernicies: et chrysocantharus, quasi aureus scarabaeus; quia figura scarabaeum, aurum colore imitatur. Unde et χρυσομηλολόνθιον vocat Aristophanes in Vespis. Ubi Scholiastes, Ζωΰφιόν τί ἐστι κατὰ κάνθαρον ξανθὸν, ὃ καὶ κατέχον-τες οἱ παῖδες δεσμοῦσιν ἐκ τοῦ ποδὸς, καὶ ἀφιᾶσι πρὸς τὸν ἀέρα· Animalculum est canthari specie fulvum, quod captum pueri pede vinciunt, et in aërem dimittunt. Quo faciunt haec ejusdem Aristophanis in Nubibus,

Accordingly, for Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] ילק jelek is neither locust nor caterpillar, but melolontha, a little creature of which Hesychius says: Μηλολόνθη· εἶδος κανθάρων, οὓς τινες χρυσοκανθάρους λέγουσι [Mēlolonthē; eidos kantharōn, hous tines chrysokantharous legousi]. It is called melolontha, namely, because it is the bane of fruit; and chrysocantharus, as it were golden beetle, because it resembles a beetle in shape and gold in colour. Hence Aristophanes too, in the Wasps, calls it χρυσομηλολόνθιον [chrysomēlolonthion]. There the Scholiast: Ζωΰφιόν τί ἐστι κατὰ κάνθαρον ξανθὸν, ὃ καὶ κατέχοντες οἱ παῖδες δεσμοῦσιν ἐκ τοῦ ποδὸς, καὶ ἀφιᾶσι πρὸς τὸν ἀέρα [Zōyphion ti estin kata kantharon xanthon, ho kai katechontes hoi paides desmousin ek tou podos, kai aphiasi pros ton aera]; It is a little creature like a beetle, tawny, which boys catch, tie by the foot, and let go into the air. To this belong these lines of the same Aristophanes in the Clouds: ¶

Ἀλλ᾽ ἀποχάλα τὴν φροντίδ᾽ ἐς τὸν ἀέρα,
Λινόδετον ὥσπερ μηλολόνθην τοῦ ποδός·

Ἀλλ᾽ ἀποχάλα τὴν φροντίδ᾽ ἐς τὸν ἀέρα, Λινόδετον ὥσπερ μηλολόνθην τοῦ ποδός· [All' apochala tēn phrontid' es ton aera, Linodeton hōsper mēlolonthēn tou podos;] ¶

Sed dimitte curam in aërem, ut melolontham, quae pedem habet limo vinctum. Ubi rursus in Scholiaste fere eadem, quae supra legas. Nec multo aliter describunt idem animal Iulius Pollux et Suidas: quorum ille pro μηλολόνθη a nonnullis μηλολάνθην scribi ait (potius μηλάνθην) ἤτοι ἐκ τῆς ἀνθήσεως τῶν μή-λων (lege μηλεῶν cum Eustathio) ἢ σὺν τῇ ἀνθήσει γιγνόμενον, sive a malorum floribus, quibus illum insidere refert Suidas, aut quia cum floribus gignatur. Quae omnia si junctim sumas, statim colliges, animalculum describi, nobis Normanis maximo malo nostro nimio quam notius: cum in pomorum flores, unde proventus apud nos est uberrimus, per singula triennia haec pestis ingravescat (1). Sed nulla mihi ratio apparet, cur ילק jelek censeatur potius esse melolontha, quam bruchi, aut locustae genus. Quomodo constanter reddunt omnes veteres, a Prophetis ipsis ita edocti, qui in multis Scripturae locis meminerunt τοῦ jelek inter locustarum genera. Exempla vide Psal. CV, 34. Ioel. I, 4. et II, 25. et Nahum III, 15. 16. Itaque ab hac versione non est temere discedendum. Quin hoc dato, non concesso, jelek esse melolontham, nihilo facilius solvetur quaestio proposita, quorsum describatur jelek tanquam ὀρθόθριξ. Quia melolontha non minus glabra est, quam locusta, aut bruchus. Fullerus quidem, qui Iunio et Tremellio hac in parte accedit Miscellaneorum libri quinti capite nono, melolontham advolantem assidua et veloci pennarum quatuor agitatione, heri- col. 456 nacei spinis undique exstantibus hispidi speciem referre sibi fingit.

But let your care go into the air, like a cockchafer that has its foot tied with thread. There again the Scholiast says much the same as you read above. Julius Pollux and Suidas describe the same animal in much the same way. The former says that some write μηλολάνθην [mēlolanthēn] for μηλολόνθη [mēlolonthē] (rather μηλάνθην [mēlanthēn]), ἤτοι ἐκ τῆς ἀνθήσεως τῶν μήλων [ētoi ek tēs anthēseōs tōn mēlōn] (read μηλεῶν [mēleōn] with Eustathius) ἢ σὺν τῇ ἀνθήσει γιγνόμενον [ē syn tē anthēsei gignomenon], either from the blossoms of apple trees, on which Suidas reports that it settles, or because it is born together with the blossoms. If you take all this together, you will at once conclude that the little animal described is one far too well known to us Normans, to our very great harm: for every third year this pest grows worse on the blossoms of the apple trees, from which our most abundant produce comes R1. But I see no reason why ילק jelek should be thought to be the melolontha [cockchafer] rather than a kind of bruchus or locust. So all the ancients consistently render it, having been so taught by the Prophets themselves, who in many places of Scripture mention the jelek among the kinds of locusts. For examples see Ps 105:34. Joel 1:4 and II, 25. and Nahum III, 15. 16. One should therefore not lightly depart from this rendering. Indeed, even if it were granted (it is not conceded) that the jelek is the melolontha, the question posed, why the jelek is described as ὀρθόθριξ [orthothrix], would be solved no more easily. For the melolontha is no less smooth than the locust or the bruchus. Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], it is true, who on this point sides with Junius and Tremellius in his Miscellanea, book 5, chapter 9, imagines that a cockchafer flying toward us, with the constant and rapid beating of its four wings, presents the appearance of a hedgehog col. 456 bristling with spines standing out on every side. ¶

III, 273 Credimus? an qui amant ipsi sibi somnia fingunt?

III, 273 Are we to believe it? Or do those who love invent dreams for themselves? ¶

Mihi certe non constat, quid alae molles, et in extremis obtusae, commune habeant cum hericii aculeis. Quamvis, vel si verum id esset, causa non fuerit, cur novi interpretes a recepta versione discederent, cum locustis etiam et bruchis sint alae quatuor, quarum agitatio tam crebra est, quam alarum melolonthae. De locustis igitur tam proclive fuit id ipsum sibi fingere (2).

I, at any rate, do not see what soft wings, blunt at their tips, have in common with the spines of a hedgehog. And even if this were true, it would have been no reason for the recent translators to depart from the accepted rendering, since locusts and bruchi also have four wings, whose beating is as rapid as that of the cockchafer's wings. It would therefore have been just as easy to imagine the very same thing of locusts R2. ¶

Sed, ut solvatur quaestio, duo sunt, quae respondeamus. 1. Non id certo constare, quod volunt, locustas omne genus esse prorsus glabras et depiles. Saltem in Alcamus vocari reperio قرن الجراد شعران في راسه, cornu locustae pilos duos, quos habet in capite (3): quorum ratione horrere aut horripi- lare quidni dicatur, cum eos sursum erigit? Quomodo de haemorrhoo scribit Aelianus Historiae libri decimi quinti capite decimo tertio, Φρίττει δὲ τὴν κεφα-λὴν οἱονεὶ κέρασί τισιν. Tanquam quibusdam cornibus caput inhorrescit. Sed et locustae descriptionem poeta magni nominis, Claudianus, ita incipit,

But to solve the question, there are two things we may answer. 1. It is not certainly established, as they would have it, that locusts of every kind are entirely smooth and hairless. At least I find in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] that قرن الجراد شعران في راسه [qarn al-jarād shaʿrān fī rāsih] is the name given to the horn of the locust, the two hairs which it has on its head R3: and on account of these, why should it not be said to bristle or to stand on end when it raises them upright? So Aelian writes of the haemorrhous in History, book 15, chapter 13: Φρίττει δὲ τὴν κεφαλὴν οἱονεὶ κέρασί τισιν [Phrittei de tēn kephalēn hoionei kerasi tisin]. Its head bristles as if with certain horns. Moreover, a poet of great name, Claudian, begins his description of the locust thus: ¶

Horret apex capitis, medio fera lumina surgunt Vertice, etc.

The crown of its head bristles, fierce eyes rise from the middle of its head, etc. ¶

Et in tractatu Talmudico Cholin fol. 65. cum locustarum aliae dicuntur ha-bere גבחת gabbachath; aliae non habere: sunt, qui gibbum intelligunt, in cameli modum dorso exstantem, in quo numero est Baal Aruch: sed aliis גבחת est cal- vitium; quomodo sumitur haec vox in aliis Talmudis locis. Itaque Talmudica haec de locustis שאין לו גבחת, Ea, cui non est Gabbachath, sic exponit Glossa marginis, שאין ראשו מרוט, cujus caput non est glabrum. Nempe, ex mente Glossatoris, locustarum aliae glabro, aliae hirsuto capite sunt. Quo fortasse al-ludit Iohannes Apoc. IX, 8. cum mysticis illis locustis tribuit τρίχας ὡς τρίχας γυναικῶν, crines ut crines foeminarum (4). Neque alio refero, quod Arabes III, 274 bruchi genus عرفان orphan vocant, tanquam ab عرف orph, id est, a juba facto nomine. Alcamus, العرفان جندب ضخم كالجرادة لا يكون الا في رمث او عنظوانة او دويبة صغيرة تكون برمل عالج او الدهنا Alorphan, bruchi genus tam crassi, quam est locusta, non alibi versari soliti, quam in ramthsa et onduana (herbis acidissimis, ex quarum esu camelis saepe venter intumescit). Aut animalculum, de- gens in arenis Aligi et Dahnae: quae sunt loca prope Medinam. Addo, in eodem Alcamus الخنتف alchanthapho vocari الجراد المنتف المنقي للطبخ locustam, quae coquenda depilatur et mundatur. Quae vera est explicatio, cum etiam dicatur الخنتوف Alchanthouph, qui barbam suam vellicat. Ex quibus plane sequitur, locustas quasdam hispidas esse et hirsutas. Quod nostri interpretes ignoraverant. Praterea ילק סמר necessario non significat locustam ὀρθότριχα, cum non solis capillis, sed et toto corpore horreamus. Neque enim de pilis agitur, cum dicit David, סמר מפחדך בשרי, Horruit prae metu tui caro mea. Potest igitur horrere etiam locusta depilis: ut, cum horrore hostis imminentis, et metu periculi perculsa, ad defensionem se col. 457 parat (5). Et cum locustis et bruchis in tali statu, a quorum invasione difficile III, 275 est se defendere, ut infra docebimus, equites, magno numero in Babylonem ingruituros, potuit conferre Propheta in loco, in quo versamur, qui sic habet: Constituite in eam imperatorem. Adducite equos, similes locustis horrentibus: tam numero, quam consilio vastandi et perdendi omnia. Quid, si Verbale Nomen סמר samar, quod alibi nusquam occurrit, non tam horrentem, quam horrendum sonet, et cuivis intuenti horrorem incutientem? In locustarum agmen id apprime quadrabit, quo ingruente, ירגזון כל יושבי ארץ, omnes incolae terrae contremiscunt, Ioel. II, 1. Hinc Hesychius, ex aliquo poeta, ut suspicor; Βρέττα-να φοβερὰ, locusta horribilis.

And in the Talmudic tractate Cholin, fol. 65, where some locusts are said to have גבחת gabbachath and others not to have it, there are those who understand a hump standing out on the back in the manner of a camel; among them is Baal Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh]. But for others גבחת is baldness, as the word is taken in other passages of the Talmud. Accordingly the marginal Gloss explains these Talmudic words about locusts, שאין לו גבחת, One that has no Gabbachath, thus: שאין ראשו מרוט, whose head is not bare. That is, in the Glossator's view, some locusts have a bare head, others a hairy one. Perhaps John alludes to this in Rev 9:8, when he attributes to those mystical locusts τρίχας ὡς τρίχας γυναικῶν [trichas hōs trichas gynaikōn], hair like the hair of women R4. Nor do I refer to anything else the fact that the Arabs III, 274 call a kind of bruchus عرفان [ʿirfān] orphan, as if the name were formed from عرف [ʿurf] orph, that is, from a mane. Alcamus: العرفان جندب ضخم كالجرادة لا يكون الا في رمث او عنظوانة او دويبة صغيرة تكون برمل عالج او الدهنا [al-ʿirfān jundub ḍakhm ka-l-jarāda lā yakūnu illā fī rimth aw ʿunẓuwāna aw duwaybba ṣaghīra takūnu bi-raml ʿālij aw al-dahnā] Alorphan, a kind of bruchus as thick as a locust, usually found nowhere but in ramthsa and onduana (very sour plants, from eating which the belly of camels often swells). Or a small creature living in the sands of Aligi and Dahna: these are places near Medina. I add that in the same Alcamus الخنتف [al-khuntuf] alchanthapho is the name given to الجراد المنتف المنقي للطبخ [al-jarād al-muntaf al-munaqqā li-l-ṭabkh], a locust that is plucked and cleaned for cooking. This is the true explanation, since one who plucks his beard is also called الخنتوف [al-khuntūf] Alchanthouph. From this it plainly follows that some locusts are shaggy and hairy, which our interpreters had not known. Moreover, ילק סמר does not necessarily mean a locust ὀρθότριχα [orthotricha], since we bristle not only with our hair but with our whole body. For hair is not in question when David says, סמר מפחדך בשרי, My flesh bristled for fear of thee. So even a hairless locust can bristle: as when, struck with dread of an approaching enemy and fear of danger, it prepares col. 457 to defend itself R5. And the Prophet, in the passage we are dealing with, could compare horsemen about to fall upon Babylon in great numbers with locusts and bruchi in such a state, against whose invasion it is difficult III, 275 to defend oneself, as we shall show below. The passage reads: Appoint a commander against her. Bring up horses like bristling locusts: both in number and in their design of laying waste and destroying everything. What if the verbal noun סמר samar, which occurs nowhere else, means not so much bristling as horrifying, striking horror into anyone who sees it? This will fit a swarm of locusts exceedingly well, at whose onset ירגזון כל יושבי ארץ, all the inhabitants of the land tremble, Joel 2:1. Hence Hesychius, from some poet, as I suspect: Βρέττανα φοβερὰ [brettana phobera], a horrible locust. ¶

Nunc dicemus, quid veteres de hac voce senserint, ne quis in illis consulendis mecum se fatiget incassum. Graeci, quos sequitur Arabs, pro כילק סמר habent ὡς ἀκρίδων πλῆθος, ut locustarum multitudinem, quasi pro סמר legerint ספר, vel מספר(6). Chaldaeus quorsum pro ילק סמר habeat זחלא דמצלהב, bruchum nitentem, aut splendentem, nemo facile dixerit. Neque enim nitori et splendori cum horrore et ὀρθοτριχίᾳ quidquam commune est. Fallor, an hic quoque aliter legit? nempe סגור pro סמר, quae sunt voces scriptura valde vicinae? Porro סגור est aurum Iob. XXVIII, 15. et passim in 1 Regum et 2 Paralipomenων. Ergo ילק סגור esset bruchus aureus, aut locusta aurea, id est, aurei coloris, et auri instar fulgens: quales sunt Arabicae, de quibus Aelianus Historiae lib. 10. cap. 13. Χρυσοειδῆ οὖν ἰνδάλματα καὶ ἐπ' αὐτῶν κατέστικται, Auricoloribus figuris distinctae sunt. Atque id ipsum locustae genus a Chaldaeo dici potuit מצלהב, splendens, vel una litera minus מצהב, aureo colore fulgens. Quomodo si legatur, firmior erit haec conjectura. Hieronymus reddidit, quasi bruchum aculeatum: quibus verbis vir sanctus neque Hebraea satis expressit, neque rei naturam. Neque enim ullis aculeis insignes sunt bruchi, aut locustae; nisi mysticae illae intelligantur, quibus Iohannes tribuit scorpionis aculeos Apoc. IX, 10.(7). Syrus solum habet ܐܝܟ ܩܡܨܐ, ut bruchum, sequenti voce סמר prorsus omissa, III, 276 sive illam non legerit, sive non intellexerit. Hebraei omnes reddunt, ut bru- chum horripilantem. Sed nemo illorum aperit, quorsum bruchus sic appelletur.

Now we shall say what the ancients thought about this word, so that no one may tire himself in vain consulting them along with me.
