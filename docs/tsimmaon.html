III, 180 col. 397 CAP. VIII.

III, 180 col. 397 Chapter 8. ¶

Probatur, צמאון tsimmaon non esse Dipsadem, ut quidam post Hicronymum reddunt, sed siticulosum locum, Deut. VIII, 15.

It is proved that צמאון tsimmaon is not the dipsas, as some render it following Jerome, but a thirsty place, Deut 8:15. ¶

Dipsas est viperae similis, sed minor, vix dodrantalis, albo corpore, cauda gracili, et duabus lineis insigni, quam incedendo sursum attollit, ut observant Arabes. Omnes in Africa describunt. Sed et in Arabia nonnulli. Aelianus Historiae lib. 6. cap. 51. Γίνονται δὲ ἄρα ἐν Λιβύῃ τε καὶ Ἀραβίᾳ μᾶλλον. Ita etiam Suidas. Et in Aegypti desertis Gregorius Nazianzenus Iambico 22.

The dipsas is similar to the viper, but smaller, scarcely a span long, with a white body and a slender tail marked with two lines, which it raises upward as it moves, as the Arabs observe. All writers describe it in Africa. But some also place it in Arabia: Aelian, History book 6, ch. 51: Γίνονται δὲ ἄρα ἐν Λιβύῃ τε καὶ Ἀραβίᾳ μᾶλλον [Ginontai de ara en Libyē te kai Arabia mallon]. So also Suidas. And Gregory of Nazianzus, Iambic 22, places it in the deserts of Egypt: ¶

Διψὰς τὶς ἐστι τῶν ἐχιδναίων γενῶν,
Τούτων, ὅσ᾽ ἡ ἔρημος Αἰγύπτου φέρει·

Διψὰς τὶς ἐστι τῶν ἐχιδναίων γενῶν, Τούτων, ὅσ᾽ ἡ ἔρημος Αἰγύπτου φέρει· [Dipsas tis esti tōn echidnaiōn genōn, Toutōn, hos' hē erēmos Aigyptou pherei] ¶

Dipsas quaedam est viperini generis serpens, ex iis, quos fert Aegypti desertum. Alii etiam in Syria. Avicenna Tom. 2. pag. 139. وقال قوم ان اكثر ما تكون هذه في بلاد لوبية والشام Nonnulli dicunt, eam ut plurimum esse in Africae et Syriae regionibus. Apud Aelianum nonnulli pre- col. 398 sterem vocant, causonem alii, et melanurum, et ammebaten, et centriden. Sed vulgare nomen est διψὰς, ab inexplebili siti, quam morsu suo excitat. Isidorus reddit situlam. Et Avicenna, per periphrasim, الحية المعطشة serpentem, sitim excitantem; quasi desit proprium nomen: quod mirum, cum dicatur esse in Arabia frequens, et in vicinis locis, ut in Aegypto et Syria. De hoc serpente plura qui volet, praeter Nicandrum et medicos, Aelianum legat, ubi supra, et Lucianum libro De dipsadibus, et Lucanum libri 9. versu 738. et Nazianzenum in loco, quem citavimus, et Epiphanium in Marcosiis, et Isidorum, qui post Solinum eum in genere aspidum perperam censet, cum ad viperam maxime accedat.

The dipsas is a serpent of the viper kind, one of those which the desert of Egypt produces. Others also place it in Syria. Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], vol. 2, p. 139: وقال قوم ان اكثر ما تكون هذه في بلاد لوبية والشام [wa-qāla qawm anna akthar mā takūn hādhihi fī bilād Lūbiya wa-l-Shām] Some say that it is found mostly in the regions of Africa and Syria. In Aelian, some call it prester, col. 398 others causon, and melanurus, and ammobates, and centris. But the common name is διψὰς [dipsas], from the insatiable thirst which its bite produces. Isidore renders it situla. And Avicenna uses a periphrasis, الحية المعطشة [al-ḥayya al-muʿṭisha], the serpent that causes thirst, as though it lacked a proper name. This is surprising, since it is said to be common in Arabia and in neighbouring places such as Egypt and Syria. Whoever wants more on this serpent should read, besides Nicander and the physicians, Aelian in the place cited above, Lucian in the book On the Dipsads, Lucan book 9, line 738, Nazianzen in the passage we have quoted, Epiphanius on the Marcosians, and Isidore, who, following Solinus, wrongly counts it among the asps, although it comes closest to the viper. ¶

Legitur etiam in Hieronymi versione Deut. VIII, 15. Et ductor tuus fuit in solitudine magna atque terribili, in qua erat serpens, flatu adurens, et scorpio, ac dipsas. Sed Hebraea voce צמאון tsimmaon, quae ibi dipsas redditur, aliud significari, nempe sitim, siccitatem, siticulosum locum, multa sunt, quae non suadent modo, sed et persuadent. Primo perpetuus vocis usus in ea notione, quam diximus. Sic Psalm. CVII, 33. Posuit flumina in desertum, et aquarum scatebras לצמאון, in siticulosum locum. Graeci, εἰς δίψαν· Vulgatus et Hieronymus, in sitim: Chaldaeus, היך צחותא, sicut siccitatem: Syrus, ܠܨܗܝܐ III, 181 in siccitatem: Arabs, عطش, sitim: Aethiops, ምጽማእ: locum sitis. Rursus Esa. XXXV, 7. Et erit locus aridus in stagnum, וצמאון, et locus siticulosus in fontes aquarum. Hieronymus, et sitiens. Nec alii multo aliter. Graeci, τὴν διψῶσαν γῆν. Arabs, الارض العطشانة, terram siticulosam. Chaldaeus, בית צחונא. Syrus, ܒܝܬ ܨܗܘܢܐ, locum siticulosum. Sed et in ipso loco, in quo versamur, idem intelligunt omnes omnino interpretes, praeter solum Hieronymum. Neque enim eorum ullus est, qui dipsadem hic intelligat, aut aliud serpentis genus. Et falsum est, quod multi scribunt, Hieronymum hic sequi Graecos, in quibus pro צמאון non legitur διψὰς, ut volunt, sed δίψα, sitis: ut in Samaritano ࠑࠌࠀࠅࠍ et in utroque Arabe edito عطش, quae itidem sitim sonant: at in MSS. مفازة, et in utroque Chaldaeo בית צחונא, et in Syro, ܒܝܬ ܨܗܘܢܐ, locus siticulosus. Praeterea post צמאון Moses addit ἐπεξηγητικῶς, אשר אין מים, id est, locus siticulosus, in quo non sunt aquae. Itaque צמאון, ipso Mose interprete, non est dipsas, sed locus, in quo non sunt aquae, quod idem est cum loco siticuloso. Res clarior erit ex totius periodi lectione, quae sic ha-bet: Qui deduxit te in solitudine vasta et terribili, in qua prester et scorpio; וצמאון, et loco siticuloso, in quo non sunt aquae. Sic incidendam esse periodum, monet punctum Zakeph-Katon super vocem עקרב, scorpio. Sed Hieronymus illam incidit post vocem וצמאון, quam reddidit ac dipsas. Et sequentia אשר אין מים exponit, ac nullae omnino aquae, vocula ac de suo addita, ne referrentur ad proximum antecedens, quia nullus fuisset sensus horum verborum, Dipsas, in qua non sunt aquae. Denique, si צמאון esset dipsas, in harum vocum ordine נחש שרף ועקרב וצמאון posset id desiderari, quod scorpio inseritur inter duas serpentum species.

It is also found in Jerome's version of Deut 8:15: "And he was your guide in the great and terrible wilderness, in which there was the serpent burning with its breath, and the scorpion, and the dipsas." But that the Hebrew word צמאון tsimmaon, rendered there dipsas, means something else, namely thirst, dryness, a thirsty place, there are many arguments that do not merely suggest but convince. First, the constant use of the word in the sense we have stated. Thus Ps 107:33: "He turned rivers into a desert, and springs of water לצמאון, into a thirsty place." The Greeks: εἰς δίψαν [eis dipsan]; the Vulgate translator and Jerome: into thirst; the Chaldee: היך צחותא, like dryness; the Syriac: ܠܨܗܝܐ [לצהיא] III, 181 into dryness; the Arabic: عطش [ʿaṭash], thirst; the Ethiopic: ምጽማእ [məṣəmāʾ], a place of thirst. Again Isa 35:7: "And the parched place shall become a pool, וצמאון, and the thirsty place springs of water." Jerome: and the thirsty [ground]. Nor do the others render it much differently. The Greeks: τὴν διψῶσαν γῆν [tēn dipsōsan gēn]. The Arabic: الارض العطشانة [al-arḍ al-ʿaṭshāna], the thirsty land. The Chaldee: בית צחונא. The Syriac: ܒܝܬ ܨܗܘܢܐ [בית צהונא], a thirsty place. Moreover, in the very passage we are dealing with, absolutely all the translators understand the same thing, except Jerome alone. For none of them takes it here as the dipsas or any other kind of serpent. And what many write is false, that Jerome here follows the Greeks; for in them צמאון is not rendered διψὰς [dipsas], as these writers claim, but δίψα [dipsa], thirst; as in the Samaritan ࠑࠌࠀࠅࠍ [צמאון] and in both printed Arabic versions عطش [ʿaṭash], which likewise mean thirst; but in the MSS. مفازة [mafāza], and in both Chaldee versions בית צחונא, and in the Syriac ܒܝܬ ܨܗܘܢܐ [בית צהונא], a thirsty place. Furthermore, after צמאון Moses adds by way of explanation [ἐπεξηγητικῶς, epexēgētikōs] אשר אין מים, that is, a thirsty place, in which there are no waters. So צמאון, with Moses himself as interpreter, is not the dipsas but a place in which there are no waters, which is the same as a thirsty place. The matter will be clearer from reading the whole period, which runs thus: "Who led you through the vast and terrible wilderness, in which were the prester and the scorpion; וצמאון, and through a thirsty place, in which there are no waters." That the period is to be divided thus is indicated by the accent Zakeph-Katon over the word עקרב, scorpion. But Jerome divides it after the word וצמאון, which he rendered and the dipsas. And he renders the following words אשר אין מים as and no waters at all, adding the little word and on his own, so that they would not be referred to the immediately preceding word; for these words would have made no sense: The dipsas, in which there are no waters. Finally, if צמאון were the dipsas, in the order of these words נחש שרף ועקרב וצמאון one might find fault with the fact that the scorpion is inserted between two species of serpents. ¶
