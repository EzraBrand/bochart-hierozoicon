III, 65 col. 311 CHAPTER 27. ¶

Ad ea respondetur, quae in Caput praecedens possunt objici.

A reply to the objections that can be made to the preceding chapter. ¶

Cum probaverimus hactenus, רחם racham esse vulturem, nonnulla videntur posse objici, unde sequatur, avem esse, a vulture diversam. Primo enim Arabes, racham definientes, aquilam esse diserte dicunt. Alkazuinius, رخمة طاير النسر Rachama est avis aquila, seu aquilini generis. Alcamus, الانوق العقاب الرخمة Anuk, aquila, rachama. Proinde in Alka- col. 312 zuinio, rachama paritura lapidem afferre dicitur ex India, lapidem alium in ventre habentem, et dolores partus sublevantem, id est, lapidem ἀετίτην, seu aquilinum. Is enim ita describitur. Plinius libri decimi capite tertio, Est lapis iste praegnans, alio, cum quatias, intus in utero sonante. Dioscorides libro quinto, Ἀετίτης λίθος, ὁ ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι ἦχον ἀποτελῶν, ὡς ἑτέρου ἐγκύμων λίθου ὑπάρχων· Lapis aquilinus, qui commotus sonat, ceu lapide alio intra se concepto praegnans. Et paullo post, Ἐν δὲ τῷ καιρῷ τῆς ἀποτέξεως, περίαπτε τῷ μηρῷ, καὶ ἐκτὸς ὠδίνων τέξεται. Hora partus femori (parturientis) alliga, et pariet sine dolore. Chrysermus apud Stobaeum Sermone nonagesimo octavo, Ἐν Εὐφράτῃ, ποταμῷ τῆς Παρθίας, γεννᾶται λίθος ἀετίτης καλούμενος· ὃν αἱ μαῖαι ταῖς δυστοκούσαις ἐπὶ τὰς γαστέρας ἐπιτιθέασι, καὶ παραχρῆμα τί-κτουσιν ἄτερ ἀλγηδόνος. In Euphrate, Parthiae amne, lapis gignitur, aëtides dictus: quem obstetrices mulieribus in difficultate partus ventri imponunt, et statim sine dolore pariunt. Porro, cum ἀετίτην, seu aquilinum, ab aquila Graeci appellent, non a vulture γυπίτην, seu vulturinum, sequi videtur, lapidem illum ab aquila, non a vulture afferri. Quod et asserunt prisci scriptores. Plinius ubi supra, Tribus primis et quinto aquilarum generi inaedificatur nido lapis aëtites. Et libri 30. cap. 14. Lapis aëtites in aquilae repertus nido. Et libri trigesimi sexti capite vigesimo primo, Aëtites lapis ex argumento nominis magnam famam habet. III, 66 Reperitur in nidis aquilarum. Sextus, Aquilae lapis in ventre ejus aut nido invenitur. Isidorus Originum libri decimi sexti capite quinto, Aëtites (lege Aëtitae) lapides reperiuntur in nidis aquilarum. Horus de aquila, libri secundi capite quadragesimo sexto; Ἐκεῖνος ἀπὸ θαλάττης, ἢ ἀπὸ γῆς λίθον ἐπαίρει, καὶ τίθησιν εἰς τὴν ἰδίαν νεοττίαν· Haec e terra, vel mari sublatum lapidem in suum infert nidum. Philostratus libri secundi capite septimo, Τὰ δὲ τῶν ὀρνίθων τίς οὐκ οἶδεν, ὡς ἀετοὶ μὲν, καὶ πελαργοὶ, καλιὰς οὐκ ἂν πήξαιντο, μὴ πρό-τερον αὐταῖς ἐναρμόσαντες, ὁ μὲν τὸν ἀετίτην λίθον, ὁ δὲ τὸν λυχνίτην; Quid aves faciant, quis non novit, ut aquilae et ciconiae, quae nunquam nidos exstruunt, quin lapides illis imponant; illa quidem aëtitem, haec vero lychnitem lapidem? Aelianus quoque Historiae libri primi capite trigesimo quinto aquilas nidis imponere scribit τὸν λίθον, ὅσπερ οὖν ἐξ αὐτῶν ἀετίτης κέκληται, lapidem, qui ab ipsis aquilinus vocatur. Sed et ipsi Arabes hunc lapidem docent in aquilae nido reperiri. Damir in capite العقاب Alokab, quae est aquilae species, حجر العقاب حجر يشبه نوي التمر هندي اذا حرك سمع له صوي فاذا كسر لا يري فيه شي يوجد في عش العقاب والعقاب يجلبه من بلد الهند Lapis alokab (seu aquilae) lapis est similis nucleo tamarindi, quo commoto sonus auditur: tamen in eo fracto nihil videtur. Reperitur in nido alokab (aquilae). Et alokab (aquila) illum affert ex India. Et ipse Alkazuinius in capite De نسر seu aquila, اذا كان اوان بيضها فالنسر الذكر يمشي الي بلاد الهند وياتي بحجر يوجد في col. 313 بعض جبالها ويترك تحت الانثي لتخف عليه الالم Cum ova positura est aquila, mas, in Indiam se conferens, inde lapidem affert, qui in nonnullis terrae illius montibus reperitur, et foeminae supponit ad dolores ejus sublevandos. Itaque apud eundem Alkazuinium, cum avis rachama hunc ipsum lapidem dicatur ex India afferre, videtur omnino rachamam pro vera aquila sumsisse. Tertio, cum in censu avium prohibitarum דיה daja tam ex rationibus, quam ex veterum auctoritate, vulturem esse probaverimus, si רחם racham etiam est vultur, eadem avis in eadem serie orationis diversis nominibus bis a Mose interdicetur: quod non videtur posse ferri. Quarto, dici potest, in vulture nihil esse, quod respondeat nomini רחם, a misericordia ducto, et naturali στοργῇ erga pullos: cum vultur sit rapax avis et carnivora,

Since we have so far proved that רחם racham is the vulture, some objections seem possible from which it would follow that it is a bird different from the vulture. First, the Arabs, when they define the racham, say expressly that it is an eagle. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: رخمة طاير النسر [rakhma ṭāyir al-nasr] "The rachama is the eagle bird", that is, of the eagle kind. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الانوق العقاب الرخمة [al-anūq al-ʿuqāb al-rakhma] "Anuk, eagle, rachama." Accordingly, in Alkazuinius col. 312 the rachama, when about to give birth, is said to bring from India a stone that has another stone in its belly and relieves the pains of birth, that is, the ἀετίτης [aetitēs] stone, or eagle-stone. For it is described thus. Pliny, book 10, chapter 3: "This stone is pregnant, another stone sounding within, in its womb, when you shake it." Dioscorides, book 5: Ἀετίτης λίθος, ὁ ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι ἦχον ἀποτελῶν, ὡς ἑτέρου ἐγκύμων λίθου ὑπάρχων· [Aetitēs lithos, ho en tō kineisthai ēchon apotelōn, hōs heterou enkymōn lithou hyparchōn] "The eagle-stone, which makes a sound when moved, as though pregnant with another stone conceived within it." And a little later: Ἐν δὲ τῷ καιρῷ τῆς ἀποτέξεως, περίαπτε τῷ μηρῷ, καὶ ἐκτὸς ὠδίνων τέξεται. [En de tō kairō tēs apotexeōs, periapte tō mērō, kai ektos ōdinōn texetai] "At the hour of birth tie it to the thigh (of the woman in labour), and she will give birth without pain." Chrysermus in Stobaeus, Discourse 98: Ἐν Εὐφράτῃ, ποταμῷ τῆς Παρθίας, γεννᾶται λίθος ἀετίτης καλούμενος· ὃν αἱ μαῖαι ταῖς δυστοκούσαις ἐπὶ τὰς γαστέρας ἐπιτιθέασι, καὶ παραχρῆμα τίκτουσιν ἄτερ ἀλγηδόνος. [En Euphratē, potamō tēs Parthias, gennatai lithos aetitēs kaloumenos; hon hai maiai tais dystokousais epi tas gasteras epititheasi, kai parachrēma tiktousin ater algēdonos] "In the Euphrates, a river of Parthia, a stone is produced called aëtides, which midwives place on the bellies of women in difficult labour, and at once they give birth without pain." Further, since the Greeks call it ἀετίτης [aetitēs], or eagle-stone, from the eagle, and not γυπίτης [gypitēs], or vulture-stone, from the vulture, it seems to follow that this stone is brought by the eagle, not by the vulture. And this is what the ancient writers assert. Pliny, in the place cited: "In the nests of the first three kinds of eagles and of the fifth the aëtites stone is built in." And book 30, ch. 14: "The aëtites stone, found in the nest of the eagle." And book 36, chapter 21: "The aëtites stone has great fame from the meaning of its name. III, 66 It is found in the nests of eagles." Sextus: "The eagle's stone is found in its belly or nest." Isidore, Origines, book 16, chapter 5: "Aëtites (read Aëtitae) stones are found in the nests of eagles." Horus on the eagle, book 2, chapter 46: Ἐκεῖνος ἀπὸ θαλάττης, ἢ ἀπὸ γῆς λίθον ἐπαίρει, καὶ τίθησιν εἰς τὴν ἰδίαν νεοττίαν· [Ekeinos apo thalattēs, ē apo gēs lithon epairei, kai tithēsin eis tēn idian neottian] "It takes up a stone from the land or the sea and brings it into its own nest." Philostratus, book 2, chapter 7: Τὰ δὲ τῶν ὀρνίθων τίς οὐκ οἶδεν, ὡς ἀετοὶ μὲν, καὶ πελαργοὶ, καλιὰς οὐκ ἂν πήξαιντο, μὴ πρότερον αὐταῖς ἐναρμόσαντες, ὁ μὲν τὸν ἀετίτην λίθον, ὁ δὲ τὸν λυχνίτην; [Ta de tōn ornithōn tis ouk oiden, hōs aetoi men, kai pelargoi, kalias ouk an pēxainto, mē proteron autais enarmosantes, ho men ton aetitēn lithon, ho de ton lychnitēn] "Who does not know what birds do: that eagles and storks never build their nests without placing stones in them, the former the aëtites, the latter the lychnites stone?" Aelian too, History, book 1, chapter 35, writes that eagles place in their nests τὸν λίθον, ὅσπερ οὖν ἐξ αὐτῶν ἀετίτης κέκληται [ton lithon, hosper oun ex autōn aetitēs keklētai], "the stone which is called aquiline after them." But the Arabs themselves also teach that this stone is found in the eagle's nest. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter on العقاب [al-ʿuqāb] Alokab, which is a species of eagle: حجر العقاب حجر يشبه نوي التمر هندي اذا حرك سمع له صوي فاذا كسر لا يري فيه شي يوجد في عش العقاب والعقاب يجلبه من بلد الهند [ḥajar al-ʿuqāb ḥajar yushbih nawā al-tamr hindī idhā ḥurrika sumiʿa lahu ṣawī fa-idhā kusira lā yurā fīhi shay yūjad fī ʿushsh al-ʿuqāb wa-l-ʿuqāb yajlibuhu min balad al-hind] "The stone of alokab (or of the eagle) is a stone like the kernel of the tamarind; when it is moved a sound is heard, yet when it is broken nothing is seen in it. It is found in the nest of alokab (the eagle). And alokab (the eagle) brings it from India." And Alkazuinius himself, in the chapter On نسر [nasr], or the eagle: اذا كان اوان بيضها فالنسر الذكر يمشي الي بلاد الهند وياتي بحجر يوجد في col. 313 بعض جبالها ويترك تحت الانثي لتخف عليه الالم [idhā kāna awān bayḍihā fa-l-nasr al-dhakar yamshī ilā bilād al-hind wa-yaʾtī bi-ḥajar yūjad fī baʿḍ jibālihā wa-yutrak taḥt al-unthā li-takhiff ʿalayhi al-alam] "When the eagle is about to lay its eggs, the male goes off to India and brings from there a stone that is found in some mountains of that land, and places it under the female to relieve her pains." Therefore, since in the same Alkazuinius the bird rachama is said to bring this very stone from India, he seems altogether to have taken the rachama for a true eagle. Third, since we have proved, both from reasons and from the authority of the ancients, that in the list of forbidden birds דיה daja is the vulture, if רחם racham is also the vulture, the same bird will be forbidden twice by Moses under different names in the same sequence of discourse, which does not seem tolerable. Fourth, it can be said that there is nothing in the vulture that answers to the name רחם, derived from mercy and natural στοργή [storgē] towards the young, since the vulture is a rapacious and carnivorous bird, ¶

Arabibus, inquiunt, رخم racham est نسر neser, id est, aquila, non vultur. Respondemus, quosdam Arabes, non tamen omnes, sic definire. Damir enim diserte dicit الرخمه طاير ابقع يشبه النسر في الخلقة Rachama est avis varia, ad aquilae naturam accedens. Itaque ex Damiris sententia non est avis eadem cum aquila, sed duntaxat illi similis. Quod de vulture verissimum, de quo Phile:

Among the Arabs, they say, رخم [rakham] racham is نسر [nasr] neser, that is, the eagle, not the vulture. We answer that some Arabs, though not all, define it so. For Damir expressly says الرخمه طاير ابقع يشبه النسر في الخلقة [al-rakhma ṭāyir abqaʿ yushbih al-nasr fī al-khilqa]: The rachama is a variegated bird, approaching the nature of the eagle. Thus in Damir's opinion it is not the same bird as the eagle, but merely similar to it. This is most true of the vulture, of which Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)] says: ¶

Cujus caput, o rex, et os si quis eminus videat, aquilam esse dicit. Maxime in aquilarum genere percnopteri vulturinam habent speciem. Unde vocantur γυπ-αίετοι, vultur-aquilae. Plinius libri decimi capite tertio, Quarti (aquilarum) generis est percnopterus, eadem oripelargus, vulturina specie. Alii aegypios vocant. Aelianus Historiae libri secundi capite quadragesimo sexto, Τοὺς δὲ αἰγυπιοὺς, ἐν μεθορίῳ γυπῶν ὄντας καὶ αἰετῶν· Aegypios, qui inter vultures et aquilas medii sunt. Sed et aquilas ex diverso coitu tandem in vultures degenerare, volunt magni scriptores. Plinius, Haliaeeti suum genus non habent, sed ex diverso aquilarum coitu nascuntur. Id quidem, quod ex iis natum est, in ossifragis genus habet, e quibus vultures progenerantur minores: et ex iis magni, qui omnino non generant. Whose head and beak, O king, if anyone sees them from afar, he calls it an eagle. Especially among the kinds of eagle, the percnopteri have a vulture-like appearance. Hence they are called γυπαίετοι [gypaietoi], vulture-eagles. Aristotle, History, book 9, chapter 32: Ἔστι δ᾽ ἕτερον γένος (ἀετῶν) περκνόπτερος, etc. γυπὶ ὅμοιος· ὀρειπέλαργος καλεῖται, καὶ γυπαίετος [Esti d᾽ heteron genos (aetōn) perknopteros, etc. gypi homoios; oreipelargos kaleitai, kai gypaietos]. Moreover, great writers hold that eagles, through mixed mating, at length degenerate into vultures. Pliny: Sea eagles have no kind of their own, but are born from the mixed mating of eagles. What is born of them in turn has its kind among the ossifrages, from which smaller vultures are produced; and from these the great ones, which do not breed at all. This is taken from these words of Aristotle's Marvels: Ἐκ τοῦ col. 314 ζεύγους δὲ τῶν ἀετῶν θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων ἁλιαίετος γίνεται παραλλὰξ, ἕως ἂν σύζυγα γένηται, ἐκ δὲ τούτων οἱ περκνοὶ, καὶ γῦπες. οὗτοι δὲ οὐκέτι διορίζουσι περὶ τοὺς γύπας, ἀλλὰ γεννῶσι τοὺς μεγάλους γύπας· οὗτοι δ᾽ εἰσὶν ἄγονοι· [Ek tou zeugous de tōn aetōn thateron tōn agonōn haliaietos ginetai parallax, heōs an syzyga genētai, ek de toutōn hoi perknoi, kai gypes. houtoi de ouketi diorizousi peri tous gypas, alla gennōsi tous megalous gypas; houtoi d᾽ eisin agonoi;] But Pliny rendered this passage of Aristotle in such a way that whatever was difficult (namely the words θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων [thateron tōn agonōn] and παραλλὰξ ἕως ἂν σύζυγα γένηται [parallax heōs an syzyga genētai]) he skipped over with a carefree leap. III, 68 I think the first words, namely θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων [thateron tōn agonōn], are corrupt, and that θάτερον τῶν ἀγενῶν [thateron tōn agenōn] should be read instead, so that the sense of the whole passage is: "From a pair of eagles, one ignoble chick is born in alternation, namely the sea eagle. And when σύζυγα [syzyga] have been born, that is, two degenerate eagles, or two sea eagles, from them are born ossifrages; from the ossifrages, percni and vultures, whose kind changes no further. For these beget other vultures, but larger and sterile ones." Since it follows from what has been said that there is a close kinship between eagles and vultures, no one will be surprised that these birds are often confused by writers and one taken for the other. To prove this by examples: that the liver of Prometheus was devoured by an eagle is related by Hesiod, Theogony, verse 523; Aeschylus in Prometheus Bound, verse 996; Apollonius, Argonautica, book 2, verse 1254, and book 3, verse 851; Accius in Prometheus; Apollodorus, books 1 and 2; Diodorus, book 4; Philostratus, book 2; Plutarch in the book On Rivers, under Phasis; Lucian in Prometheus and in Sacrifices; Pherecydes, book 2; Agroetas in Scythica, book 13; Duris and Herodorus in the Scholiast on Apollonius; Servius on Virgil's sixth Eclogue, etc. Yet Fulgentius, in book 2 of the Mythologies, describes to us Prometheus offering his ever-renewed liver to a fierce vulture, and cites as authorities Nicagoras in the book Distemithea (I read On Prometheus), Aristoxenus in the book of Epimetheia, and Petronius Arbiter in these Hendecasyllables: ¶

Cui vultur jecur ultimum a) pererrat, Et pectus trahit intimasque fibras, Non est, quem tepidi b) vocant poëtae; Sed cordis mala, livor atque luxus.

The vulture that roams through the depths of the liver a), and tears at the breast and the innermost fibres, is not the one the idle b) poets speak of, but the evils of the heart: envy and excess. ¶

Ubi tamen, pro Non est, quem tepidi, aut lepidi, vocant poetae, quidam non male legunt, Non est, quem Tityum, etc. Quae lectio si admittatur, de Prometheo non egerit Petronius, sed de Tityo; de quo poeta summus libro sexto Aeneidos,

There, however, instead of There is none whom the warm or the charming poets call, some read, not badly, There is none whom Tityus, etc. If this reading is accepted, Petronius would be speaking not of Prometheus but of Tityus, of whom the greatest poet says in book 6 of the Aeneid: ¶

— rostroque immanis vultur obunco
immortale jecur tundens. — —

— and a monstrous vulture with hooked beak, pecking at the immortal liver. — — ¶

Et multo ante fabularum parens Odyss. λ'.

And long before, the father of fables, Odyss. λ' [l]: ¶

Γῦπε δέ μιν ἑκάτερθε παρημένῳ ἧπαρ ἔκειρον,
Δέρτρον ἔσω δύνοντες,

Γῦπε δέ μιν ἑκάτερθε παρημένῳ ἧπαρ ἔκειρον, Δέρτρον ἔσω δύνοντες, [Gype de min hekaterthe parēmenō hēpar ekeiron, Dertron esō dynontes,] ¶

Duo autem vultures utrimque incumbentes jecur fodiebant, cute intus penetrata. Fortasse igitur Petronii verborum prava lectio Fulgentium decepit. At illud Valerii Flacci de herba Promethea Argonauticῶn libri secundi versu 357.

And two vultures, perched on either side, were digging into his liver, having pierced the skin within. Perhaps, then, a corrupt reading of Petronius' words deceived Fulgentius. But consider the passage of Valerius Flaccus on the Promethean herb, Argonautica book 2, line 357: ¶

III, 69 — Prometheae florem de sanguine fibrae
Caucaseum tonitru, nutrita gramina, promit,
Quae sacer ille, nives inter, tristesque pruinas,
Durat, editque cruor, cum gutture vultur adeso
Tollitur e scopulis, et rostro irrorat aperto.

III, 69 — it brings forth the Caucasian flower from the blood of the Promethean flesh, herbage nourished by the thunder,
which that sacred gore, amid the snows and the grim frosts,
hardens and puts forth, when the vulture, its gullet sated,
rises from the crags and sprinkles it from its open beak. ¶

Illud, inquam, Valerii si conferas cum his Apollonii de hac eadem herba Argonauticῶn libri tertii versu 850.

If, I say, you compare this passage of Valerius with these lines of Apollonius on this same herb, Argonautica book 3, line 850: ¶

Πρωτοφυὲς τό γ᾽ ἀνέσχε καταστάξαντος ἔραζε
Αἰετοῦ ὠμηστέω κνημοῖς ἐνὶ Καυκασίοισιν,
Αἱματόεντ᾽ ἰχῶρα Προμηθῆος μογεροῖο.

Πρωτοφυὲς τό γ᾽ ἀνέσχε καταστάξαντος ἔραζε Αἰετοῦ ὠμηστέω κνημοῖς ἐνὶ Καυκασίοισιν, Αἱματόεντ᾽ ἰχῶρα Προμηθῆος μογεροῖο. [Prōtophyes to g' anesche katastaxantos eraze Aietou ōmēsteō knēmois eni Kaukasioisin, Haimatoent' ichōra Promēthēos mogeroio.] ¶

Palam videbis, Valerium ex aquila vulturem fecisse: quomodo e nostris Theophylactus, cum aquilas, de quibus Christus Matth. XXIV, 28. vult pro vulturibus haberi. Ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα, inquit, συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή. Ad cadaver aquilae, id est, vultures, statim congregantur. Gesnerus col. 315 quoque, Albertum et alios quosdam recentiores vulturem cum aquila passim confundere, observat. Et se tuetur Albertus Aristotelis auctoritate, quem aquilam et vulturem ad idem genus referre scribit: quod Gesnerus merito negat. Tamen Alberto causa fuit, cur id crederet. Is enim neque Aristotelis Graecum textum legerat, neque Latinam versionem ex Graeco textu, sed ex Arabico expressam. Ita enim ferebant tempora, quibus vixit Albertus. Tum nemo erat inter Latinos, qui Graecos scriptores Graece legeret, aut Latine redderet: tantum Arabes consulebantur, qui, Graece quidquid extabat, in suam linguam transtulerant, et ex illorum versionibus Graeca Latine utcunque reddebant barbari interpretes. Arabes porro vocem نسر neser, quae proprie aquilam sonat, ad vulturem usque extendunt. Unde est, quod τῷ neser ea quandoque tribuunt, quae sunt vulturis propria. Sic ad vulturem pertinet, quod dicit Alkazuinius, النسر يتبع العساكر لطعمه في لحم القتال τὸν neser castra sequi, ut occisorum carnibus vescatur. Item, ياكل الجيف حتي لا يقدر علي الطيران Cadaveribus ita saturari, ut volare amplius non possit. Sed et vulturis potius, quam aquilae, sunt propria haec Damiris in cap. نسر Neser, وهو حاد البصر يري الجيفة من اربع ماية فرسخ وكذلك حاسة شمه الي النهايه لكنه اذا شم الطيب مات لوقت Est acutissimi visus, ita, ut cadaver videat a quadringentis parasangis. Et odoratus pariter in eo est intensissimus. Proinde, cum sentit unguentum, ipso momento moritur. Nam visu quidem III, 70 aquila maxime valet, ut suo loco c) probatum; sed odorandi vis in vulture major esse creditur. Unde Lucretius libro quarto,

you will plainly see that Valerius has made a vulture out of the eagle, just as among our own writers Theophylact does when he wants the eagles of which Christ speaks in Matth. XXIV, 28. to be taken as vultures. Ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα, he says, συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή [Epi nekron sōma … synagontai oxeōs hoi aetoi, hoi gypes dēladē]. To a corpse the eagles, that is, the vultures, quickly gather. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] col. 315 also observes that Albertus and certain other more recent writers often confuse the vulture with the eagle. And Albertus defends himself with the authority of Aristotle, who, he writes, assigns the eagle and the vulture to the same genus; this Gesner rightly denies. Yet Albertus had a reason for believing it. For he had read neither the Greek text of Aristotle nor a Latin version made from the Greek text, but one made from the Arabic. Such were the times in which Albertus lived. Then there was no one among the Latins who read the Greek writers in Greek or rendered them into Latin: only the Arabs were consulted, who had translated into their own language whatever existed in Greek, and from their versions barbarous translators rendered the Greek works into Latin after a fashion. The Arabs, moreover, extend the word نسر [nasr] neser, which properly means eagle, even to the vulture. Hence they sometimes ascribe to the neser things that are proper to the vulture. Thus what Alkazuinius says belongs to the vulture: النسر يتبع العساكر لطعمه في لحم القتال [al-nasr yatbaʿu al-ʿasākir li-ṭuʿmihi fī laḥm al-qitāl] that the neser follows armies, in order to feed on the flesh of the slain. Likewise, ياكل الجيف حتي لا يقدر علي الطيران [yākulu al-jiyaf ḥattā lā yaqdiru ʿalā al-ṭayarān] that it gorges itself on carcasses so much that it can no longer fly. But these words of Damir too, in the chapter نسر [nasr] Neser, are proper to the vulture rather than to the eagle: وهو حاد البصر يري الجيفة من اربع ماية فرسخ وكذلك حاسة شمه الي النهايه لكنه اذا شم الطيب مات لوقت [wa-huwa ḥādd al-baṣar yarā al-jīfa min arbaʿ māya farsakh wa-kadhālika ḥāssat shammihi ilā al-nihāya lākinnahu idhā shamma al-ṭīb māta li-waqtihi] It has very keen sight, so that it sees a carcass from four hundred parasangs. Its sense of smell is likewise extremely intense. Accordingly, when it smells perfume, it dies on the instant. For in sight the III, 70 eagle is indeed strongest, as was proven in its place c); but the power of smell is believed to be greater in the vulture. Hence Lucretius, book 4: ¶

Mellis apes quamvis longe ducantur odore,
Vulturiique cadaveribus. — —

Though bees are drawn from far by the scent of honey,
and vultures by carcasses. — — ¶

Et Apuleius in Apologia prima, Sin vero e naribus nidorem domesticum praesensit, vincit idem sagacitate odorandi canes et vulturios. Cui enim cani, cui vulturio Alexandrini coeli quidquam ab usque Aeensium finibus oboleat? Et Sidonius libri septimi Epistola decima quarta, Proferunt odorandi subtilitatem, tanquam non praecedat vultur olfactu. Hinc versus in ore vulgi,

And Apuleius in the first Apology: But if he has caught with his nostrils the smell of home cooking, he surpasses dogs and vultures in keenness of scent. For to what dog, to what vulture would anything of the Alexandrian sky be perceptible as far away as the borders of the Aeenses? And Sidonius, book 7, letter 14: They display subtlety of smell, as if the vulture did not excel in scent. Hence the verse in common use: ¶

Vultur odoratu, praecellit aranea tactu.

The vulture excels in smell, the spider in touch. ¶

Isidorus tamen, Originum libri duodecimi capite septimo, vulturibus aquilas jungens, Vultures, inquit, sicut et aquilae, etiam ultra maria cadavera sentiunt. Sed illum, haec scribentem, visum potius, quam odoratum respexisse, palam faciunt sequentia: Altius quippe volantes, multa, quae montium obscuritate celantur, ex alto illi conspiciunt. Verum, ut caetera taceam, hoc saltem, quod addit Arabs, neser, seu aquilam unguenti odore offendi, nonnisi de vulture a veteribus scribi constat. Sic Aristoteles in Mirabilibus, Λέγεται καὶ τοὺς γῦπας ὑπὸ τῆς τῶν μύρων ὀσμῆς ἀποθνήσκειν· Ajunt etiam vultures ab unguentorum odore exanimari. Theophrastus libri sexti De causis plantarum capite quarto quaedam animalia asserit ὑπὸ τῶν ἡδίστων ἡμῖν ἀναιρεῖσθαι, καθάπερ οἱ γῦπες ὑπὸ τῶν μύρων, ab iis perimi, quae nobis suavissima sunt, ut vultures ab unguentis. Et in libro De odoribus, Πονεῖν δ᾽ ἔνια φαίνεται ταῖς ὀσμαῖς, καὶ ταῖς col. 316 εὐωδίαις, εἴπερ ἀληθὲς τὸ ἐπὶ τῶν γυπῶν· Quaedam etiam odore et fragrantia laedi apparet, si verum est, quod de vulturibus traditur. Plinius libri undecimi capite quinquagesimo tertio, Vultures, unguento qui fugantur, alios appetunt odores. Aelianus Historiae libri tertii capite septimo, Εὐωδία δὲ, καὶ μῦρον γυ-ψὶν αἰτία θανάτου· Fragrantia et unguentum vulturibus mortem affert. Et libri quarti capite decimo octavo, Γυπῶν γε μὴν τὸ μῦρον ὄλεθρός ἐστι· Vulturum pernicies unguentum est. Clemens libri secundi Paedagogi capite octavo, Μὴ λά-θωμεν, ὥσπερ οἱ γῦπες τὰ μύρα βδελυττόμενοι· Ne inscii, tanquam vultures, ab unguentis abhorreamus. Eustathius in Hexaëmeron, Φασὶ δὲ τὸν γῦπα τὰ ῥόδινα μύρα χρισθέντα ἀποθνήσκειν· Vulturem ajunt rosaceo unguento unctum interimi. Phile De animalibus capite secundo, ubi de vulturibus;

Isidore, however, in Etymologies book 12, chapter 7, joining eagles to vultures, says: "Vultures, like eagles, perceive carcasses even beyond the seas." But what follows makes it plain that in writing this he had sight rather than smell in view: "For, flying higher, they see from on high many things that are hidden by the darkness of the mountains." But, to pass over the rest, at least what the Arab adds, that the neser or eagle is harmed by the smell of ointment, is clearly written by the ancients only of the vulture. Thus Aristotle in the Mirabilia: Λέγεται καὶ τοὺς γῦπας ὑπὸ τῆς τῶν μύρων ὀσμῆς ἀποθνήσκειν [Legetai kai tous gypas hypo tēs tōn myrōn osmēs apothnēskein]; "They say also that vultures are killed by the smell of ointments." Theophrastus, On the Causes of Plants book 6, chapter 4, asserts that certain animals ὑπὸ τῶν ἡδίστων ἡμῖν ἀναιρεῖσθαι, καθάπερ οἱ γῦπες ὑπὸ τῶν μύρων [hypo tōn hēdistōn hēmin anaireisthai, kathaper hoi gypes hypo tōn myrōn], "are destroyed by those things which are most pleasant to us, as vultures by ointments." And in the book On Odours: Πονεῖν δ᾽ ἔνια φαίνεται ταῖς ὀσμαῖς, καὶ ταῖς col. 316 εὐωδίαις, εἴπερ ἀληθὲς τὸ ἐπὶ τῶν γυπῶν [Ponein d᾽ enia phainetai tais osmais, kai tais euōdiais, eiper alēthes to epi tōn gypōn]; "Some creatures also appear to be harmed by smell and fragrance, if what is reported of vultures is true." Pliny, book 11, chapter 53: "Vultures, which are put to flight by ointment, seek other odours." Aelian, History book 3, chapter 7: Εὐωδία δὲ, καὶ μῦρον γυψὶν αἰτία θανάτου [Euōdia de, kai myron gypsin aitia thanatou]; "Fragrance and ointment bring death to vultures." And book 4, chapter 18: Γυπῶν γε μὴν τὸ μῦρον ὄλεθρός ἐστι [Gypōn ge mēn to myron olethros esti]; "Ointment is the bane of vultures." Clement, Paedagogus book 2, chapter 8: Μὴ λάθωμεν, ὥσπερ οἱ γῦπες τὰ μύρα βδελυττόμενοι [Mē lathōmen, hōsper hoi gypes ta myra bdelyttomenoi]; "Let us not unwittingly, like vultures, abhor ointments." Eustathius on the Hexaemeron: Φασὶ δὲ τὸν γῦπα τὰ ῥόδινα μύρα χρισθέντα ἀποθνήσκειν [Phasi de ton gypa ta rhodina myra christhenta apothnēskein]; "They say that the vulture, anointed with rose ointment, is killed." Philes, On Animals chapter 2, where he treats of vultures: ¶

Οἱ τῷ μύρῳ θνήσκουσι, κανθάρων δίκην· III, 71 Qui unguento perimuntur, ut canthari. Vides, lector, ubique de vulturibus id tradi, nusquam de aquilis. Apparet igitur, Arabes, qui τῷ neser hoc idem tribuunt, nomine neser non solam aquilam, sed et vulturem significasse. Itaque cum apud Arabes nomen neser ad aquilas et vultures generale sit, ex eo, quod rachamam censent inter species τοῦ neser, non sequitur, rachamam ab illis non haberi pro vulture. Quid, quod etiam Philosophus hoc vulturis genus inter aquilas censet, ut infra docebitur, quamvis vulturinam speciem et mores illi assignet, ac proinde vocet γυπαίετον?

Οἱ τῷ μύρῳ θνήσκουσι, κανθάρων δίκην [Hoi tō myrō thnēskousi, kantharōn dikēn]· III, 71 "Who are killed by ointment, like beetles." You see, reader, that this is everywhere reported of vultures, nowhere of eagles. It is evident, therefore, that the Arabs, who attribute this same thing to the neser, meant by the name neser not the eagle alone but also the vulture. And so, since among the Arabs the name neser is general to eagles and vultures, it does not follow from their counting the rachama among the species of the neser that the rachama is not taken by them for a vulture. What of the fact that even the Philosopher counts this kind of vulture among the eagles, as will be shown below, although he assigns it a vulturine form and habits, and accordingly calls it γυπαίετον [gypaieton]? ¶

II. Secunda objectio haec erat, rachamam in Alkazuinio ex India afferre lapidem abutacion, concavum et sonorum, et ad partus utilem: qui cum non possit alius esse, quam lapis ἀετίτης, seu aquilinus, etiam avem, a qua affertur, videri esse aquilam. Respondeo, Veteres quidem soli aquilae, sed abhinc aliquot saeculis recentiores etiam vulturi id tribuisse. Hieronymus, vel quisquis auctor est Epistolae De cereo Paschali sub Hieronymi nomine; Vultur, cum coeperit ova edere, primum de terra Indiae instintium sumit: et est tanquam nux, et intus habet aliquid, quod movetur et sonum reddit. Hoc sibi apponit, et multos quidem facit foetus, unus vero remanet. Eustathius in Hexaëmeron de vulture. Καὶ ἔγγυος (lege ἔγκυος) γενόμενος πορεύεται εἰς τὴν Ἰνδικὴν, καὶ λαβὼν τὸν εὐτόκιον λίθον οὕτω καλούμενον, ἐπάνω αὐτοῦ καθέζεται, συνεχόμενος ὠδῖσι, καὶ ἀποτίκτει. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ λίθος περιφερὴς ὡς κάρυον· καὶ κινούμενος ἦχον ἐκ-πέμπει ὡς κώδων, ἔνδοθεν ἑτέρου λίθου κινουμένου. Cum concepit vultur, in Indiam proficiscitur, et lapidem, quem vocant eutocion, suscipit, super quem in partus doloribus sedet, et parit. Est autem lapis rotundus nucis instar: et, cum quatitur, sonum edit ut tintinnabulum, alio lapide intus moto. Recte εὐτόκιον λίθον vocat ab argumento, quia partus promovet, quamvis alii ὠκυτόκιον malint. Harpocratio Alexandrinus, Ὠκυτόκιος λίθος ἐστὶν ἐοικὼς τῷ ἀετίτῃ, μικρότε-ρος δὲ ἐκείνου· πλὴν ἀπηχῶν ὡς ἐκεῖνος ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι παρὰ τὸ οὖς. ἔστι δὲ καὶ λεῖος τῇ θέᾳ. Ocytocius lapis est aëtiti similis, illo minor: sed, ad aurem cum commovetur, eodem modo sonans. Levis autem apparet. Hac voce Dioscorides aliquoties utitur, sed adjective solum: ut, cum libro quinto scribit, jaspides esse φυλακτήρια περίαπτα, καὶ ὠκυτόκια μηρῷ περιαπτόμενα, inter amuleta, quae alligantur, et femori adalligata partum accelerare. Ex his emenda voces nihili, in Epistola De cereo, Instintium; et in Alkazuinio ابوطاقيون. Et اوطغيون εὐτόκιον rescribe, vel, si mavis اقوطغيون ὠκυτόκιον.

II. The second objection was this: that the rachama in Alkazuinius brings from India the stone abutacion, hollow and resonant, and useful for childbirth; and since this can be no other than the ἀετίτης [aetitēs] stone, or eagle-stone, the bird which brings it also seems to be an eagle. I reply that the ancients indeed attributed this to the eagle alone, but that some centuries ago more recent writers attributed it also to the vulture. Jerome, or whoever is the author of the Epistle On the Paschal Candle under Jerome's name: "The vulture, when it has begun to lay eggs, first takes from the land of India the instintium: and it is like a nut, and has within something which moves and gives out a sound. This it places by itself, and it produces many young, but one remains." Eustathius on the Hexaemeron, of the vulture: Καὶ ἔγγυος (read ἔγκυος) γενόμενος πορεύεται εἰς τὴν Ἰνδικὴν, καὶ λαβὼν τὸν εὐτόκιον λίθον οὕτω καλούμενον, ἐπάνω αὐτοῦ καθέζεται, συνεχόμενος ὠδῖσι, καὶ ἀποτίκτει. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ λίθος περιφερὴς ὡς κάρυον· καὶ κινούμενος ἦχον ἐκπέμπει ὡς κώδων, ἔνδοθεν ἑτέρου λίθου κινουμένου [Kai engyos (engkyos) genomenos poreuetai eis tēn Indikēn, kai labōn ton eutokion lithon houtō kaloumenon, epanō autou kathezetai, synechomenos ōdisi, kai apotiktei. Esti de ho lithos peripherēs hōs karyon; kai kinoumenos ēchon ekpempei hōs kōdōn, endothen heterou lithou kinoumenou]. "When the vulture has conceived, it sets out for India, and takes up the stone which they call eutocion, on which it sits in the pains of labour, and gives birth. The stone is round like a nut; and when it is shaken it gives out a sound like a bell, another stone moving inside." He rightly calls it εὐτόκιον λίθον [eutokion lithon] from its property, because it promotes childbirth, though others prefer ὠκυτόκιον [ōkytokion]. Harpocration of Alexandria: Ὠκυτόκιος λίθος ἐστὶν ἐοικὼς τῷ ἀετίτῃ, μικρότερος δὲ ἐκείνου· πλὴν ἀπηχῶν ὡς ἐκεῖνος ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι παρὰ τὸ οὖς. ἔστι δὲ καὶ λεῖος τῇ θέᾳ [Ōkytokios lithos estin eoikōs tō aetitē, mikroteros de ekeinou; plēn apēchōn hōs ekeinos en tō kineisthai para to ous. esti de kai leios tē thea]. "The ocytocius stone is like the aetites, but smaller than it; yet, when shaken by the ear, it sounds in the same way. And it appears smooth." Dioscorides uses this word several times, but only as an adjective: as when he writes in book 5 that jaspers are φυλακτήρια περίαπτα, καὶ ὠκυτόκια μηρῷ περιαπτόμενα [phylaktēria periapta, kai ōkytokia mērō periaptomena], "among the amulets that are tied on, and, tied to the thigh, hasten childbirth." From these, correct the meaningless words, in the Epistle On the Candle, Instintium; and in Alkazuinius ابوطاقيون [abūṭāqiyūn]. And write instead اوطغيون [awṭghiyūn] εὐτόκιον [eutokion], or, if you prefer, اقوطغيون [aqūṭghiyūn] ὠκυτόκιον [ōkytokion]. ¶

III. Ad tertiam objectionem respondemus, דיה daja, et רחם racham, a Mose separatim prohiberi; quamvis utraque vox vulturem sonet: quia sunt col. 317 vulturum diversae species. Cum enim דיה daja sit vultur niger, ut supra III, 72 d) probavimus, qui Arabice dicitur انوق anuk: רחם racham est طاير ابقع avis varia, ut scribit Damir; quia solis alis nigricat, caetera alba. Ita definiunt Bellunensis et Giggeius. Proinde equus, quem a colorum mixtura vernaculo sermone picam vocamus, Arabice dicitur ارخم archam: et ovis ita varia رخما rachma. Alcamus شاة رخما بيضا راسها سودا سيرها Ovis rachma, quae capite albo est, caetera nigra. Item, فرس ارخم Equus archamo, qui est ita varius. Tanquam ab ave sumto nomine, cui colores pariter misti. Proinde jure merito conjicit Vir, in his literis princeps, hac voce significari Aristotelis περκνόπτερον, seu ὀρειπέλαργον· qui percnopterus vocatur a nigris alis: et oripelargus, id est, ciconia montana, quia, ciconiae instar, partim albus est, partim niger. Idem a Philosopho sic censetur inter aquilas, ut γυπάετος, id est, vultur-aquila, nominetur; quia externa specie est γυπὶ ὅμοιος, vulturi similis, et vulturinos habet mores. Non possum capere, unde haec suboluerint Anglicano interpreti, qui Gier-eagle, id est, vultur-aquilam, hic habet. An Gesnerum sequitur, qui Bellunensem viderat? Sunt etiam ex Arabibus, quibus rachama est ossifraga. Golius noster, Per Persicum استخوان رند id est, ossifraga, exponitur apud Meidanum et alios. Pandectarius Arabs, Arathamathi, id est, frangens ossa. Arathamathi corruptum ex الرخمة arrachamath: et frangens ossa est ossifraga. Sed prius verum.

III. To the third objection we reply that דיה daja and רחם racham are prohibited separately by Moses, although both words mean vulture, because there are col. 317 different species of vultures. For while דיה daja is the black vulture, as we proved above III, 72 d), which in Arabic is called انوق [anūq] anuk, רחם racham is طاير ابقع [ṭāyir abqaʿ] a variegated bird, as Damir writes, since it is blackish only in its wings and white in the rest. So Bellunensis and Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)] define it. Accordingly a horse which, from the mixture of its colours, we call in the vernacular a pica [magpie, piebald] is called in Arabic ارخم [arkham] archam, and a sheep so variegated رخما [rakhmā] rachma. Alcamus: شاة رخما بيضا راسها سودا سيرها [shāh rakhmā bayḍā rāsuhā sūdā sayruhā] "A rachma sheep, which has a white head and the rest black." Likewise فرس ارخم [faras arkham] an archamo horse, which is variegated in this way. The name is taken, as it were, from the bird, in which the colours are likewise mixed. Hence a man foremost in these studies rightly conjectures that this word signifies Aristotle's περκνόπτερον [perknopteron], or ὀρειπέλαργον [oreipelargon]; it is called percnopterus from its black wings, and oripelargus, that is, mountain stork, because, like a stork, it is partly white and partly black. The Philosopher counts this same bird among the eagles in such a way that it is named γυπάετος [gypaetos], that is, vulture-eagle, because in outward appearance it is γυπὶ ὅμοιος [gypi homoios], like a vulture, and has the habits of a vulture. I cannot understand how the English translator got wind of this, who has Gier-eagle, that is, vulture-eagle, here. Does he follow Gesner, who had seen Bellunensis? There are also some among the Arabs for whom the rachama is the ossifrage. Our Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: "It is explained by Meidanus and others by the Persian استخوان رند [ustukhwān rand], that is, ossifrage." The Arabic Pandectarius: "Arathamathi, that is, breaking bones." Arathamathi is corrupted from الرخمة [al-rakhma] arrachamath, and breaking bones is the ossifrage. But the former is true. ¶

IV. Ad quartam objectionem, Nomen רחם racham, ductum ἀπὸ τῆς φι-λοστοργίας, parum convenire vulturi, cum sit avis immanissima: Respondeo, Vulturem apud vulgus male audire, et pro ave immani haberi; quia humana carne vescitur: sed Graecos et Aegyptios, qui vulturis naturam penitius introspexerunt, longe aliter de illo pronuntiare. Herodorus Ponticus apud Plutarchum in Romulo, et in Romanis Quaestionibus, vulturem omnium sui generis animalium esse δικαιότατον, καὶ ἀβλαβέστατον, justissimum, et minime noxium, tribus probat rationibus. Prima est, quod οὐδενὸς ἅπτεται ζῶντος, οὐδὲ ἀπο-κτίννυσιν ἔμψυχον οὐδὲν, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, καὶ τὰ νυκτινόμα, χρῆται δὲ τοῖς ἄλλοις ἀποθανοῦσιν, nihil vivum attingit, neque animatum quidquam interficit, ut aquilae, et accipitres, et nocturnae aves, sed morticinis solum utitur. Secunda, quod καὶ τούτων τὰ ὁμόφυλα παρίησι· πετεινοῦ γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἑώρακε γῦπα γευόμενον, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, μάλιστα τὰ συγγενῆ διώκουσι, καὶ κόπτουσιν· etiam e morticinis ea praeterit, quae sunt sui generis: aves enim a vulture gustari nemo vidit, quas aquilae et accipitres potissimum, insectantur et occidunt. III, 73 Tertia, quod ἀνθρώποις ἀβλαβέστατός ἐστιν, οὔτε καρπὸν ἀφανίζων, οὔτε φυτὸν, οὔτε ζῶον ἥμερον κακουργῶν, hominibus minime nocet, cum neque fruges perdat, neque plantas, neque ullum mansuetum animal laedat. Quod sequutus Eustathius in Hexaëmeron observat, σπέρματα μὴ βλάττειν αὐτὸν, μηδέ τι ἔμψυχον διαλυμαίνεσθαι, καὶ μηδὲ νεκρῷ προσέρχεσθαι πετεινῷ διὰ τὴν συγγένειαν, eum neque sata laedere, neque nocere cuiquam animato, neque ad ul-lius avis cadaver accedere propter cognationem. Proinde vultur a Romanis habebatur pro fausta alite. Et Hercules quoque (teste Herodoro apud Plutarchum) πάντων μάλιστα γυψὶν ἐπὶ πράξεως ἀρχῇ φανεῖσιν ἔχαιρεν, in rerum agendarum initiis vulturibus oblatis maxime delectabatur. Quid, quod Indos mortuorum corpora vulturibus objecisse, auctor est Sextus Philosophus libri tertii Pyr- col. 318 rhoniarum Hypotheseωn capite decimo quarto? Quale quid in usu fuisse etiam apud Persas, multis probat Brissonius in fine libri secundi De regno Persico. Sed et Hispanos idem factitasse, testantur haec Silii libri decimi tertii versu 470.

IV. To the fourth objection, that the name רחם racham, derived ἀπὸ τῆς φιλοστοργίας [apo tēs philostorgias], is ill suited to the vulture, since it is a most savage bird, I reply that the vulture has a bad name among the common people and is held to be a savage bird because it feeds on human flesh; but the Greeks and Egyptians, who looked more deeply into the nature of the vulture, pronounce very differently about it. Herodorus Ponticus, in Plutarch's Romulus and in the Roman Questions, proves by three arguments that the vulture is of all animals of its kind δικαιότατον, καὶ ἀβλαβέστατον [dikaiotaton, kai ablabestaton], the most just, and the least harmful. The first is that οὐδενὸς ἅπτεται ζῶντος, οὐδὲ ἀποκτίννυσιν ἔμψυχον οὐδὲν, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, καὶ τὰ νυκτινόμα, χρῆται δὲ τοῖς ἄλλοις ἀποθανοῦσιν [oudenos haptetai zōntos, oude apoktinnysin empsychon ouden, hōs aetoi, kai hierakes, kai ta nyktinoma, chrētai de tois allois apothanousin], it touches nothing living, nor kills any animate creature, as eagles and hawks and nocturnal birds do, but feeds only on carcasses. The second, that καὶ τούτων τὰ ὁμόφυλα παρίησι· πετεινοῦ γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἑώρακε γῦπα γευόμενον, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, μάλιστα τὰ συγγενῆ διώκουσι, καὶ κόπτουσιν· [kai toutōn ta homophyla pariēsi; peteinou gar oudeis heōrake gypa geuomenon, hōs aetoi, kai hierakes, malista ta syngenē diōkousi, kai koptousin], even among carcasses it passes over those of its own kind: for no one has seen birds tasted by a vulture, which eagles and hawks above all pursue and kill. III, 73 The third, that ἀνθρώποις ἀβλαβέστατός ἐστιν, οὔτε καρπὸν ἀφανίζων, οὔτε φυτὸν, οὔτε ζῶον ἥμερον κακουργῶν [anthrōpois ablabestatos estin, oute karpon aphanizōn, oute phyton, oute zōon hēmeron kakourgōn], it does very little harm to men, since it neither destroys crops nor plants, nor injures any tame animal. Following this, Eustathius on the Hexaëmeron observes σπέρματα μὴ βλάττειν αὐτὸν, μηδέ τι ἔμψυχον διαλυμαίνεσθαι, καὶ μηδὲ νεκρῷ προσέρχεσθαι πετεινῷ διὰ τὴν συγγένειαν [spermata mē blattein auton, mēde ti empsychon dialymainesthai, kai mēde nekrō proserchesthai peteinō dia tēn syngeneian], that it neither damages sown fields, nor harms any animate creature, nor approaches the carcass of any bird on account of kinship. Hence the vulture was held by the Romans to be a bird of good omen. And Hercules too (according to Herodorus in Plutarch) πάντων μάλιστα γυψὶν ἐπὶ πράξεως ἀρχῇ φανεῖσιν ἔχαιρεν [pantōn malista gypsin epi praxeōs archē phaneisin echairen], was delighted above all when vultures appeared at the outset of his undertakings. What of the fact that the Indians threw the bodies of the dead to the vultures, as Sextus the Philosopher reports in book 3 of the Pyr- col. 318 rhonian Hypotheses, chapter 14? That something of the kind was also customary among the Persians is proved at length by Brissonius at the end of book 2 of De regno Persico. And that the Spaniards did the same is attested by these lines of Silius, book 13, line 470: ¶

Tellure, ut perhibent, is mos antiquus Ibera,
Exanima obscoenus consumit corpora vultur.

In the Iberian land, as they say, this is the ancient custom:
the foul vulture consumes the lifeless bodies. ¶

Quod de iis intelligendum, qui in bello occubuerant pro patria fortiter dimicantes. Ita enim idem libri tertii versu 340.

This is to be understood of those who had fallen in war, fighting bravely for their country. For so the same poet says in book 3, line 340: ¶

Venere et Celtae sociati nomen Iberis.
His pugna cecidisse decus, corpusque cremari
Tale nefas. Coelo credunt Superisque referri,
Impastus carpat si membra jacentia vultur.

The Celts came too, who had joined their name to the Iberians. For them it is an honour to have fallen in battle, and to burn the body such a one is a sacrilege. They believe they are borne to heaven and the gods above if the hungry vulture tears at their limbs as they lie. ¶

Et Aelianus Historiae libri decimi capite vigesimo secundo, Βαρκαῖοι, γένος δὲ τοῦτό ἐστιν Ἑσπέριον, τῶν ἀποθανόντων νόσῳ τοὺς νεκροὺς ὑβρίζοντες, ὡς ἀνάνδρως καὶ μαλακῶς τεθνεώτων, θάπτουσι πυρί· τοὺς δὲ ἐν πολέμῳ βίον καταστρέψαν-τας, ὡς καλοὺς, καὶ ἀγαθοὺς, καὶ ἀρετῆς μετειληχότας, γυψὶ προβάλλουσιν ἱερὸν τὸ ζῶον εἶναι πεπιστευκότες. Barcaei, gens Hispanica, ex aliquo morbo mortuos per contumeliam igne cremant, ut muliebriter et ignaviter defunctos: eos vero, qui in bello occubuerunt, ut viros bonos, et fortes, et eximiae virtutis, vulturibus objiciunt, quia sacrum putant esse hoc animal. Barcaei quidem in Hispania nulli sunt: sed legendum Βακκαῖοι, ut in Stephano; Βακκαῖοι Ἱσπανίας ἔθνος, Baccaei gens Hispanica: nempe qui aliis Vaccaei. Neque dubium, quin sic scripserit Aelianus, qui, post inserta nonnulla de Romuli duodecim vulturibus, addit, apud Aegyptios vulturem esse Iunoni sacrum, et ejus pennis ornari caput Isidis, et τῶν προπυλαίων ὀρόφους, vestibulorum fastigia.

And Aelian, History, book 10, chapter 22: Βαρκαῖοι, γένος δὲ τοῦτό ἐστιν Ἑσπέριον, τῶν ἀποθανόντων νόσῳ τοὺς νεκροὺς ὑβρίζοντες, ὡς ἀνάνδρως καὶ μαλακῶς τεθνεώτων, θάπτουσι πυρί· τοὺς δὲ ἐν πολέμῳ βίον καταστρέψαντας, ὡς καλοὺς, καὶ ἀγαθοὺς, καὶ ἀρετῆς μετειληχότας, γυψὶ προβάλλουσιν ἱερὸν τὸ ζῶον εἶναι πεπιστευκότες. [Barkaioi, genos de touto estin Hesperion, tōn apothanontōn nosō tous nekrous hybrizontes, hōs anandrōs kai malakōs tethneōtōn, thaptousi pyri; tous de en polemō bion katastrepsantas, hōs kalous, kai agathous, kai aretēs meteilēchotas, gypsi proballousin hieron to zōon einai pepisteukotes.] "The Barcaei, a Spanish people, burn with fire, by way of insult, those who have died of some disease, as having died in a womanly and cowardly fashion; but those who have fallen in war, as good and brave men of outstanding virtue, they cast to the vultures, because they consider this animal sacred." There are, indeed, no Barcaei in Spain; but we should read Βακκαῖοι [Bakkaioi], as in Stephanus: Βακκαῖοι Ἱσπανίας ἔθνος [Bakkaioi Hispanias ethnos], "the Baccaei, a Spanish people", namely those whom others call Vaccaei. Nor is there any doubt that Aelian wrote it so; after inserting some remarks about Romulus' twelve vultures, he adds that among the Egyptians the vulture is sacred to Juno, and that the head of Isis is adorned with its feathers, as are τῶν προπυλαίων ὀρόφους [tōn propylaiōn orophous], the gables of the porches. ¶

Sed et aliud est, quod a lectore nolim oscitanter praeteriri; quia inde nostrae interpretationi magnum pondus accedit. Horus Apollo auctor est, eosdem III, 74 Aegyptios in Hieroglyphicis suis misericordem picto vulture significasse. Verba sunt libri primi capite undecimo, Μητέρα δὲ γράφοντες, ἢ ἐλεήμονα, γῦπα ζωγραφοῦσι. Ut matrem, aut misericordem significent, vulturem pingunt. At-que ut scias, hanc ipsam misericordiam intelligi, quam Hebraei proprio nomine רחם rechem appellant, id est, τὴν πρὸς τὰ τέκνα φιλοστοργίαν, hanc causam affert idem scriptor, cur vulturis imagine pingatur misericors: Quia, nimirum, centum illis ac viginti diebus, quibus foetus suos mater educat, fere nunquam evolat, sed de pullis suis tantum, ac eorum nutricatione sollicita est. Iis porro diebus, cum, quo eos sustentet, nihil suppetit, τὸν ἑαυτῆς μηρὸν ἀνατεμοῦσα, παρέχει τοῖς τέκνοις τοῦ αἵματος μεταλαμβάνειν, ὡς μὴ ἀπορήσαντα τροφῆς ἀναίρε-θῆναι, proprio exsecto femore, pullis suis praebet sanguinem suum exsugendum, ne cibi penuria conficiantur. Quae eadem refert Pisidas versu 1064. ubi de vulturibus,

But there is something else which I would not have the reader pass over carelessly, since it lends great weight to our interpretation. Horapollo reports that these same III, 74 Egyptians in their hieroglyphics signified a merciful person by a painted vulture. His words are in book 1, chapter 11: Μητέρα δὲ γράφοντες, ἢ ἐλεήμονα, γῦπα ζωγραφοῦσι. [Mētera de graphontes, ē eleēmona, gypa zōgraphousi.] "To signify a mother, or a merciful person, they paint a vulture." And so that you may know that the mercy meant is precisely that which the Hebrews call by the proper name רחם rechem, that is, τὴν πρὸς τὰ τέκνα φιλοστοργίαν [tēn pros ta tekna philostorgian] [tender love toward one's young], the same writer gives this reason why a merciful person is depicted by the image of a vulture: Because, namely, during the hundred and twenty days in which the mother rears her young, she almost never flies out, but is concerned only with her chicks and their feeding. Moreover, during those days, when she has nothing with which to sustain them, τὸν ἑαυτῆς μηρὸν ἀνατεμοῦσα, παρέχει τοῖς τέκνοις τοῦ αἵματος μεταλαμβάνειν, ὡς μὴ ἀπορήσαντα τροφῆς ἀναιρεθῆναι [ton heautēs mēron anatemousa, parechei tois teknois tou haimatos metalambanein, hōs mē aporēsanta trophēs anairethēnai], "cutting open her own thigh, she offers her blood to her chicks to suck, so that they may not perish from lack of food." Pisides relates the same things at verse 1064, where he speaks of vultures: ¶

Τὸν μηρὸν ἐκτέμνοντες, ἡματωμένοις
Γάλακτος ὁλκοῖς ζωπυροῦσι τὰ βρέφη.
Καί μοι δοκεῖ τὸν γῦπα σεμνῦναι πλέον.
Τῇ συμπαθείᾳ τῆς γόνης τετμημένον,
῎Η τὸν μέγιστον εἰς ἀσέλγειαν Δία
Τμηθέντα μηρὸν εἰς γονὴν ὀλεθρίαν.

Τὸν μηρὸν ἐκτέμνοντες, ἡματωμένοις Γάλακτος ὁλκοῖς ζωπυροῦσι τὰ βρέφη. Καί μοι δοκεῖ τὸν γῦπα σεμνῦναι πλέον. Τῇ συμπαθείᾳ τῆς γόνης τετμημένον, ῎Η τὸν μέγιστον εἰς ἀσέλγειαν Δία Τμηθέντα μηρὸν εἰς γονὴν ὀλεθρίαν. [Ton mēron ektemnontes, hēmatōmenois / Galaktos holkois zōpyrousi ta brephē. / Kai moi dokei ton gypa semnynai pleon. / Tē sympatheia tēs gonēs tetmēmenon, / Ē ton megiston eis aselgeian Dia / Tmēthenta mēron eis gonēn olethrian.] ¶

Femore exsecto, sanguineo lactis defluxu suos foetus refocillant. Et apud me quidem pluris est vultur, qui prolis suae commiseratione se ipsum exsecat, quam Iupiter ille maximus, qui propter perniciosum foetum (id est, Bacchum) femur suum abscidit. Haec igitur vera ratio nominis רחם racham: cujus historiam Patres interpolarunt, ut pelecano tribuerent, quod vulturis erat proprium. De ejusdem pietate erga pullos sic Vincentius Doctrinalis libri decimi sexti capite centesimo septuagesimo, Pullos suos, usque quo possint volare, nutrit, seque pro eis periculo exponit: dum etiam fortiorem se, volentem eos diripere, suis unguibus vulnerat, et alis percutit. Neque veteres Graecos latuit summa illa vulturis στοργὴ col. 319 et pietas in suos foetus. In quam rem insignis est Aeschyli locus in Agamemnone, sed a scribis et interprete pessime acceptus. Ibi Agamemnonem et Menelaum ereptae Helenae causa Trojam navigasse dicit,

"Cutting out the thigh, they revive their young with a bloody stream of milk. And in my eyes the vulture, which cuts itself out of pity for its offspring, is worth more than that greatest Jupiter, who for the sake of a destructive offspring (that is, Bacchus) cut open his own thigh." This, then, is the true reason for the name רחם racham; the Fathers distorted its story so as to attribute to the pelican what was proper to the vulture. Of the same bird's devotion to its chicks Vincent, Doctrinale, book 16, chapter 170, says: "It feeds its chicks until they can fly, and exposes itself to danger for them; it even wounds with its talons, and strikes with its wings, one stronger than itself that wants to carry them off." Nor was that supreme στοργὴ [storgē] col. 319 and devotion of the vulture toward its young unknown to the ancient Greeks. A notable passage on this is in Aeschylus' Agamemnon, but very badly handled by the copyists and the translator. There he says that Agamemnon and Menelaus sailed to Troy on account of the abduction of Helen, ¶

Τρόπον αἰγυπιῶν· οἵ τ᾽ ἐκπατίοις
Ἄλγεσι παίδων ὕπατοι λεχέων στροφοδινοῦνται,
Πτερύγων ἐρετμοῖσιν ἐρεσσόμενοι, δεμνιοτήρη
Πόνον ὀρταλίχων ὀλέσαντες. — —

Τρόπον αἰγυπιῶν· οἵ τ᾽ ἐκπατίοις Ἄλγεσι παίδων ὕπατοι λεχέων στροφοδινοῦνται, Πτερύγων ἐρετμοῖσιν ἐρεσσόμενοι, δεμνιοτήρη Πόνον ὀρταλίχων ὀλέσαντες. — — [Tropon aigypiōn; hoi t᾽ ekpatiois / Algesi paidōn hypatoi lecheōn strophodinountai, / Pterygōn eretmoisin eressomenoi, demniotērē / Ponon ortalichōn olesantes. — —] ¶

More vulturum; qui per devia dolore ereptorum pullorum sublimes a nidis per gyros circumaguntur, remigiis alarum remigantes, amisso labore, quem in pullorum III, 75 strato (seu nido) servando insumpserant. Pro ἄλγεσι παίδων, dolore pullorum (supple ereptorum) ridicule habent editiones, ἄλγεσσι ποδῶν, dolore pedum. Unde est, quod Latinus interpres a Poetae mente abit quam longissime. Sed editio Stephani recte habet Ἄλγεσι παίδων, neque aliter vetus Scholiastes. Aeschylo praeiverat Homerus, qui Odyss. π΄. versu 216. Ulyssem et Telemachum in mutuo amplexu refert uberius flevisse, quam ossifragae, aut vultures, quibus rustici pullos ademerunt, antequam ad volatum instructi essent. Verba sunt,

"Like vultures, which, in their distraught grief for their stolen chicks, wheel round in circles high above their nests, rowing with the oars of their wings, having lost the toil they had spent in guarding the III, 75 bed (or nest) of their chicks." Instead of ἄλγεσι παίδων [algesi paidōn], "grief for the chicks" (supply stolen), the editions absurdly have ἄλγεσσι ποδῶν [algessi podōn], "pain of the feet." This is why the Latin translator strays as far as possible from the poet's meaning. But Stephanus' edition rightly has Ἄλγεσι παίδων [Algesi paidōn], and the old scholiast reads no differently. Homer had preceded Aeschylus: in Odyssey π, verse 216, he relates that Ulysses and Telemachus, in their mutual embrace, wept more abundantly than ossifrages or vultures whose chicks peasants have taken away before they were fledged for flight. The words are: ¶

Cur vulturibus ossifragas, ut pariter pias, junxerit, est notissimum. Nam et ossifraga in Oppiano, libro primo Halieuticῶn, legitur, mulieris instar gemere et plorare, si quis ei unum ex pullis suffuratus fuerit, neque nidum deinceps relinquere, cibo neglecto. Neque solum in pullos suos, sed et in alienos est misericors. Ejectos enim ab aquilis pullos (inquit Plinius) cognatum genus ossifragae excipiunt, et educant cum suis. Et Antigonus Historia quinquagesima secunda, Ὁ δὲ Ἀριστοτέλης φησὶ τὴν φήνην τοὺς ἐκβληθέντας τῶν νεοττῶν ὑπὸ τοῦ ἀε-τοῦ λαμβάνειν, καὶ ἐκτρέφειν· Aristoteles dicit, ossifragam pullos, ab aquila ejectos, excipere et educare. Nempe Historiae libri noni capite trigesimo quarto, Ἡ δὲ καλουμένη φήνη ἐστὶν ἤπιος, καὶ τὰ τέκνα ἐκτρέφει, καὶ τὰ αὐτῆς, καὶ τὰ τοῦ ἀετοῦ· Quae phene vocatur (id est, ossifraga) est pia, et praeter suos etiam aquilae pullos alit. Ἤπιος hic idem, quod pius, et pertinet ad pietatem et misericordiam erga pullos, quos alit, ut sit fere idem, quod רחום rachum. Quomodo Paulus usurpans 1 Thessal. II, 7. Ἐγενήθημεν, inquit, ἤπιοι ἐν μέ-σω ὑμῶν, ὡς ἂν τροφὸς θάλπῃ τὰ ἑαυτῆς τέκνα. Itaque inter vulturem et ossifragam magna est morum similitudo. Ex quo factum, ut Philosophus in libro Mirabilium, et ex eo Plinius ab ossifragis vultures progenerari asserat. Et non pauci ossifragam inter vulturum species recenseant, in his Kiranides, Gesnerus, Dalechampius: ut quosdam Arabum jam diximus رخمة rachamam, pro vulture, ossifragam interpretari. Interim ex his jam constat, falli graviter Diodorum Tarsensem, cum vultures scribit οὐ τρέφειν τὰ γεννήματα, foetus suos non alere: nisi vulturum nomine aquilas intelligat; quamvis ne de aquilis quidem sit simpliciter verum, ut suo loco e) docuimus.

Why he joined ossifrages to vultures, as equally devoted to their young, is very well known. For in Oppian, book 1 of the Halieutica, we read that the ossifrage groans and wails like a woman if anyone has stolen one of its chicks, and thereafter does not leave the nest, neglecting food. Nor is it merciful only toward its own chicks, but toward those of others as well. For the chicks cast out by eagles (says Pliny) are taken up by the kindred race of the ossifrage, which rears them with its own. And Antigonus, Story 52: Ὁ δὲ Ἀριστοτέλης φησὶ τὴν φήνην τοὺς ἐκβληθέντας τῶν νεοττῶν ὑπὸ τοῦ ἀετοῦ λαμβάνειν, καὶ ἐκτρέφειν [Ho de Aristotelēs phēsi tēn phēnēn tous ekblēthentas tōn neottōn hypo tou aetou lambanein, kai ektrephein]· Aristotle says that the ossifrage takes up and rears the chicks cast out by the eagle. Namely in History, book 9, chapter 34: Ἡ δὲ καλουμένη φήνη ἐστὶν ἤπιος, καὶ τὰ τέκνα ἐκτρέφει, καὶ τὰ αὐτῆς, καὶ τὰ τοῦ ἀετοῦ [Hē de kaloumenē phēnē estin ēpios, kai ta tekna ektrephei, kai ta autēs, kai ta tou aetou]· The bird called phene (that is, the ossifrage) is kindly, and besides its own it also rears the chicks of the eagle. Ἤπιος [ēpios] here is the same as pius [devoted, kindly], and refers to devotion and mercy toward the chicks it rears, so that it is almost the same as רחום rachum. So Paul uses it in 1 Thessal. II, 7: Ἐγενήθημεν, he says, ἤπιοι ἐν μέσω ὑμῶν, ὡς ἂν τροφὸς θάλπῃ τὰ ἑαυτῆς τέκνα [Egenēthēmen ēpioi en mesō hymōn, hōs an trophos thalpē ta heautēs tekna]. Thus there is a great similarity of habits between the vulture and the ossifrage. Hence it came about that the Philosopher in the book of Marvels, and Pliny following him, asserts that vultures are begotten from ossifrages. And not a few count the ossifrage among the species of vultures, among them the Kiranides, Gesner and Dalechamp: just as we have already said that some of the Arabs interpret رخمة [rakhama] rachamam, instead of vulture, as ossifrage. Meanwhile it is already clear from this that Diodorus of Tarsus is gravely mistaken when he writes that vultures οὐ τρέφειν τὰ γεννήματα [ou trephein ta gennēmata], do not rear their offspring: unless by the name vultures he means eagles; although even of eagles it is not simply true, as we have shown in its place e). ¶

Vulturis clementia et benignitas ex eo quoque maxime apparet, quod non solum proprios foetus, sed et reliquas aves ab accipitre tuetur, si Cassiodoro credimus III, 76 libri secundi Epistola decima nona. Proh dolor! inquit, pietas in avibus invenitur, quae ab humana conditione deseritur. Vultur ipse, cui vita est cada- ver alienum, tantae magnitudinis corpus nec exiguis alitibus probatur infestus: sed magis accipitrem, vitam plumigerarum avium persequentem, alis caedit, ore dilaniat, totoque suo pondere periclitantibus nititur subvenire. Et paullo post de servis agens, qui dominum occiderant: Fiat ergo pastus pii vulturis, qui necem potuit crudeliter desiderare pastoris. Vides, lector, pium vocari vulturem, et in pietatis exemplum proponi, ut qui non solum proprios pullos, sed et quas- col. 320 cunque volucres in clientelam suam recipiat, ut pro viribus sartas tectas ab omni periculo conservet. Quale quid de tinnunculo scribit Plinius libri decimi capite trigesimo septimo, Defendit tinnunculus columbas, terretque accipitres naturali potentia in tantum, ut visum vocemque ejus fugiant.

The vulture's clemency and kindness also appears most clearly from the fact that it protects not only its own offspring but also other birds from the hawk, if we believe Cassiodorus III, 76, book 2, Letter 19. Alas! he says, devotion is found in birds which is abandoned by the human condition. The vulture itself, whose life is the carcass of another, a body of such great size, is not shown to be hostile even to small birds: rather it strikes with its wings the hawk that pursues the life of feathered birds, tears it with its beak, and strives with its whole weight to come to the aid of those in danger. And a little later, dealing with slaves who had killed their master: Let him therefore become the food of the devoted vulture, who could cruelly desire the death of his shepherd. You see, reader, that the vulture is called devoted and set forth as an example of devotion, as one that takes into its protection not only its own chicks but any birds whatever, col. 320 so as to keep them safe and sound from every danger as far as it can. Pliny writes something similar of the kestrel, book 10, chapter 37: The kestrel defends doves and terrifies hawks by a natural power, so much that they flee its sight and its voice. ¶

But Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)], in his Exercise 231 against Cardano, taught me another proof of στοργῆς [storgēs] in the vulture, in these words: They say that the African vulture in Libya and on Mount Atlas, where they call it sner, lives so long that, stripped of its feathers, it is fed by its own young; so that the Greeks must now coin another word than ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein], namely ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein]. Since these words harm no one, I do not understand why they so stirred that cymbal of the world, Leo Allatius, that he took from them material for raging without restraint against a man by far the greatest, in his Notes on Eustathius' Hexaemeron, p. 225. Most wittily, he says, he jests at the Greeks, as if on the strength of this story they must coin another name than ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein], namely ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein]. Yet so great a man, corrector and propagator of the Greek and Latin languages, ἀποῤῥοιβδεῖ [aporrhoibdei], καὶ τοῖς ἐκ τρίποδος βδέει λιβανωτοὺς [kai tois ek tripodos bdeei libanōtous], and fancies that he smells sweet. The name the Greeks are to coin ought to be taken from the vulture, whose nature and character are to be expressed, not from birds, most of whom [quorum plerique] are found to be undutiful to their parents. In this, note the words quorum (of birds) plerique impii. For Scaliger's critic did not know that aves [birds] is of feminine gender, which boys learn under the cane. But these are trifles compared with what follows: But to Scaliger everything sounds as it pleases him, and so does this. And Scaliger coins it thus, but a Greek does not; if he wished to form it from the vulture, he would certainly not say, in correct form, ἀντιγυπίζειν [antigypizein]. And a little later, as though he had done the job well: Behold (he says) the smatterers of Greeklings whom you unjustly tear apart against all right! Such as they are, they nevertheless recognize both your ignorance and your trifles. Sell smoke to others; they possess the light. As if he tears apart the Greeks who writes that they must now say ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein] of the vulture, as ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein] of the stork. For that is all that Scaliger wrote.
