II, 503 col. 1078 CHAPTER VI. ¶

Probatur, תנשמת thinsemeth Levit. XI, 30. esse Chamaeleontem.

It is proved that תנשמת thinsemeth in Levit. XI, 30. is the chameleon. ¶

In loco, in quo versamur, sola superest תנשמת thinsemeth, id est, talpa, si Graecos sequimur et Hieronymum, et e Chaldaeis Onkelosum, et Hebraeos recentis aevi, Selomonem, Kimchium, Pomarium, Aquinatem; et novos Bibliorum interpretes, Pagninum, Munsterum, Ariam, Genevenses, Anglos, Italos, etc. Quorum omnium auctoritas cum apud me multum valeat, tamen quo minus in assensum trahat, duo potissimum obstant, quae jam alibi observavi. Unum, quod praecedenti versu de talpa jam actum est. Id enim esse חולד choled, iis rationibus probavimus a), quas nemo facile refellet. Aliud igitur est in hoc versu תנשמת thinsemeth: nisi Mosem velimus in eadem contextus serie idem animal bis vetasse, et eum fingamus sic loquutum: Inter reptilia, quae repunt super terram, haec erunt vobis immunda, talpa, et mus, et crocodilus terrestris, col. 1079 etc. et talpa. Praeterea nomen תנשמת thinsemeth, quod ab halitu et aspiratione ductum est, quomodo in talpam quadret, nullus video, cui tam tenuis est aspiratio, II, 504 ut totos dies sub terra degat, quomodo pisces sub aqua. Unde est, quod talpae et muris subterranei cuniculi hac nota distinguuntur: quod terra obrutos mus statim aperit, ne suffocetur, ac talpa saepe nonnisi secunda, aut tertia die reserat. Proinde תנשמת non esse talpam, viderunt multi veterum, apud quos extant hujus vocis tres aliae versiones diversissimae.

In the passage we are dealing with, only תנשמת thinsemeth remains, that is, the mole, if we follow the Greeks and Jerome, and among the Chaldees Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], and the Hebrews of recent times, Solomon, Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]; and the modern translators of the Bible, Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Münster, Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], the Genevans, the English, the Italians, etc. Although the authority of all these carries great weight with me, two things above all keep it from drawing my assent, which I have already noted elsewhere. The first is that the mole was already dealt with in the preceding verse. For we have proved, with arguments that no one will easily refute a), that it is חולד choled. Therefore תנשמת thinsemeth in this verse is something else, unless we wish Moses to have forbidden the same animal twice in one and the same sequence of text, and imagine him to have spoken thus: Among the creeping things that creep upon the earth, these shall be unclean to you: the mole, and the mouse, and the land crocodile, col. 1079 etc. and the mole. Moreover, I do not see at all how the name תנשמת thinsemeth, which is derived from breath and breathing, can fit the mole, whose breathing is so slight II, 504 that it spends whole days under the earth, as fish do under water. Hence the burrows of the mole and of the underground mouse are distinguished by this mark: when they are buried with earth, the mouse opens them at once so as not to suffocate, whereas the mole often unseals them only on the second or third day. Accordingly many of the ancients saw that תנשמת is not the mole; among them three other, very different, renderings of this word are found. ¶

Primo enim Samaritano interpreti תנשמת est [?], id est, mustela, quae Chaldaeis est כרכושתא, et Syris ܟܒܟܘܫܬܐ, prima Radicali geminata, cum Samaritani secundam geminent.

First, to the Samaritan translator תנשמת is [?], that is, the weasel, which in Chaldee is כרכושתא, and in Syriac ܟܒܟܘܫܬܐ [כבכושתא], with the first radical doubled, whereas the Samaritans double the second. ¶

Secundo, pro eadem voce תנשמת Syrus habet ܝܘܕܥܝܕܐ jodaida, multipedam: et Arabes manu-scripti, البدال وهو أم اربعة اربعين Albedal, quae mater est quadraginta quatuor, supple pedum; seu cui sunt pedes XLIV, id est, julum, aut scolopendram. Sed pro بدال bedal, voce nihili, legendum ندال nedal. Chaldaeis enim et Talmudicis נדל nedal, vel nedel est scolopendra. Sic infra versu quadragesimo secundo, ubi e reptilibus terrae prohibetur כל מרבה רגלים, quodcumque reptile multos habet pedes, Gr. ὃ πολυπληθεῖ πο-σὶν, Ionathan habet נדל דמסגי ריגלין, nedal, qui est multipes, id est, scolopendram. Et Talmudici in Cholin cap. 3. fol. 67. b. מרבה רגלים זה נדל, qui multos habet pedes, id est, nedal. Et ibi Glossa, נדל מאה רגלים קורין לו Nedal centi-pedam vulgo vocant. Numero scilicet certo pro incerto. Sed Arabes, qui solent praecise loqui, matrem pedum XLIV. appellant. Sic Avicenna lib. 4. Fen. 6. Tract. 6. ubi de animalculis, quorum morsus est venenosus, capite decimo nono animal الاربعة والاربعين id est, quadraginta quatuor pedum, ita describit, هو الحيوان المعروف بدخال الاذن وربما كان في طول شبر وله في كل جانب اثنان وعشرون قايمة وقد يمشي قدما وقد ينكص بحاله Illud est animal notum; quia in aurem ingreditur. Plerumque est palmi longitudine. Ab unoquoque latere pedes habet viginti duos, et modo incedit antrorsum, modo in posteriora fertur. Scolopendram in aurem ingredi, alibi nusquam legi. Sed eam ἐπὶ τὴν οὐρὰν, caudam versus ferri, dicit Philosophus, Historiae libri quarti capite septimo. Unde a Nicandro Theriacῶν versu 812. vocatur ἀμφικαρὴς σκολόπενδρα. Ad quae verba Scholiastes, Ἀμφικαρὴς δὲ, ἤγουν δικέφαλος. Ψευδῶς δέ φησιν, οὐ γάρ ἐστιν. Ἀλλ᾽, ὡς Ἀριστοτέλης φησὶν, εἰς τοὐπίσω πολλάκις ἕρπει, καὶ παρ-έχει δόξαν τοῦ δικέφαλος εἶναι. Pedum numerum quod attinet, illum alii aliter definiunt, ut ubique locorum eundem esse necesse non est. Utut sit, scolopendram hoc nomine intelligi, nos docet Abenbitar ad caput Dioscoridis Περὶ σκολοπένδρας θαλασσίας, cujus nomen, postquam Arabicis literis ita expressit, II, 505 سقولوفندريا تالاسيا هو حيوان بحري وسمي باسم الحيوان الذي يقال له اربعة واربعون Hoc est marinum animal, col. 1080 quod XLIV pedum vocant.

Second, for the same word תנשמת the Syriac has ܝܘܕܥܝܕܐ [יודעידא] jodaida, the many-footed one; and the Arabic manuscripts have البدال وهو أم اربعة اربعين [al-badāl wa-huwa umm arbaʿa arbaʿīn] Albedal, which is the mother of forty-four, supply feet; that is, which has 44 feet, i.e. the iulus or scolopendra. But for بدال [badāl] bedal, a meaningless word, one should read ندال [nadāl] nedal. For in Chaldee and among the Talmudists נדל nedal or nedel is the scolopendra. Thus below in verse 42, where among the creeping things of the earth כל מרבה רגלים is forbidden, whatever creeping thing has many feet, Gr. ὃ πολυπληθεῖ ποσὶν [ho polyplēthei posin], Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] has נדל דמסגי ריגלין, nedal, which is many-footed, that is, the scolopendra. And the Talmudists in Chullin ch. 3, fol. 67b: מרבה רגלים זה נדל, that which has many feet, that is, nedal. And there the Gloss: נדל מאה רגלים קורין לו, Nedal is commonly called the hundred-footed, a definite number, of course, for an indefinite one. But the Arabs, who usually speak precisely, call it the mother of 44 feet. Thus Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], book 4, Fen 6, tract. 6, where he treats of small animals whose bite is venomous, in chapter 19 describes the animal الاربعة والاربعين [al-arbaʿa wa-l-arbaʿīn], that is, of forty-four feet, as follows: هو الحيوان المعروف بدخال الاذن وربما كان في طول شبر وله في كل جانب اثنان وعشرون قايمة وقد يمشي قدما وقد ينكص بحاله [huwa al-ḥayawān al-maʿrūf bi-dakhkhāl al-udhn wa-rubbamā kāna fī ṭūl shibr wa-lahu fī kull jānib ithnān wa-ʿishrūn qāyima wa-qad yamshī quduman wa-qad yankuṣu bi-ḥālihi] It is a well-known animal, because it enters the ear. Usually it is a span in length. On each side it has twenty-two feet, and it sometimes walks forward, sometimes moves backward. That the scolopendra enters the ear I have read nowhere else. But that it moves ἐπὶ τὴν οὐρὰν [epi tēn ouran], toward the tail, is said by the Philosopher, History, book 4, chapter 7. Hence Nicander, Theriaca verse 812, calls it ἀμφικαρὴς σκολόπενδρα [amphikarēs skolopendra]. On which words the Scholiast: Ἀμφικαρὴς δὲ, ἤγουν δικέφαλος. Ψευδῶς δέ φησιν, οὐ γάρ ἐστιν. Ἀλλ᾽, ὡς Ἀριστοτέλης φησὶν, εἰς τοὐπίσω πολλάκις ἕρπει, καὶ παρέχει δόξαν τοῦ δικέφαλος εἶναι [Amphikarēs de, ēgoun dikephalos. Pseudōs de phēsin, ou gar estin. All', hōs Aristotelēs phēsin, eis toupisō pollakis herpei, kai parechei doxan tou dikephalos einai]. As for the number of feet, different authors define it differently, so that it need not be the same in every place. However that may be, that the scolopendra is meant by this name is taught us by Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār] on the chapter of Dioscorides Περὶ σκολοπένδρας θαλασσίας [Peri skolopendras thalassias], whose name, after he has rendered it in Arabic letters thus, II, 505 سقولوفندريا تالاسيا هو حيوان بحري وسمي باسم الحيوان الذي يقال له اربعة واربعون [sqūlūfandriyā tālāsiyā huwa ḥayawān baḥrī wa-summiya bi-ism al-ḥayawān alladhī yuqāl lahu arbaʿa wa-arbaʿūn] This is a sea animal, col. 1080 which they call the 44-footed. ¶

Third, for Jonathan תנשמת thinsemeth is סלמנדרא salamandra. And for Erpenius' Arabic translator it is صنبرص [ṣnbrṣ] senabras, a corruption of سامابرص [sāmābrṣ] sammabras, as the Paris edition has it. I think these have come closest to the truth, and that, as with the five preceding names, this name too denotes some species of lizard, namely the chameleon, which is so called from breathing. For in Hebrew נשם is to breathe, and נשמה means not only spirit but also breath or breathing, as does נשימה. Hence, on these words of Ps 150:6. כל נשמה תהלל יה, Let every spirit praise God, the author of Bereshit Rabba, ch. 14, says: על כל נשימה ונשימה שאדם נישם צריך לקלס לבוראו מה טעם כל נשמה תהלל יה כל הנשימה תהלל יה For every breath that a man breathes he must praise his Creator. For what reason? (Because it is said) „Let every spirit praise the Lord“, that is, Let every breath praise the Lord. Anyone who has a clear understanding of its nature must admit that the chameleon is rightly named from breathing, or from breathing in: for since it keeps its mouth open in a perpetual gape and draws in air with its breath, many believe that it feeds on air alone. Pliny, book 8, chapter 33: It holds itself erect, with its mouth always open, and alone of animals is nourished neither by food nor by drink, nor by any nourishment other than air. Solinus: Its gaping is perpetual and serves no use; for it takes no food, is not nourished by drink, and lives on no other nourishment than the air it draws in. Ovid, Metamorphoses, book 15: ¶

Id quoque, quod ventis animal nutritur, et aura.

That too, the animal that is fed on winds and air. ¶

Its lung, namely, is very large. Pliny, book 11, chapter 37: The chameleon has a lung very large in proportion, and nothing else inside. Plutarch, in the book Utra anim.: Συνέπεται δὲ καὶ πνεύματος πλῆθος, ὡς Θεόφραστος. Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)]: Since it is furnished with large lungs extending along the sides of the belly, it draws air inward with great force and swells up R1. Tertullian II, 506 in the book De pallio: It feeds by gaping, ruminates by puffing like bellows; its food comes from the wind, etc. But, what is more surprising, it is also believed to go on breathing for a long time after being dissected. The Philosopher, in that excellent description of the chameleon in book 2, chapter 11 of the History: Ἐνεργεῖ δὲ καὶ τῷ πνεύματι, ἀνατετμημένος ὅλος ἐπὶ πολὺν χρόνον, βραχείας ἰσχυρῶς ἔτι κινήσεως ἐν αὐτῷ περὶ τὴν καρδίαν οὔσης [energei de kai tō pneumati, anatetmēmenos holos epi polyn chronon, bracheias ischyrōs eti kinēseōs en autō peri tēn kardian ousēs]; Even when it has been dissected entirely, it retains the power of breath for a long time, that is, as long as some motion, however slight, remains around the heart. This breath is called in Arabic نسيم [nasīm] nasim, from the very root from which its name תנשמת thinsemeth comes. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: يغتذي بالنسيم ويعيش بالبرد الهوا [yaghtadhī bi-l-nasīm wa-yaʿīsh bi-l-bard al-hawā] It is nourished by τῷ [tō] nasim (that is, by breath) and feeds on cold air. Yet by a very great error he transfers this to the land crocodile, as we noted above b), although it is proper to the chameleon. And yet it is not quite truly written that the chameleon lives on air alone. For it has a very long tongue, sticky at the tip, by darting which it catches flies and gnats from the branch of the tree on which it sits. Damir: لسانه طويل جدا مقدار col. 1081 ذراع وذلك دليل علي انه يكون مطوي في حلقه وهو يبلع ما بعد عنه من الذباب [lisānuhu ṭawīl jiddan miqdār dhirāʿ wa-dhālika dalīl ʿalā annahu yakūn maṭwī fī ḥalqihi wa-huwa yablaʿ mā baʿuda ʿanhu min al-dhubāb] Its tongue is very long, namely a cubit in measure. Accordingly it keeps it folded in its throat, and shoots it out in order to (catch and) eat flies that are at a distance from it. Algazel in the Mirabilia: والحربا خلق الله لسانها بخلاف المعتاد لتلحق به ما بعد عنها بثلاث اشبار ونحوه وتصتاد به علي هذه المسافة [wa-l-ḥirbā khalaqa Allāh lisānahā bi-khilāf al-muʿtād li-talḥaqa bihi mā baʿuda ʿanhā bi-thalāth ashbār wa-naḥwihi wa-taṣtād bihi ʿalā hādhihi al-masāfa] God created for the chameleon a tongue quite different from the usual, so that with it it might reach things about three spans away from it, and hunt what lies within this distance. Johannes Landius, cited by Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)], supports the Arabs: he says that when he was in farthest Syria he saw one of the five chameleons he had bought pick off, by a sudden, instantaneous dart of its tongue, a fly that was on its breast. And Belon, who had seen them alive in Egypt and Arabia: It has a round tongue, a palm and a half long, which it darts from afar at the insects on which it chiefly feeds; and with the spongy slime it has at the tip it draws flies, beetles, locusts and ants to itself R2. Hence its name حربا [ḥirbā] chirba, as if one were to say the darter. For حربة [ḥarba] charba is a kind of javelin or short lance. Accordingly the herb lonchitis, whose seed resembles the point of a lance, is called in Arabic حربة [ḥarba] II, 507 charba R3. But why it is called χαμαιλέων [chamaileōn] in Greek is less clear, since this most sluggish and timid animal seems to have scarcely anything in common with the lion. Tertullian, in the place cited above: When you hear of a chameleon, not knowing it before, you will at once fear something more than a lion. But when you come upon it in a vineyard, almost entirely under a vine leaf, you will immediately laugh at the boldness of the name. Yet by the Arab poets too it is called باقرة [bāqira] bakira, that is, lioness. For with them باقر [bāqir] bakir is lion. And an animalcule similar to the chameleon is called ليث عفرين [layth ʿIfrīn] lion of Iphrin, from the place where it is born. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: ليث عفرين دابة كالحربا يتعرض للراكب ويضربه بذنبه [layth ʿIfrīn dābba ka-l-ḥirbā yataʿarraḍ li-l-rākib wa-yaḍribuhu bi-dhanabihi] The lion of Iphrin, an animal like the chameleon, which attacks a rider and strikes him with its tail. So it is rightly called a lion, since in a chameleon's body it has a lion's spirit. Accordingly, if it were established that this animal was known to the Greeks, I would say that the name chameleon was first given to it and afterwards transferred to the other, similar animal. But since none of the ancients mentions this animalcule, it seems to me more probable that, just as the Arabs call the chameleon جمل [jamal] gemel, or جمل يهود [jamal yahūd] gemel jehoud, that is, the Jewish camel, as Damir and Abenbitar report, so the Phoenicians in Africa called it גמל gamal, or גמליון gamalion, from which the word χαμαιλέων [chamaileōn] was formed, inflected in the Greek manner so that it would seem II, 508 to be Greek. Nor is it obscure why the Arabs called it a camel. For, just as they attribute to it the head of a calf, the body of a little fish and the feet of a gecko, so too, in Damir, سنام col. 1082 البعير [sanām al-baʿīr] the hump of a camel, because like a camel it has a humped back. There are also those who call it مجوسي [majūsī], that is, a Magian or Magusaean, because it seems to worship the sun, since it is constantly turned towards it. It is said to change hour by hour, according as the sunlight is stronger or its heat more intense: in the morning it is ash-grey, then yellow, and finally green when the sun is hottest R4. But in fear too, or if something is put in its way, it takes on other colours. Hence the proverb about one who does not remain in the same state: تلون الحرباء [talawwana al-ḥirbāʾ] The chameleon has changed colour. So Alcibiades was once called χαμαιλέοντος εὐμεταβολώτερος [chamaileontos eumetabolōteros], more changeable than a chameleon. The Arabs forbid eating it, because it is venomous and a species of وزغ [wazagh] guezga, or gecko. In Avicenna and Abenbitar, its blood removes the hair of the eyelids. In Damir, its gall removes dimness of sight, and its fat is applied to ulcers and scars. In Alkazuini, remedies from the chameleon are prescribed against epilepsy, hail, locusts and enchantment. But to dwell on these any longer is not a matter for our leisure. ¶

Tantum observabo, Hebraeos usque adeo ignorasse, quod animal sit chamaeleon, ut avem esse voluerint. Testem habeo Kimchium, qui, in Lexico disquirens, quid sit לילית lilith, Es. XXXIV, 14. scribit, quosdam asserere, שהוא עוף גדל מן הרוח כמו סלמנדרא מן האש שקורין לו קמליאן Lilith avem esse, quae aëre vescitur (ut igne salamandra), quam chamaeleonem vocant. Quale est, quod in Tanchuma sectione וישב fol. 18. a. salamandra describitur araneae similis. Verba sic habent, Vitrarii, qui vitrum conficiunt, cum fornacem accende- runt septem diebus et 7 noctibus continuis, ex vi ignis היוצא משם בריה הדומה לעכבית והבריה קורין אותה סלמנדרא egreditur animal, simile araneae, quod animal vocatur salamandra. Sunt, qui pro לעכבית, araneae, legi volunt לעכבר, muri; quod nihilo est sanius, cum salamandra non sit muri similior, quam araneae. Hebraeis igitur animalium natura cum sit tam parum perspecta, illis temere credendum non est in harum vocum explicatione.

I shall only observe that the Hebrews were so ignorant of what kind of animal the chameleon is that they took it to be a bird. My witness is Kimchi, who, inquiring in his Lexicon what לילית lilith is, Isa 34:14, writes that some assert שהוא עוף גדל מן הרוח כמו סלמנדרא מן האש שקורין לו קמליאן that Lilith is a bird which feeds on air (as the salamander on fire), which they call the chameleon. Of the same kind is the description of the salamander in the Tanchuma, section וישב, fol. 18a, as similar to a spider. The words run thus: When glassmakers, who make glass, have kept the furnace burning for seven days and 7 nights without interruption, from the force of the fire היוצא משם בריה הדומה לעכבית והבריה קורין אותה סלמנדרא there comes forth an animal similar to a spider, which animal is called the salamander. Some would read לעכבר, to a mouse, instead of לעכבית, to a spider; but this is no sounder, since the salamander is no more like a mouse than like a spider. Since, then, the nature of animals was so little understood by the Hebrews, they are not to be trusted rashly in explaining these words. ¶

II, 509 In Graeca versione de Ninive dicitur Soph. II, 4. χαμαιλέοντες, καὶ ἐχῖ-νοι ἐν τοῖς φατνώμασιν αὐτῆς κοιταθήσονται· Chamaeleontes et hericii in laquearibus ejus cubabunt. At in Hebraeo textu pro chamaeleonte est קאת, quod illi ipsi pelicanum reddunt Levit. XI, 18. et Ps. CI, (Hebr. CII,) 7. Quorsum igitur hic vox eadem pro chamaeleonte sumitur? Si cum Complutensibus λέοντες pro χαμαιλέοντες scribamus, malo non medebimur; quia Hebraeum קאת non magis leonem, quam chamaeleontem sonat. Graecos pro קאת legisse suspicor לטאה, lacertae genus, quod illis in Levitico καλαβώτης, stellio, hic χαμαιλέων fuerit, ut hae species non raro confunduntur: aut καλαβώτης, vel quod idem est, γαλεώτης, mutatum in χαμαιλέοντες (5), Huc facit, quod chamaeleon ἐν τοῖς φατνώμασιν, in laquearibus recubare nusquam legitur. Sed proprium id του καλαβώτου, vel γαλεώτου, stellionis. Hinc Dioscorides libri sexti capite primo sub dio parari jubet esculenta; aut, si parentur ἐν ὑποστέγῳ, in loco operto, ἐπιμελῶς καταμανθάνειν τὰς ὀροφὰς, πολλάκις γὰρ ἐκ τῶν ὑπερκειμέ-νων πίπτει ζωΰφια φθοροποιὰ, ὥσπερ φαλάγγια, καὶ ἄλλα τινὰ ἑρπετὰ, καὶ ὁ καλούμενος ἀσκαλαβώτης, tecti convexa, aut laquearia (si mavis) studiose considerare: saepe enim superne decidunt lethifera animalcula, ut araneae, et alia quaedam reptilia, et qui vocatur stellio. Et in Nubibus Aristophanis Actu primo de Socrate dicitur,

II, 509 In the Greek version it is said of Nineveh, Soph. II, 4.: χαμαιλέοντες, καὶ ἐχῖνοι ἐν τοῖς φατνώμασιν αὐτῆς κοιταθήσονται [chamaileontes, kai echinoi en tois phatnōmasin autēs koitathēsontai]; Chameleons and hedgehogs shall lie in its coffered ceilings. But in the Hebrew text, in place of the chameleon, there is קאת, which the same translators render pelican in Lev 11:18 and Ps. CI, (Hebr. CII,) 7. Why, then, is the same word taken here for the chameleon? If, with the Complutensian editors, we write λέοντες [leontes] for χαμαιλέοντες [chamaileontes], we shall not cure the fault, because the Hebrew קאת no more means lion than chameleon. I suspect that the Greeks read לטאה in place of קאת, a kind of lizard, which for them in Leviticus is καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs], stellio [gecko], and here would have been χαμαιλέων [chamaileōn], since these species are not infrequently confused; or else that καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs], or, what is the same, γαλεώτης [galeōtēs], was changed into χαμαιλέοντες [chamaileontes] R5. In support of this is the fact that the chameleon is nowhere said to lie ἐν τοῖς φατνώμασιν [en tois phatnōmasin], in coffered ceilings. But this is characteristic of the καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs], or γαλεώτης [galeōtēs], the stellio. Hence Dioscorides, book 6, chapter 1, directs that food be prepared in the open air; or, if it is prepared ἐν ὑποστέγῳ [en hypostegō], in a covered place, ἐπιμελῶς καταμανθάνειν τὰς ὀροφὰς, πολλάκις γὰρ ἐκ τῶν ὑπερκειμένων πίπτει ζωΰφια φθοροποιὰ, ὥσπερ φαλάγγια, καὶ ἄλλα τινὰ ἑρπετὰ, καὶ ὁ καλούμενος ἀσκαλαβώτης [epimelōs katamanthanein tas orophas, pollakis gar ek tōn hyperkeimenōn piptei zōyphia phthoropoia, hōsper phalangia, kai alla tina herpeta, kai ho kaloumenos askalabōtēs], to examine carefully the vault of the roof, or the coffered ceiling (if you prefer): for deadly little creatures often fall from above, such as spiders and certain other reptiles, and the one called the stellio. Eum, lunae vias et circuitus quaerentem, et sursum hiantem, e tecti laqueari noctu concacavit stellio. Sunt, quibus τὰ φατνώματα sunt praesepia. In quo errore perridiculo fuisse video Graeci textus interpretes. Quos tamen si sequaris, nihil egeris. Quia tam in praesepibus, quam in tectorum laquearibus versari solet stellio. Aristoteles Historiae libri noni capite primo, Κωλωτῇ δ᾽ ὄνος πολέμιος. Κοιμᾶται γὰρ ἐν τῇ φάτνῃ αὐτῆς, καὶ κωλύει ἐθίειν, εἰς τοὺς μυκτῆρας ἐνδυό-μενος· Stellioni asinus hostis est. In ejus enim praesepi dormit stellio, et in nares penetrans prohibet, ne comedat. Cum chamaeleon in desertis locis degat. Sed capite sequenti de stellione plura dicentur.

As he was investigating the paths and revolutions of the moon, and gaping upward, a stellio from the ceiling of the roof dropped its excrement on him at night. Some take τὰ φατνώματα [ta phatnōmata] to be mangers. I see that the translators of the Greek text were in this very ridiculous error. Yet even if you follow them, you will gain nothing, because the stellio is accustomed to frequent mangers as much as the coffered ceilings of roofs. Aristotle, History, book 9, chapter 1: Κωλωτῇ δ᾽ ὄνος πολέμιος. Κοιμᾶται γὰρ ἐν τῇ φάτνῃ αὐτῆς, καὶ κωλύει ἐθίειν, εἰς τοὺς μυκτῆρας ἐνδυόμενος· [Kōlōtē d᾽ onos polemios. Koimatai gar en tē phatnē autēs, kai kōlyei ethiein, eis tous myktēras endyomenos] The ass is an enemy to the stellio. For the stellio sleeps in its manger and, entering its nostrils, prevents it from eating. Whereas the chameleon lives in desert places. But more will be said about the stellio in the following chapter. ¶
