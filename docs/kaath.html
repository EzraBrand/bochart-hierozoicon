III, 40 col. 292 CHAPTER XXIV. ¶

Probatur, קאת kaath esse Pelicanum, et Ardeam, praecipue stellarem, id est, Butaurum.

It is proved that קאת kaath is the pelican and the heron, especially the starred heron, that is, the bittern. ¶

Graecis hic, et Psalmi centesimi primi (Hebraeis centesimi secundi) versu septimo קאת kaath est pelicanus. Hieronymo etiam in loco Psalmi, quamvis alibi reddat onocrotalum. Onkelos et Samaritanus vocem Hebraeam retinent. Et pro illa Ionathan et Syrus habent ܩܩܐ kaka; Arabs Erpenianus القوق alkuk, et alter القيق alkik. Neque aliter Talmudici scriptores. Ita Talmud Babylonicum tractatu Cholin cap. 3. fol. 63. a. אמר רב יהודה קאת זו הקוק, Dixit R. Iuda, Kaath est kuk. Ita etiam R. Ismael in Talmude Hierosolymitano. Et pro קוק kuk nonnulli קיק kik scribunt. Proinde in tractatu De Sabbatho cap. 2. fol. 21. a. Rabbi Samuel rogatus, unde sumatur שמן קיק, oleum kik, ita respondet: שאלתי נהו לכל נחותי ימא ואמרו לי עוף אחד יש בכרכי הים וקיק שמו, Illud ipsum quaesivi ab iis omnibus, qui in mare descendunt, qui dixerunt, avem kik nomine reperiri in maritimis oppidis. Et Kimchius in Lexico loca Talmudis sic citat, quasi ubique kik legerit. Verba sunt, ובתלמוד אמר רב יהודה קאת זה הקיק ובתלמוד ירושלמי תני רבי ישמעאל קאת זה הקיק, Et in Talmude dixit Rabbi Iuda, kaath esse kik. Etiam in Hierosolymitano Talmude tradidit Rabbi Ismael, kaath esse kik. Arabes quoque paraphrastas notavimus utroque modo scribere. Tamen apud illos قوق kuk, et قيق kik, multum differunt. Nam قوق kuk est طاير ماي طويل العنق avis aquatica longo collo. Ita definiunt Alcamus et Damir, ex libro, cui titulus العباب Alobab, id est, Universitas. Sed القيق alkik est cornix. Damir ita describit, القيق طاير علي قدر اليمامة واهل الشام يسمونه ابو زريق وهو الوف للناس فيه قبول للتعليم وسرعة ادراك لما يعلم ويحاكي كلما يسمع وقد تقدم الكلام col. 293 عليه في باب الزاي المعجمة Alkik est avis, accedens ad turturis magnitudinem, quae ab incolis Syriae vocatur abu-zarik (id est, pater caesius). Habitat cum hominibus. In ea est doctrinae capacitas et facilitas apprehensionis eorum, quae docentur. Et recitat, quidquid audit. Sed de ea jam actum in capite de Ze. Nempe ad vocem زاغ Zag, quod est cornicis vulgare nomen, quamvis alii picam exponant (1) Cum igitur III, 41 avis קיק kik nomine ab ave קוק kuk ita differat, non debuerunt confundi (2).

For the Greeks, here and in the seventh verse of Psalm 101 (102 for the Hebrews), קאת kaath is the pelican. So too for Jerome in the passage of the Psalm, although elsewhere he renders it onocrotalus. Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] and the Samaritan retain the Hebrew word. In its place Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and the Syriac have ܩܩܐ [קקא] kaka; Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] has القوق [al-qūq] alkuk, and the other القيق [al-qīq] alkik. Nor do the Talmudic writers say otherwise. Thus the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Cholin, ch. 3, fol. 63a: אמר רב יהודה קאת זו הקוק, R. Judah said: Kaath is kuk. So also R. Ishmael in the Jerusalem Talmud. And for קוק kuk some write קיק kik. Accordingly, in the tractate On the Sabbath, ch. 2, fol. 21a, Rabbi Samuel, asked where שמן קיק, oil of kik, comes from, answers thus: שאלתי נהו לכל נחותי ימא ואמרו לי עוף אחד יש בכרכי הים וקיק שמו, I asked this very thing of all those who go down to the sea, and they told me that a bird named kik is found in the coastal towns. And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon cites the passages of the Talmud as if he had read kik everywhere. His words are: ובתלמוד אמר רב יהודה קאת זה הקיק ובתלמוד ירושלמי תני רבי ישמעאל קאת זה הקיק, And in the Talmud Rabbi Judah said that kaath is kik. Also in the Jerusalem Talmud Rabbi Ishmael taught that kaath is kik. We have noted that the Arabic paraphrasts also write it both ways. Yet among them قوق [qūq] kuk and قيق [qīq] kik differ greatly. For قوق [qūq] kuk is طاير ماي طويل العنق [ṭāyir māʾī ṭawīl al-ʿunuq], a water bird with a long neck. So Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] and Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] define it, from the book entitled العباب [al-ʿUbāb] Alobab, that is, The Universe. But القيق [al-qīq] alkik is the crow. Damir describes it thus: القيق طاير علي قدر اليمامة واهل الشام يسمونه ابو زريق وهو الوف للناس فيه قبول للتعليم وسرعة ادراك لما يعلم ويحاكي كلما يسمع وقد تقدم الكلام col. 293 عليه في باب الزاي المعجمة [al-qīq ṭāyir ʿalī qadr al-yamāma wa-ahl al-Shām yusammūnahu abū zurayq wa-huwa alūf li-l-nās fīhi qabūl li-l-taʿlīm wa-surʿat idrāk li-mā yuʿallam wa-yuḥākī kullamā yasmaʿ wa-qad taqaddama al-kalām ʿalayhi fī bāb al-zāy al-muʿjama] Alkik is a bird approaching the size of a turtledove, which is called by the inhabitants of Syria abu-zarik (that is, the grey-blue father). It lives with people. It has a capacity for learning and ease in grasping what it is taught. And it repeats whatever it hears. But it has already been dealt with in the chapter on Ze. That is, under the word زاغ [zāgh] Zag, which is the common name of the crow, although others explain it as the magpie R1 Since, then, the bird III, 41 named קיק kik differs so from the bird קוק kuk, they ought not to have been confused R2. ¶

But those Talmudists are also gravely mistaken who will have שמן קיק, oleum kik [kik oil], taken from a bird, since it is κίκινον ἔλαιον [kikinon elaion], that is, oil pressed from the seeds of the plant which the Egyptians called KIKI` [kiki]. Many ancient writers mention it. Thus Herodotus, book 1, chapter 92, says that the marsh-dwelling Egyptians use an ointment ἀπὸ τῶν σιλλικυπρίων τοῦ καρποῦ, τὸ καλεῦσι μὲν Αἰγύπτιοι KIKI` [apo tōn sillikypriōn tou karpou, to kaleusi men Aigyptioi kiki], from the fruit of the syllicyprium, which the Egyptians call KIKI. And Strabo, book 17, in his description of Egypt: Καὶ τὸ KIKKI` (read KIKI`) καρπός τις σπειρόμενος ἐν ἀρούραις, ἐξ οὗ ἔλαιον ἀποθλίβεται [Kai to kikki … karpos tis speiromenos en arourais, ex hou elaion apothlibetai]. And KIKI is a certain fruit, sown in the fields, from which oil is pressed. And Dioscorides, book 4: KIKI`, ἢ κρότων [kiki, ē krotōn], etc. ἐξ οὗ ἀποθλίβεται τὸ λεγόμενον κίκινον ἔλαιον [ex hou apothlibetai to legomenon kikinon elaion]. And Pliny, book 15, chapter 7: Oil is also made from the CICI tree, abundant in Egypt. Hence Hesychius: Κίκη· φυτόν [Kikē· phyton]; but it should be corrected to Κικί [Kiki]. And this is the very kikajon קיקיון of Jonah, and the elcheroa الخروع [al-kharwaʿ] of the Arabs. This was already observed by Jerome nearly thirteen hundred years ago. For the gourd, or ivy (he says on Jonah) we read in the Hebrew קיקיון, which in the Syriac and Punic language is also called elkeroa. Accordingly, in the Coptic Lexicon a) the Egyptian word KOYKI` [kouki] kuki III, 42 (for which I would write KIKI` [kiki]) is rendered in Arabic حب الخروع [ḥabb al-kharwaʿ], the seed of elcheroa or alcheroa. And in Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], in the chapter خروع [kharwaʿ] cheroa, the words of Dioscorides on κικὶ [kiki], ἐξ οὗ ἀποθλίβεται τὸ λεγόμενον κίκινον ἔλαιον [ex hou apothlibetai to legomenon kikinon elaion], are rendered in Arabic thus: ومنها يتعصر الدهن المسمي قيقي وهو دهن الخروع [wa-minhā yutaʿaṣṣar al-duhn al-musammī qīqī wa-huwa duhn al-kharwaʿ], And from it is pressed the oil which they call the oil KIKI, which is the oil ALCHEROA. Indeed there are even some among the Talmudists for whom kik, from which the oil comes, is the same as kikajon. The Talmud, in the place cited above: ריש לקיש אמר קיקיון דיונה אמר רבה בר בר חנה לדידי חזי לי קיקיון דיונה ולצלוליבה דמי, Resh Lakish said: It is the kikajon of Jonah. Rabba Bar son of Hana said: As for me, I have seen the kikajon of Jonah, and it is a plant resembling the selulibah. Indeed it is the same, if selulibah is truly the alcheroa الخروع [al-kharwaʿ] of the Arabs, as is found in the Responsa of the Geonim, that is, of the illustrious doctors, where seluliba is defined: אילן סרק יש במקומינו ועושה גרעינים ועושין מהן שמן ומין סממנין וכל מי שיש בו צנה הרבה שותה ממנו ושמו בלשון ערבי אלכרו״ע ושמנו קורין דהן כרוע, A barren tree growing in our lands, bearing seeds from which oil is made, and a kind of red colouring. And whoever is seized by a severe chill drinks of this oil. In Arabic it is called alcheroa, and its oil the oil cheroa. So Baal Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh] cites it. But in Kimchi's Commentaries, where this same passage is quoted, the words ומין סממנין, and a kind of red colouring, appear nowhere. col. 294 I therefore suspect that they were either added from elsewhere or corrupted, and that in their place we should read ומין שמשומין, and a kind of sesame, so that seliluba, or alcheroa, is said to be among the kinds of sesame, an opinion held by some of the ancients. Pliny, book 15, chapter 7, on manufactured oil: Next is that made from the CICI tree, abundant in Egypt; some call it croton, others trixis (or rather sili, as Salmasius [=Claude Saumaise (1588–1653), Plinianae exercitationes] on Solinus would have it read), others wild sesame. III, 43 Both the Arabs and the Talmudists have taught us that the Hebrew קאת kaath is the same as קוק huk and kuk, a water bird with a long neck. But since this is not enough, because there are many water birds with long necks, the Greeks and Jerome point us to the pelican; and I gladly embrace this rendering as the truest. For the origin of the word leads us to it, which plainly derives from the verb קאה kaah, that is, to vomit. Hence Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah]: הקאת דרכו להקיא מאכלו, "the kaath is accustomed to vomit up its food." And lest the final Tav be thought an obstacle, Rabbi Jonah teaches that it is a formative of the noun, and that the true root is קאה kaah. And Kimchi cites a poet who says קאות kaoth in the plural. So, just as from פרה comes פרת for the Euphrates, and שחת, pit, from שחה, so קאת, pelican, comes from קאה R4. And why it is named from vomiting is not obscure to those who know that the pelican brings back up shells it has already swallowed, after they have been opened by the heat of its belly, so as to pick out the edible parts from them. The ancient writers assert this with such agreement that the matter cannot be called into doubt. R2 Rosenmüller, III, 41 This, however, is exactly what J. E. Faber [=J. E. Faber, notes to the German edition of Harmer's Observations (Beobachtungen über den Orient, 1772–79)] did in his dissertation de Animalibus, quorum mentio sit Zephan. II, 14 (Göttingen 1769; reprinted at the end of Cramer's book entitled Scythische Denkmaeler in Palaestina), where he contends that قيق [qyq], قوق [qwq], ܩܩܐ [קקא], קיק, קוק, קקא and קקתא are names of a single bird, the cornix [crow], differing from one another only in spelling. But even if the Hebrew masters sometimes take קוק and קיק indiscriminately for the crow, it is nevertheless clear from what Bochart has adduced from Damir that the Arabic قيق [qyq] and قوق [qwq] differ from each other; and that the Syriac ܩܩܐ [קקא] designates the pelican is clearly taught by the Physiologus Syrus edited by Tychsen, ch. 20, pp. 13 and 110, and by Tychsen himself, pp. 111–114. The Syriac name of the crow is rather ܩܘܪܩܣܐ [קורקסא] Korakso, which arose from the sound and is the Greek κόραξ [korax]; see the same Physiolog. Syr. Chapter 24. Faber, therefore, interprets the Hebrew word as the crow on the authority of the Arabic translator in the Polyglots alone, who puts قيق [qyq] for קאת. Schoder copied Faber in Specim. Hieroz. II, Tit. 9. But in truth the crow in no way fits the passages of the Psalms and Zephaniah. For Zephaniah II, 14 places the קאת among the ruins and deserted places of the cities of Nineveh and Zion, whereas the crow frequents the surroundings of cities and houses; and David, Ps 102:7, compares his weeping with the laments and groans of the קאת; yet no one, so far as I know, has ever yet heard laments from a crow. Moreover, Moses in Lev 11:18 has already included the crow under the genus of ravens, ערב, so that there was no need to name this bird separately, as Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde] aptly points out, fasc. 3, Chapter 6, p. 58.
