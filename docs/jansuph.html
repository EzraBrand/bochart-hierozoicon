III, 24 Chapter 22. col. 281 ¶

Probatur, ינשוף jansuph esse Bubonem.

It is proved that ינשוף jansuph is the eagle owl. ¶

Nec satis notum, quid sit ינשוף jansuph et jansoph, avis nomen, quod jam sequitur. Nam, ut Samaritanos praeteream, qui ࠁࠓࠁࠓࠉ barbari, id est, Barbaricam avem, vertunt, qua de causa, haud quaquam scio, quatuor alias reperio hujus vocis interpretationes, ab invicem remotissimas.

Nor is it well enough known what ינשוף jansuph or jansoph is, the name of the bird that follows next. For, to pass over the Samaritans, who translate ࠁࠓࠁࠓࠉ [ברברי] barbari, that is, the Barbary bird, for what reason I do not know at all, I find four other interpretations of this word, very far removed from one another. ¶

Prima Graecorum est, et Hieronymi, qui ἶβιν ibin interpretantur, hic, et Esa. XXXIV, 11. ubi de Idumaea dicitur, ינשוף Iansoph, et corvus in ea habitabunt. Tamen is locus palam docet, jansoph non esse ibin. Ibis enim Aegyptia est avis, ut ex Herodoto, Strabone, Plinio, Solino et Ammiano scimus. Εἰ δέ τις ἐπιθέμενος αὐτῇ τὸ καρτερὸν ἐξαγάγοι, ἣ δὲ ἀμύνεται τὸν ἐπιβουλεύσαντα, εἰς οὐδὲν αὐτῷ τὴν σπουδὴν προσαγαγοῦσα. ἑαυτὴν γὰρ ἀποκτείνει λιμῷ, καὶ ἀνόνητον τὴν προθυμίαν ἀποφαίνει τῷ προῃρημένῳ. Si quis autem vim inferens eam abducat, illa ab insidiatore poenas reposcit, hoc ejus studium in nihilum redigens. Mortem enim sibi inedia consciscens, inutilem hoc volenti conatum esse ostendit. Aeliani verba sunt III, 25 Historiae animalium libri secundi capite trigesimo octavo. Itaque in Idumaea nulla fuit ibis. (1).

The first is that of the Greeks and of Jerome, who interpret it as ἶβιν [ibin], ibis, both here and at Isa 34:11, where it is said of Idumaea: ינשוף Iansoph, and the raven shall dwell in it. Yet that passage plainly shows that jansoph is not the ibis. For the ibis is an Egyptian bird, as we know from Herodotus, Strabo, Pliny, Solinus and Ammianus. Εἰ δέ τις ἐπιθέμενος αὐτῇ τὸ καρτερὸν ἐξαγάγοι, ἣ δὲ ἀμύνεται τὸν ἐπιβουλεύσαντα, εἰς οὐδὲν αὐτῷ τὴν σπουδὴν προσαγαγοῦσα. ἑαυτὴν γὰρ ἀποκτείνει λιμῷ, καὶ ἀνόνητον τὴν προθυμίαν ἀποφαίνει τῷ προῃρημένῳ. [Ei de tis epithemenos autē to karteron exagagoi, hē de amynetai ton epibouleusanta, eis ouden autō tēn spoudēn prosagagousa. heautēn gar apokteinei limō, kai anonēton tēn prothymian apophainei tō proērēmenō.] But if anyone takes it away by force, it exacts punishment from the one who laid the snare, bringing his effort to nothing. For by starving itself to death it shows the attempt to be useless to the one who wanted it. These are the words of Aelian, III, 25 History of Animals, book 2, chapter 38. Therefore there was no ibis in Idumaea. R1. ¶

Secunda versio est Syri interpretis, et utriusque Chaldaei, id est, Ionathanis et Onkelosi: a quibus vox eadem constanter redditur קפופא kiphupha, id est, bubo, vel noctua; ut ex Talmude probavimus capite vigesimo, ubi de ave כוס thos. De qua versione plura dicemus infra.

The second version is that of the Syriac translator and of both Chaldees, that is, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], by whom the word is consistently rendered by the same word קפופא kiphupha, that is, eagle owl or little owl, as we proved from the Talmud in chapter 20, on the bird כוס thos. We shall say more about this version below. ¶

Tertia est Arabum Pentateuchi interpretum: quorum uterque pro ינשוף hic habet الباشق albasak, accipitris speciem, quam Alkazuinius ita descri-bit: طير احسن الصورة اصغر الجوارح جثة يصطاد العصافير, avis est specie pulcherrima, et rapacium corpore minima, quae venatur passeres. Quae descriptio niso, seu sparverio, convenit. Sed multo aliter definit Damir in capite De البازي albazi, seu accipitre. Ibi scilicet ha-bentur accipitrum quinque species. 1. بازي bazi proprie dictus, cui ab impetu nomen est (2). 2. زرق zorrak, id est, oculis caesius. 3. باشق basak Arabice dictus ab acuto visu, quamvis Persicum esse plerique velint. 4. بيذق col. 282 id est, vilis. 5. عصفي asphi, id est, qui fertur in medium turbinis. In his basak sic describitur. هو ايضا جار المزاج عليه القلق الزعارة يانس وقتا ويستوحش وقتا وهو قوي النفس فاذا انس منه الصغير بلغ صاحبه من صيده المراد وهو خفيف المحمل ظريف الشمايل يليق بالملوك ان تخدمه لانه يصيد افخر ما يصيده البازي وهو الدراج والحمام والورشان وهو كثير الشبق وهو قوي عليه صيده لا يتركه الي ان يتلف احدهما واجمل اوصافه ان يكون صغيرا في المنظر ثقيلا في الميزان طويل الساقين قصير الفخذين Ille etiam est calidi III, 26 temperamenti, ac proinde inquietus, et pravae indolis, et quandoque est cicur, quandoque ferus. Respiratio est fortis. Cum parvus cicuratur, dominus ejus secundo vento praedam suam consequitur. Et facilius fertur, et natura est solers. Usus ejus regibus maximé convenit; quia venatur nobiliora quam albazi, nempe attagenes, columbas et turtures. Est multae libidinis. Si avem aggreditur, se fortiorem, eam non dimittit, donec pereat alteruter. Maxime probantur, qui sunt statura parvi, pondere graves, longi cruribus, femoribus breves (3).

The third is that of the Arabic translators of the Pentateuch, both of whom have here for ינשוף الباشق [al-bāshiq] albasak, a species of hawk, which Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] describes thus: طير احسن الصورة اصغر الجوارح جثة يصطاد العصافير [ṭayr aḥsan al-ṣūra aṣghar al-jawāriḥ juththa yaṣṭād al-ʿaṣāfīr], it is a bird most beautiful in appearance, and the smallest in body of the birds of prey, which hunts sparrows. This description fits the sparrowhawk, or sparverius. But Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] defines it very differently in the chapter On البازي [al-bāzī] albazi, or the hawk. There, namely, five species of hawks are given. 1. بازي [bāzī] bazi properly so called, which is named from its onrush R2. 2. زرق [zurraq] zorrak, that is, blue-grey-eyed. 3. باشق [bāshiq] basak, so called in Arabic from its sharp sight, although most hold it to be Persian. 4. بيذق [baydhaq] col. 282 that is, worthless. 5. عصفي [ʿaṣfī] asphi, that is, one that is carried into the middle of a whirlwind. Among these the basak is described thus: هو ايضا جار المزاج عليه القلق الزعارة يانس وقتا ويستوحش وقتا وهو قوي النفس فاذا انس منه الصغير بلغ صاحبه من صيده المراد وهو خفيف المحمل ظريف الشمايل يليق بالملوك ان تخدمه لانه يصيد افخر ما يصيده البازي وهو الدراج والحمام والورشان وهو كثير الشبق وهو قوي عليه صيده لا يتركه الي ان يتلف احدهما واجمل اوصافه ان يكون صغيرا في المنظر ثقيلا في الميزان طويل الساقين قصير الفخذين [huwa ayḍan jār al-mizāj ʿalayhi al-qalaq al-zaʿāra yaʾnas waqtan wa-yastawḥish waqtan wa-huwa qawī al-nafs fa-idhā anisa minhu al-ṣaghīr balagha ṣāḥibuhu min ṣaydihi al-murād wa-huwa khafīf al-maḥmil ẓarīf al-shamāʾil yalīq bi-l-mulūk an takhdumahu li-annahu yaṣīd afkhar mā yaṣīduhu al-bāzī wa-huwa al-durrāj wa-l-ḥamām wa-l-warashān wa-huwa kathīr al-shabaq wa-huwa qawī ʿalayhi ṣaydihi lā yatrukuhu ilā an yatlafa aḥaduhumā wa-ajmal awṣāfihi an yakūna ṣaghīran fī al-manẓar thaqīlan fī al-mīzān ṭawīl al-sāqayn qaṣīr al-fakhdhayn] It too is of a hot III, 26 temperament, and therefore restless and of bad disposition, and sometimes it is tame, sometimes wild. Its breathing is strong. When it is tamed young, its master obtains his prey with a favourable wind. It is carried more easily and is clever by nature. Its use is most suited to kings, because it hunts nobler game than albazi, namely francolins, pigeons and turtledoves. It is very lustful. If it attacks a bird stronger than itself, it does not let it go until one of the two perishes. Most esteemed are those that are small in stature, heavy in weight, long in the shanks, short in the thighs R3. ¶

Quarta interpretatio est in Arabica Esaiae paraphrasi, ubi pro Hebraeo ינשוף jansuph, et Graeco ἴβις, legere est الحباري alhubara, quod Ga- briel Sionita perperam bubones reddit, cum حباري hubara sit otis, seu tar- da si mavis (4). Ita reddunt Avicennae interpretes lib. 2. in capite De ovis: neque aliter reddiderant capite De stercore; quamvis scribarum imperitia tardam in turdum mutaverit. Ita etiam Andreas Bellunensis in Explicatione nominum Arabicorum Avicennae. Lexicographi quoque, ut Giggeius, Golius, etc. Et veram esse explicationem, docet ejus descriptio in Abenbitare ex Assariphe, هو طاير كبير العنق رمادي اللون في منقاره بعض الطول وهو مشهور لحمه بين لحم الدجاج ولحم البط وهو اخف من لحم البط لانه بري وفيه شي col. 283 من الغلظ Avis est magni colli, cinerei coloris, rostri utcunque longi, no- tissima (nempe in Oriente) carnis inter gallinaceam et anserinam mediae; sed an- serina levioris: quia terrestris est avis, quamvis aliquid crassum habeat (5). Addit III, 27 Damir, وسلاحها سلاحها Et stercus ejus illi pro telo est, ludens in ambiguo vocis سلاح, quae et stercus et telum sonat. Rem ita explicat Alka- zuinius, اذا تصدها الصقر لا يزل يعلوا وينزل مع الصقر حتي تجد قصد فرماه بدرقه يبقا الصقر مقيدا مثل المكتوف فعند ذلك يجتمع الحباريات وينتف ريشه وفي ذلك هلاك الصقر Cum eam insequitur accipiter, non cessat cum accipitre ascendere et descendere, donec in ejus pupillam incidens stercus in eam conjecerit. Quo facto, constrictus manet accipiter, ut qui manibus pedi- busque vinctus est. Tum vero convenientes otides accipitris plumas vellicant, un- de illi certa mors (6). Quo Poëta alludens haec habet de quodam, qui inter fugiendum prae metu cacaverat:

A fourth interpretation is found in the Arabic paraphrase of Isaiah, where for the Hebrew ינשוף jansuph and the Greek ἴβις [ibis] one reads الحباري [al-ḥubārā] alhubara. Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)] wrongly renders this as "eagle owls", since حباري [ḥubārā] hubara is the otis [bustard], or tarda, if you prefer R4. So the translators of Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] render it in book 2, in the chapter On eggs; nor had they rendered it otherwise in the chapter On dung, although the ignorance of scribes changed tardam into turdum [thrush]. So also Andreas Bellunensis in his Explanation of the Arabic names of Avicenna; and the lexicographers too, such as Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)], Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)], etc. That this explanation is correct is shown by the description of the bird in Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], taken from Assariph [=al-Sharif (probably al-Idrisi), a source that Ibn al-Baytar quotes]: هو طاير كبير العنق رمادي اللون في منقاره بعض الطول وهو مشهور لحمه بين لحم الدجاج ولحم البط وهو اخف من لحم البط لانه بري وفيه شي col. 283 من الغلظ [huwa ṭāyir kabīr al-ʿunuq ramādī al-lawn fī minqārihi baʿḍ al-ṭūl wa-huwa mashhūr laḥmuhu bayna laḥm al-dajāj wa-laḥm al-baṭṭ wa-huwa akhaff min laḥm al-baṭṭ li-annahu barrī wa-fīhi shī min al-ghilaẓ] "It is a bird with a large neck, of ashen colour, with a somewhat long beak, very well known (that is, in the East); its flesh is midway between that of the hen and that of the goose, but lighter than goose, because it is a land bird, although it has something coarse about it" R5. III, 27 Damir adds: وسلاحها سلاحها [wa-silāḥuhā silāḥuhā] "And its dung serves it as a weapon", playing on the ambiguity of the word سلاح [silāḥ], which means both "dung" and "weapon". Alkazuinius explains the matter thus: اذا تصدها الصقر لا يزل يعلوا وينزل مع الصقر حتي تجد قصد فرماه بدرقه يبقا الصقر مقيدا مثل المكتوف فعند ذلك يجتمع الحباريات وينتف ريشه وفي ذلك هلاك الصقر [idhā taṣaddahā al-ṣaqr lā yazal yaʿlū wa-yanzil maʿa al-ṣaqr ḥattā tajid qaṣd fa-ramāhu bi-darqihi yabqā al-ṣaqr muqayyadan mithl al-maktūf fa-ʿinda dhālika yajtamiʿ al-ḥubāriyyāt wa-yantif rīshahu wa-fī dhālika halāk al-ṣaqr] "When the hawk pursues it, it does not cease to rise and descend with the hawk until, hitting its eye, it has cast its dung into it. When this is done, the hawk remains bound, like one tied hand and foot. Then the bustards gather and pluck the hawk's feathers, whence certain death for it" R6. Alluding to this, a poet says the following of someone who had befouled himself out of fear while fleeing: ¶

وهم تركوه اسلح من حباري ✡ راي صقرا واشرد من نعام ✡

وهم تركوه اسلح من حباري ✡ راي صقرا واشرد من نعام ✡ [wa-hum tarakūhu aslaḥ min ḥubārā ✡ raʾā ṣaqran wa-ashrad min naʿām ✡] ¶

Et eum reliquerunt otide quae accipitrem vidit merdosiorem, et struthione fugacio- rem. Eandem ajunt esse moestissimam, et moerore confici, cum plumae post defluvium tarde recrescunt: atque inde natum esse vulgatum adagium, اكبد من الحباري Otide moestior. Additur, ova propria deserere, ut aliena foveat. Sed hoc crimen Damir diluit, ut alibi a) diximus. Ovis ejus utuntur ad denigrandos capillos, sed non nisi masculinis, id est, unde mares nascituri sunt; quae filo inserto dignoscuntur: illud enim e masculino ovo nigrum educitur. Lapis in ingluvie latet, quo profluvium sistitur, et prohibentur somnia. Otis Persice vocatur جرز gerez. Mas Aegyptiace حبرج hubarig. Pullus Arabice نهار nehar, ut perdicis ليل lail. Quae duae voces cum diem et noctem vulgo sonent, in aequivocatione poëta quidam jocum ita quaesiit:

"And they left him more beshitten than a bustard that has seen a hawk, and more given to flight than an ostrich." They say too that it is most melancholy and is consumed with grief when its feathers grow back slowly after moulting; and that from this arose the common proverb اكبد من الحباري [akbad min al-ḥubārā] "More melancholy than a bustard." It is added that it deserts its own eggs in order to brood those of others. But Damir clears it of this charge, as we have said elsewhere a). Its eggs are used for blackening the hair, but only the male ones, that is, those from which males are to be hatched; these are distinguished by inserting a thread, for from a male egg it is drawn out black. In its crop lies a stone which stops a flux and prevents dreams. In Persian the bustard is called جرز [jurz] gerez; the male in Egyptian usage حبرج [ḥubruj] hubarig. The chick is called in Arabic نهار [nahār] nehar, as that of the partridge is ليل [layl] lail. Since these two words commonly mean "day" and "night", a certain poet sought a jest in the double meaning thus: ¶

ونهار رايت نصف ليل ✡ وليل رايت وسط النهار ✡ III, 28 Quibus videtur dicere, se diem vidisse media nocte, et noctem medio die, cum ibi نهار et ليل diem et noctem non significent, sed otidis et perdicis pullos. Multa hujusmodi possem addere, si haec res ageretur, ex Abenbitare, Damire et Alkazuinio petita: sed observasse sufficiet, in hujus avis descriptione unum esse, in quo Arabes Graecis sunt διαῤῥήδην oppositi. Graeci enim eam asserunt col. 284 brevis esse et tardi volatus, propter corporis gravitatem. Xenoph. in Cyro minore lib. 1. Τὰς δ᾽ ὠτίδας, ἄν τις ταχὺ ἀνίστῃ, ἔστι λαμβάνειν· πέτονται γὰρ βραχὺ, ὥσπερ πέρδικες, καὶ ταχὺ ἀπαγορεύουσι. Otidas autem, si quis gravius instet, capere licet: parum enim volant, ut et perdices, et cito fatiscunt. Et Athenaeus libro nono ad hunc Xenophontis locum, Ἀληθῆ λέγειν φησὶ τὸν Ξενο-φῶντα ὁ Πλούταρχος περὶ τῶν ὠτίδων· De otidibus Xenophontem vera prodidisse refert Plutarchus. Aelianus libri quinti capite vigesimo quarto, Solae avium otides canes timent; τὸ αἴτιον, βαρεῖαί τε εἰσι, καὶ σαρμῶν (lege σαρκῶν) ὄγκον περιφέρουσιν, quia graves sunt, et carnium pondus circumferunt. Ac proinde eas alae non facile attollunt et allevant. Unde est, quod, mole corporis onustae, demisso volatu utuntur, et prope terram, et a canibus saepe capiuntur. Atque ob id ipsum tardae videntur dictae. Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo secundo, Proximae eis (struthionibus) sunt, quas Hispania aves tardas appellat, Graecia otidas. Glossae veteres, Avis tarda, ὦτος· lege ὠτίς. Nam otus alia est avis. Glossae Philoxeni, Ὠτὶς τὸ ὄρνεον, Avis tarda. Hispani proinde hodieque abestardas vocant, ut nos oustardas. Illas tamen Arabes etiam a volatu commendant. Damir, وهي من اشد الطير طيرانا وابعدها سوطا وذلك انها تصاد بالبصرة فيوجد في حواصلها الحبة الخضر التي شجرها البطم ومنابتها في تخوم ارض الشام ولذلك قالوا في المثل اطير من الحباري Avem esse fortissimi volatus, longissimeque evagari, ex eo patet, quod, cum capitur circa Basorain (ad Euphratis ostia) in ingluvie ejus reperitur granum viride, cujus arbor terebinthus crescit in finibus Syriae Damascenae. Unde est, quod vulgato proverbio dicimus aliquem esse „Otide volaciorem,“ si verbo datur venia (7).

ونهار رايت نصف ليل [wa-nahār raʾaytu niṣfa layl] ✡ وليل رايت وسط النهار [wa-layl raʾaytu wasaṭa al-nahār] ✡ III, 28 In these lines he seems to say that he saw day at midnight and night at midday, whereas there نهار [nahār] and ليل [layl] do not mean day and night, but the young of the bustard and of the partridge. I could add many things of this kind, drawn from Abenbitar, Damir and Alkazuinius, if this were the matter at hand; but it will suffice to have observed that in the description of this bird there is one point on which the Arabs are διαῤῥήδην [diarrhēdēn] opposed to the Greeks. For the Greeks assert col. 284 that it has a short and slow flight because of the heaviness of its body. Xenoph. in the Cyrus the Younger, book 1: Τὰς δ᾽ ὠτίδας, ἄν τις ταχὺ ἀνίστῃ, ἔστι λαμβάνειν· πέτονται γὰρ βραχὺ, ὥσπερ πέρδικες, καὶ ταχὺ ἀπαγορεύουσι [Tas d᾽ ōtidas, an tis tachy anistē, esti lambanein; petontai gar brachy, hōsper perdikes, kai tachy apagoreuousi]. Bustards, however, can be caught if one presses them hard: for they fly only a little, like partridges, and quickly tire. And Athenaeus, in book 9, on this passage of Xenophon: Ἀληθῆ λέγειν φησὶ τὸν Ξενοφῶντα ὁ Πλούταρχος περὶ τῶν ὠτίδων [Alēthē legein phēsi ton Xenophōnta ho Ploutarchos peri tōn ōtidōn]; Plutarch reports that Xenophon told the truth about bustards. Aelian, book 5, chapter 24: Of birds, bustards alone fear dogs; τὸ αἴτιον, βαρεῖαί τε εἰσι, καὶ σαρμῶν (read σαρκῶν) ὄγκον περιφέρουσιν [to aition, bareiai te eisi, kai sarmōn (sarkōn) onkon peripherousin], because they are heavy and carry about a mass of flesh. And so their wings do not easily raise and lift them. Hence, burdened by the bulk of their body, they fly low and near the ground, and are often caught by dogs. And for this very reason they seem to have been called tardae [slow]. Pliny, book 10, chapter 22: Next to them (the ostriches) are the birds which Spain calls aves tardas, and Greece otides. The old Glosses: Avis tarda, ὦτος [ōtos]; read ὠτίς [ōtis]. For the otus is another bird. The Glosses of Philoxenus: Ὠτὶς τὸ ὄρνεον [Ōtis to orneon], Avis tarda. Accordingly the Spaniards still today call them abestardas, as we call them oustardas. Yet the Arabs actually praise them for their flight. Damir: وهي من اشد الطير طيرانا وابعدها سوطا وذلك انها تصاد بالبصرة فيوجد في حواصلها الحبة الخضر التي شجرها البطم ومنابتها في تخوم ارض الشام ولذلك قالوا في المثل اطير من الحباري [wa-hiya min ashadd al-ṭayr ṭayarānan wa-abʿadihā sawṭan wa-dhālika annahā tuṣād bi-l-Baṣra fa-yūjad fī ḥawāṣilihā al-ḥabba al-khaḍr allatī shajaruhā al-buṭm wa-manābituhā fī tukhūm arḍ al-Shām wa-li-dhālika qālū fī al-mathal aṭyar min al-ḥubārā] That the bird has a very strong flight and roams very far is clear from this: when it is caught around Basra (at the mouth of the Euphrates), a green seed is found in its crop, whose tree, the terebinth, grows in the borders of Damascene Syria. Hence we say in a common proverb that someone is „more volatile than the bustard,“ if the word may be pardoned R7. ¶

III, 29 Porro ex tot versionibus vocis ינשוף jansuph Syram et Chaldaeam praefero, qua קפופא kiphupha redditur, id est, bubo. Constat enim, ינשוף jansuph ab Hebraeo נשף neseph, deduci, quod crepusculum et tenebras sonat. Unde valde verisimili ratione colligitur, hanc avem esse nocturnam. Sic Arabice رامسات ramisath in Alcamus sunt aves, quae noctu volant, الطير التي تطير بالليل quia Chaldaeis et Syris ܪܡܫܐ ramsa est vespera. Et Es. XXXIV, 11. ad Idumaeae vastitatem pertinet, non ut ibis, aut accipiter, aut otis, sed ut in ea habitet bubo; de quo Plinius libri decimi capite duodecimo: Deserta incolit, nec tantum desolata, sed dira etiam et inaccessa, noctis monstrum. Et Festus, Buteo vastitatis est causa, his locis, quae intraverit, ut bubo. Et idem cum corvo recte jungitur, cum avis utraque feralis et inauspicata sit, et in voce dirum aliquid et ominosum habeat. Proinde caput integrum Plinius habet De inauspicatis avibus, cornice, corvis, bubone. Ubi de cornice dicit, Ipsa est ales inauspicatae garrulitatis. Et de corvo, Pessima eorum significatio, cum glutiunt vocem, veluti strangulati. Et de bubone, Bubo funebris, et maxime abominatus publicis auspiciis, etc. Nec cantu aliquo vocalis, sed gemitu. Itaque in urbibus, aut in luce visus, dirum omnino ostentum est. Hinc Ovidius Metamorphoseως libri quinti Fabula octava de Ascalapho, col. 285

III, 29 Further, of so many versions of the word ינשוף jansuph I prefer the Syriac and the Chaldee, in which it is rendered קפופא kiphupha, that is, eagle owl. For it is agreed that ינשוף jansuph is derived from the Hebrew נשף neseph, which means twilight and darkness. From this it is inferred, on very probable grounds, that this bird is nocturnal. Thus in Arabic, in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], رامسات [rāmisāt] ramisath are birds that fly by night, الطير التي تطير بالليل [al-ṭayr allatī taṭīr bi-l-layl], because for the Chaldeans and Syrians ܪܡܫܐ [רמשא] ramsa is evening. And Es. XXXIV, 11. it belongs to the desolation of Idumaea not that the ibis, or the hawk, or the bustard, but that the eagle owl should dwell in it; of which Pliny, book 10, chapter 12: It inhabits deserts, and not only desolate places but also dreadful and inaccessible ones, a monster of the night. And Festus: The buzzard is a cause of desolation to those places which it has entered, like the eagle owl. And the same bird is rightly joined with the raven, since both birds are funereal and of ill omen, and have something dreadful and ominous in their voice. Accordingly Pliny has an entire chapter On birds of ill omen: the crow, ravens, the eagle owl. There he says of the crow, It is itself a bird of ill-omened garrulity. And of the raven, Their worst omen is when they swallow their voice, as if strangled. And of the eagle owl, The eagle owl is funereal, and most abominated in public auspices, etc. Nor is it vocal with any song, but with a groan. And so, if seen in cities or by daylight, it is an altogether dreadful portent. Hence Ovid, Metamorphoses book 5, Fable 8, on Ascalaphus, col. 285 ¶

Foeda fit volucris, venturi nuntia luctus,

the sluggish eagle owl, a dire omen for mortals. ¶

Et Apulejus libro tertio Metamorphoseως de bubonibus, Quid quod istas nocturnas aves, cum penetraverint larem quempiam, sollicite prehensas foribus videmus ad-figi, ut quod infaustis volatibus familiae minantur exitium, suis luant cruciatibus? Iam, si Historicos consulas, apud Dionem in morte Augusti, cum decrevisset Senatus, ut publice supplicaretur propter ejus valetudinem, τότε συνέδριον κε-κλεισμένον εὑρέθη, καὶ βύας, ὑπερ αὐτου καθήμενος, ἔβυξε, curia clausa reperta est, buboque, qui super eam consederat, bubulavit. Et in morte Commodi, Βύας ἀπ' αὐτου (Καπιτωλίου) ἔβυξε. Bubo ex Capitolio bubulavit. Ita ille ipse Herodes Agrippa, qui Caesareae ab insana plebe pro Deo salutabatur Act. XII, 22. ἀνακύψας μετ' ὀλίγον, τὸν βουβῶνα τῆς ἑαυτου κεφαλῆς ὑπερκαθε-ζόμενον εἶδεν ἐπὶ σχοινίου τινὸς, ἄγγελον τοῦτον εὐθὺς ἐνόησεν κακῶν εἶναι, τὸν καὶ πότε τῶν ἀγαθῶν γενόμενον, paullo post suspiciens, bubonem supra caput suum vidit, funi cuidam insidentem, quem sensit malorum esse nuntium, ut olim bonorum fuerat. Nempe cum olim, jussu Caji Caligulae vinctus, arbori inniteretur, cui bubo insederat, praedixerat Germanus quidam, fore, ut brevi, mutatis rerum vicibus, ad amplissimam dignitatem eveheretur. Memento tamen, inquiebat, ὁπότε εἰς αὖθις τὸν ὄρνιν θεάσαιο τοῦτον, πέντε ἡμέραις σοι τὴν τε-λευτὴν ἐσομένην, quandocunque hanc avem denuo conspexeris, intra quinque dies te esse moriturum. Utrumque in Iosepho legitur libri decimi octavi capite octavo, et libri decimi noni capite septimo. Unde apparet, cur Agrippa moriturus eundem bubonem malorum ἄγγελον, seu nuntium esse dixerit, qui bonorum olim fuerat. Tamen hunc Iosephi locum Eusebius ita descripsit: Ἀνακύψας δὲ μετ' ὀλίγον τῆς ἑαυτου κεφαλῆς ὑπερκαθεζόμενον εἶδεν ἄγγελον, bubonis mentione omissa; quasi significetur ille angelus, a quo rex percussus fuisse legitur Actor. XII, 23. quod non leve est παρόραμα.

And Apuleius, in book 3 of the Metamorphoses, says of eagle owls: “What of the fact that we see these night birds, when they have entered some house, carefully caught and nailed to the doors, so that by their own torments they may atone for the ruin which they threaten the family with their ill-omened flights?” Now, if you consult the historians: in Dio, at the death of Augustus, when the Senate had decreed that public supplications be made on account of his health, τότε συνέδριον κεκλεισμένον εὑρέθη, καὶ βύας, ὑπερ αὐτου καθήμενος, ἔβυξε [tote synedrion kekleismenon heurethē, kai byas, hyper autou kathēmenos, ebyxe], “the senate house was found shut, and an eagle owl, which had settled upon it, hooted.” And at the death of Commodus, Βύας ἀπ' αὐτου (Καπιτωλίου) ἔβυξε [Byas ap' autou (Kapitōliou) ebyxe], “An eagle owl hooted from the Capitol.” So too that very Herod Agrippa who at Caesarea was hailed as a god by the frenzied populace, Act. XII, 22: ἀνακύψας μετ' ὀλίγον, τὸν βουβῶνα τῆς ἑαυτου κεφαλῆς ὑπερκαθεζόμενον εἶδεν ἐπὶ σχοινίου τινὸς, ἄγγελον τοῦτον εὐθὺς ἐνόησεν κακῶν εἶναι, τὸν καὶ πότε τῶν ἀγαθῶν γενόμενον [anakypsas met' oligon, ton boubōna tēs heautou kephalēs hyperkathezomenon eiden epi schoiniou tinos, angelon touton euthys enoēsen kakōn einai, ton kai pote tōn agathōn genomenon], “looking up a little later, he saw an eagle owl above his head, sitting on a rope, and he perceived that it was a messenger of evil, as it had once been of good.” For formerly, when by order of Gaius Caligula he had been bound and was leaning against a tree on which an eagle owl had settled, a certain German had foretold that shortly, with a change of fortune, he would be raised to the highest dignity. “But remember,” he said, ὁπότε εἰς αὖθις τὸν ὄρνιν θεάσαιο τοῦτον, πέντε ἡμέραις σοι τὴν τελευτὴν ἐσομένην [hopote eis authis ton ornin theasaio touton, pente hēmerais soi tēn teleutēn esomenēn], “whenever you see this bird again, you will die within five days.” Both accounts are read in Josephus, book 18, chapter 8, and book 19, chapter 7. Hence it is clear why Agrippa, when about to die, said that the same eagle owl was an ἄγγελον [angelon], or messenger, of evil which had once been one of good. Yet Eusebius transcribed this passage of Josephus thus: Ἀνακύψας δὲ μετ' ὀλίγον τῆς ἑαυτου κεφαλῆς ὑπερκαθεζόμενον εἶδεν ἄγγελον [Anakypsas de met' oligon tēs heautou kephalēs hyperkathezomenon eiden angelon], omitting the mention of the eagle owl, as if that angel were meant by whom the king is read to have been struck, Actor. XII, 23, which is no slight παρόραμα [parorama]. ¶
