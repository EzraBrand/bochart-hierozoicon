Locustarum nomina et species.

The names and kinds of locusts. ¶

Vt Hebraeorum terra locustis, ita lingua locustarum nominibus est maxime foecunda. Nam, ut Talmudica taceam, quae magno numero occurrunt, in solis Prophetarum scriptis doctores decem numerant. Ea sunt 1. ארבה arbe. 2. גוב gob, vel גובי gobai. 3. גזם gazam. 4. חגב chagab. 5. חנמל chanamal. 6. חסיל chasil. 7. חרגל chargal. 8. ילק jelek. 9. סלעם solam. 10. צלצל col. 442 tselatsal. Quarum vocum origines, cum pleraeque parum notae sint, hic obiter explicare operae pretium esse duximus: quia res latet in verbis, et ex nominibus multa eruuntur, quae ad horum animalium naturam pertinent.

As the land of the Hebrews is most fertile in locusts, so is their language in names for locusts. For, to say nothing of the Talmudic ones, which occur in great number, in the writings of the Prophets alone the doctors count ten. They are: 1. ארבה arbe. 2. גוב gob, or גובי gobai. 3. גזם gazam. 4. חגב chagab. 5. חנמל chanamal. 6. חסיל chasil. 7. חרגל chargal. 8. ילק jelek. 9. סלעם solam. 10. צלצל col. 442 tselatsal. Since the origins of these words are for the most part little known, we have thought it worthwhile to explain them here in passing: for the thing lies hidden in the words, and much that pertains to the nature of these animals is drawn out of their names. ¶

I. Primum ארבה arbe, Aben Ezra in Leviticum recte deducit מלשון רבוי, ex significatione multitudinis. Nam רבה raba multum esse, vel multiplicari significat. Unde ארבה arbe, praefixo Aleph, ut passim. Nec obscurum, cur a copia locustae nomen traxerint, cum nullum animal sit foecundius. Hinc de Ma- col. 443 dianitis Iud. VI, 5. Et veniebant ut locustae, ipsi, et cameli eorum innumeri. Et Iudic. VII, 12. Diffusi erant in ea convalle, tanquam locustae multitudine. Et Psal. CV, 34. Et venit locusta et bruchus; cujus non erat numerus. Et Ierem. XLVI, 23. de sylvae arboribus, Quia plures sunt locustis, neque illis est numerus. Et LI, 14. Nisi te impleam hominibus, quasi bruchis. Et Ioel. I, 6. de locustis, Iudaeam devastaturis, Gens ascendit contra terram tuam valida et innumerabilis. Et Nahum III, 15. Esto tam copiosa quam bruchus, tam copiosa quam locusta. Et III, 252 Iudith. II, 11. Conveniebant ad eos ut locustae, et ut arena terrae: innumerabiles enim erant prae multitudine. Et de locustis Aegypti Exod. X, 5. Operient super- ficiem terrae, ita, ut non possit quisquam videre terram. Hinc Hieronymus in Ioelem, Quid locustis innumerabilius? Et vulgare Arabum proverbium, مال كالدبا Opes (vel pecudes) ut locustae, id est, valde copiosae. Sed haec nemini non nota sunt (1).

I. The first, ארבה arbe, Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] on Leviticus rightly derives מלשון רבוי, from the meaning of multitude. For רבה raba means to be much or to be multiplied. Hence ארבה arbe, with Aleph prefixed, as often. Nor is it obscure why locusts took their name from abundance, since no animal is more prolific. Hence of the Midianites col. 443 Iud. VI, 5: And they came like locusts, they and their camels without number. And Judg 7:12: They were spread out in that valley like locusts in multitude. And Ps 105:34: And the locust came, and the bruchus, of which there was no number. And Jer 46:23, of the trees of the forest: Because they are more than locusts, and there is no number of them. And LI, 14: Surely I will fill you with men, as with bruchi. And Joel 1:6, of the locusts that were to lay waste Judaea: A nation is come up against your land, strong and without number. And Nahum III, 15: Be as numerous as the bruchus, as numerous as the locust. And III, 252 Jdt 2:11: They gathered to them like locusts, and like the sand of the earth: for they were innumerable for multitude. And of the locusts of Egypt, Exod 10:5: They shall cover the face of the earth, so that no one can see the earth. Hence Jerome on Joel: What is more innumerable than locusts? And the common proverb of the Arabs, مال كالدبا [māl ka-l-dabā] Wealth (or cattle) like locusts, that is, very abundant. But these things are known to everyone R1. ¶

II. At locusta cur Hebraice dicitur גוב gob, vel גובי gobai, Amos VII, 1. et Nahum III, 17. et Arabice جاب gab, vel جابي gabi, adhuc nescirem; nisi me docuissent Arabes, apud quos جبا gaba Verbum e terra emergere significat, quod locustarum proprium. Aristoteles lib. 5. Historiae capite 28. Ὅταν τέκωσιν (αἱ ἀκρίδες) ἐνταῦθα γίνονται σκώληκες ὠοειδεῖς, οἳ περιλαμβάνονται III, 253 ὑπό τινος γῆς λεπτῆς, ὥσπερ ὑφ᾽ ὑμένος· ἐν ταύτῃ δὲ ἐκπέτονται. Cum locustae pepererunt, ibi fiunt vermes ovorum specie, qui terra quadam praetenui, tanquam membranula, ambiuntur, unde evolant. Gaza, Qua dissecta, emergunt locustae, ac evolant. Plinius lib. II. cap. 29. Hae pariunt in terram demisso spinae caule ova condensa autumni tempore. Ea durant hyeme sub terra: subsequente autem exitu veris, emittunt parvas, etc. Eodem refero, quod ab Aethiopibus et Troglodytis locustae vocantur አንበጣ፡ anbat. Nempe Arabice نبط nabata est scaturire, atque e terra emanare ac emergere. Quod quidem fontium proprium, sed et ad alia transfertur καταχρηστικῶς (2).

II. But why the locust is called in Hebrew גוב gob, or גובי gobai, Amos 7:1 and Nahum III, 17., and in Arabic جاب [jāb] gab, or جابي [jābī] gabi, I would still not know, had not the Arabs taught me, among whom the verb جبا [jabā] gaba means to emerge from the earth, which is characteristic of locusts. Aristotle, History book 5, chapter 28: Ὅταν τέκωσιν (αἱ ἀκρίδες) ἐνταῦθα γίνονται σκώληκες ὠοειδεῖς, οἳ περιλαμβάνονται [Hotan tekōsin (hai akrides) entautha ginontai skōlēkes ōoeideis, hoi perilambanontai] III, 253 ὑπό τινος γῆς λεπτῆς, ὥσπερ ὑφ᾽ ὑμένος· ἐν ταύτῃ δὲ ἐκπέτονται [hypo tinos gēs leptēs, hōsper hyph᾽ hymenos; en tautē de ekpetontai]. When the locusts have laid, there arise there egg-shaped grubs, which are enclosed by a very thin earth, as by a little membrane, from which they fly out. Gaza: When this is split open, the locusts emerge and fly out. Pliny, book 11, ch. 29: These lay their eggs, packed close, in the ground in autumn, letting down the stalk of their spine. They last through the winter under the ground; and at the following end of spring they send forth small ones, etc. To the same I refer the fact that by the Ethiopians and Troglodytes locusts are called አንበጣ፡ [ʾanəbaṭā] anbat. For in Arabic نبط [nabaṭa] nabata is to gush forth, and to issue and emerge from the earth. This indeed is proper to springs, but it is also transferred to other things καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs] R2. ¶

III. Tertium locustae nomen גזם gazam, Ioel I, 4. et II, 25. et Amos IV, 9. Hebraei deducunt a גוז guz, vel גזז gazaz, quod tondere est et abscindere. Et Finale Mem Servile esse volunt; quamvis simile Nomen nullum proferant. Quippe חנם, gratis, ריקם, frustra, דומם, quiete, יומם, interdiu, אמנם, vere, quae hic citant, non sunt Nomina, sed Adverbia. Itaque vera Radix est Verbum גזם gazam, quod Talmudici in abscindendi et amputandi notione quandoque usurpant: ut in Avoda Zara fol. 50. b. אין מגזמין וסכין שמן בגזום, Non amputant ramos arborum, sed oleum ungunt in locum amputationis. Sic Arabice in Alcamus, جَزَمَ النحل, Putavit palmam. Aethiopice etiam occurrit pro ἐκκόπτειν Matth. III, 10. et VII, 19. Luc. III, 9. et XIII, 7. 9. In quibus locis aeque agitur de arborum amputatione. Inde igitur locustae dictae; quia praeacutis dentibus non solum gramen, et segetes, atque arborum folia, sed et cortices, ipsosque arborum teneriores ramusculos ita erodunt, ac si amputarent. Sed et Amos IV, 9. Hortos vestros, et vineas vestras, et ficeta vestra, et oliveta vestra יאכל הגזם, comedet Gazam (seu locusta). III. The third name of the locust, גזם gazam, Joel 1:4 and II, 25. and Amos 4:9, the Hebrews derive from גוז guz or גזז gazaz, which means to shear and to cut off. And they hold that the final Mem is servile, although they produce no similar noun. For חנם, gratis [freely], ריקם, frustra [in vain], דומם, quiete [quietly], יומם, interdiu [by day], אמנם, vere [truly], which they cite here, are not nouns but adverbs. Therefore the true root is the verb גזם gazam, which the Talmudists sometimes use in the sense of cutting off and pruning: as in Avoda Zara fol. 50b, אין מגזמין וסכין שמן בגזום, They do not prune the branches of trees, but they smear oil on the place of the pruning. So in Arabic, in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], جَزَمَ النحل [jazama al-nakhl], He pruned the palm. In Ethiopic too it occurs for ἐκκόπτειν [ekkoptein], Matt 3:10 and VII, 19. Luke 3:9 and XIII, 7. 9. In these places too the subject is the cutting down of trees. From this, then, locusts were named, because with their very sharp teeth they gnaw not only grass, crops and the leaves of trees, but also the bark and the more tender twigs of the trees themselves, as if they were cutting them off. Pliny, in the place cited above: Scorching many things by their touch, but gnawing everything with their bite, even the doors of houses. Jerome on Joel ch. 1: The Jews think that in the days of Joel so innumerable a multitude of locusts came upon Judaea that they filled everything, and left, I will not say the crops, but not even the bark and branches of the vines and trees. And also Amos 4:9: Your gardens, and your vineyards, and your fig groves, and your olive groves יאכל הגזם, the Gazam (or locust) shall eat. Accordingly col. 444 Pisides attributes to the swarms of locusts a μυριόστομον ξίφος [myriostomon xiphos], a sword of ten thousand edges. So much according to the mind of the Hebrews, who with the greatest agreement hold that גזם gazam is a species of locust: following, of course, the Chaldee, for whom gazam is זחלא, the creeping locust, III, 254 or ἄπτερος [apteros], as we shall show below. The Syriac also renders it ܡܫܘܛܐ [משוטא] in the same sense. For ܡܫܘܛܐ [משוטא] is the Chaldee שמוטא inverted. And שמוטא is counted among the species of locust, as is the Hebrew חסיל, for which it stands in Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] on 1 Reg. VIII, 37. and Joel 1:4 R3. But for the Greeks and Jerome גזם gazam is κάμπη [kampē], caterpillar. Although Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)] in his Miscellanea, book 5, ch. 20, tries to defend this version with many arguments, he by no means persuades me: both because the Hebrews, Chaldeans and Syrians think otherwise, and because the name גזם gazam, formed from the pruning of branches, seems to suit the caterpillar less than the locust, whose bite is stronger. For the bite of the caterpillar scorches rather than cuts off. Columella: ¶

— — — serpitque eruca per hortos,
Quos super ingrediens exurit semina morsu.

— — — and the caterpillar creeps through the gardens,
And, advancing over them, scorches the seedlings with its bite. ¶

Therefore, just as it is called ERUCA from gnawing [erodere], so it is also called URUCA from burning [urere]. The old Latin-Greek Glosses edited by Stephanus, p. 347: Uruca, κάμπη [kampē]. And the Greek-Latin ones, p. 501: Κάμπη λαχάνου [Kampē lachanou], Eruca, uruca. And p. 516: Λαχάνων κάμπη [Lachanōn kampē], urica. Since these three places confirm one another, none of them ought to have been suspect to Fuller R4. ¶

IV. Quartum locustae nomen חגב chagab notum esse asserit Aben Ezra in Leviticum מלשון ישמעאל, ex lingua Ismaelis, id est, Arabica: quod falsissimum. Quamvis enim apud Arabes quamplurima sint locustarum nomina, tamen huic simile nullum occurrit. Interim origo vocis potest esse Arabica. Nam III, 255 Arabicum Verbum حجب chagaba velare significat. Sic Avicenna capite De anatomia oculi, tunicam ἀραχνοειδῆν ideo subtilem esse docet, quia, si densa esset, يحجب الضو عن الجليدية lumen velaret ab hu- more glaciali, seu crystallino, id est, lumen ab eo arceret, tanquam oppanso velo. Unde حجاب chigab est velum Matth. XXVII, 51. et Hebr. VI, 19. et حاجب chagib, id est, janitor, Gen. XLIII, 16. 19. editionis Parisiensis; quia januam claudens ei quasi velum obtendit. Locustas igitur indidem חגבים cha- gabim, dictas fuisse putaverim; quia, cum turmatim volant, solis lumini velum videntur obtendere, ut infra docebimus in Ioel. II, 20. (5).

IV. Aben Ezra on Leviticus asserts that the fourth name of the locust, חגב chagab, is known מלשון ישמעאל, from the language of Ishmael, that is, Arabic: which is utterly false. For although among the Arabs there are very many names for locusts, none similar to this one occurs. Yet the origin of the word may be Arabic. For III, 255 the Arabic verb حجب [ḥajaba] chagaba means to veil. Thus Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], in the chapter On the anatomy of the eye, teaches that the ἀραχνοειδῆν [arachnoeidēn] tunic is thin for this reason, that if it were dense, يحجب الضو عن الجليدية [yaḥjub al-ḍaw ʿan al-jalīdiyya] it would veil the light from the glacial humour, or crystalline, that is, it would keep the light from it as if by a veil spread before it. Hence حجاب [ḥijāb] chigab is the veil, Matt 27:51 and Heb 6:19, and حاجب [ḥājib] chagib, that is, doorkeeper, Gen 43:16 19. in the Paris edition; because in closing the door he as it were draws a veil over it. I would therefore think that locusts were called חגבים chagabim from the same source, because when they fly in swarms they seem to draw a veil over the light of the sun, as we shall show below on Joel 2:20 R5. ¶

V. Quintum חנמל chanamal, Psal. LXXVIII, 47. pro locusta sumitur a R. Selomone et R. Chasane, quem citat Aben Ezra. Sed alii melius chanamal statuunt aliquid esse grandini analogum. Ut de more sint ἀντίστροφα duo ejusdem versus hemistichia, quae sic habent, Et occidit בברד, in grandine vites eorum, et sycomoros eorum בחנמל, in Chanamal. Ita Hebraea ad verbum. Itaque Aben Ezra lapides grandinis hic intelligit. Sed Saadias et Kimchius, id, quod Arabes vocant יאלצקיע, id est, pruinam (6). Recte omnino. Ita enim Graeci veteres, Καὶ τὰς συκαμίνους αὐτῶν ἐν τῇ πάχνῃ. Unde Vulgatus, Et moros eorum in pruina. Et Arabicum Psalterium vetus apud me manuscriptum وتوثهم بالصقيع. Et Aethiopicum, ወበለሶሞን፡ በአስሐትያ፡ omnes eodem sensu. Praeterea in Midras חנמל chanamal per metathesin dici asseritur pro נחמל nachamal; quia נח ומל nach vemal, quiescit et incidit. Quorum utrumque pruinae valde convenit. Hinc Theophrastus lib. 5. De causis cap. 19. nivem cum pruina conferens, Ἡ μὲν οὐκ ἐπιμένει, inquit, ἀλλ᾽ ἀποτήκεται ἀπὸ τῶν col. 445 κλημάτων, καὶ τῶν βλαστῶν· ἡ δὲ πάχνη (ἐπιμένουσα, id enim supplendum, ut oppositio constet) ταῦτ᾽ ἀποκαίει, illa quidem non diu manet, sed a palmiti- bus et germinibus liquescens delabitur: at pruina (diu manens) haec exurit. Et infra, Τμητικοτέρα δοκεῖ ἡ πάχνη τῆς χίονος εἶναι, Vim incidendi pruina quam III, 256 nix majorem habere videtur. Plinius quoque pruinam satis persidere scribit, id est, diu insidere. Verba sunt libri 17. cap. 24. Pruina perniciosior natura est, quoniam lapsa persidet. Pruinam igitur potius quam locustam voce חנמל significari, vero valde consentaneum est. Aliud ἐρυσίβη, rubigo. Tamen in Graeco Scholiaste in Psalmos ἐρυσίβη ἐςὶ κατὰ τὸν Ἡρωδιανὸν τὸ ἐπικάθιζον τοῖς καρ-ποῖς παχνῶδες, καὶ διαφθεῖρον αὐτοὺς, rubigo est juxta Herodianum pruinosum id, quod fructibus insidens eos corrumpit (7).

V. The fifth, חנמל chanamal, Ps 78:47, is taken for the locust by R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] and R. Chasan, whom Aben Ezra cites. But others more rightly hold that chanamal is something analogous to hail, so that, as usual, the two hemistichs of the same verse correspond (ἀντίστροφα [antistropha]). They read: And he destroyed בברד, with hail their vines, and their sycomores בחנמל, with Chanamal. So the Hebrew word for word. Accordingly Aben Ezra understands hailstones here. But Saadias and Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] understand what the Arabs call יאלצקיע, that is, hoarfrost R6. Quite rightly. For so the ancient Greeks: Καὶ τὰς συκαμίνους αὐτῶν ἐν τῇ πάχνῃ [Kai tas sykaminous autōn en tē pachnē]. Hence the Vulgate translator: And their mulberry trees with hoarfrost. And the old Arabic Psalter in manuscript in my possession: وتوثهم بالصقيع [wa-tūthahum bi-l-ṣaqīʿ]. And the Ethiopic: ወበለሶሞን፡ በአስሐትያ፡ [wabalasomon baʾasəḥatəyā]; all in the same sense. Moreover, in the Midrash it is asserted that חנמל chanamal is said by metathesis for נחמל nachamal, because נח ומל nach vemal, it rests and cuts. Both of these suit hoarfrost very well. Hence Theophrastus, book 5, De causis, ch. 19, comparing snow with hoarfrost, says: Ἡ μὲν οὐκ ἐπιμένει, ἀλλ᾽ ἀποτήκεται ἀπὸ τῶν col. 445 κλημάτων, καὶ τῶν βλαστῶν· ἡ δὲ πάχνη [Hē men ouk epimenei, all᾽ apotēketai apo tōn klēmatōn, kai tōn blastōn; hē de pachnē] (ἐπιμένουσα [epimenousa], for this must be supplied for the opposition to hold) ταῦτ᾽ ἀποκαίει [taut᾽ apokaiei], the former does not stay long, but melts and slips off the shoots and buds; but hoarfrost (staying long) burns them. And below: Τμητικοτέρα δοκεῖ ἡ πάχνη τῆς χίονος εἶναι [Tmētikotera dokei hē pachnē tēs chionos einai], Hoarfrost seems to have a greater power III, 256 of cutting than snow. Pliny too writes that hoarfrost persidere [settles in], that is, stays settled long. The words are in book 17, ch. 24: Hoarfrost is more destructive by nature, because once fallen it stays settled. It is therefore very consistent with the truth that the word חנמל signifies hoarfrost rather than the locust. Ἐρυσίβη [erysibē], blight, is something else. Yet in the Greek Scholiast on the Psalms: ἐρυσίβη ἐςὶ κατὰ τὸν Ἡρωδιανὸν τὸ ἐπικάθιζον τοῖς καρποῖς παχνῶδες, καὶ διαφθεῖρον αὐτοὺς [erysibē esti kata ton Hērōdianon to epikathizon tois karpois pachnōdes, kai diaphtheiron autous], blight, according to Herodian, is that frost-like thing which settles on the fruits and spoils them R7. ¶

VI. Sextum locustae nomen חסיל chasil, factum est a Verbo חסל chasal, quod consumere significat, et de locusta proprie dicitur. Sic in Hebraeo textu Deut. XXVIII, 38. Semen multum in agrum jacies, et parum colliges; כי יחסלנו הארבה, quia consumet illud locusta. Et in Chaldaeo Onkelosi ארי יחסליניה גובא. Et Nahum III, 16. in Ionathane יחסלונך כגובא, Consument te ut locusta. Unde, significatione nonnihil immutata, חסל etiam in absolvendi et perficiendi notione passim occurrit, quasi tonsummare fere idem sit, quod consumere (8).

VI. The sixth name of the locust, חסיל chasil, is formed from the verb חסל chasal, which means to consume and is properly said of the locust. So in the Hebrew text, Deut 28:38: You shall cast much seed into the field and gather little, כי יחסלנו הארבה, for the locust shall consume it. And in the Chaldee of Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos]: ארי יחסליניה גובא. And Nahum III, 16., in Jonathan: יחסלונך כגובא, They shall consume you like the locust. Hence, with the meaning somewhat altered, חסל also occurs frequently in the sense of finishing and completing, as if to consummate were nearly the same as to consume R8. ¶

III, 257 VII. Septimum חרגל chargol, unde fiat, Hebraei prorsus ignorant. Sed Arabice حرجل chargala sonat longum esse, aut longa serie ferri et produci. Unde حرجل chargal, vel حرجلة chorgola, in Alcamus, الجماعة من الخيل والقطعة مي الجراد de equis, agmen significat; et de locustis, gregem. Quem quandoque patere per duodecim milliaria, scribit Cada- mustus Navigationum cap. 13. Et quamvis in Europa minus pateant, tamen etiam in ea quandoque vastissima videntur locustarum agmina. Aldrovandus lib. 4. De insectis pag. 423. Scribunt anno 1475. Sirediensem et Lenciciensem oram, at- que Masoviam, ingentem locustarum vim ex Ungaria, Moravia et Silesia adveni- entem, pervagatam esse, etc. continuatis ad sesquimilliarium in latitudinem, et alte- rum tantum in longitudinem, examinibus, id est, ad tria milliaria. Surius, ad annum 1541. de locustis circa Poloniam: Hae tanta multitudine provolarunt, ut duo milliaria in longum et latum cubito tenus ita occuparent, ut solis quoque splen- dorem a toto illo terrae spatio sua densitate excluderent. Ita, ut a longa serie non immerito nomen habeant haec locustarum agmina. Tamen a Mose non sumitur חרגל chargol pro locustarum grege, sed pro locustae certa specie. Ita etiam in tractatu Talmudico De Sabbatho fol. 67. in Misna, יוצאין בביצת החרגל ובשן של שועל ובמסמר מן הצלוב משום רפואה, Egrediuntur (licite die Sabbathi) cum ovo chargol, et cum dente vulpis, et cum clavo patibuli medicinae causa (appenso): et ibi Gemara dicit ovum chargol adhibitum fuisse לשיחלא. Et Glossa שיחלא esse כאבה און והרלי באזן, dolorem auris, aut morbum in aure (9).

III, 257 VII. Where the seventh, חרגל chargol, comes from, the Hebrews are wholly ignorant. But in Arabic حرجل [ḥarjala] chargala means to be long, or to move and stretch out in a long line. Hence حرجل [ḥarjal] chargal, or حرجلة [ḥurjula] chorgola, in Alcamus, الجماعة من الخيل والقطعة مي الجراد [al-jamāʿa min al-khayl wa-l-qiṭʿa my al-jarād]: of horses it means a column; and of locusts, a swarm. Cadamustus, Navigations, ch. 13, writes that such a swarm sometimes extends over twelve miles. And although in Europe they extend less far, yet even there immense columns of locusts are sometimes seen. Aldrovandi [=Ulisse Aldrovandi, Ornithologia (1599–1603)], book 4, On insects, p. 423: They write that in the year 1475 a huge mass of locusts coming from Hungary, Moravia and Silesia roamed over the Sieradz and Łęczyca region and Masovia, etc., with swarms continuous for a mile and a half in breadth and as much again in length, that is, for three miles. Surius, at the year 1541, on the locusts around Poland: These flew forth in such a multitude that they covered two miles in length and breadth a cubit deep, so that by their density they shut out even the brightness of the sun from that whole tract of land. So it is not without reason that these columns of locusts take their name from a long line. Yet Moses does not use חרגל chargol for a swarm of locusts, but for a particular kind of locust. So too in the Talmudic tractate On the Sabbath, fol. 67, in the Mishnah: יוצאין בביצת החרגל ובשן של שועל ובמסמר מן הצלוב משום רפואה, They go out (lawfully on the Sabbath day) with the egg of the chargol, and with the tooth of a fox, and with a nail from a gallows, for the sake of healing (hung on the body); and there the Gemara says that the egg of the chargol was used לשיחלא. And the Gloss says that שיחלא is כאבה און והרלי באזן, pain of the ear, or a disease in the ear R9. ¶

III, 258 VIII. Octavum ילק jelek, a lingendo, deductum שילוק בלשונו; quia lingua sua lingit sata et fruges. Sic Aben Ezra in Ioelem. His concinunt Arabes, quibus لحس lahasa Verbum est lingere. Et Nomen لحس lahas, id est, linctus, de locusta dicitur, quae terrae proventum, ut tinea lanam, lingendo absumit. Alcamus, اللحس اكل الدود الصوف واكل col. 446 الجراد الخضر. Quale est, quod in Giauhari et Damire العثة, tinea (Hebr. עש) definitur دويبة تلحس الصوف Animalculum, quod lanam lingit, id est, rodit. Hinc in Scriptura lingere quandoque idem est, quod absumere. Sic Num. XXII, 4. Linget haec congregatio omnia, quae cir- cum nos sunt, ut lingit bos gramen agri. Et 1 Reg. XVIII, 20. Tum ignis Do- mini delapsus consumpsit holocaustum, et ligna, et lapides, et pulverem ipsum, at- que aquam etiam linxit, quae erat in aquae-ductu (10).

III, 258 VIII. The eighth is ילק jelek, from licking, derived from שילוק בלשונו, because with its tongue it licks the crops and the fruits of the earth. So Aben Ezra on Joel. The Arabs agree with this: for them the verb لحس [laḥasa] lahasa means to lick. And the noun لحس [laḥs] lahas, that is, a licking, is said of the locust, which consumes the produce of the earth by licking it, as the moth does wool. Alcamus: اللحس اكل الدود الصوف واكل col. 446 الجراد الخضر [al-laḥs akl al-dūd al-ṣūf wa-akl al-jarād al-khuḍar]. Similar is the fact that in Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] العثة [al-ʿuththa], the moth (Hebr. עש), is defined as دويبة تلحس الصوف [duwaybba talḥasu al-ṣūf], a little animal that licks wool, that is, gnaws it. Hence in Scripture to lick sometimes means the same as to consume. So Num 22:4. This multitude will lick up everything that is around us, as the ox licks up the grass of the field. And 1 Reg. XVIII, 20. Then the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the burnt offering, and the wood, and the stones, and the very dust, and also licked up the water that was in the trench R10. ¶

IX. Nonum סלעם solam, Aben Ezrae est locustae genus, quod יעלה בסלעים, conscendit petras. Quasi fiat a סלע, Mem addito in fine vocis, cujus formae nullum Nomen ab illis proferri jam docuimus. Ut taceam, nullam esse locustae speciem, quae petris sit addictior. Itaque in סלעם finale Mem putamus ad Radicem pertinere, ut in גזם, de quo prius a). Nempe Chaldaice סלעם est vorare et absumere, ut Hebraice בלע, pro quo passim occurrit in Paraphrastis. III, 259 Hinc igitur locusta merito vocatur סלעם solam; ut Graece βροῦχος, vel βροῦκος, et ἀκρὶς, de quibus infra (11).

IX. The ninth, סלעם solam, is for Aben Ezra a kind of locust that יעלה בסלעים, climbs the rocks, as though it were formed from סלע with Mem added at the end of the word; but we have already shown that they cite no noun of this form. Not to mention that there is no species of locust more given to rocks than another. We therefore think that in סלעם the final Mem belongs to the root, as in גזם, discussed above a). For in Chaldee סלעם means to devour and to consume, like the Hebrew בלע, in place of which it frequently occurs in the Paraphrasts. III, 259 Hence the locust is rightly called סלעם solam, just as in Greek it is called βροῦχος [brouchos] or βροῦκος [broukos], and ἀκρὶς [akris], on which see below R11. ¶

X. Decimum צלצל tselatsal, Deut. XXVIII, 42. Graecis ἐρυσίβη, Vulgato RUBIGO: sed Hebraeis omnibus, Onkeloso et Arabi Erpeniano locusta. Multi a strepitu alarum sic dici volunt; quia צלל tinnire est, et צלצלים sunt cymbala, vel tintinnabula. Sed et צלל est obumbrare. Unde locustas appellari mihi fit verosimilius; quia solem obumbrant, ut suo loco b) probamus ex Plinio et aliis. Alarum sonum ad cymbali, aut tintinnabuli tinnitum accedere, nullus observat (12).

X. The tenth is צלצל tselatsal, Deut 28:42. For the Greeks it is ἐρυσίβη [erysibē], for the Vulgate BLIGHT; but for all the Hebrews, for Onkelos and for Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] it is the locust. Many hold that it is so called from the noise of its wings, because צלל means to tinkle, and צלצלים are cymbals or bells. But צלל also means to overshadow. Hence it seems more probable to me that locusts are so called because they overshadow the sun, as we prove in its proper place b) from Pliny and others. No one observes that the sound of their wings comes close to the ringing of a cymbal or a bell R12. ¶

Recensuimus hactenus multa locustarum nomina, in Propheticis libris hinc inde sparsa. Quae ne quis putet esse prorsus synonyma, sciendum est, harum plerisque certas species significari. Patet id ex Ioel. I, 4. ubi residuum τοῦ gazam comedit arbe, et residuum τοῦ arbe comedit jelek, et residuum τοῦ jelek comedit chasil. Ita, ut appareat, gazam, arbe, jelek et chasil species esse distinctas (13). Rursus Levit. XI, 21. 22. quatuor numerantur locustarum species, nimirum arbe, solam, chargol et chagab; quibus omnibus hoc commune est, quod sunt alatae, et quadrupedes, et ad esum concessae (14). Locustas enim III, 260 varie distingui, docent Arabes. Damir, وهى اصناف فبعضه اصفر وبعضه ابيض وبعضه احمر وبعضه اسود وبعضه كبير الجثة وبعضه صغيرها Locustarum sunt variae species. Quaedam enim sunt flavae, quaedam albae, quaedam rubrae, quaedam nigrae. Quaedam magni corporis, et quaedam parvi. Sed multo disertius Marcellus Virgilius in Dioscoridem lib. 2. Variavit admirabili artificio locustas natura, etc. Sunt enim, quae virides, quae nigrae, quae liventes, quae aliqua parte hunc, aliqua illum colorem ostentant. Sunt, quae volantes tantum latentem antea colorem aliquem proferant. Sunt, quae simplicibus, quae pluribus alis volent, quae alis carentes saltent, quae, utroque hoc motu privatae, ingrediantur tantum. Sunt, quae longioribus, quae brevioribus cruribus sint: et in his, pluribus brevioribusque, et paucioribus longioribusque internodiis. Sunt, quae canant, quae mutae sint, quae per diem, quae in tenebris tantum audiantur. Et, ne longiores simus, sunt, quae nihil homini in re rustica noceant, innocentesque a pueris capi se patiantur. Sunt contra, quae pestem et calamitatem satorum omnium totis regionibus afferant, illataque fame antiquos agros deserere saepenumero col. 447 gentes coegerint. Itaque, ut locustarum diversae sunt species, ita et diversa nomina. Sic formae aut coloris ratione Arabice سرعوف surouph est locusta longa, جندخ giondoch locusta obesa: برقان borkan locusta varia: جردم giardam locusta viridis capite nigro (15); contra, كدم chudam locusta nigra capite viridi (16). Distinguuntur etiam ratione locorum, et حبشان chobsan est locusta Aethiopica; عرفان orphan, quae reperitur in arenis Aligi, aut Dahnae, III, 261 prope Medinam: et ratione sexus, nam locustae mas dicitur عصغور usphour (17), et عظاري adari, et عسقول uscoul; foemina دباسا dibasa, et عيسا aisa: et ratione aetatis, ut probant haec Damiris, اذا خرج الجرد من بيضة يقال لها الدبا (18) فاذا طلعت اجنحته وكبرت فهو الغوغا وذلك حين عيوج بعضه في بعض فاذا بدت فيه الالوان واصفرت الذكور واسودت الاناث سمي جراد حينيذ Cum exit ex ovo locusta, vocatur daba (18): cum alae suboriuntur et crescunt, dicitur gauga, idque cum aliae in alias tumultuantur. Variis autem coloribus insigniri cum occipiunt, ita, ut flavescant mares, et foeminae nigrescant, tum demum appellatur girad (19).

So far we have reviewed many names of locusts, scattered here and there in the Prophetic books. Lest anyone think they are wholly synonymous, it should be known that most of them denote definite species. This is clear from Joel 1:4, where what the gazam left the arbe ate, and what the arbe left the jelek ate, and what the jelek left the chasil ate. So it appears that gazam, arbe, jelek and chasil are distinct species R13. Again, in Lev 11:21–22. four species of locusts are counted, namely arbe, solam, chargol and chagab; common to all of these is that they are winged, four-footed, and permitted as food R14. For the Arabs teach that locusts are III, 260 distinguished in various ways. Damir: وهى اصناف فبعضه اصفر وبعضه ابيض وبعضه احمر وبعضه اسود وبعضه كبير الجثة وبعضه صغيرها [wa-hiya aṣnāf fa-baʿḍuhu aṣfar wa-baʿḍuhu abyaḍ wa-baʿḍuhu aḥmar wa-baʿḍuhu aswad wa-baʿḍuhu kabīr al-juththa wa-baʿḍuhu ṣaghīruhā] There are various species of locusts. For some are yellow, some white, some red, some black. Some are of large body, and some of small. But Marcellus Virgilius on Dioscorides, book 2, puts it much more eloquently: Nature has varied locusts with wonderful artistry, etc. For there are some that are green, some black, some livid, some that display this colour in one part and that in another. There are some that only when flying reveal a colour previously hidden. There are some that fly with single wings, some with several, some that lack wings and leap, some that, deprived of both these motions, only walk. There are some with longer legs, some with shorter: and among these, some with more and shorter joints, some with fewer and longer. There are some that sing, some that are mute, some that are heard by day, some only in the dark. And, not to be too long, there are some that do no harm to man in farming and harmlessly let themselves be caught by boys. There are others, on the contrary, that bring pestilence and ruin to all crops throughout whole regions, and by the famine they cause have often forced peoples to abandon their ancestral fields col. 447. Therefore, as there are different species of locusts, so there are also different names. Thus by reason of form or colour in Arabic سرعوف [surʿūf] surouph is the long locust, جندخ [jundukh] giondoch the fat locust, برقان [burqān] borkan the variegated locust, جردم [jardam] giardam the green locust with a black head R15; conversely, كدم [kudam] chudam the black locust with a green head R16. They are also distinguished by reason of place: حبشان [ḥubshān] chobsan is the Ethiopian locust; عرفان [ʿurfān] orphan, the one found in the sands of Aligi or Dahnae, III, 261 near Medina; and by reason of sex, for the male locust is called عصغور [ʿuṣghūr] usphour R17, and عظاري [ʿaẓārī] adari, and عسقول [ʿusqūl] uscoul; the female دباسا [dibāsā] dibasa, and عيسا [ʿaysā] aisa; and by reason of age, as these words of Damir prove: اذا خرج الجرد من بيضة يقال لها الدبا R18 فاذا طلعت اجنحته وكبرت فهو الغوغا وذلك حين عيوج بعضه في بعض فاذا بدت فيه الالوان واصفرت الذكور واسودت الاناث سمي جراد حينيذ [idhā kharaja al-jurd min bayḍa yuqāl lahā al-dabā fa-idhā ṭalaʿat ajniḥatuhu wa-kaburat fa-huwa al-ghawghā wa-dhālika ḥīna ʿywj baʿḍuhu fī baʿḍ fa-idhā badat fīhi al-alwān wa-iṣfarrat al-dhukūr wa-iswaddat al-ināth summiya jarād ḥīnaydh] When the locust comes out of the egg, it is called daba R18; when the wings sprout and grow, it is called gauga, and that is when they swarm tumultuously over one another. But when they begin to be marked with various colours, so that the males turn yellow and the females black, then at last it is called girad R19. ¶

Latine دبا daba est bruchus, غوغا gauga attelabus, et جراد girad locusta, si vera sunt haec Remigii Altissiodorensis in Ioelem: Bruchus appellatur foetus locustae, vel filius, antequam habeat alas. Deinde, ubi parumper inceperit volitare, vocatur attelabus. Deinde, ubi penitus volare coeperit, efficitur locusta. Nec multo aliter Hieronymus in Nahum III. Quod bruchus nihil aliud faciat, nisi semper in terra sit, et absque alis cibo et ventri serviat. Attelabus autem saltem modicas assumat alas, et, cum in altum volare non possit, tamen de terra exsilire notatur, et tandem perveniens in locustam volitat, etc. Et rursus, Attelabus, III, 262 quem significantius comessorem interpretatus est Aquila, parva locusta est inter locustam et bruchum, et modicis pennis reptans potius quam volans, semperque subsiliens. Et Augustino quoque in Psalmum CIV. bruchus est locustae foetus. Una, inquit, plaga est locustae et bruchi; quoniam altera est parens, et alter est foetus. Et Isidorus bruchi pene totum corpus in ventrem colligi asserit: id est, vix quidquam esse quam ventrem, ut qui alis prorsus careat. Quod habet ex Gregorio in Iobum lib. 33. cap. 17. Itaque βροῦχος, vel βροῦκος, quomodo scribebant Iönes, auctore Hesychio, dicitur παρὰ τὸ βρύκειν, τὸ ἐσθίειν, ab edendo, seu vorando, ut μάσταξ παρὰ τὸ μασᾶσθαι, a mandendo; quasi nihil aliud quam ventri serviat, cum sit volatus expers.

In Latin دبا [dabā] daba is the bruchus, غوغا [ghawghā] gauga the attelabus, and جراد [jarād] girad the locust, if these words of Remigius of Auxerre on Joel are true: The offspring or young of the locust, before it has wings, is called bruchus. Then, when it has begun to fly a little, it is called attelabus. Then, when it has begun to fly fully, it becomes a locust. And Jerome on Nahum III says much the same: That the bruchus does nothing else but stays always on the ground and, without wings, serves food and belly. The attelabus, however, takes on at least modest wings, and though it cannot fly high, it is nevertheless noted to leap up from the ground, and finally, becoming a locust, it flies, etc. And again: The attelabus, III, 262 which Aquila more expressively rendered 'devourer', is a small locust between the locust and the bruchus, creeping rather than flying on small wings, and always leaping. For Augustine too, on Psalm 104, the bruchus is the offspring of the locust. The plague, he says, of the locust and of the bruchus is one; since the one is the parent and the other the offspring. And Isidore asserts that almost the whole body of the bruchus is gathered into the belly: that is, it is scarcely anything but belly, since it wholly lacks wings. He has this from Gregory on Job, book 33, ch. 17. Accordingly βροῦχος [brouchos], or βροῦκος [broukos], as the Ionians wrote it according to Hesychius, is so called παρὰ τὸ βρύκειν, τὸ ἐσθίειν [para to brykein, to esthiein], from eating or devouring, as μάσταξ [mastax] παρὰ τὸ μασᾶσθαι [para to masasthai], from chewing; as if it served nothing but the belly, being incapable of flight. ¶

This is the view of the Latin Fathers. Which is surprising, since in the Vulgate version one reads at Nahum III, 16. The bruchus spread out and flew away, and in the Greek, Βροῦχος ὥρμησε, καὶ ἐξεπετάσθη [Brouchos hōrmēse, kai exepetasthē]. And at Lev 11:22 the bruchus is counted among the winged col. 448 locusts. Accordingly the Greek Fathers attribute flight and wings to the bruchus. Thus Cyril says of it on Nahum ch. 3: Φασὶ γὰρ, ὅτι, πιπτούσης χαλάζης, καὶ ὑετῶν καταρηγγνυμένων ἀδρανὴς εἰς πτῆσιν ὁ βροῦχος, καταδεδευμένων αὐτῷ τῶν πτερῶν [Phasi gar, hoti, piptousēs chalazēs, kai hyetōn katarēngnymenōn adranēs eis ptēsin ho brouchos, katadedeumenōn autō tōn pterōn]; For they say that when hail falls and rains burst forth, the bruchus becomes weak for flight, its wings being soaked. And Theodoret in the same place, of the same: Τῆς ἡλιακῆς προσβαλλούσης ἀκτῖνος ἀνίσταται, καὶ πετάννυσι τὰ πτερὰ, καὶ εἰς ἕτερον μεταβαίνει τόπον [Tēs hēliakēs proballousēs aktinos anistatai, kai petannysi ta ptera, kai eis heteron metabainei topon]; When the ray of the sun strikes it, it rises, spreads its wings, and moves elsewhere. Indeed the same author, on Amos 7:1, openly distinguishes the bruchus from the young of locusts: Ἐπιγονὴν δὲ ἀκρίδων, he says, ἐκάλησε τὸν Ἀσσύριον, βροῦχον δὲ τὸν Βαβυλώνιον [Epigonēn de akridōn … ekalēse ton Assyrion, brouchon de ton Babylōnion]; He called the Assyrian the young of locusts, but the Babylonian the bruchus. The Greek translators too, at Lev 11:22, seem to have distinguished the bruchus from the common locust not only by age but also by species. But the use of the word varied. For among the Ionians βροῦκος [broukos] was a species of locust, and among the Cypriots βροῦκας [broukas] was the green locust, but among the Tarentines the attelabus. Hesychius: Βροῦκος· ἀκρίδων εἶδος, Ἴωνες. Κύπριοι δὲ τὴν χλωρὰν ἀκρίδα βροῦκαν· Ταραντῖνοι δὲ ἀττέλεβον [Broukos; akridōn eidos, Iōnes. Kyprioi de tēn chlōran akrida broukan; Tarantinoi de attelebon], that is, ἀττέλαβον [attelabon]. Thus for Theophrastus, in the little book On the animals that appear suddenly, the βροῦκος [broukos] is a species of attelabus: Χαλεπαὶ μὲν οὖν, he says, αἱ ἀκρίδες, χαλεπώτεροι δὲ οἱ ἀττέλαβοι, καὶ τούτων μάλιστα οὓς καλοῦσι βρούκους [Chalepai men oun … hai akrides, chalepōteroi de hoi attelaboi, kai toutōn malista hous kalousi broukous]; Locusts indeed are harmful, but attelabi are more harmful, and most of all those they call bruci. So one must read, instead of the corrupt βαρούκτους [baroukt ous]. ¶

Sic vetustioribus attelabus non potest esse immaturus locustae foetus: cum attelabi partus atque ovorum non semel meminerint, Aristoteles Historiae lib. 5. cap. 29. Ὁμοίως δὲ τίκτουσι καὶ οἱ ἀττέλαβοι, Pari modo pariunt etiam attelabi. Et paullo post, Φθείρεται δὲ αὐτῶν τὰ ὠὰ ὑπὸ τῶν μετοπωρινῶν ὑδά-των· Corrumpuntur eorum ova aquis autumnalibus. Plutarchus in Iside, Λήμνιοι III, 263 κορύδους ἐτίμησαν τὰ τῶν ἀττελάβων εὑρίσκοντας ὠὰ, καὶ κόπτοντας· Alau- das coluerunt Lemnii; quia reperta attelaborum ova frangebant. Idem passim ἀττέλαβον vel ἀττέλεβον videtur sumere pro locusta in genere: ut in Sympo- siacῶn lib. 2. cap. 3. ubi ovum generare dicit δίποδα τὸν ὄρνιν, καὶ ἄποδα τὸν ὄφιν, καὶ πολύποδα τὸν ἀττέλεβον, avem bipedem, serpentem expedem, et at- telabum multipedem. Et paullo infra in Sicilia servili bello, cum fusum fuisset multum sanguinis, et cadavera putrescerent super terram insepulta, πλῆθος, inquit, ἀττελέβων ἐξήνθησε, καὶ τὸν σῖτον ἔφθειρον, πανταχοῦ σχεδασθέν-τες ἐπὶ τὴν νῆσον, attelaborum copia pullulavit, qui, per insulam ubique sparsi, segetes corruperunt. Quale est, quod in Eubuli gripho apud Athenaeum lib. 10. ichneumon pro ἀκριδοφθάλμῳ vocatur ἀττελεβόφθαλμος. Tamen hoc certum, attelabum in locustarum genere parvum fuisse, atque ἄπτερον. Hesychius, ἀττέλεβος· ἀκρὶς μικρά· Plinius lib. 29. cap. 5. Locustarum minimae sine pen- nis, quas attelabos vocant. Unde Graeci interpretes Nahum III, 17. Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος, Exsiliit ut attelabus, illi non volatum, sed saltum tribuentes. Quin videtur a saltu dictus. Notum, φ et β saepe transmutari. Sic Macedoni- bus Φίλιππος erat Βίλιππος· Φερονίκη, Βερονίκη· Φρύγες, Βρίγες. Idem sonant φάλαινα et balaena; ἀμφιλαφὴς et ἀμφιλαβής. Πιστάκιον scribitur quoque φιστάκιον et βιστάκιον· Ita putaverim, ἀττέλαβον idem fuisse, quod ἀτ-τέλαφον, id est, salientem cervum, vel instar cervi salientem. Nam ἄττειν sa- lire est, et ἔλαφος cervus, cujus in saltu agilitas laudatur Es. XXXV, 6. Huc facit, quod a Talmudicis inter locustarum species censetur איילא קמצא , quasi dicas ἀκριδέλαφον, seu cervi-locustam. Nahumi interpres Arabs, qui Graeco textui haeret mordicus, Graeca verba, Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος, sic reddit: وثب مثل جرادة سودا Exsiliit ut locusta nigra.

Thus for the older writers the attelabus cannot be the immature young of the locust, since they more than once mention the brood and eggs of the attelabus: Aristotle, History book 5, ch. 29: Ὁμοίως δὲ τίκτουσι καὶ οἱ ἀττέλαβοι [Homoiōs de tiktousi kai hoi attelaboi], The attelabi too give birth in the same way. And a little later: Φθείρεται δὲ αὐτῶν τὰ ὠὰ ὑπὸ τῶν μετοπωρινῶν ὑδάτων [Phtheiretai de autōn ta ōa hypo tōn metopōrinōn hydatōn]; Their eggs are destroyed by the autumn rains. Plutarch in Isis: Λήμνιοι III, 263 κορύδους ἐτίμησαν τὰ τῶν ἀττελάβων εὑρίσκοντας ὠὰ, καὶ κόπτοντας [Lēmnioi korydous etimēsan ta tōn attelabōn heuriskontas ōa, kai koptontas]; the Lemnians honoured larks, because they found the eggs of the attelabi and broke them. The same author seems everywhere to take ἀττέλαβον [attelabon] or ἀττέλεβον [attelebon] for the locust in general: as in the Symposiaca book 2, ch. 3, where he says that the egg generates δίποδα τὸν ὄρνιν, καὶ ἄποδα τὸν ὄφιν, καὶ πολύποδα τὸν ἀττέλεβον [dipoda ton ornin, kai apoda ton ophin, kai polypoda ton attelebon], the two-footed bird, the footless serpent, and the many-footed attelabus. And a little below, when in the servile war in Sicily much blood had been shed and corpses lay rotting unburied on the ground, πλῆθος, he says, ἀττελέβων ἐξήνθησε, καὶ τὸν σῖτον ἔφθειρον, πανταχοῦ σχεδασθέντες ἐπὶ τὴν νῆσον [plēthos … attelebōn exēnthēse, kai ton siton ephtheiron, pantachou schedasthentes epi tēn nēson], a multitude of attelabi sprang up, which, scattered everywhere over the island, ruined the crops. Of the same kind is the fact that in Eubulus' riddle in Athenaeus book 10 the ichneumon is called ἀττελεβόφθαλμος [attelebophthalmos] instead of ἀκριδοφθάλμῳ [akridophthalmō]. Yet this is certain: the attelabus was small within the locust kind, and ἄπτερον [apteron, wingless]. Hesychius: ἀττέλεβος· ἀκρὶς μικρά [attelebos; akris mikra]. Pliny book 29, ch. 5: The smallest of the locusts, without wings, which they call attelabi. Hence the Greek translators at Nahum III, 17: Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος [Exēlato hōs attelabos], It leapt like an attelabus, attributing to it not flight but leaping. Indeed it seems to be named from leaping. It is well known that φ and β are often interchanged. Thus among the Macedonians Φίλιππος [Philippos] was Βίλιππος [Bilippos]; Φερονίκη [Pheronikē], Βερονίκη [Beronikē]; Φρύγες [Phryges], Βρίγες [Briges]. φάλαινα [phalaina] and balaena [whale] sound the same; so do ἀμφιλαφὴς [amphilaphēs] and ἀμφιλαβής [amphilabēs]. Πιστάκιον [Pistakion] is also written φιστάκιον [phistakion] and βιστάκιον [bistakion]. So I would think that ἀττέλαβον [attelabon] was the same as ἀττέλαφον [attelaphon], that is, leaping deer, or leaping like a deer. For ἄττειν [attein] is to leap, and ἔλαφος [elaphos] is deer, whose agility in leaping is praised at Isa 35:6. To this point is the fact that the Talmudists count among the species of locusts איילא קמצא, as if you said ἀκριδέλαφον [akridelaphon], or deer-locust. The Arabic translator of Nahum, who clings tenaciously to the Greek text, renders the Greek words Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος [Exēlato hōs attelabos] thus: وثب مثل جرادة سودا [wathaba mithl jarāda sūdā] It leapt like a black locust. ¶

Aliud locustae nomen ἀττάκης vel ἀττακὸς Levit. XI, 22. videri possit col. 449 etiam ἀπὸ τοῦ ἄττειν, a saliendo nomen habere, ut vulgari lingua saltarella. Nisi putetur ἀττακὸς idem esse, quod ἀστακὸς, Aeolum dialecto: quibus πίστις est πίττις, unde fides; et βλάστη βλάττη, unde planta. Huc facit 1. quod inter marinas locustas est ἀστακὸς, astacus (Normanis houmar). 2. Quod in optimis Glossis lucusta, seu locusta redditur ἀκρὶς, ἀστακός. Neque aliud sibi volunt haec Isidori, alioqui valde inepta. Locusta, quod pedibus sit longis velut hasta: unde eam Graeci tam maritimam, quam terrestrem hastago appellant. Apparet enim, illum captasse frigidas allusiones inter locustam, hastam et astacum; quasi astacus, illi hastago, tam sit terrestris, quam marinae locustae genus.

Another name of the locust, ἀττάκης [attakēs] or ἀττακὸς [attakos] in Levit. XI, 22., might seem col. 449 also to take its name from ἄττειν [attein], from leaping, as in the vulgar tongue saltarella. Unless ἀττακὸς [attakos] is thought to be the same as ἀστακὸς [astakos] in the dialect of the Aeolians, for whom πίστις [pistis] is πίττις [pittis], whence fides [faith]; and βλάστη [blastē] is βλάττη [blattē], whence planta [plant]. In favour of this are: 1. that among the sea locusts there is the ἀστακὸς [astakos], astacus (in Norman houmar); 2. that in the best Glosses lucusta, or locusta, is rendered ἀκρὶς [akris], ἀστακός [astakos]. Nor do these words of Isidore, otherwise very inept, mean anything else: "The locust is so called because it has long legs like a spear [hasta]; hence the Greeks call it, both the marine and the terrestrial kind, hastago." For it is evident that he was reaching after frigid wordplays between locusta, hasta and astacus, as though the astacus, his hastago, were a kind of terrestrial as well as of marine locust. ¶

Est et Levit. XI, 22. locustae species ὀφιομάχης, ophiomachus, quasi ser- pentes oppugnet. Quod utut παράδοξον, tamen affirmat Philosophus Historiae III, 264 libri noni capite sexto, Πολλοὶ δὲ καὶ τὴν ἀκρίδα ἑωράκασιν, ὅτι ὅταν μάχηται τοῖς ὄφεσι, λαμβάνεται τοῦ τραχήλου τῶν ὄφεων. Unde Plinius libri undecimi capite vigesimo nono, Serpentem, cum libuit, necant singulae, faucibus ejus apprehensis mordicus. Sed et Simon Majolus, Vulturiacensis episcopus, Dialogo decimo olitorem suum narrat, ex agro venientem, locustam conspexisse, quae serpentem gutture apprehenderit, atque ita citra pugnam interemerit. Notandum est, ὀφιομάχην in Lege poni pro Hebraeo חרגל chargol, aut argol. Nam ex usu veterum potest utroque modo scribi. Sic idem Mesopotamiae fluvius חבר Graece scribitur Χαβόρας et Ἀβόρας. Idem Arabiae Felicis populus חצרמות, Χατραμῶται et Ἀτραμῶται. Et in Leuco-Syris Alybes iidem, qui Chalybes. Et חסידים modo Chasidaei, modo Asidaei dicti. Et avis חסידה chasida scribitur ἄσιδα in Graeca versione Iobi. Sic Hebraeum ophiomachi nomen חרגול chargol puto veteres argol scripsisse, aspiratione dempta. Atque inde natam esse fabulam de argolis ophiomachis, quos pro locustis serpentes fuisse nugantur, et ideo dictos argolas, quod ex Argo Pelasgico in Aegyptum ab Alexandro translati sint, ut aspides interficerent. Ita refert Suidas, Ἀργόλαι· εἶδος ὄφεων, οὓς ἤνεγκε Μακεδὼν ὁ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐκ τοῦ Ἄργους τοῦ Πελασγικοῦ εἰς Ἀλεξάνδρειαν, καὶ ἐνέβαλεν εἰς τὸν ποταμὸν, πρὸς ἀναίρεσιν τῶν ἀσπίδων. Neque auctorem citat. Sed aliquid simile reperio in Synopsi de vita et morte Prophetarum, quam Dorotheo, martyri, falso tribuunt, capite De Ieremia. Sed pro locusta ophiomacho in Argo fuit locusta scorpiomachus, de qua sic Philosophus in libro Mirabilium [cap. 151.]: Ἐν Ἄργει δέ φασι γίνεσθαι ἀκρίδος τινὸς γένος, ὃ καλεῖ-ται σκορπιομάχον· ὅταν γὰρ ἴδῃ τάχιστα σκορπίον, ἀνθίσταται αὐτῷ, ὡσαύ-τως δὲ καὶ ὁ σκορπίος ἐκείνῃ· In Argo locustae genus esse ferunt, scorpiomachum nomine, quod, quamprimum scorpium viderit, in eum insurgit, ut scorpius vicissim in illam (20). Caetera ibi vide, et apud Stobaeum capite nonagesimo octavo, qui est De morbis, ubi haec ipsa citat; non tamen ex Aristotele, sed ex Trophilo.

There is also in Lev 11:22 a species of locust called ὀφιομάχης [ophiomachēs], ophiomachus, as if it fought serpents. However παράδοξον [paradoxon] this is, the Philosopher nevertheless affirms it in the History III, 264, book 9, chapter 6: Πολλοὶ δὲ καὶ τὴν ἀκρίδα ἑωράκασιν, ὅτι ὅταν μάχηται τοῖς ὄφεσι, λαμβάνεται τοῦ τραχήλου τῶν ὄφεων [Polloi de kai tēn akrida heōrakasin, hoti hotan machētai tois ophesi, lambanetai tou trachēlou tōn opheōn]. Hence Pliny, book 11, chapter 29: "Each one by itself kills a serpent, when it pleases, seizing its throat with its bite." And Simon Majolus, bishop of Volturara, in Dialogue 10, relates that his gardener, coming from the field, saw a locust that had seized a serpent by the throat and so killed it without a fight. It should be noted that ὀφιομάχης [ophiomachēs] is put in the Law for the Hebrew חרגל chargol, or argol. For by the usage of the ancients it can be written either way. Thus the same river of Mesopotamia, חבר, is written in Greek Χαβόρας [Chaboras] and Ἀβόρας [Aboras]. The same people of Arabia Felix, חצרמות, Χατραμῶται [Chatramōtai] and Ἀτραμῶται [Atramōtai]. And among the Leuco-Syrians the Alybes are the same as the Chalybes. And the חסידים are called now Chasidaei, now Asidaei. And the bird חסידה chasida is written ἄσιδα [asida] in the Greek version of Job. So I think that the ancients wrote the Hebrew name of the ophiomachus, חרגול chargol, as argol, with the aspiration dropped. And from this arose the fable of the argolae ophiomachi, which they idly claim were serpents rather than locusts, and were called argolae because they were brought by Alexander from Pelasgian Argos into Egypt to kill the asps. So Suidas reports: Ἀργόλαι· εἶδος ὄφεων, οὓς ἤνεγκε Μακεδὼν ὁ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐκ τοῦ Ἄργους τοῦ Πελασγικοῦ εἰς Ἀλεξάνδρειαν, καὶ ἐνέβαλεν εἰς τὸν ποταμὸν, πρὸς ἀναίρεσιν τῶν ἀσπίδων [Argolai; eidos opheōn, hous ēnenke Makedōn ho Alexandros ek tou Argous tou Pelasgikou eis Alexandreian, kai enebalen eis ton potamon, pros anairesin tōn aspidōn]. Nor does he cite an authority. But I find something similar in the Synopsis on the Life and Death of the Prophets, which they falsely ascribe to Dorotheus the martyr, in the chapter On Jeremiah. But instead of a locust ophiomachus there was in Argos a locust scorpiomachus, of which the Philosopher says this in the book of Marvels [ch. 151.]: Ἐν Ἄργει δέ φασι γίνεσθαι ἀκρίδος τινὸς γένος, ὃ καλεῖται σκορπιομάχον· ὅταν γὰρ ἴδῃ τάχιστα σκορπίον, ἀνθίσταται αὐτῷ, ὡσαύτως δὲ καὶ ὁ σκορπίος ἐκείνῃ [En Argei de phasi ginesthai akridos tinos genos, ho kaleitai skorpiomachon; hotan gar idē tachista skorpion, anthistatai autō, hōsautōs de kai ho skorpios ekeinē]: "In Argos they say there is a kind of locust, called scorpiomachus, which, as soon as it sees a scorpion, rises against it, as the scorpion in turn against it" R20. See the rest there, and in Stobaeus, chapter 98, which is On Diseases, where he cites these very words, though not from Aristotle but from Trophilus. ¶

Ἀκρὶς etiam, locustis omnibus commune nomen, in eodem Levitici loco pro certa specie sumitur. Quia κὰρ, vel κάρη est caput, ἀκρὶς a nonnullis dici putatur, quasi ἀκαρὶς, vel a parvo, vel a magno capite, prout Alpha praefixum vel στερητικὴν, vel ἐπιτατικὴν vim habet. Ab aliis παρὰ τὸ ἄκρας τῶν ἀσταχύων καὶ τῶν φυτῶν νέμεσθαι. Ita Etymologus. At Eustathio ἀκρὶς est quasi ἀγρὶς, ἀπὸ τοῦ γράω, τὸ ἐσθίω. Quae, quia sunt remotiora, mihi fit verosimilius, III, 265 a stridore dici τὴν ἀκρίδα ὀνοματοποιητικῶς. In pugna cum scorpione locusta κύκλῳ περιϊοῦσα τρύζει περὶ αὐτὸν, circumambiens circa eum stridet. Ita habet Philosophus loco nuper citato.

Ἀκρὶς [Akris] too, the name common to all locusts, is taken in the same passage of Leviticus for a particular species. Because κὰρ [kar] or κάρη [karē] is head, some think it is called ἀκρὶς [akris] as if ἀκαρὶς [akaris], either from a small or from a large head, according as the prefixed Alpha has privative or intensive force. Others derive it παρὰ τὸ ἄκρας τῶν ἀσταχύων καὶ τῶν φυτῶν νέμεσθαι [para to akras tōn astachyōn kai tōn phytōn nemesthai]. So the Etymologicum. But for Eustathius ἀκρὶς [akris] is as if ἀγρὶς [agris], from γράω [graō], that is, ἐσθίω [esthiō]. Since these are rather far-fetched, it seems more probable to me III, 265 that ἡ ἀκρίς [hē akris] is named ὀνοματοποιητικῶς [onomatopoiētikōs] from its chirping. In its fight with the scorpion the locust κύκλῳ περιϊοῦσα τρύζει περὶ αὐτὸν [kyklō periiousa tryzei peri auton], "going round about it, chirps around it." So the Philosopher has it in the passage just cited. ¶

Ut in Aristophane τετραπτερυλλίδες, a quatuor alis sic appellatae. Et col. 450 Attici agri πάρνοπες, a quibus pulsis Apollinem Parnopium fuisse dictum, tradit Pausanias in Atticis: ut Oetaeorum κόρνοπες nomen fecerunt Herculi Kornopioni apud Strabonem libro decimo tertio. Et μάσακες, et μολουρίδες in Nicandri Theriacis versu 416. et 802. Et Theocriti Idyll. 10. μάντις ἁ καλαμαία, quae aliis ἀρουραία, vates calamorum, aut arvorum. These are the species of locusts which the sacred translators mention. Many others could be added to them, drawn from the storehouses of the Greeks, if that were the matter in hand. For example, in Aristophanes the τετραπτερυλλίδες [tetrapterullides], so called from their four wings. And col. 450 the πάρνοπες [parnopes] of the Attic countryside, from whose expulsion, as Pausanias relates in his Attica, Apollo Parnopius was named; just as the κόρνοπες [kornopes] of the Oetaeans gave its name to Hercules Kornopion in Strabo, book 13. And the μάσακες [masakes] and μολουρίδες [molourides] in Nicander's Theriaca, lines 416 and 802. And in Theocritus, Idyll 10, μάντις ἁ καλαμαία [mantis ha kalamaia], which others call ἀρουραία [arouraia], the prophetess of the reeds, or of the fields. So too in Cyprus the Cerynetae called small cicadas καλαμίνδας [kalamindas], as Hesychius attests under Καλαμίς [Kalamis]. But the locust was called not only καλαμαία [kalamaia] but μάντις καλαμαία [mantis kalamaia], because it foretells barrenness to the reeds, that is, the crops. So in Arabic a kind of locust is called جندب [jundab] gindab, which takes its name from جدب [jadb] gidab, that is, a barren year. To these must be added the tryxalis, of which Pliny speaks in book 30, chapter 6: There is an animal like a locust, without wings, which is called TRYXALIS in Greek. It has no Latin name, etc. (I would prefer troxalis, τρωξαλὶς [trōxalis], as it is written by Aelian, History, book 6, chapter 19.) And Dioscorides' ἀσίρακος [asirakos], in the chapter On locusts in book 2 (where some copies wrongly have ἡ ῥιξαχόσκη· [hē rhixachoskē]): a species of locust around Leptis in Africa, ἄπτερος [apteros] and μεγαλόκωλος [megalokōlos], without wings and with long legs, which in Greek is called ὄνος [onos], ass, but in the native tongue of the inhabitants (that is, in Punic) ἀσίρακος [asirakos] R21. From this, by the transposition of a single letter, came the Talmudists' אסקרא askra, or iskra, a kind of locust of which the doctors treat in the tractate Cholin, ch. 3, fol. 65a: מאי זחל אמר רבי אסקרא. What is zachal? Rabbi said: Ascra. That is, it is the same kind of locust as the ascra. And the Gloss: זחל מין חגב בלשון ארמי אסקרא ואין לו קרצולים בקטנותו וגדלים לאחר זמן, ZACHAL is a species of locust, called ASKRA in Syriac, which does not have long legs while it is small, but in time they grow. Then indeed it becomes μεγαλόκωλος [megalokōlos], as Dioscorides has it. Yet it is slow in its gait, whence it is called זחל by the Hebrews and ὄνος [onos] by the Greeks. In the Arabic-Syriac-Latin Thesaurus of Thomas a Novaria, for ܐܣܩܪܐ [אסקרא] askra is wrongly written ܐܣܩܕܐ [אסקדא] askda, and it is rendered in Arabic المذنب [al-mudhannab], the tailed locust. Cum constet igitur hactenus, tam Graecos, quam Arabes varias habuisse voces, quibus locustarum species inter se distinguerent; et de Chaldaeis et Syris idem nullo negotio doceri possit: quidni Hebraeos quoque credamus per diversa illa vocabula, quorum origines aperuimus, harum diversa genera designasse? Non est tamen, quod quis exspectet singularum definitiones, et notas διακριτι-κὰς, quibus inter se differunt. Quamvis Talmudici doctores ea de re multis disputent in tractatu Cholin cap. 3. fol. 65. a. b. et 66. a. Sed more suo verbis tam obscuris et intricatis, ut ex iis certi vix quidquam possit elici. Quale est, quod a magistris suis se didicisse profitentur, ארבה זה גובאי סלעם זה רשון col. 451 חרגול זה ניפול חגב זה גדיאן, ARBE, id est, gobai: SOLAM, id est, rason: CHARGOL, id est, niphul: CHAGAB, id est, gadjan. Ignotum per ignotius. Rursus, quia in Mose legitur, ARBE secundum speciem suam, SOLAM secundum speciem suam, CHARGOL secundum speciem suam, CHAGAB secundum speciem suam, unicuique afferunt speciem congenerem. Sic locusta, quae dicitur צפורת כרמים tsipporeth cheramim, id est, avis vinearum, congener esse dicitur τῷ arbe: et יוחנא ירושלמית, jochana Ierosolymitana, τῷ solam: et ערצוביא artsubia, τῷ chargol: et הרזבית harzabith, τῷ chagab. Et per multas ambages in eo differre notantur: quod chargol habet גבחת וזנב, gibbum et caudam; arbe neutrum; solam gibbum, sed non caudam; chagab caudam sine gibbo. Quae eadem asseruntur de quatuor congeneribus. Ibidem locustae mundae leguntur habere quatuor notas; ארבעה רגלים וארבע כנפים וקרצולים וכנפיו חופים את III, 267 רובו, nempe quatuor pedes, quatuor alas, crura longiora, et quod alae tegunt totum corpus. Et הצרצור, gryllum habere has quatuor notas, nec tamen esse mundum; quia quinta nota deest, nempe caput longius. Caetera ibi vide; quae cum R. Selomo non solum accurate legerit, sed et notis illustraverit, ut reliquum corpus Talmudis, tamen in Notis ad Leviticum profitetur, se locustarum species ignorare. Verba sunt, אין אנו בקיאין בהם, In his exercitati non sumus. Et rursus, אין אנו יודעין להבדיל ביניהון, Illas inter se distinguere nescimus. Neque veterum versiones hic quidquam prosunt. Quia Chaldaei, Syri, Arabes, Samaritani interpretes, vel retinent voces Hebraeas, vel aliis utuntur, nihilo magis notis. Nec in Graeca et Vulgata versione satis scitur, quid a se invicem differant locusta, bruchus, attacus, ophiomachus. Et multo minus, an Graecae et Latinae voces Hebraeis accurate respondeant. Tamen usque ad Christi tempora Iudaeos hoc non latuisse, ex eo colligitur, quod Iohannes, baptista, locustis vescebatur, iis nimirum, quarum esum in Lege Mosis sciebat esse concessum. Neque id potuit aliter sciri, quam ex Prophetae vocibus recte explicatis. Sed, aliquanto post Christum excisis Hierosolymis, Iudaei, hinc inde dispersi, linguae suae sic obliti sunt, ut nonnulla sint in ipsa Lege, quae illorum doctissimis sunt plane ignota.

Since, then, it has so far been established that both the Greeks and the Arabs had various words by which they distinguished the kinds of locusts from one another, and since the same could be shown without any trouble for the Chaldeans and Syrians, why should we not believe that the Hebrews too designated different kinds of them by those different words whose origins we have laid open? Yet no one should expect definitions of each one, and the distinguishing marks (διακριτικὰς [diakritikas]) by which they differ from one another. The Talmudic doctors, it is true, discuss this matter at length in the tractate Cholin, ch. 3, fol. 65 a. b. and 66 a. But, as is their custom, they do so in words so obscure and intricate that hardly anything certain can be drawn from them. Such is what they profess to have learned from their masters: ארבה זה גובאי סלעם זה רשון col. 451 חרגול זה ניפול חגב זה גדיאן, ARBE, that is, gobai; SOLAM, that is, rason; CHARGOL, that is, niphul; CHAGAB, that is, gadjan. The unknown explained by the more unknown. Again, because one reads in Moses, ARBE after its kind, SOLAM after its kind, CHARGOL after its kind, CHAGAB after its kind, they assign to each a kindred species. Thus the locust called צפורת כרמים tsipporeth cheramim, that is, bird of the vineyards, is said to be kindred to the arbe; and יוחנא ירושלמית, the jochana of Jerusalem, to the solam; and ערצוביא artsubia, to the chargol; and הרזבית harzabith, to the chagab. And after many circumlocutions they are noted to differ in this: that the chargol has גבחת וזנב, a hump and a tail; the arbe neither; the solam a hump but no tail; the chagab a tail without a hump. The same is asserted of the four kindred species. In the same place clean locusts are said to have four marks: ארבעה רגלים וארבע כנפים וקרצולים וכנפיו חופים את III, 267 רובו, namely four feet, four wings, longer legs, and wings that cover the whole body. And the הצרצור, the cricket, is said to have these four marks and yet not to be clean, because the fifth mark is lacking, namely a longer head. See the rest there. Although R. Selomo not only read these things carefully but also illustrated them with notes, like the rest of the body of the Talmud, yet in his Notes on Leviticus he professes that he does not know the kinds of locusts. His words are אין אנו בקיאין בהם, We are not versed in them. And again, אין אנו יודעין להבדיל ביניהון, We do not know how to distinguish them from one another. Nor are the ancient versions of any help here. For the Chaldee, Syriac, Arabic and Samaritan translators either retain the Hebrew words or use others that are no better known. Nor is it sufficiently known, in the Greek and Vulgate versions, how the locust, bruchus, attacus and ophiomachus differ from one another; and much less whether the Greek and Latin words correspond accurately to the Hebrew. Yet that this was not unknown to the Jews down to the times of Christ is gathered from the fact that John the Baptist ate locusts, namely those whose eating he knew to be permitted in the Law of Moses. And this could not have been known otherwise than from the words of the Prophet rightly explained. But when, some time after Christ, Jerusalem was destroyed, the Jews, scattered here and there, so forgot their language that there are some things in the Law itself that are entirely unknown to the most learned of them. ¶

Itaque, cum non liceat in locustarum species accuratius inquirere, quanto satius est ignorantiam nostram hic ingenue fateri, quam in devia ferri cum celeberrimo interprete, qui verba Legis ita reddidit Levit. XI, 20. 21. 22. Omne reptile volucre, ambulans quatuor pedibus, abominationi esto vobis. Verumtamen hoc comedetis ex omni reptili volucri, quod ambulat quatuor pedibus, quod non habet crura super pedes suos, quibus saliat super terram: ex quibus comeditis ista, locustam secundum speciem suam, et bombycem secundum speciem suam, et cantharum secundum speciem suam, et cicadam secundum speciem suam. In qua versione multiplex est peccatum. Primo enim, versu vigesimo primo, Affirmativam mutat in Negativam contra mentem Legis apertissimam. Nam versu vigesimo prohibetur esus omnis insecti, volucris et quadrupedis. Sed excipiuntur versu vigesimo primo insecta volucria, quae praeter quatuor pedes habent duo crura saltatoria, ut quatuor locustarum species, quae nominantur versu vigesimo secundo. At sic agit ipse interpres, quasi ex reptilibus, seu insectis volucribus et quadrupedibus, Lex solum illa concederet, quae non habent crura illa saltatoria. Quo posito, cur statim addit, comedetis locustam, quam majora illa crura habere nemo nescit? Praeterea quorsum in hoc censu ponuntur bombyx, et cantharus, seu scarabaeus: quorum ille non volat quamdiu manet bombyx: et neuter est quadrupes, neuter col. 452 in cibis ab ulla gente adhibitus, multo minus a Iudaeis; quorum summa fuit in esu mundities. In Aethiopia et alibi fuisse scimus ἀκριδοφάγους et ὀφιοφάγους· III, 268 sed τῶν βομβυκοφάγων, aut τῶν κανθαροφάγων, qui meminerit, nullus exstat. A tam foedis et foetidis cibis natura hominum semper abhorruit. Illos igitur Iudaicis mensis apponere non debuit vir doctissimus, idque tanquam auctore Deo. Dicam tamen, cur haec verba, אשר לו כרעים ממעל לרגליו , quod habet crura super pedes suos, addita Negatione reddiderit, quod non habet, etc. Notum est, quasdam Prophetarum voces aliter legi, quam scribi. In his est vocula לו , quam Masorethae notant quindecim locis scribi לא , et legi לו : quorum prius sonat non, alterum ei. Ita hoc loco cum in textu scribatur אשר לא כרעים quod crura non habet, in margine habetur, אשר לו כרעים , cui sunt crura. Cui lectioni standum esse constat, cum ex tota textus serie, tum ex consensu interpretum. Ita enim legunt Hebraei omnes. Neque aliud sequuntur Graeci, Hieronymus, Chaldaei duo (Ionathan et Onkelos) Syrus, Arabes quatuor interpretes (quos consulere licuit, excusi duo, et duo manu-scripti in Suecia) Samaritanus, etc., ne dicam Hebraicum Samaritanorum exemplar habere in ipso textu לו per Vau, non per Aleph (22).

Therefore, since it is not possible to inquire more precisely into the kinds of locusts, how much better it is here to confess our ignorance frankly than to be carried off the path with a very famous translator, who rendered the words of the Law thus, Lev 11:20–21. 22. Every flying creeping thing that goes on four feet shall be an abomination to you. Yet this you shall eat of every flying creeping thing that goes on four feet: that which does not have legs above its feet with which to leap upon the earth. Of these you shall eat these: the locust after its kind, and the silkworm after its kind, and the beetle after its kind, and the cicada after its kind. In this version there are many errors. First, in verse twenty-one, it changes an affirmative into a negative, against the most evident meaning of the Law. For in verse twenty the eating of every insect that flies and goes on four feet is forbidden. But in verse twenty-one an exception is made for flying insects which, besides four feet, have two leaping legs, such as the four kinds of locust named in verse twenty-two. Yet the translator proceeds as if, of the creeping things, or flying four-footed insects, the Law permitted only those that do not have those leaping legs. On that assumption, why does he at once add, you shall eat the locust, which everyone knows has those larger legs? Moreover, why are the bombyx [silkworm] and the cantharus [beetle], or scarabaeus [scarab], placed in this list? The former does not fly as long as it remains a silkworm; and neither is four-footed, neither col. 452 has been used as food by any nation, much less by the Jews, whose cleanliness in eating was of the highest. We know that in Ethiopia and elsewhere there were ἀκριδοφάγους [akridophagous] and ὀφιοφάγους [ophiophagous]; III, 268 but there is no one who has mentioned τῶν βομβυκοφάγων [tōn bombykophagōn] or τῶν κανθαροφάγων [tōn kantharophagōn]. Human nature has always shrunk from such foul and fetid foods. So that most learned man ought not to have set them on Jewish tables, and that as though on God's authority. I will say, however, why he rendered these words, אשר לו כרעים ממעל לרגליו, which has legs above its feet, with an added negation, which does not have, etc. It is well known that certain words of the Prophets are read otherwise than they are written. Among these is the little word לו, which the Masoretes note is written לא in fifteen places and read לו; of which the former means not, the latter to it. So in this place, while the text writes אשר לא כרעים, which has not legs, the margin has אשר לו כרעים, which has legs. It is certain that this reading must be followed, both from the whole sequence of the text and from the agreement of the translators. For all the Hebrews read thus. Nor do the Greeks, Jerome, the two Chaldees (Jonathan and Onkelos), the Syriac, the four Arabic translators (whom I was able to consult, two printed, and two in manuscript in Sweden), the Samaritan, etc., follow anything else, not to mention that the Hebrew copy of the Samaritans has in the text itself לו with Vav, not with Aleph R22. ¶

And the old Scholiast, and from him Suidas, explain this word: Τετραπτερυλλίδων [Tetrapterylidōn]· τῶν ἀκρίδων [tōn akridōn]· παρὰ τὸ τέσσαρα ἔχειν πτερά [para to tessara echein ptera]. The two words τῶν ἀκρίδων [tōn akridōn], omitted in Suidas, are to be supplied from the Scholiast. You will be surprised, however, that Moses attributes only four feet to locusts, since others count six. Aristotle, in De Partibus, book 4, chapter 6, says this of locusts, gnats, etc.: Ἑξάποδα δὲ τὰ τοιαῦτα III, 269 πάντα εἰσί [Hexapoda de ta toiauta panta eisi]. But all these have six feet. And Damir the Arab: ولجرادة ستة ارجل [wa-l-jarāda sitta arjul] And the locust has six feet. But these are easily reconciled. Of the locust's six feet, the two hindmost, which are longer, are fitted not for walking but for leaping. Accordingly Moses calls them legs, not feet: You shall eat, he says, of every flying creeping thing that goes upon four feet, which has כרעים ממעל לרגליו, legs above its feet (that is, besides its feet), with which to leap upon the earth. Augustine, therefore, in Retractations, book 2, chapter 15, observes that these flying creatures are called four-footed in the Law, because the Law does not count among the feet the two hind legs with which locusts leap. But the Philosopher, who calls them ἑξάποδα [hexapoda], expressly notes that among those six feet he also counts the parts by whose effort they leap: Ἑξάποδα δὲ τὰ τοιαῦτα πάντα εἰσὶ, σὺν τοῖς ἁλτικοῖς μορίοις [Hexapoda de ta toiauta panta eisi, syn tois haltikois moriois]. In these words it is plain that by ἁλτικὰ μόρια [haltika moria], leaping parts, are meant those two legs which Moses writes the locust has, לנתר בהן על הארץ, to leap with them upon the earth R24. The Philosopher calls them πηδάλια [pēdalia] in History, book 4, chapter 9, where we read αἱ ἀκρίδες τοῖς πηδαλίοις τρίβουσαι ποιεῖν τὸν ψόφον [hai akrides tois pēdaliois tribousai poiein ton psophon], that locusts produce their sound by rubbing with their pedalia (or by the friction of their pedalia). And the great Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] renders it leaping legs, as if they were so called ἀπὸ τοῦ πηδᾷν [apo tou pēdan], from leaping. Rather, πηδάλια [pēdalia] are rudders, by which name they are rightly called because they are col. 453 πηδαλιώδη [pēdaliōdē], that is, they imitate the shape of rudders. This explanation is not ours but that of the Philosopher himself, who speaks thus in De partibus, book 4, chapter 6: Οὐκ ἔμπροσθεν δὲ, ἀλλ᾽ ὄπισθεν μόνον ἔχουσιν αἱ ἀκρίδες τὰ πηδαλιώδη [Ouk emprosthen de, all᾽ opisthen monon echousin hai akrides ta pēdaliōdē]. Locusts have parts formed in the shape of rudders not in front, but only behind. Indeed, just as locusts have two legs of this kind, so many ships of old had two rudders. Aelian, Varia, book 9, chapter 40, expressly asserts this of the ships of the Carthaginians: Δύο μὲν πηδάλια ἔχειν [Dyo men pēdalia echein], and accordingly also δύο κυβερνήτας [dyo kybernētas]. Hence it is that in Heliodorus, Aethiopica, book 5, Theagenes and Chariclea, carried in a Tyrian ship, put in at the Cretan promontory of Ithaca, τῶν πηδαλίων θάτερον ἀποβαλόντες [tōn pēdaliōn thateron apobalontes], having lost one of the two rudders. And of Paul's ship Luke said in the plural τὰς ζευκτηρίας τῶν πηδαλίων [tas zeuktērias tōn pēdaliōn], the bands of the rudders, Acts 27:40. On these words I do not quite approve what the very learned commentator says: He said rudders in the plural for the singular, since one ship does not have several rudders. For we have proved that the ships of the Phoenicians (or, what is the same, the Punic peoples) were furnished with a double rudder. Such was Paul's ship, if the Vulgate reads correctly at Acts 27:2. Embarking on a ship of Adrumetum. And in the Ethiopic ሐመር፡ አድሩመጠቂት፡ [ḥamar ʾadərumaṭaqit] chamar Adrumatavith, that is, a ship from Adrumetum, a very well-known Punic city on the coast of Africa. III, 270 Or, if it was πλοῖον Ἀδραμυττηνὸν [ploion Adramyttēnon], from Adramyttium in Mysia, as the Greek has it, still it need not have had only one rudder. For this custom spread from the Punic peoples to others as well. Hence Hyginus on the ship Argo: It has at the stern, at each of the rudders, at the first five stars, at the other four. And Germanicus on the same: Which, marked (read changed), Pallas figured in the sky, but from the rudders up to the mast. And in Apuleius, The Ass, book 2, Diophanes on a ship, Greek as it seems, sunk off Euboea: The very ship in which we were carried, having lost both its steering gear, sank headlong. Both its steering gear, that is, having lost both helms: which escaped Beroaldus. Petronius too more than once speaks of the rudders of the Tarentine ship in the plural.
