II, 777 CHAPTER 7. col. 191 ¶

Milvum potius a volatu dici דאה daa, ut in Levitico; quam a visu ראה raa, ut in Deuteronomio.

That the kite is named from flying, דאה daa, as in Leviticus, rather than from sight, ראה raa, as in Deuteronomy. ¶

Sequitur דאה daa, vel ראה raa. Neque enim certum est, utro modo sit scribendum. Quia eadem avis in Levitico דאה vocatur, et in Deuteronomio ראה raa: quorum unum a volatu, alterum a visu sumptum. Nam דאה daa verbum volare est, et ראה raa, videre. Nempe literas Daleth et Res, quia sunt valde affines, librarii saepe confundunt. Sic iidem sunt דודנים Dodanim, Gen. X, 4. et רודנים Rodanim, 1 Par. I, 7. ריפת Riphath, Gen. X, 3. et דיפת Diphath, 1 Par. I, 6. הדר Hadar, Gen. XXXVI, 39. et הדד Hadad, 1 Par. I, 46. הדד־עזר Hadad-ezer, 2 Sam. VIII, 3. et הדר־עזר Hadar-ezer, 1 Par. XVIII, 3. ההרודי Harodites, 2 Sam. XXIII, 25. et ההרורי Harorites, 1 Paral. XI, 27. חמדן Hamdan, Gen. XXXVI, 26. et חמרן Hamran, 1 Par. I, 39. (al. 41.). דעואל Daguel, Num. I, 14. et VII, 42. et רעואל Raguel, Num. II, 14. Et de Deo dicitur 2 Sam. XXII, 11. וירא vajera, et apparuit, pro וידא vajeda, et volavit, Psal. XVIII, 11. Qua omnino ratione eadem avis alibi דאה daa vocatur; alibi ראה raa. Sed illa avis, si vere milvus est, prout statuunt plerique veterum et recentiorum interpretum, in his Hieronymus, Arabes, Munsterus, Castalio, Iunius, Deodatius, Buxtorfius, Schindlerus, etc. malim דאה daa scribi, quam ראה raa; a volatu ducto nomine potius, quam a visu. Quia cum in milvi visu peculiare nihil observent rerum naturae scriptores: in volatu primo hoc habet eximium, quod alis prope immotis in aëre omnium avium diutissime libratur. Munsterus, רחף, Motus est, ut milvus scilicet in aëre movetur, alis non motis, etc. Budaeus, Horam integram, aut plus eo, milvos observavimus, non jactatis alis, captare praedam, etc. Hoc Itali aucupes tendere vocant, Galli planare. Elata namque paullisper ala, motum aëris in reliquum etiam corpus excipiunt, at-que ita sustinentur. Praeterea vix ulla est avis, quae milvo volet altius. Martialis lib. 9. Epigrammate 55.

Next comes דאה daa, or ראה raa. For it is not certain which way it should be written, since the same bird is called דאה in Leviticus and ראה raa in Deuteronomy; of these, one is taken from flying, the other from sight. For דאה daa is the verb to fly, and ראה raa to see. The letters Daleth and Resh, being very similar, are often confused by scribes. Thus דודנים Dodanim, Gen 10:4 and רודנים Rodanim, 1 Chr 1:7 are the same; so are ריפת Riphath, Gen 10:3 and דיפת Diphath, 1 Chr 1:6; הדר Hadar, Gen 36:39 and הדד Hadad, 1 Chr 1:46; הדד־עזר Hadad-ezer, 2 Sam 8:3 and הדר־עזר Hadar-ezer, 1 Chr 18:3; ההרודי Harodites, 2 Sam 23:25 and ההרורי Harorites, 1 Chr 11:27; חמדן Hamdan, Gen 36:26 and חמרן Hamran, 1 Chr 1:39 (var. 41.); דעואל Daguel, Num 1:14 and VII, 42. and רעואל Raguel, Num 2:14. And of God it is said in 2 Sam 22:11. וירא vajera, and he appeared, instead of וידא vajeda, and he flew, Ps 18:11. In exactly the same way the same bird is called דאה daa in one place and ראה raa in another. But if that bird is truly the kite, as most of the ancient and more recent interpreters hold, among them Jerome, the Arabs, Munster, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], Diodati, Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], Schindler, etc., I would rather have it written דאה daa than ראה raa, with the name derived from flying rather than from sight. For while writers on natural history observe nothing peculiar in the kite's sight, in its flight it has this first distinctive feature: with its wings almost motionless it hovers in the air longer than any other bird. Munster: רחף, It moved, namely as the kite moves in the air without moving its wings, etc. Budaeus: We have watched kites for a whole hour or more catching prey without beating their wings, etc. This the Italian fowlers call tendere, the French planare. For by raising the wing a little they take the movement of the air into the rest of the body as well, and are thus held up. Moreover, there is scarcely any bird that flies higher than the kite. Martial, book 9, Epigram 55: ¶

Hinc prope summa rapax milvus in astra volat.

Hence the rapacious kite flies almost to the highest stars. ¶

Bellonius, De Avibus lib. 2. cap. 26. Cum accipiter in milvum immittitur, utrique in summum aërem ita evehuntur, ut hominum visus fugiant. Budaeus in Philologia, Nec ardeae, nec milvi, intra nubes conditi, evadere humanas manus possunt. Volando denique milvus non facile delassatur. Unde Persius de Vectidio Satyra 4.

Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], De Avibus book 2, ch. 26: When a hawk is let loose at a kite, both are carried up into the highest air, so that they escape human sight. Budaeus in his Philologia: Neither herons nor kites, though hidden within the clouds, can escape human hands. Finally, the kite does not easily tire in flying. Hence Persius on Vectidius, Satire 4: ¶

— arat Curibus quantum non milvus oberret.

— he ploughs at Cures more than a kite could fly over. ¶

Ubi Cornutus, Quod dici solet secundum proverbium, „Quantum milvi non volant“, quia milvi multum volant. Et Iuvenalis Satyra 9.

Where Cornutus comments: As is commonly said in the proverb, "As far as kites do not fly", because kites fly a great deal. And Juvenal, Satire 9: ¶

II, 778 — — cui tot montes, tot praedia servas Appula, tot milvos intra tua pascua lassos.

II, 778 — — for whom are you keeping so many mountains, so many Apulian estates, so many kites wearied within your pastures? ¶

Et ibi vetus Scholiastes, Vult ostendere magnitudinem possessionum Poeta, quam latae sint agris, ut nec milvi transvolare eas possint. Id igitur naturae milvi con- col. 192 venit magis, ut a volatus praestantia dictus fuerit דאה daa potius quam a visu ראה raa. Si objicias haec Apuleji lib. 6. Metamorphoseωs, Nec tamen astutulae anus milvinos oculos effugere potui: quibus milvo tribui videtur insignis perspicacitas. Respondebo, 1. rapacibus omnibus idem tribui, 2. singularem esse hunc Apuleji locum. Quod his verbis agnoscit illustris commentator Philippus Beroaldus, Milvinos oculos scriptor nullus, quod quidem sciam, celebrat, velut acumine oculorum praepollentes. Addo, in Samaritano exemplari, tam in Deu- teronomio, quam in Levitico legi ࠃࠀࠄ per Daleth, nusquam ࠓࠀࠄ per Res. Et ex Hebraeo דאה daa factum videri Arabicum حدا hida, Gutturali praeposita; ut in Hebraeo Tigris nomine חדקל Hiddekel, facto ex Chaldaeo דגלה Digla. Saltem id certum, apud Arabes milvum حداة hida vocari (1). Quod ex Abenbitare clarum, capite قاطاننقي Catananke, in quo Graeca haec Dioscoridis de exsiccato catanankes herbae semine, Ἰκτίνου ὄνυξιν ὁμοιοῦται νεκροῦ, Milvi mortui unguibus assimilatur, in Arabica paraphrasi sic redduntur: وكان شكلها شبيها شكل مخالب الحداة الميتة Et forma ejus similis est formae unguium hidae mortuae. Hidae, id est, milvi. Ut de Damire taceam, qui in capite De حداة hida illi tribuit, quidquid de milvo habet Philosophus Hist. lib. 6. cap. 6. Et de Andrea Bellunensi, cujus verba sunt: Alhada est avis, quae dicitur milvus. Recte, nisi quod alhida scribendum, non alhada. Nam الحداة alhada per Fata est bipennis, ut notant Damir et Alca- mus: sed alhida per Kesra est avis nomen. Sunt, qui tam דאה daa, quam ראה raa, multo aliter interpretentur: sed illorum versiones ideo praetermitto, quia non videntur ullo modo verisimiles (2).

And there the old Scholiast says: The poet wishes to show the size of the estates, how widely they extend in fields, so that not even kites can fly across them. This, then, suits the nature of the kite col. 192 better: that it was called דאה daa from the excellence of its flight rather than ראה raa from its sight. You may object these words of Apuleius, Metamorphoses book 6: Yet I could not escape the kite's eyes of the crafty little old woman, in which remarkable keenness of sight seems to be attributed to the kite. I shall reply, 1. that the same is attributed to all birds of prey; 2. that this passage of Apuleius is unique. This the illustrious commentator Philippus Beroaldus acknowledges in these words: No writer, so far as I know, celebrates the eyes of the kite as excelling in keenness of sight. I add that in the Samaritan copy, both in Deuteronomy and in Leviticus, ࠃࠀࠄ [דאה] is read with Daleth, nowhere ࠓࠀࠄ [ראה] with Resh. And from the Hebrew דאה daa the Arabic حدا [ḥdā] hida seems to have been formed, with a guttural prefixed, as in the Hebrew name of the Tigris, חדקל Hiddekel, formed from the Chaldee דגלה Digla. At least this is certain: among the Arabs the kite is called حداة [ḥidāh] hida R1. This is clear from Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], in the chapter قاطاننقي [qāṭānanqī] Catananke, in which these Greek words of Dioscorides on the dried seed of the herb catananke, Ἰκτίνου ὄνυξιν ὁμοιοῦται νεκροῦ [Iktinou onyxin homoioutai nekrou], It resembles the claws of a dead kite, are rendered thus in the Arabic paraphrase: وكان شكلها شبيها شكل مخالب الحداة الميتة [wa-kāna shakluhā shabīhan shakla makhālib al-ḥidāh al-mayyita] And its shape is similar to the shape of the claws of a dead hida. Hida, that is, kite. To say nothing of Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], who in the chapter On حداة [ḥidāh] hida attributes to it whatever the Philosopher has about the kite, Hist. book 6, ch. 6. Nor of Andrea Bellunensis, whose words are: Alhada is a bird which is called the kite. Correct, except that alhida should be written, not alhada. For الحداة [al-ḥadāh] alhada with Fatha is a double axe, as Damir and Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] note; but alhida with Kasra is the name of the bird. There are some who interpret both דאה daa and ראה raa quite differently; but I pass over their versions because they do not seem in any way probable R2. ¶
