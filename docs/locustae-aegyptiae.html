III, 282 col. 460 Chapter III. ¶

De Locustis Aegyptiis, Exod. X, 4.

On the Egyptian Locusts, Exod 10:4. ¶

In Prophetarum scriptis tres de locustis occurrunt vel historiae, vel prophetiae, prae caeteris insignes, ac proinde hic explicandae. Prima est de Aegyptiis locustis, id est, in Aegyptum a Deo immissis, post septem plagas atrocissimas, quibus frangi non potuerat Pharaonis contumacia [Exod. X, 4. seqq.]. In qua col. 461 historia, ut intelligantur, quae habentur de locustis, haec tria sunt observanda. 1. In genere, quantam locustae perniciem creent herbis, segetibus, arboribus, atque omni terrae proventui. 2. Quid in his locustis fuerit peculiare. 3. Quomodo in Aegyptum adductae fuerint, et ex Aegypto abductae. Et de primo frustra ageremus, si locustae nobis tam notae essent, quam Arabibus et Africanis, qui experiuntur indies, quam grave sit hoc malum. Cum jam paratis messibus, aut pratis, et pascuis, aut hortis, et pomariis incumbunt locustarum mille myriades, quae, totius anni laborem paucis horis absumentes, miseris incolis famem indicunt. Quam voracitatem et ingluviem Arabes exprimunt verbis احتنك ingurgitare (a حنك id est, faucibus, deducta voce) نعص vel لعص, vorare (Lam et Nun permutatis); دبش, decorticare (quod tamen de terra et agris, non de arboribus accipiunt, quasi fruges et herbae, quas absumunt locustae, sint terrae cortex); بشر, corii instar radere, et laevigare: جرد, deglabrare et depilare: unde et ipsi locustae nomen جراد. Nimirum haec animalcula, quidquid in agris viret, ingurgitant, vorant, excorticant, radunt, deglabrant. Neque solo morsu nocent, sed et contactu adurunt. Quin لعابهم سم ناقع للنبات والشجر لا يقع علي شي منه الا اهلكه saliva earum virus est, herbis et arboribus tam noxium, ut, in quodcunque incidat, id deperdat; ut in Damire legere est. Quae tamen levia videri queant, cum saepe multo plus damni mortuae quam vivae inferant, tetro et viroso odore aërem inficientes, unde gravissima sequitur lues, adeoque plures hominum myriades tali peste paucis diebus abripiuntur, quam diuturnis bellis; ut dicetur suo loco. Unde idem Damir, ليس في الحيوان اكثر افسان Nullum est animal perniciosius. Et Mahumetes, ut ajunt, الله خلق الف امة منها ستماية في البحر واربعماية في البر وان اول هلاك هذه الامم الجراد واذا اهلك الجراد تتابع هلاك الامم كنظم السلك Mille animalium species creavit III, 283 Deus, sexcentas in mari, et quadringentas in terra. Sed horum prima pestis est locusta, qua destruente sequitur reliquorum exitium, ut monile consertum: id est, ut baccae, in monili consertae, se mutuo subsequuntur (1).

In the writings of the Prophets there occur three histories or prophecies about locusts that are remarkable above the rest, and which must therefore be explained here. The first concerns the Egyptian locusts, that is, those sent by God into Egypt after the seven most dreadful plagues, by which the obstinacy of Pharaoh could not be broken [Exod 10:4 ff.]. In this col. 461 history, in order to understand what is said about the locusts, these three things must be observed. 1. In general, how great a destruction locusts cause to herbs, crops, trees and every produce of the earth. 2. What was peculiar in these locusts. 3. How they were brought into Egypt and carried away out of Egypt. On the first point we would labour in vain if locusts were as well known to us as to the Arabs and Africans, who learn daily how grievous this evil is. When the harvests are already ripe, or upon meadows and pastures, or upon gardens and orchards, a thousand myriads of locusts descend, which, consuming the labour of a whole year in a few hours, impose famine upon the wretched inhabitants. This voracity and gluttony the Arabs express by the words احتنك [iḥtanaka] to gulp down (a word derived from حنك [ḥanak], that is, the jaws); نعص [naʿaṣa] or لعص [laʿaṣa], to devour (Lam and Nun being interchanged); دبش [dabasha], to strip the bark (which, however, they take of the earth and the fields, not of trees, as if the crops and herbs that the locusts consume were the bark of the earth); بشر [bashara], to scrape like a hide, and to make smooth: جرد [jarada], to make bare and to strip of hair: whence also the very name of the locust, جراد [jarād]. Indeed these little creatures gulp down, devour, strip, scrape and make bare whatever is green in the fields. Nor do they harm by their bite alone; they also scorch by their touch. Moreover لعابهم سم ناقع للنبات والشجر لا يقع علي شي منه الا اهلكه [luʿābuhum samm nāqiʿ li-l-nabāt wa-l-shajar lā yaqaʿu ʿalā shayʾ minhu illā ahlakahu] their saliva is a poison so harmful to herbs and trees that whatever it falls upon, it destroys; as one may read in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]. Yet these things may seem slight, since they often inflict much more damage dead than alive, infecting the air with a foul and poisonous stench, from which a most severe pestilence follows, so that more myriads of men are carried off by such a plague in a few days than by long wars; as will be said in its place. Hence the same Damir says: ليس في الحيوان اكثر افسان [laysa fī al-ḥayawān akthar afsān] There is no animal more destructive. And Muhammad, as they say: الله خلق الف امة منها ستماية في البحر واربعماية في البر وان اول هلاك هذه الامم الجراد واذا اهلك الجراد تتابع هلاك الامم كنظم السلك [Allāh khalaqa alf umma minhā sittumiʾa fī al-baḥr wa-arbaʿumiʾa fī al-barr wa-inna awwal halāk hādhihi al-umam al-jarād wa-idhā halaka al-jarād tatābaʿa halāk al-umam ka-naẓm al-silk] God created a thousand species of animals, III, 283 six hundred in the sea and four hundred on land. But the first plague of these is the locust; when it destroys, the ruin of the rest follows, like a strung necklace: that is, as the beads strung on a necklace follow one another R1. ¶

III, 284 Sed in Aegyptiis locustis aliquid fuit solito gravius. Cum enim in Aegypto pestis nulla sit frequentior, tamen Moses diserte dicit a), his locustis neque pares unquam fuisse, neque in posterum futuras; sive fuerint mole majores; ut locustae illae Indicae, quas Plinius scribit lib. 9. cap. 3. quaterna cubita implere, et lib. 10. cap. 29. ternum pedum longitudinis esse, cruribus et feminibus serrarum usum praebentes, cum inaruerint: sive, quod credo potius, numero superaverint quidquam unquam fuit uspiam locustarum in uno loco, et magis noxiae fuerint. Quod suadent Prophetae verba, has terrae superficiem ita cooperuisse, col. 462 ut non potuerit quisquam videre terram; adeoque resedisse in universa Aegypti terra, cum in hunc vel illum agrum involare soleant, aliis praeteritis; et absumpsisse omnem herbam, et omnem fructum arborum, et quidquid residuum fecerat, quae praecesserat grando.

III, 284 But in the Egyptian locusts there was something more grievous than usual. For although no plague is more frequent in Egypt, Moses nevertheless says expressly a) that there had never been locusts like these, nor would there be in the future; whether because they were larger in bulk, like those Indian locusts which Pliny writes, book 9, ch. 3, fill four cubits, and book 10, ch. 29, are three feet in length, their legs and thighs serving as saws when they have dried out; or, as I rather believe, because they surpassed in number whatever swarm of locusts had ever been anywhere in one place, and were more harmful. This is suggested by the words of the Prophet: that they so covered the surface of the earth col. 462 that no one could see the ground; and that they settled over the whole land of Egypt, whereas locusts usually fly into this or that field, passing over others; and that they consumed every herb, and all the fruit of the trees, and whatever the hail that had gone before had left. ¶

Ad quae verba si roges, Quod fuerit hoc residuum, cum grando percussisse legatur, quidquid erat in agris in tota terra Aegypti, et omnem herbam laesisse, et omnem arborem confregisse, Exod. IX, 25.? Respondeo, 1. Triticum et zeam a grandine non fuisse laesa. Ita enim habet sacer textus versib. 31. et 32. Linum autem et hordeum laesum fuit; quia hordeo erat calamus, et lino culmus. Triti- cum vero et zea laesa non fuerunt, eo quod erant serotina. Nempe triticum in il-lis locis hordeo serius provenit. Unde Clemens Alexandrinus lib. 6. Stromatum, III, 285 Προπετεστέρα εἰς ὥραν θέρους τοῦ πυροῦ, μᾶλλον ἡ κριθή· Hordeum est tritico citius tempore aestatis. Et Plinius lib. 18. cap. 17. In Aegypto hordeum sexto a satu mense, frumenta septimo metuntur: id est, uno mense serius (2). Hinc, cum hordeo collata אפילות, id est, serotina, vocantur. Non, quod sub terra plane laterent, ut ex Kimchio reddunt Iunius et Tremellius; pridem enim se exeruerant: sed quod, adhuc tenera, lenta flexilique sua mollitie ingruentes grandinis ictus evitarent et subterfugerent; ut a Fullero recte observatur Miscellaneorum lib. 3. cap. 11. Porro ut triticum et zeam, sic herbas quasdam, et gramina, nonnullarum quoque arborum ramos et fructus, grandinis impetum evasisse, vero consentaneum est. Itaque non citra hyperbolen grando laesisse legitur omnem herbam atque omnem arborem. Quod sequens exceptio aperte docet. Vel omnes herbas atque arbores ita laesisse dicenda est, ut non prorsus corruperit.

If at these words you ask what this remainder was, since we read that the hail struck whatever was in the fields in all the land of Egypt, and damaged every herb, and broke every tree, Exod 9:25? I answer: 1. The wheat and the spelt were not damaged by the hail. For so the sacred text has it in verses 31 and 32: But the flax and the barley were damaged, because the barley had its stalk, and the flax its stem. But the wheat and the spelt were not damaged, because they were late. For in those places wheat comes up later than barley. Hence Clement of Alexandria, book 6 of the Stromata, III, 285 Προπετεστέρα εἰς ὥραν θέρους τοῦ πυροῦ, μᾶλλον ἡ κριθή [propetestera eis hōran therous tou pyrou, mallon hē krithē]: Barley comes sooner than wheat in the summer season. And Pliny, book 18, ch. 17: In Egypt barley is reaped in the sixth month from sowing, wheat in the seventh: that is, one month later R2. Hence, compared with barley, they are called אפילות, that is, late. Not because they were still entirely hidden under the earth, as Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] render it following Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]; for they had long since come up. Rather, being still tender, by their pliant and flexible softness they avoided and escaped the blows of the falling hail, as is rightly observed by Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], Miscellanea book 3, ch. 11. Further, it is likely that, like the wheat and the spelt, certain herbs and grasses, and also the branches and fruits of some trees, escaped the force of the hail. Thus it is not without hyperbole that the hail is said to have damaged every herb and every tree. The exception that follows plainly shows this. Or it must be said that it damaged all herbs and trees in such a way that it did not destroy them entirely. ¶

Plerique, ut hunc nodum solvant, sibi fingunt multos menses, aut annum etiam intercessisse inter plagam grandinis et plagam locustarum: atque interea temporis crevisse nova gramina, novas segetes, novos arborum fructus, in eorum locum, quos grando perdiderat. Quae videntur frustra dici, cum Moses diserte scribat, locustas absumpsisse, quidquid grando reliquum fecerat, Exod. X, 5. 12. 15. Nec dici possit grando id reliquum fecisse, quod nondum erat, aut super terram nondum exstabat. Praeterea ab eo tempore, quo in Aegypto coepit Moses patrare miracula, usque ad ejusdem obitum, fluxerunt anni praecise quadraginta. Agebat enim annum octogesimum, cum convenit Pharaonem, Exod. VII, 7. Et anno aetatis centesimo vigesimo mortuus est in ora terrae Chanaan, quam viderat a monte Nebo, Deut. XXXIV, 1. 5. Per hos porro quadraginta annos dicuntur Israelitae per desertum iter fecisse Ios. V, 6. Quod stare non posset, si Moses annum integrum in Aegypto trivisset in miraculis patrandis. Nam annus ille demendus esset ex annis quadraginta morae Israelitarum in deserto. Falsum est igitur, quod hi supponunt, inter Mosis miracula multum spatium intercessisse. Neque id satis convenit cum Dei bonitate, ut, populo suo jam ad incitas redacto, tam lente subvenisset, et tot moris interpositis. Sed et justitia ejus requirebat, ut in tam gravem Pharaonis σκληροκαρδίαν continuis plagis animadverteret, nec ullo ei dato respirandi spatio. Proinde iis accedimus, qui decem plagas, Aegyptiis inflictas, intra breve tempus contrahunt, puta menstruum, aut circiter.

Most, in order to untie this knot, imagine that many months, or even a year, passed between the plague of hail and the plague of locusts, and that in the meantime new grass, new crops and new fruits of the trees grew in place of those that the hail had destroyed. This seems to be said in vain, since Moses writes expressly that the locusts consumed whatever the hail had left remaining, Exod 10:5 12. 15. Nor could the hail be said to have left remaining what did not yet exist, or did not yet stand above the ground. Moreover, from the time when Moses began to perform miracles in Egypt until his death, exactly forty years passed. For he was in his eightieth year when he met Pharaoh, Exod 7:7. And in the hundred and twentieth year of his age he died on the border of the land of Canaan, which he had seen from Mount Nebo, Deut 34:1 5. Through these forty years, further, the Israelites are said to have journeyed through the desert, Josh 5:6. This could not stand if Moses had spent a whole year in Egypt performing miracles. For that year would have to be subtracted from the forty years of the Israelites' stay in the desert. It is therefore false, as these suppose, that a long interval passed between the miracles of Moses. Nor does it accord well with the goodness of God that, when his people had already been reduced to extremity, he should have come to their aid so slowly and with so many delays. And his justice too required that he punish so grave a σκληροκαρδίαν [sklērokardian] of Pharaoh with continuous plagues, granting him no breathing space. We therefore side with those who compress the ten plagues inflicted on the Egyptians into a short time, say a month or thereabouts. ¶

III, 286 Quod prope omiseram, non solum agros, sed et Aegyptiorum domos implebant hae locustae, Exod. X, 6. (3). Neque tantum terrae frugibus arborum, sed et hominibus putantur fuisse noxiae: atque eo haec pertinere de ecclesiae hostibus; Sap. XVI, 9. Οὓς μὲν γὰρ ἀκρίδων καὶ μυιῶν ἀπέκτεινε δήγματα, καὶ οὐχ εὑρέθη ἴαμα τῇ ψυχῇ αὐτῶν, ὅτι ἄξιοι ἦσαν ὑπὸ τοιούτων κολασθῆναι. Quos quidem locustarum et muscarum morsus occiderunt, neque inventum est reme- col. 463 dium animae (id est, vitae) ipsorum, quia digni erant, qui ab hujusmodi rebus punirentur. Sed libri auctor niti videtur verbis, quibus Pharao liberari petiit ab hac plaga, Exod. X, 17. Rogate Dominum Deum vestrum, ut auferat a me mortem istam. Unde tamen haudquaquam sequitur, has locustas hominibus mortem inflixisse. Mors enim etiam agrorum est, et herbarum, atque arborum. Sic Aegyptii dicunt Gen. XLVII, 19. Cur moreremur coram te, tam nos, quam terra nostra. Et de arbore Iobus XIV, 8. Si senuerit in terra radix ejus, et in pulvere mortuus fuerit truncus ejus. Et David Psal. LXXVIII, 47. Occidit grandine vites eorum. Et Paulus 1 Cor. XV, 36. Quod tu seminas, non vivificatur, nisi prius moriatur. De qua locutione plura habentur statim initio hujus operis (4). Praeterea locustae homines et bruta occidunt, saltem ex consequenti, III, 287 quod ajunt; quia consumptis illis, quae fuissent vitae subsidio, sic ad mortem eos adigunt (5). Utroque igitur respectu Pharao potuit rogare, ut Deus auferret mortem istam, id est, mortiferas istas locustas.

III, 286 A point I had nearly omitted: these locusts filled not only the fields but also the houses of the Egyptians, Exod 10:6 R3. And they are thought to have been harmful not only to the fruits of the earth and of the trees, but also to men; and to this, it is thought, refers what is said of the enemies of the church, Wis 16:9 Οὓς μὲν γὰρ ἀκρίδων καὶ μυιῶν ἀπέκτεινε δήγματα, καὶ οὐχ εὑρέθη ἴαμα τῇ ψυχῇ αὐτῶν, ὅτι ἄξιοι ἦσαν ὑπὸ τοιούτων κολασθῆναι [Hous men gar akridōn kai myiōn apekteine dēgmata, kai ouch heurethē iama tē psychē autōn, hoti axioi ēsan hypo toioutōn kolasthēnai]. For the bites of locusts and flies killed them, and no remedy was found for their soul col. 463 (that is, their life), because they deserved to be punished by such things. But the author of the book seems to rely on the words with which Pharaoh asked to be freed from this plague, Exod 10:17. Entreat the Lord your God, that he take away from me this death. Yet from this it by no means follows that these locusts inflicted death on men. For there is also a death of fields, of herbs and of trees. So the Egyptians say, Gen 47:19. Why should we die before you, both we and our land? And of a tree, Job XIV, 8. If its root grow old in the earth, and its stock die in the dust. And David, Ps 78:47: He killed their vines with hail. And Paul, 1 Cor 15:36: What you sow is not quickened unless it first dies. On this expression more is said at the very beginning of this work R4. Moreover, locusts kill men and beasts at least by consequence, III, 287 as they say, since by consuming what would have served to sustain life they thus drive them to death R5. In either respect, therefore, Pharaoh could ask that God take away this death, that is, these deadly locusts. ¶

De caetero, ut vento קדים Kadim, id est, Orientali, in Aegyptum illatae fuerant hae locustae: ita vento ים, maris, , id est, Occidentali, abductae sunt, et in mare rubrum projectae. Locustas enim vento vehi, tralatitium est. Plinius ubi supra, Est et alius earum obitus. Gregatim sublatae vento in maria, aut stagna, decidunt. Hieronymus in Ioelem cap. 2. Etiam nostris temporibus vidimus agmina locustarum terram texisse Iudaeam: quae postea misericordia Domini, etc. vento surgente, in mare primum et novissimum praecipitatae sunt. Orosius lib. 4. cap. 11. de locustis Africanis, Repentino arreptae vento, atque in globos coactae, portataeque diu per aërem, Africano pelago immersae sunt (6). Ventus autem קדים Kadim, qui locustas in Aegyptum advexit, quamvis proprie Orientalem sonet, tamen etiam pro Australi sumitur, ut probavimus b) in capite De miraculo Coturnicum. Itaque Graeci interpretes Νότον et Λίβα reddunt, id est, Notum, et Africum, et καύσωνα, ventum urentem. Neque fortasse erit extra rem ita hic interpretari: cum ventus Orientalis nonnisi ex Arabia locustas potuerit in Aegyptum advehere: sed Australis ex Aethiopia, quae hac peste multo est foecundior. Proinde الحبشان alhabsan, locustae genus, nomen habet III, 288 ab Abasenis, seu Abyssinis, in quorum solo nascitur. Et in Aethiopia nonnullae gentes ex solis locustis vixerunt, quas copia vix credenda eo advehebant venti, partim occidentales, partim meridionales. Idque circa vernum aequinoctium, quo ipso anni tempore in Aegytum adductae sunt. Agatharchides ubi de Acridophagis, Ὑπὸ δὲ τὴν ἐαρινὴν ἰσημερίαν, ὅτε Λίβες παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς καὶ Ζέφυροι πνέουσι παμμεγέθεις, ἀκρίδων πλῆθος ἀμύθητον ἐκ τῆς ἀνιστορήτου μετὰ τῶν ἀνέμων παραγίνεται χώρας. Et Diodorus lib. 3. Κατὰ γὰρ τὴν ἐαρινὴν ὥραν παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς Ζέφυροι καὶ Λίβες παμμεγέθεις ἐκρίπτουσιν ἐκ τῆς ἐρήμου πλῆθος ἀκρίδων ἀμύθητον. Et Strabo lib. 16. Ζῶσι δ᾽ ἀπὸ ἀκρίδων, ἃς οἱ ἐαρινοὶ Λί-βες, καὶ Ζέφυροι, πνέοντες μεγάλοι, συνελαύνουσιν εἰς τοὺς τόπους τούτους. Proinde hi ipsi venti locustas ex iisdem locis videntur in Aegyptum attulisse, id est, ex Aethiopia (7). Nam, quod eas plerique volunt a Deo fuisse creatas, mihi vix persuaserint; quia Scriptura nihil tale refert, sed a vento fuisse advectas, qui tota die flaverit, et tota nocte. Nec miracula confingenda sunt absque necessitate. Qualis hic nulla apparet, cum in Aethiopia prope Aegyptum plures locustae procreentur, quam in ulla parte terrarum. Miraculum igitur non fuit in eo, quod produxit Deus, sed in eo, quod adduxit nutu suo tam prodigiosam locustarum multitudinem. Et quod idem, qui adduxerat, ad Mosis, servi sui, col. 464 orationem statim abduxit, alio vento excitato, occidentali scilicet, quem Hebraei vocant רוח ים , ventum maris; quia mare habent ad occasum. Et, illo vento magna vi flante, in mare rubrum projectae sunt locustae omnes, ne una quidem relicta in cunctis Aegypti finibus. Cum enim ad Orientem Aegypti sit mare rubrum, has occidentali vento eo propelli oportuit.

Moreover, just as these locusts had been brought into Egypt by the קדים Kadim wind, that is, the east wind, so they were carried away by the ים wind, the wind of the sea, that is, the west wind, and cast into the Red Sea. For that locusts are carried by the wind is commonplace. Pliny, in the place cited above: "There is also another way in which they perish. Lifted up in swarms by the wind, they fall into seas or pools." Jerome on Joel ch. 2: "In our own times too we have seen swarms of locusts cover the land of Judaea: which afterwards, by the mercy of the Lord, etc., when a wind arose, were hurled into the first and the last sea." Orosius, book 4, ch. 11, on the African locusts: "Seized by a sudden wind, and driven together into masses, and carried for a long time through the air, they were plunged into the African sea" R6. But the קדים Kadim wind, which brought the locusts into Egypt, although properly it means the east wind, is nevertheless also taken for the south wind, as we proved b) in the chapter On the Miracle of the Quails. Thus the Greek translators render it Νότον [Noton] and Λίβα [Liba], that is, Notus and Africus, and καύσωνα [kausōna], the burning wind. And perhaps it will not be beside the point to interpret it so here: since the east wind could have brought locusts into Egypt only from Arabia, but the south wind from Ethiopia, which is far more fertile in this pest. Accordingly الحبشان [al-ḥbshān] alhabsan, a kind of locust, takes its name III, 288 from the Abaseni, or Abyssinians, in whose soil it is born. And in Ethiopia some peoples lived on locusts alone, which the winds, partly westerly, partly southerly, carried there in an almost incredible abundance. And this around the spring equinox, the very time of year at which they were brought into Egypt. Agatharchides, where he speaks of the Acridophagi: Ὑπὸ δὲ τὴν ἐαρινὴν ἰσημερίαν, ὅτε Λίβες παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς καὶ Ζέφυροι πνέουσι παμμεγέθεις, ἀκρίδων πλῆθος ἀμύθητον ἐκ τῆς ἀνιστορήτου μετὰ τῶν ἀνέμων παραγίνεται χώρας [Hypo de tēn earinēn isēmerian, hote Libes par᾽ autois kai Zephyroi pneousi pammegetheis, akridōn plēthos amythēton ek tēs anistorētou meta tōn anemōn paraginetai chōras]. And Diodorus, book 3: Κατὰ γὰρ τὴν ἐαρινὴν ὥραν παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς Ζέφυροι καὶ Λίβες παμμεγέθεις ἐκρίπτουσιν ἐκ τῆς ἐρήμου πλῆθος ἀκρίδων ἀμύθητον [Kata gar tēn earinēn hōran par᾽ autois Zephyroi kai Libes pammegetheis ekriptousin ek tēs erēmou plēthos akridōn amythēton]. And Strabo, book 16: Ζῶσι δ᾽ ἀπὸ ἀκρίδων, ἃς οἱ ἐαρινοὶ Λίβες, καὶ Ζέφυροι, πνέοντες μεγάλοι, συνελαύνουσιν εἰς τοὺς τόπους τούτους [Zōsi d᾽ apo akridōn, has hoi earinoi Libes, kai Zephyroi, pneontes megaloi, synelaunousin eis tous topous toutous]. Accordingly these same winds seem to have brought the locusts into Egypt from the same places, that is, from Ethiopia R7. For as to the view of most, that they were created by God, they will hardly persuade me of it; because Scripture relates nothing of the kind, but says that they were brought by a wind which blew all day and all night. Nor are miracles to be invented without necessity. None such appears here, since more locusts are produced in Ethiopia, near Egypt, than in any part of the world. The miracle, therefore, lay not in God's producing so prodigious a multitude of locusts, but in his bringing it at his nod. And in that the same God who had brought them, at the prayer of Moses, his servant, col. 464 immediately took them away, raising another wind, namely the west wind, which the Hebrews call רוח ים, the wind of the sea, because they have the sea to the west. And as that wind blew with great force, all the locusts were cast into the Red Sea, not even one being left in all the borders of Egypt. For since the Red Sea lies to the east of Egypt, they had to be driven there by a west wind. ¶
