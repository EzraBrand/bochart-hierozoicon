III, 187 col. 402 CHAPTER X. ¶

Explicantur loca quinque Scripturae de Basilisco.

The five passages of Scripture on the basilisk are explained. ¶

Iam, si libet percurrere Scripturae loca de hoc serpente, Primus est, Prov. col. 403 XXIII, 32. ubi de vino dicit Solomon, Ultimum ejus ut serpens mordet, וכצפעני יפרש, et ut tsiphoni (seu basiliscus) pungit. Non ut Tatiano faveat, et Encratitis, et Manichaeis, qui vinum appellabant fel draconis, et venenum an-tiqui serpentis, et ab eo abstinebant etiam in sacra coena. Neque enim hic vini usus, sed abusus damnatur. Deus vino laetificat cor hominis, Psalm. CIV, 15. Adeoque vinum tam ipsum Deum quam homines exhilarare dicitur Iud. IX, 13. In sacrificiis scilicet, ad quae adhibebatur Exod. XXIX, 40. et Num. XV, 5. Quamvis eo loco nomine אלהים elohim, pro vero Deo, nonnulli judices et magnates significari velint: ut Exod. XXI, 6. et XXII, 8. 9. 28. et Psal. LXXXII, 6. Quibus opponantur אנשים, homines, id est, plebeii. Quomodo Gen. VI, 2. filiis Dei opponuntur filii hominum; et Psal. LXXXII, 6. 7. אדם (id est, homo) Diis et filiis excelsi. Ut sensus sit, vini potu divites et pauperes, principes et subditos, judices et clientes ex aequo exhilarari. Quod ipsissimum de vino dicit primus Darii satellitum 3 Esdr. III, 19. 20. Utut sit, vini proventus Dei bonitati et liberalitati adscribitur, Ierem. XXXI, 12. et Os. II, 8. Et promittitur Israelitis pluvia tempestiva et serotina, ut colligant frumentum et vinum, Deut. XI, 14. Atque uvae botrus, in desertum allatus, iis fuit pro specimine fertilitatis terrae Chanaan, Num. XIII, 24. Nec dicendum, quod sub Lege promissum et permissum fuerat, sub Evangelio fuisse prohibitum; cum Christus, ad nuptias invitatus, aquam in vinum mutaverit, quod convivis offerretur, Ioh. II, 8. At-que ipse cum Apostolis vinum multoties biberit, adeoque in ultima illa coena consecraverit, ut esset toti ecclesiae sanguinis sui sacramentum. Quid, quod Timotheo Paulus usum vini commendat propter stomachum, 1 Tim. V, 23. Sed res, in se bona, hic damnatur propter ebrietatis mala. Quae si quis non perpendit satis, cum Lacedaemoniorum pueris in ebrio homine vultum turbatum spectet, et titubantes pedes, et linguam balbutientem, et gestus incompositos, et rationem caecutientem, ac de sua sede motam. Cogitet idem helluonum lippientes oculos, nasum, bile suffusum, dentes putridos, foetentem halitum, membra tremula, et abdomen, arvina distentum; ut paralysim taceam, et podagram, et vertiginem, et convulsiones, et lethargum, et apoplexiam, et mille morborum examina, qui, quantumlibet graves, plus tamen infamiae, quam doloris afferunt: quia non sunt fortuiti, sed accersiti; nec infirmitati naturae tribuuntur, sed intemperantiae viribus. Praeterea cum potu suffocatur ratio, accenduntur pravi affectus. Unde Solomon Prov. XX, 1. Luxuriosa res vinum et tumultuosa III, 188 ebrietas: quisquis eo delectatur, non sapit. Et Prov. XXIII, 29. Cui vae? cui evoë? cui contentiones? cui loquacitas? cui sine causa vulnera? cui oculorum suffu- sio? Commorantibus in vino, etc. Et infra a), Oculi tui respicient alienas, et cor tuum loquetur perversa. Itaque hujusmodi poculis homines mutantur in bestias. Alii in leones: ut Alexander, qui Clytum, amicorum charissimum, occidit inter pocula; et Herodes, qui, e convivio excedens, Dei servum morti addixit. Alii, in canes: ut Nabal, qui, vino plenus, Davidem allatravit. Alii in porcos, qui volutantur in foedis voluptatibus: ut Lot, qui, potus, incesto concubitu cum filiabus se polluit. Denique per alia vitia imago Dei, per crapulam et ingluviem etiam hominis imago in nobis extinguitur. Quin abjicimur infra pecudes, quae non bibunt, nisi cum opus est, neque sitim arte excitant, sed naturam sequuntur ducem. Cum igitur tot modis perniciosa sit ebrietas, non immerito vinum col. 404 dicitur nos mordere ut serpens, et basilisci instar pungere. Punget Hebraice est יפרש, cum Sin dextro. Quae vox alibi disponere aut dissipare significat, hic pungere ex Chaldaica lingua (1), qua פרש peras est boum stimulus, et פריש paris est calcar. Unde Hebraeis etiam פרש paras eques, qui pungit equum calcaribus. Sed פרש Verbum cum Sin sinistro est extendere. Quo respiciens Hierony- mus reddidit, Sicut regulus venena diffundet: et Graeci, Ὥσπερ ὑπὸ κεράστου διαχεῖται αὐτοῦ ὁ ἰός· Tanquam a ceraste diffunditur ejus venenum (2). At Chaldaeus et Syrus hic habent, Ut basiliscus דפרח, qui volat; quasi legerint יפרח pro יפרש (3). Quod esset valde absurdum. Neque enim quisquam meminit volantis basilisci. Absit igitur a nobis, ut alas addamus tali monstro, quod vel sola reptitatione tam est terribile. Ex prima versione, Ultimum ejus mordebit, ut serpens, et punget ut basiliscus, duo hemistichia maxime sunt ἀν-τίστροφα. Nam, ut serpens et basiliscus, ita mordere et pungere fere idem sunt.

Now, if we may run through the passages of Scripture about this serpent, the first is Prov. col. 403 XXIII, 32., where Solomon says of wine, At the last it bites like a serpent, וכצפעני יפרש, and stings like a tsiphoni (or basilisk). This is not to favour Tatian, the Encratites and the Manichaeans, who called wine the gall of the dragon and the poison of the ancient serpent, and abstained from it even in the Holy Supper. For here it is not the use of wine but its abuse that is condemned. God by wine makes glad the heart of man, Ps 104:15. Indeed wine is said to cheer both God himself and men, Iud. IX, 13., namely in the sacrifices, in which it was used, Exod 29:40 and Num 15:5. Some, however, would have the word אלהים elohim in that passage signify not the true God but judges and magnates, as in Exod 21:6 and XXII, 8. 9. 28. and Ps 82:6; to these would be opposed אנשים, men, that is, commoners. In the same way, in Gen 6:2 the sons of men are opposed to the sons of God, and in Ps 82:6–7. אדם (that is, man) to gods and sons of the Most High. The sense would then be that by drinking wine rich and poor, princes and subjects, judges and clients are cheered alike. This is exactly what the first of Darius's bodyguards says of wine in 3 Ezra 3:19–20. However that may be, the yield of wine is ascribed to God's goodness and liberality, Jer 31:12 and Hos 2:8. And the Israelites are promised the early and the late rain, that they may gather in their grain and wine, Deut 11:14. And a cluster of grapes, brought into the wilderness, served them as a sample of the fertility of the land of Canaan, Num 13:24. Nor should it be said that what had been promised and permitted under the Law was forbidden under the Gospel. For Christ, invited to a wedding, changed water into wine to be offered to the guests, John 2:8. He himself drank wine many times with the Apostles, and at that Last Supper he even consecrated it to be the sacrament of his blood for the whole church. What is more, Paul recommends to Timothy the use of wine for his stomach's sake, 1 Tim. V, 23. But here a thing good in itself is condemned because of the evils of drunkenness. If anyone does not weigh these sufficiently, let him, like the boys of the Lacedaemonians, look at a drunken man: his disordered face, his tottering feet, his stammering tongue, his ungainly gestures, and his reason blinded and moved from its seat. Let him likewise consider the bleary eyes of gluttons, the nose suffused with bile, the rotten teeth, the foul breath, the trembling limbs, and the belly distended with fat; not to mention paralysis, gout, vertigo, convulsions, lethargy, apoplexy and a thousand swarms of diseases. However grave these are, they bring more disgrace than pain, because they are not accidental but self-inflicted, and are ascribed not to the weakness of nature but to the force of intemperance. Moreover, when reason is drowned in drink, the depraved passions are kindled. Hence Solomon, Prov 20:1: Wine is a luxurious thing, and drunkenness III, 188 riotous: whoever delights in it is not wise. And Prov 23:29: Who has woe? Who has evoë? Who has contentions? Who has babbling? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes? Those who tarry long at wine, etc. And below a): Your eyes shall look upon strange women, and your heart shall utter perverse things. Thus by such cups men are changed into beasts. Some into lions: like Alexander, who killed Clitus, the dearest of his friends, over the cups; and Herod, who, leaving a banquet, consigned a servant of God to death. Others into dogs: like Nabal, who, full of wine, barked at David. Others into swine, who wallow in foul pleasures: like Lot, who, when drunk, defiled himself by incestuous intercourse with his daughters. In short, by other vices the image of God is extinguished in us, but by drunkenness and gluttony even the image of man. Indeed, we are cast down below the cattle, which do not drink except when there is need, nor stir up thirst by art, but follow nature as their guide. Since, then, drunkenness is pernicious in so many ways, wine is not undeservedly col. 404 said to bite us like a serpent and to sting like a basilisk. Will sting in Hebrew is יפרש, with right-hand Sin. Elsewhere this word means to arrange or to scatter; here it means to sting, from the Chaldee language R1, in which פרש peras is an ox-goad and פריש paris is a spur. Hence among the Hebrews too פרש paras is a horseman, who pricks his horse with spurs. But the verb פרש with left-hand Sin means to spread out. With this in view Jerome rendered, As a regulus it will spread poisons, and the Greeks, Ὥσπερ ὑπὸ κεράστου διαχεῖται αὐτοῦ ὁ ἰός [Hōsper hypo kerastou diacheitai autou ho ios]: As by a cerastes its poison is spread R2. But the Chaldee and the Syriac have here, As a basilisk דפרח, which flies, as if they had read יפרח for יפרש R3. This would be quite absurd, for no one mentions a flying basilisk. Far be it from us, then, to add wings to such a monster, which is so terrible by its creeping alone. According to the first version, At the last it will bite like a serpent, and sting like a basilisk, the two hemistichs are thoroughly ἀντίστροφα [antistropha]. For as serpent and basilisk, so to bite and to sting are nearly the same. ¶

III, 189 Nempe serpens dicitur morsu pungere; quia dentes habet acutissimos. Varro apud Priscianum, Admota aspis cum pupugerit. Proinde morsus chersydri et draconis, in Nicandri Theriacis νύγμα dicitur, id est, punctio, versibus 362. et 446. ut Aeliani Historiae libri decimi quinti capite decimo tertio, haemorrhous morsu suo νύγμα ἐργάζεται, punctum excitat, etc. Et Arabica tria verba لدغ ladaga, لسع lasaa, et وكع vvachaa, cum proprie pungere significent, et de scorpione saepe usurpentur, tamen etiam ad serpentis morsum pertinent; quia punctionis est species.

III, 189 That is, a serpent is said to prick with its bite, because it has very sharp teeth. Varro in Priscian: When the asp, brought near, has pricked. Accordingly, the bite of the chersydrus and of the dragon is called νύγμα [nygma] in Nicander's Theriaca, that is, a pricking, at lines 362 and 446; just as in Aelian's History, book 15, chapter 13, the haemorrhous by its bite νύγμα ἐργάζεται [nygma ergazetai], produces a puncture, etc. And the three Arabic verbs لدغ [ladagha] ladaga, لسع [lasaʿa] lasaa, and وكع [wakaʿa] vvachaa, although they properly mean to prick and are often used of the scorpion, nevertheless also apply to the bite of a serpent, because it is a kind of pricking. ¶

Secundum locum Es. XI, 8. Oblectabit se infans super foramen aspidis, et super cavernam basilisci puer ablactatus manum mittit, jam ante b) explicavimus in capite De Aspide. Proinde hic solum observa, cavernam basilisci dici Hebraice מאורת צפעוני, et vocem מאורת, pro caverna alibi nusquam occurrere. Unde est, quod in ejus versione laborant interpretes. Et recentiores quidem, cum Hieronymo, cavernam reddunt: sed Graeci κοίτην, ut Arabs مضجع, cubile, quasi pro מאורת meürath legerint מעונת meonath, quod κοίτην exponunt Iob. XXXVII, 8. et XXXVIII, 40. et Ierem. X, 22. Chaldaeo rursus מאורת est plane aliud, nimirum pupilla oculi, tanquam a Radice אור, quae lucidum esse, aut illuminari significat. Unde nata illa paraphrasis, על חיור גלגלי עיני חיוי חרמן חסילא ידוהי יושיט, Super aspectum (id est, superficiem) pupillae oculi basilisci ablactatus manum suam mittet. Hinc Aben-Ezra, מאורת העין שהיא בעלת האור Meürath, oculus, quia luce est praeditus. Nempe, ut hic habet Kimchius, cum serpens est in foramine suo, et in tenebroso illo loco, lucet pupilla oculi ejus, puer, lumen videns forinsecus, et lapidem pretiosum aut crystallum esse putans, manum extendit, ut hoc capiat. Haec ille ex mente Chaldaei interpretis. Sed idem ex sua מאורת, cavernam dici vult ab אור, seu luce, quam per foramen admittit. Quomodo Iud. VI, 2. Israelitae dicuntur Madianitarum metu sibi fecisse in montibus את המנהרות, id est, specus, sic dictos propter fenestram, per quam נהרה lux ingreditur. In Selomone R. Menachem confert cum voce אור ur, quam vallem explicant Gen. XI, 28. et Es. XXIV, 15. Ego cum Arabico أار aar, quod Arabes, apud Giggeium, speluncam interpretantur. Nempe illud آر ad Radicem أور referri, docet noster Golius. Itaque آر et מאורה sunt voces ejusdem originis, quantumvis diversae formae (4).

The second passage, Isa 11:8. The infant shall delight itself over the hole of the asp, and the weaned child puts his hand upon the den of the basilisk, we have already explained earlier b) in the chapter On the Asp. Here, therefore, note only that the den of the basilisk is called in Hebrew מאורת צפעוני, and that the word מאורת occurs nowhere else for den. That is why translators struggle with rendering it. The more recent ones, with Jerome, render it den; but the Greeks have κοίτην [koitēn], as the Arabic has مضجع [maḍjaʿ], lair, as if they had read מעונת meonath instead of מאורת meürath, which they render κοίτην [koitēn] in Job 37:8 and XXXVIII, 40. and Jer 10:22. For the Chaldee, again, מאורת is something quite different, namely the pupil of the eye, as if from the root אור, which means to be bright or to be illuminated. Hence arose that paraphrase, על חיור גלגלי עיני חיוי חרמן חסילא ידוהי יושיט, Upon the sight (that is, the surface) of the pupil of the eye of the basilisk the weaned child shall put his hand. Hence Aben-Ezra: מאורת העין שהיא בעלת האור, Meürath, the eye, because it is endowed with light. That is, as Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] has it here: when the serpent is in its hole, and in that dark place the pupil of its eye shines, the child, seeing the light from outside and thinking it a precious stone or crystal, stretches out his hand to take it. So he, following the mind of the Chaldee translator. But the same author, in his own view, holds that מאורת, den, is named from אור, or light, which it admits through its opening; just as in Iud. VI, 2. the Israelites are said, out of fear of the Midianites, to have made for themselves in the mountains את המנהרות, that is, caves, so called because of the window through which נהרה, light, enters. In Selomoh, R. Menachem compares it with the word אור ur, which they explain as valley in Gen 11:28 and Isa 24:15. I compare it with the Arabic أار [aʾār] aar, which the Arabs, in Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)], interpret as cave. For our Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)] teaches that آر [ār] is to be referred to the root أور [ʾawr]. Thus آر [ār] and מאורה are words of the same origin, however different their form R4. ¶

III, 190 III. Tertius locus est Es. XIV, 29. (al. XV, 2.) Ne laetare tu, Palaestina tota, quod confracta fuerit virga percutientis te. Nam e radice serpentis exit צפע, basilicus. Rex Ozias (sub quo Esaias ad munus propheticum a Deo vocatus col. 405 fuerat Es. I, 1.) Palaestinos ad tempus gravi bello divexaverat, dirutis Gethae, Iabniae et Azoti moenibus, atque urbibus in media Palaestina aedificatis, 2 Paral. XXVI, 6. Sed, cum sacerdotale munus usurpare voluisset, ab regno abdicatus in domo semota obierat: et successerant Iothamus et Achazus, sub quo Palaestini animos et vires resumpserant, et irruerant in campestria et meridiana Iudaeae oppida, ceperantque Bethsamem, Ajalonem, Gaderotha, Sochonem, Thamnam et Gamzonem, cum vicinis pagis, ibique consederant. Iudaeos enim depresserat Dominus propter Achazi impietatem, 2 Par. XXVIII, 18. 19. Sed illo ipso anno, quo obiit Achazus (Es. XIV, 28.) Esaias, Deo plenus, magnam praedicit rerum mutationem, brevi futuram, sub Achazi successore, Ezechia, qui Palaestinos Gazam usque subegit, 2 Reg. XVIII, 8. Proinde hic dicit Propheta Sanctus, Ne laetare tu, Palaestina tota, quod confracta fuerit virga percutientis te: id est, Vos omnes, Palaestini, qui olim exultavistis propter Oziae, regis, qui vos male mulctaverat, abdicationem et mortem, et sub Achazo ausi estis in nostros insurgere; ne laetamini amplius: nam e radice serpentis, id est, e stirpe Oziae, qui serpentis instar vos pupugerat, exit alius serpens, Ezechias scilicet, isque unus e gravissimis, basiliscus, quem ipso Ozia graviorem et infensiorem experiemini maximo vestro malo. Unde Hieronymus in hunc locum, Ne laeteris, o Philistaea, ne insultes populo meo, etc. quod baculus, qui te ferire solitus erat, videatur morte contritus, quod coluber interfectus. Pro hoc enim nocentior nascetur Ezechias, regulus, quem Graeci vocant basiliscum, qui te suo aspectu interficiat, et spiritu oris occidat. Quomodo enim nulla avis potest reguli aspectum illaesa transire, sed, quamvis procul fuerit, ejus ore sorbetur, ita et a conspectu regis Ezechiae totus peribis. Et pulchre servavit metaphoram; quia enim serpentem et regulum nominarat, ore ejus et spiritu dicit volucres devorari. Nempe in Hieronymi versione sic habet integer versus, quem tractamus; De radice enim colubri egredietur regulus, et semen ejus absorbens volucrem. Cujus ul-tima verba vir sanctus accommodat ad basilisci historiam, de quo Solinus: Ipsas etiam corrumpit auras, ita, ut aëra nulla alitum impune transvolet, infectum spiritu pestilenti. Et ex illo Isidorus, Ad ejus aspectum nulla avis volans illaesa transit: sed, quamvis procul sit, ejus ore combusta devoratur. Et Avicenna, انّ حاني مسكنها طاير سقط Cadit avis quandocunque est e regione latibuli ejus. Et Alkazuinius, ان طار طاير فوقها سقط عليها Si avis supervolat, in illum cadit. Ita etiam Damir. Sed haec non sunt admodum certa, neque ante Solinum, qui non est optimus auctor, a quoquam III, 191 tradita, quod meminerim. Et Esaias frustra profertur, cum Hebraea sic habeant, ut in Notis agnoscit ipse Hieronymus, ופריו שרף מעופף, Et fructus ejus serpens (aut potius hydrus) volans, quae infra explicabuntur. Quisquis igitur illa verba primus ita reddidit, Et semen ejus absorbens volucrem, pro שרף legit שואף, quod absorbentem sonat. Verum enimvero nihil opus est hic operose causas conquirere, cur cum serpente aut basilisco conferatur rex Ezechias, cum non sit novum, ut rex potens per serpentem adumbretur. Ita enim apud Aegyptios, qui Palaestinis vicini erant, scribit Aelianus Historiae libri sexti capite trigesimo octavo, Τοὺς βασιλεῖς ἐπὶ τῶν διαδημάτων φορεῖν πεποικιλμένας ἀσπίδας, τῆς ἀρχῆς αἰνιττομένους τὸ ἀνίκητον, Reges in diadematibus gessisse pictas aspides, quae invictam imperii potentiam significarent. Quia scilicet τῶν ὑπ᾽ ἀσπίδος δηχθέντων οὐ μνημονεύεται οὐδεὶς ἐξάντης γεγονέναι τοῦ κακοῦ, eorum, qui ab aspide morsi sunt, nemo unquam evasisse legitur. Et Hori libri pri- col. 406 mi capite quinquagesimo sexto, Βασιλέα δὲ κάκισον (lege κράτισον) δηλοῦντες, ὄφιν ζωγραφοῦσι κοσμοειδῶς ἐσχηματισμένον, οὗ τὴν οὐρὰν ἐν τῷ στόματι ποιοῦσι· Ut regem potentissimum significent, serpentem pingunt cum cauda in ore in orbis figuram: nempe ut illum innuerent toti terrarum orbi imperare. Lege etiam capita, quinquagesimum septimum, et quinquagesimum octavum, et sexagesimum primum: in quibus omnibus per serpentem, vel vigilantem, vel inflexum, vel dimidium, vel integrum, regum variae virtutes aut officia significantur. Sed in omni serpentum genere basiliscus maxime est figura regis. Id enim Arabico ملكة melecha, Graeco βασιλίσκου, et Latino REGULI nomine significatur. Nempe illum ἑρπηστῶν βασιλῆα, reptilium regem, vocat Nicander Theriacῶν versu 397. Et Plinius describit candida in capite macula, ut quodam diademate insignem. Et Solinus, alba quasi mitrula lineatum caput. Galenus quoque et Aëtius, θηρίον ἐπὶ τῇ κεφαλῇ τρεῖς ὑπεροχὰς ἔχον, Serpentem, in capite tres habentem eminentias. Et Avicenna, مكللة الراس capite coronato. Et Damir, علي راسه خطوط بيض مثل التاج albas in capite lineas habentem coronae instar. Et Alkazuinius, قال جالينوس انها حية شقرا علي راسها ثلاث قنازع مثل التاج Dixit Galenus, anguem esse rufum, et super caput tres habere cristas in coronae modum (5). Illum addunt, non, reliquos, toto corpore III, 192 pronum serpere, sed erecto capite et media parte corporis. III, 190 III. The third passage is Isa 14:29 (var. XV, 2.): Rejoice not, thou whole Palestine, because the rod of him that smote thee is broken. For out of the serpent's root shall come forth צפע, a basilisk. King Uzziah (under whom Isaiah had been called by God to the prophetic office, col. 405 Isa 1:1) had for a time harassed the Philistines with a grievous war, tearing down the walls of Gath, Jabneh and Ashdod, and building cities in the midst of Philistia, 2 Chr 26:6. But when he tried to usurp the priestly office, he was removed from the kingship and died in a separate house. He was succeeded by Jotham and Ahaz. Under Ahaz the Philistines recovered their spirit and strength, invaded the towns of the lowland and the south of Judah, and took Beth-shemesh, Aijalon, Gederoth, Socho, Timnah and Gimzo with the neighbouring villages, and settled there. For the Lord had brought the Jews low because of the impiety of Ahaz, 2 Chr 28:18–19. But in the very year in which Ahaz died (Isa 14:28), Isaiah, full of God, foretells a great reversal of affairs soon to come under Ahaz's successor, Hezekiah, who subdued the Philistines as far as Gaza, 2 Reg. XVIII, 8. Accordingly the holy Prophet says here, Rejoice not, thou whole Palestine, because the rod of him that smote thee is broken; that is: All you Philistines, who once exulted at the deposition and death of King Uzziah, who had punished you severely, and who under Ahaz dared to rise against our people, rejoice no longer. For out of the serpent's root, that is, from the stock of Uzziah, who had stung you like a serpent, comes forth another serpent, namely Hezekiah, and he one of the most dangerous kind, a basilisk, whom to your very great harm you will find more grievous and more hostile than Uzziah himself. Hence Jerome on this passage: Rejoice not, O Philistia, do not insult my people, etc., because the staff that used to strike thee seems broken by death, because the snake has been killed. For in his place will be born a more harmful one, Hezekiah, a regulus, which the Greeks call basilisk, who will kill thee with his look and slay thee with the breath of his mouth. For just as no bird can pass the regulus's gaze unharmed, but, however far off it may be, is swallowed by its mouth, so thou too shalt wholly perish at the sight of King Hezekiah. And he has kept the metaphor beautifully: because he had named a serpent and a regulus, he says that birds are devoured by its mouth and breath. For in Jerome's version the whole verse we are discussing reads thus: For from the root of the snake shall come forth a regulus, and its seed swallowing the bird. The holy man applies these last words to the story of the basilisk, of which Solinus says: It corrupts even the very air, so that no bird flies through the air with impunity, infected as it is by its pestilential breath. And from him Isidore: At its look no flying bird passes unharmed; but, however far off it is, it is burned and devoured by its mouth. And Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)]: انّ حاني مسكنها طاير سقط [inna ḥānī maskanihā ṭāyir saqaṭa] A bird falls whenever it is opposite its lair. And Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: ان طار طاير فوقها سقط عليها [in ṭāra ṭāyir fawqahā saqaṭa ʿalayhā] If a bird flies over it, it falls upon it. So also Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]. But these things are not very certain, nor, as far as I remember, were they reported by anyone before Solinus, who is not the best of authors. III, 191 And Isaiah is cited for this in vain, since the Hebrew reads, as Jerome himself acknowledges in his Notes, ופריו שרף מעופף, And its fruit a flying serpent (or rather hydrus), which will be explained below. Whoever, then, first rendered those words And its seed swallowing the bird read שואף, which means swallowing, instead of שרף. But in truth there is no need here to search laboriously for reasons why King Hezekiah is compared with a serpent or a basilisk, since it is nothing new for a powerful king to be figured by a serpent. Thus among the Egyptians, who were neighbours of the Philistines, Aelian writes in History, book 6, chapter 38: Τοὺς βασιλεῖς ἐπὶ τῶν διαδημάτων φορεῖν πεποικιλμένας ἀσπίδας, τῆς ἀρχῆς αἰνιττομένους τὸ ἀνίκητον [Tous basileis epi tōn diadēmatōn phorein pepoikilmenas aspidas, tēs archēs ainittomenous to anikēton], that the kings wore painted asps on their diadems, to signify the invincible power of their rule; because, namely, τῶν ὑπ᾽ ἀσπίδος δηχθέντων οὐ μνημονεύεται οὐδεὶς ἐξάντης γεγονέναι τοῦ κακοῦ [tōn hyp᾽ aspidos dēchthentōn ou mnēmoneuetai oudeis exantēs gegonenai tou kakou], of those bitten by an asp no one is ever recorded to have escaped. And Horapollo, book 1, col. 406 chapter 56: Βασιλέα δὲ κάκισον (read κράτισον) δηλοῦντες, ὄφιν ζωγραφοῦσι κοσμοειδῶς ἐσχηματισμένον, οὗ τὴν οὐρὰν ἐν τῷ στόματι ποιοῦσι· [Basilea de kakison (kratison) dēlountes, ophin zōgraphousi kosmoeidōs eschēmatismenon, hou tēn ouran en tō stomati poiousi] To signify a most powerful king, they paint a serpent with its tail in its mouth in the shape of a circle: namely, to indicate that he rules the whole circle of the earth. Read also chapters 57, 58 and 61, in all of which various virtues or duties of kings are signified by a serpent, whether watchful, coiled, half or whole. But of all kinds of serpents the basilisk is above all the figure of a king. This is signified by the Arabic name ملكة [malika] melecha, the Greek βασιλίσκου [basiliskou], and the Latin REGULUS. Thus Nicander, Theriaca line 397, calls it ἑρπηστῶν βασιλῆα [herpēstōn basilēa], king of reptiles. And Pliny describes it as marked with a white spot on the head, as if distinguished by a kind of diadem. And Solinus: its head lined with white as if with a little mitre. Galen too and Aëtius: θηρίον ἐπὶ τῇ κεφαλῇ τρεῖς ὑπεροχὰς ἔχον [thērion epi tē kephalē treis hyperochas echon], a serpent having three prominences on its head. And Avicenna: مكللة الراس [mukallalat al-rās] with crowned head. And Damir: علي راسه خطوط بيض مثل التاج [ʿalī rāsihi khuṭūṭ bīḍ mithl al-tāj] having white lines on its head like a crown. And Alkazuinius: قال جالينوس انها حية شقرا علي راسها ثلاث قنازع مثل التاج [qāla Jālīnūs innahā ḥayya shaqrā ʿalī rāsihā thalāth qanāziʿ mithl al-tāj] Galen said that it is a reddish snake, and has on its head three crests in the manner of a crown R5. They add that it does not, like the others, crawl prone with its whole body, III, 192 but with its head and the middle part of its body raised. Pliny: It does not propel its body with manifold coils like the other serpents, but advances lofty and erect in its middle. Solinus: When it moves, it crawls with the middle part of its body, while the other half is upright and lofty. As if this were the emblem of kingship, as it was for the kings of the Persians τὴν τιάραν ὀρθὴν ἔχειν [tēn tiaran orthēn echein]. Moreover, every kind of reptile yields to it, however small it is, as though acknowledging it as king; for it puts them to flight with its hiss, as we showed above c), ¶

— — — late sibi submovet omne
Vulgus, et in vacua regnat basiliscus arena.

— — — far and wide it drives off the whole
crowd, and the basilisk reigns over the empty sand. ¶

Ita Lucanus libro nono: quod pluribus explicant Nicander, et apud Aelianum Archelaus. Quin et deorum capitibus hoc animal inserebatur, tanquam μόνον ἀθάνατον, solum immortale, id est, omnium sui generis longe vivacissimum: et, quia quodvis aliud animal solo flatu perimit, videbatur ζωῆς καὶ θανάτου κυριεύειν, habere jus vitae et necis. Quae ideo hic observo, quia non solum Ezechiae, regis, sed et Christi θεανθρώπου symbolum esse creditur basiliscus. Proinde locum, in quo versamur, Ionathan sic παραφράζει· Ne laetemini, vos omnes, Philistaei, quia contritum est dominium, cui serviebatis; quia de filiis fili- orum Iesse exibit Messias. In quibus verbis Messiam pro basilisco poni constat, quasi basilisci nomine Christum Propheta intellexerit: ut multis ante saeculis il-lum significaverat Moses per serpentem, in deserto erectum. Ibidem Iesse est pro voce נחש nahas, quae serpentem sonat. Neque id sine causa. Nam, ut observat Kimchius, Davidis pater, Iesse, binomius fuit: neque solum Iesse, sed et נחש Nahas dictus est. Proinde 2 Sam. XVII, 25. legitur Abigail filia fuisse τοῦ Nahas, qui idem est cum Iesse. Itaque in Esaiae verbis, Ex radice τοῦ נחש nahas exibit basiliscus, intellexit Chaldaeus paraphrastes Messiam, nasciturum ex stirpe Iessae.

So Lucan in book 9; this is set out at greater length by Nicander, and by Archelaus in Aelian. Indeed, this animal was even placed on the heads of the gods, as μόνον ἀθάνατον [monon athanaton], alone immortal, that is, by far the longest-lived of all of its kind; and, because it kills any other animal by its breath alone, it seemed ζωῆς καὶ θανάτου κυριεύειν [zōēs kai thanatou kyrieuein], to hold the power of life and death. I note this here because the basilisk is believed to be a symbol not only of King Hezekiah but also of Christ the θεανθρώπου [theanthrōpou, God-man]. Accordingly Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases the passage we are dealing with thus: Do not rejoice, all of you, Philistines, because the dominion that you served has been broken; for from the sons of the sons of Jesse the Messiah will come forth. In these words it is clear that Messiah is put for basilisk, as if the Prophet had meant Christ by the name basilisk, just as many centuries earlier Moses had signified him by the serpent raised up in the desert. In the same place Jesse stands for the word נחש nahas, which means serpent. And not without reason. For, as Kimchi observes, David's father Jesse had two names: he was called not only Jesse but also נחש Nahas. Hence in 2 Sam 17:25 we read that Abigail was the daughter of Nahas, who is the same as Jesse. Therefore in Isaiah's words, From the root of נחש nahas a basilisk will come forth, the Chaldee paraphrast understood the Messiah, who was to be born from the stock of Jesse. ¶

IV. Quartus locus est Es. LIX, 5. ביצי צפעני בקעו, Ova basilisci excluse- runt. Hinc in malam partem sumitur, ut antea in bonam. Nam ova basilisci sunt crimina et scelera, quae, cogitatione concepta, per opera excluduntur. Proinde ad hunc locum Kimchius, Cogitationes et opera eorum cum ovis basilisci col. 407 confert, ex quibus exclusis exeunt basilisci, quomodo excluduntur eorum consilia et opera mala. Et paullo post, Ut ovum pulli, quod is rostro fodicat, testam paullatim III, 193 frangens, donec in lucem exeat: ita illi jugiter haerent in malis cogitationibus, donec ad opus illas perduxerint. Sequitur in eodem Esaiae loco, Qui comedit ex ovis eorum, morietur: et, si quis ea comprimat, excludetur אפעה, vipera. Itaque in eodem versu basilisci et viperae ovis comparantur eadem facta. Unde tamen non sequitur, quod vult Kimchius, אפעה et צפעני ejusdem serpentis esse nomina. Quin צפעני est basiliscus, אפעה, vipera, ut probatum est hactenus. Sed id in Scriptura tralatitium est, ut per varias serpentum species res eadem significetur. Sic Deut. XXXII, 33. Fel draconum est vinum illorum, et venenum aspidum crudele. Iob. XX, 16. Fel aspidum suget, et occidet eum lingua viperae. Psal. XCI, 13. Super aspidem et basiliscum ambulabis, etc. Es. XI, 8. Oblectabitur infans super foramen aspidis, et super basilisci cavernam, etc. Es. XIV, 29. Ex stirpe serpentis exibit basiliscus, et fructus ejus erit hydrus volans. Quod notandum in Gregorium Nazianzenum, qui sub finem Irenici secundi sic agit, quasi ex urino aspidis ovo posset edi basiliscus. Ibi enim ita habet scriptor elegantissimus, Ἐπειδ᾽ ἂν τὰ ὠὰ τῶν ἀσπίδων ῥήξαντες, ἢ συντρίψαντες λόγῳ σκληρῷ, καὶ ἀντιτύπῳ, οὔρια καὶ ἀνεμιαῖα ταῦτα ἐλέγχωμεν, καὶ τὸν ἐν αὐτοῖς τῆς ἀσεβείας κρυπτόμενον βασιλίσκον εἰς μέσον ἀγάγωμεν· Cum ruptis, aut solida disputatione contritis, aspidum ovis, urina ea et subventanea esse demonstraveri- mus, abditumque in ipsis impietatis basiliscum in medium protulerimus. Alludens scilicet ad haec Esaiae juxta Graecam editionem, Ὠὰ ἀσπίδων ἔῤῥηξαν, etc. καὶ ὁ μέλλων τῶν ὠῶν αὐτῶν φαγεῖν, συντρίψας οὔριον, εὗρεν καὶ ἐν αὐτῷ βασι-λίσκον. In quibus tamen illud αὐτῶν non refertur ad aspides, sed ad impios il-los, quorum opera priore hemistichio aspidum, posteriore basiliscorum ovis comparantur.

IV. The fourth passage is Isa 59:5. ביצי צפעני בקעו, They have hatched the eggs of the basilisk. Here it is taken in a bad sense, as before in a good one. For the eggs of the basilisk are crimes and wicked deeds, which, conceived in thought, are hatched through deeds. Accordingly, on this passage Kimchi says: He compares their thoughts and deeds with the eggs of the basilisk, from which, when hatched, basilisks come forth, just as their evil counsels and col. 407 deeds are hatched. And a little later: Like the egg of a chick, which pecks at it with its beak, breaking the shell little by little III, 193 until it comes out into the light: so they cling continually to evil thoughts until they have brought them to action. There follows in the same passage of Isaiah: Whoever eats of their eggs will die; and if one presses them, a אפעה, viper, will be hatched. Thus in the same verse the same deeds are compared to the eggs of the basilisk and of the viper. Yet it does not follow from this, as Kimchi would have it, that אפעה and צפעני are names of the same serpent. On the contrary, צפעני is the basilisk and אפעה the viper, as has been proved so far. But it is customary in Scripture for the same thing to be signified by various species of serpents. So Deut 32:33. Their wine is the gall of dragons, and the cruel poison of asps. Job 20:16 He will suck the gall of asps, and the tongue of the viper will kill him. Ps 91:13 You will walk upon the asp and the basilisk, etc. Isa 11:8 The infant will play over the hole of the asp, and over the den of the basilisk, etc. Isa 14:29 From the stock of the serpent a basilisk will come forth, and its fruit will be a flying hydrus. This should be noted against Gregory of Nazianzus, who toward the end of the Second Irenic Oration writes as if a basilisk could be produced from an addled egg of an asp. For there that most elegant writer says: Ἐπειδ᾽ ἂν τὰ ὠὰ τῶν ἀσπίδων ῥήξαντες, ἢ συντρίψαντες λόγῳ σκληρῷ, καὶ ἀντιτύπῳ, οὔρια καὶ ἀνεμιαῖα ταῦτα ἐλέγχωμεν, καὶ τὸν ἐν αὐτοῖς τῆς ἀσεβείας κρυπτόμενον βασιλίσκον εἰς μέσον ἀγάγωμεν [Epeid' an ta ōa tōn aspidōn rhēxantes, ē syntripsantes logō sklērō, kai antitypō, ouria kai anemiaia tauta elenchōmen, kai ton en autois tēs asebeias kryptomenon basiliskon eis meson agagōmen]; When, having broken the eggs of asps or crushed them by solid argument, we have shown them to be addled and wind-eggs, and have brought into the open the basilisk of impiety hidden within them. He is alluding, namely, to these words of Isaiah according to the Greek edition: Ὠὰ ἀσπίδων ἔῤῥηξαν [Ōa aspidōn errhēxan], etc. καὶ ὁ μέλλων τῶν ὠῶν αὐτῶν φαγεῖν, συντρίψας οὔριον, εὗρεν καὶ ἐν αὐτῷ βασιλίσκον [kai ho mellōn tōn ōōn autōn phagein, syntripsas ourion, heuren kai en autō basiliskon]. In these words, however, that αὐτῶν [autōn] does not refer to the asps but to those wicked men whose deeds are compared in the first hemistich to the eggs of asps, in the second to the eggs of basilisks. ¶

V. Ultimus de basilisco locus est Ierem. VIII, 17. Nam ego in vos immit- tam serpentes basiliscos, in quos nulla est incantatio, qui mordebunt vos, dicit Do- minus. Ubi Chaldaeus, Nam ego in vos excitabo עממיא קטולין כחוין חרמנין דלית להון לחשא, populos occidentes, sicut serpentes regulos, in quos nulla est incantatio. Theodoretus hostes intelligit, qui ταῖς ἡμετέραις οὐκ εἴκουσι παρα-κλήσεσιν, nostris obtestationibus non cedunt, Hieronymus serpentem antiquum, et adversarias potestates, quibus tradentur, qui Dei praecepta contempserint. Nos potius Assyrios, quibus Israelitae proxime erant tradendi. Secunda editio pro basiliscis habuit σκοποὺς, speculatores, id est, צפים, pro צפענים. Serpentem quemvis nugatur R. Selomo, postquam vixit annos septuaginta, fieri צפע, ba- siliscum, et tum obturare aurem, ne incantatorem audiat. Quod probat ex Psal. LVIII, 5. ubi tamen de צפע, seu basilisco, nihil. Sed פתן, aspis aurem obturare d) legitur, etc. quod abunde supra e) explicatum.

V. The last passage on the basilisk is Jer 8:17. For I will send among you serpents, basilisks, against which there is no charm, and they shall bite you, says the Lord. Here the Chaldee has: For I will stir up against you עממיא קטולין כחוין חרמנין דלית להון לחשא, murderous peoples, like serpents, reguli, against which there is no charm. Theodoret understands this of enemies who ταῖς ἡμετέραις οὐκ εἴκουσι παρακλήσεσιν [tais hēmeterais ouk eikousi paraklēsesin], do not yield to our entreaties; Jerome understands it of the ancient serpent and of the hostile powers to whom those who have despised God's commandments will be handed over. We prefer to understand it of the Assyrians, to whom the Israelites were soon to be handed over. In place of basilisks the second edition had σκοποὺς [skopous], watchmen, that is, צפים instead of צפענים. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] idly claims that any serpent, once it has lived seventy years, becomes a צפע, a basilisk, and then stops its ear so as not to hear the charmer. He proves this from Ps 58:5, where, however, there is nothing about the צפע or basilisk. Rather, what we read there is that the פתן, the asp, stops its ear d), etc., which has been fully explained above e). ¶
