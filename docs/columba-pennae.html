De Columbae pennis, collo, oculis, gemitu et volatu.

On the dove's feathers, neck, eyes, moaning and flight. ¶

Columbae descriptionem a plumis et pennis ordiri lubet, quarum pulchritudi- col. 9 nem sacer vates ita commendat Psal. LXVIII, 14. Alae columbae argento obductae, et pennae ejus pallore auri. Alae et pennae per συνεκδοχὴν hic pro toto corpore. Argento obductae, id est, argenti instar summe albae, et cum splendore quodam candidae. Quippe argentum Graece ἀργύριον dicitur παρὰ τὸ ἀργὸν, τὸ λευκόν· et argentea vocantur, quae maxime candent. Unde poetae ἀργυρόπεζα Θέτις, id est, λευκόπους. Ita est in Hesychio, qui idem ἀργυρο-δίνην κρήνην explicat λευκὰ ῥεύματα ἔχουσαν, et Aeneidos libro octavo,

I am pleased to begin the description of the dove with its plumage and feathers, whose beauty col. 9 the sacred poet commends thus, Ps 68:14: The wings of the dove covered with silver, and its feathers with the paleness of gold. Wings and feathers stand here by συνεκδοχὴν [synekdochēn] for the whole body. Covered with silver, that is, supremely white like silver, and bright with a certain splendour. For silver is called in Greek ἀργύριον [argyrion], from τὸ ἀργὸν, τὸ λευκόν [to argon, to leukon, "the bright, the white"]; and things that are most brilliantly white are called silver. Hence in the poets ἀργυρόπεζα Θέτις [argyropeza Thetis], that is, λευκόπους [leukopous]. So it is in Hesychius, who likewise explains ἀργυροδίνην κρήνην [argyrodinēn krēnēn] as λευκὰ ῥεύματα ἔχουσαν [leuka rheumata echousan]; and in the eighth book of the Aeneid, ¶

— — — — argenteus anser,

— — — — the silver goose, ¶

teste Servio est epithetum coloris. Corvus quoque tum ales erat argentea, cum columbas aequabat albedine. Ovidius Metam. lib. 2. Fab. 7.

Paleness of gold, that is, gold of paleness, or pale gold. Similar is Hab 2:6. עב טיט, thickness of mud, for mud of thickness, or thick mud. התואר במקום המתואר, the predicate for the subject, as Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] has it here. Gold is called חרוץ charuts, that is, dug out of the earth, here and in Zech 9:3 and frequently in Solomon's Proverbs R2. From this comes the Greek χρυσός [chrysos]. And the paleness of gold is ירקרק, with doubled letters, so that the meaning is intensified. Accordingly from this very word comes the Ethiopic ወርቅ፡ [warəq] vvark for gold. The Greek translators rightly call the paleness of gold χλωρότητα χρυσίου [chlōrotēta chrysiou]. For this is the true colour of gold. Hence Diogenes, asked Διὰ τί τὸ χρυσίον χλωρόν ἐστιν; [Dia ti to chrysion chlōron estin?] Why II, 535 is gold pale?, answered Ὅτι πολλοὺς ἔχει τοὺς ἐπιβουλεύοντας [Hoti pollous echei tous epibouleuontas], Because it has many plotting against it. Now, if you ask why this golden colour is attributed to doves, Kimchi answers: שיש בכנפי היונה נוצה שהמצהבת כזהב, In the wings of doves there is plumage yellow like gold: namely in the wings of the younger birds, after they have left the nest. The Talmudists observe the same of turtledoves, and say that from this one learns when they are fit or unfit for sacrifice. For since the Law commands that of doves only the young, and of turtledoves only the adults, be offered, they hold that the former become unfit for sacrifice, and the latter fit, precisely when the feathers begin to turn yellow like gold. The words are in the tractate Cholin, chapter 1, folio 22b: מאימתי התורים כשרים משיצהיבו מאימתי בני יונה פסולים משיצהיבו When are turtledoves (for offering) fit? And, so that the Argo may be believed to have been a very swift ship, the poets describe it as quick ὅσον πελειάδος [hoson peleiados], like a dove. The Arabs and Hebrews agree with this. Thus Damir, after teaching that الشهرمان والشاهين [al-shahramān wa-l-shāhīn], the saharman and the sahin, species of hawk, are most hostile to the dove, says: وهو اطير منهما ومن ساير الطير كلها [wa-huwa aṭyar minhumā wa-min sāyir al-ṭayr kullihā] And it surpasses them in flight, and all birds whatsoever. Indeed, in the twelfth century after Christ, when Aben Ezra wrote, this was much practised. Paulus [=H. E. G. Under its wings was found a letter written in Arabic, both in words and in letters. And in book 5: While they were encamped before the walls (of Tyre), some authors say that a dove flew up. When our men, taught by experience, recognized it as a letter-carrier, so great a shout was raised by the land and naval forces of the Latins that the bird, as if driven by a whirlwind of the disturbed air, fell to the ground. The letter taken from it, written in barbarian characters to the Tyrians, said that help would soon come, that they should be of good courage and endure the siege a few days. Our men, on the other hand, had another letter written in the hand and language of those barbarians whom they held in their power etc. and sent the bird back with the forged and deceitful letter, telling the besieged to take counsel for themselves according to their present need, etc. that no hope of help near at hand could be held out, etc. The Tyrians yielded to the trick. Marmol, in his Africa, book 2, chapter 31, refers this to the year 1123. On the same matter see Jacques de Vitry, Histor. Orient. book 1; Arnold of Lübeck in the History of the Slavs; Vincent in the Doctrinale book 16, ch. 154; and Wildebrand of Oldenburg in the Itinerarium Hierosolymitanum, where he wrongly ascribes to the people of Tripoli what others attribute to the Tyrians. But older histories too offer us examples of the same art. For, to pass over fables, such as what the Samaritans write in chapter 20 of their spurious book of Joshua, that after defeating the five kings he informed Eleazar of this victory by a letter tied to the wings of birds, and in chapter 34 that he sent a letter to Nebichus by the service of a dove; and what we read in Aelian, History, book 6, chapter 7, that Mares, king of Egypt, used a crow to carry letters wherever he wished: to pass over these, I say, since they are plainly fabulous, why should we not believe what is found in Aelian's Varia, book 9, chapter 2, that Taurosthenes, the Ὀλυμπιονίκην [Olympionikēn], announced his victory to his father in Aegina from Olympia on the same day by a dove as messenger? They say, namely, περιστερὰν τὸν Ταυροσθένην ἐπάγεσθαι ἀπολιποῦσαν τοὺς ἑαυτῆς νεοσσοὺς ὑγροὺς ἔτι, καὶ ἀπτῆνας, νικήσαντα, καὶ ἀφεῖναι τὴν πελειάδα, προσάψαντα πορφύραν αὐτῇ· τὴν δὲ ἐπειγομένην πρὸς τοὺς νεοττοὺς, ἀπαυθημερῆσαι ἐκ Πίσσης εἰς Αἴγιναν· [peristeran ton Taurosthenēn epagesthai apolipousan tous heautēs neossous hygrous eti, kai aptēnas, nikēsanta, kai apheinai tēn peleiada, prosapsanta porphyran autē; tēn de epeigomenēn pros tous neottous, apauthēmerēsai ek Pissēs eis Aiginan] that Taurosthenes took with him a dove taken away from its chicks, still tender and unfledged; and when he had won, he sent it back with a piece of purple tied to it, and it, hastening with all speed to its chicks, flew on the same day from Pisa to Aegina. Pliny and Frontinus report that something similar happened at the siege of Mutina, although they hardly agree with each other. For the latter, in book 3 of the Stratagems, writes that Hirtius sent letters, tied with a bristle to a dove's neck, to Decimus Brutus, besieged in Mutina by Antony; the former, in book 10, chapter 37, says that doves were messengers in great affairs, with letters attached to their feet, at the siege of Mutina, when D. Brutus sent them to the consuls' camp. But in the same siege II, 544 the same Brutus could have both received and sent back letters by the service of doves. This art, which had nearly died out in our fathers' time, the Dutch revived. So too did the Turks: Linschoten, in his description of Ormuz, chapter 6, writes that their emperor learns within a short time by means of doves what is happening in the most remote parts of his empire. Accordingly, in the book On Animals, which Calonymus translated from the Arabic of Abulsaphe into Hebrew in the year of Christ 1316, it is said of the dove: היונה המסולסל הוא זה המגביה לעוף נושה כתבים ואגרות מארץ אל ארץ לחבונתו, The dove, raising itself on high, that is, which is exalted in its flight, when by its intelligence it carries letters from place to place. Nor does it stop climbing and looking about until it notices some sign of its own region. Then it descends to it II, 545 in a short time. And Damir: في طبعه انه يطلب وكره ولو ارسل من الف فرسخ ويحمل الاخبار وياتي بها من المسافة البعيدة في المده القريبة وفيه ما يقطع ثلاثة الاف فرسخ في يوم واحد [fī ṭabʿihi annahu yaṭlub wakrahu wa-law ursila min alf farsakh wa-yaḥmil al-akhbār wa-yātī bihā min al-masāfa al-baʿīda fī al-mudda al-qarība wa-fīhi mā yaqṭaʿ thalāthat ālāf farsakh fī yawm wāḥid] And its nature is to seek its nest again, even if it is sent from a thousand parasangs, and to carry messages from the most remote places in the shortest time, so that some are said to have covered three thousand parasangs in a single day. This exceeds all belief, and I think it was invented by those who tell of great things as still greater. The little letter that is tied to the dove is called in Arabic by a special name, بطاقة [biṭāqa] bittaka, that is, πιττάκιον [pittakion], pittacium [slip].
