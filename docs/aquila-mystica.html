II, 766 CHAPTER IV. col. 182 ¶

Aquilae mysticae; Deus, angeli, fideles, magni reges, maxime Assyrius et Assyriorum copiae.

Mystical eagles: God, the angels, the faithful, great kings, above all the Assyrian king and the forces of the Assyrians. ¶

Propter mirabiles dotes, quas in aquila notavimus, quidquid in coelo sublime est, aut in terris maxime eximium (Deus puta, angeli, fideles, imperia et maximi reges), a sacris scriptoribus cum aquila confertur. Sic Deus dicit Israelitis, quos ex Aegypto eduxerat, Exod. XIX, 4. Vidistis, quae fecerim Aegyptiis, et quomodo vos sustulerim super alas aquilarum, i. e. ὡσεὶ ἐπὶ πτερύγων ἀετοῦ, tanquam super alas aquilarum, ut Graeci recte reddunt, et Chaldaei tres, et Syrus, et unus Arabum, nota similitudinis addita, quae hoc loco necessario supplenda est. Illam tamen divinus scriptor non sine causa suppressit. Nempe, ut similitudo esset expressior, atque animum feriret magis, Deo tribuit alas aquilae, quibus Israelitas ex Aegypto detulerit in Arabiam. Ut illum sciamus eodem modo erga populum suum se gessisse, quomodo solet aquila erga pullitiem suam. Ita enim solet Scriptura rei significatae signi nomen indere, et signo vicissim nomen rei significatae. Porro figurae ratio varie explicatur. Alii enim referunt ad aquilae velocitatem; quia tot hominum myriades uno die ex Aegypto Deus eduxerat. Alii ad volandi facilitatem; quia id factum nullo negotio, et ex tam immani servitute tam facile evaserat Israel, quam facile volat aquila. Alii ad sublimem aquilae volatum, quo tam se, quam pullos suos extra omnem teli jactum evehit. Ita enim suos potenti manu asservaverat Deus, et tanquam in sublimi posuerat, ne tela hostium eos attingerent. Ionathan sacri textus verba sic παραφράζει· טעית יתכון על עננין היך על גדפי נשרין מן פילוסין, Tuli vos super nubes, tanquam super alas aquilarum, ab urbe Pelusio. Nec multo aliter Hierosolymitanus interpres. Nempe miraculum hoc sibi fingunt, de quo Scriptura altum silet, Deum a Pelusiaco tractatu usque ad montem Sinai Israelitas super nubes vexisse. Nec desunt, qui aquilam observent e duobus pullis solum unum alere et educare, altero abjecto, ut ad Deum id referant, qui Iacobum dilexit, et Esavum odio habuit, e duobus populis unum eligere contentus, II, 767 et pro suo agnoscere. Sed nemo est verborum Mosis melior interpres ipso Mose in his Cantici sui verbis Deut. XXXII, 11. Ut aquila excitat nidum col. 183 suum, motitat se super pullos suos expansis alis, assumitque et portat eos super pennas suas, sic Dominus solus deduxit eum, etc. Vexit eum super excelsa terrae etc. In quibus cum jam explicata sint, quae de aquila dicuntur, superest, ut doceamus, quomodo ad Deum haec pertineant. Neque vero res est obscura. Aquila pullos, in nidi sui sordibus torpentes, clangore suo excitat, ut ad volatum provocet. Ita Deus Israelitas, misera servitute sic oppressos, ut ad liberationem non aspirarent, verbo suo sollicitavit, ut libertatis assertoribus, sibi a Deo suscitatis, obsequerentur. Aquila super pullos suos se motitat alis expansis. Ita se movet Spiritus super eos, in quos agit efficaciter. Eodem enim verbo Moses utitur, cum in creatione dicit, Spiritum Dei se movisse super aquas. Et vis illa divini Spiritus Israelitas, alioqui immorigeros, ad obsequium inflexit. Sed et in eos expandit alas providentiae suae, de quibus Scriptura toties. Ut cum Ruth. II, 12. ea venisse legitur sub alas Dei, ut se obtegeret. Et cum pii dicuntur abscondi, se recipere, canere, et protegi sub umbra, aut in occulto alarum ejus, Psal. XVII, 8. et XXXVI, 8. et LVII, 2. et LXI, 5. et LXIII, 8. et XCI, 4. etc. Quod significabat nubes, quae Israelitas obtegebat, Num. XIV, 14. Denique iisdem alis eos gestabat per hoc iter, ut aquila pullos suos, quoties prohibebat a lapsu, aut ne impingerent in via lubrica, et salebrosa, et multis periculis obnoxia. Itaque alibi Deum legas eos portasse brachiis, ut παῖδας ὑπ-αγκαλίους. Sic Deut. I, 31. Portavit te Dominus tuus, ut solet homo gestare parvulum filium suum, in omni via, per quam ambulastis, donec veniretis ad locum istum. Et Os. XI, 1. 3. Cum puer erat Israel, dilexi eum, et ex Aegypto vocavi filium meum, etc. Ubi Chaldaea paraphrasis, Et ego per angelum missum a facie mea deduxi Israel per congruam viam: ונטלתנון כר על דרעין, et portavi eos tanquam super brachia. Nempe pio viro dicitur Psal. XCI, 11. Angelis suis mandavit de te, ut te custodiant in omnibus viis tuis. Manibus portabunt te, ne offendas ad lapidem pedem tuum. Notandum, ut in Lege, ita etiam in Evangelio ecclesiam pullis conferri, quos mater sub alis fovet et tuetur. Sed matrem illam in Lege esse aquilam, et in Evangelio gallinam: et sic adumbrari, ut volunt, diversam administrationem sub utroque Testamento. Unam potentiae, quam exeruit Deus, cum populum suum forti dextera et brachio extenso ex Aegypto eduxit, et in terram promissam introduxit. Alteram gratiae, cum Christus venit humilis, et formam servi accepit, et se ipsum demisit usque ad mortem crucis. Ita tamen, ut utrobique Dei amor summus eluceat et cura, quam suorum gerit. Hoc interim agnoscendum est, etiam in Novo testamento Deum quandoque suos potenter asserere. Proinde, ut ecclesia Iudaica, a Pharaone jamjam opprimenda, aquilarum alis subvecta est, et in desertum translata: sic II, 768 Apoc. XXII, 14. Christianae quoque dantur alae duae magnae, quibus avolet in desertum a conspectu draconis. Cujus figura fuit Pharao, Aegypti rex, qui et ipse draco vocatur Ezech. XXIX, 3.

Because of the wonderful endowments we have noted in the eagle, whatever is lofty in heaven or most excellent on earth (God, that is, the angels, the faithful, empires and the greatest kings) is compared with the eagle by the sacred writers. Thus God says to the Israelites, whom he had led out of Egypt, Exod 19:4. You have seen what I have done to the Egyptians, and how I bore you up on eagles' wings, i.e. ὡσεὶ ἐπὶ πτερύγων ἀετοῦ [hōsei epi pterygōn aetou], as upon eagles' wings, as the Greeks rightly render it, and the three Chaldees, and the Syriac, and one of the Arabic versions, adding the mark of comparison, which must necessarily be supplied in this place. Yet the divine writer did not suppress it without reason. That is, so that the comparison might be more vivid and strike the mind more, he attributes to God eagle's wings, with which he carried the Israelites from Egypt into Arabia. So that we may know that he behaved toward his people in the same way as the eagle usually does toward its brood. For Scripture is accustomed to give the thing signified the name of the sign, and in turn to give the sign the name of the thing signified. Further, the reason for the figure is explained in various ways. Some refer it to the eagle's swiftness, because God had led so many myriads of people out of Egypt in a single day. Others to the ease of its flight, because this was done without any effort, and Israel had escaped from so monstrous a servitude as easily as the eagle flies. Others to the eagle's lofty flight, by which it carries both itself and its chicks beyond all range of missiles. For so God had preserved his own with a mighty hand, and had set them, as it were, on high, so that the weapons of the enemy might not reach them. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases (παραφράζει [paraphrazei]) the words of the sacred text thus: טעית יתכון על עננין היך על גדפי נשרין מן פילוסין, I carried you upon clouds, as upon eagles' wings, from the city of Pelusium. Not much differently the Jerusalem translator. That is, they invent for themselves this miracle, about which Scripture is entirely silent: that God carried the Israelites upon clouds from the region of Pelusium all the way to Mount Sinai. Nor are there lacking those who observe that of two chicks the eagle feeds and rears only one, casting out the other, so as to refer this to God, who loved Jacob and hated Esau, content to choose one of two peoples II, 767 and acknowledge it as his own. But no one is a better interpreter of the words of Moses than Moses himself, in these words of his Song, Deut 32:11. As an eagle stirs up its nest col. 183 and hovers over its young with outspread wings, and takes them and carries them on its pinions, so the Lord alone led him, etc. He made him ride on the heights of the earth etc. Since what is said there of the eagle has already been explained, it remains for us to show how these things apply to God. Nor is the matter obscure. The eagle rouses with its cry the chicks lying sluggish in the filth of the nest, to provoke them to flight. So God, by his word, stirred up the Israelites, so oppressed by wretched servitude that they did not aspire to liberation, to obey the champions of liberty raised up for them by God. The eagle hovers over its chicks with outspread wings. So the Spirit moves over those in whom he works effectually. For Moses uses the same word when he says at the creation that the Spirit of God moved upon the waters. And that power of the divine Spirit bent the Israelites, otherwise intractable, to obedience. Moreover, he spread over them the wings of his providence, of which Scripture speaks so often. As when in Ruth 2:12 she is said to have come under the wings of God to shelter herself. And when the godly are said to be hidden, to take refuge, to sing, and to be protected under the shadow or in the covert of his wings, Ps 17:8 and XXXVI, 8. and LVII, 2. and LXI, 5. and LXIII, 8. and XCI, 4. etc. This was signified by the cloud that covered the Israelites, Num 14:14. Finally, with the same wings he carried them on this journey, as the eagle carries its chicks, whenever he kept them from falling or from stumbling on a slippery and rough road exposed to many dangers. Accordingly you read elsewhere that God carried them in his arms, like παῖδας ὑπαγκαλίους [paidas hypankalious]. Thus Deut 1:31. The Lord your God carried you, as a man carries his little son, all the way that you went until you came to this place. And Hos 11:1 3. When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son, etc. Where the Chaldee paraphrase has: And I, through an angel sent from my presence, led Israel by a fitting way: ונטלתנון כר על דרעין, and I carried them as upon arms. Indeed, it is said to the godly man in Ps 91:11. He has commanded his angels concerning you, to guard you in all your ways. They will carry you in their hands, lest you strike your foot against a stone. It should be noted that, as in the Law, so also in the Gospel the church is compared to chicks which the mother warms and protects under her wings. But in the Law that mother is an eagle, and in the Gospel a hen; and thus, as they would have it, the different dispensation under each Testament is foreshadowed. The one of power, which God displayed when he led his people out of Egypt with a strong right hand and an outstretched arm and brought them into the promised land. The other of grace, when Christ came lowly, took the form of a servant, and humbled himself even to the death of the cross. Yet in such a way that in both God's supreme love shines forth, and the care he takes of his own. Meanwhile it must be acknowledged that in the New Testament too God sometimes delivers his own with power. Accordingly, as the Jewish church, when on the point of being crushed by Pharaoh, was borne up on eagles' wings and carried into the desert, so II, 768 in Rev 22:14 the Christian church too is given two great wings, with which to fly into the desert from the sight of the dragon. Of him Pharaoh, king of Egypt, was a figure, who himself is also called a dragon in Ezek 29:3. ¶

Secundo, per aquilas angelos significari, docet quatuor animalium descriptio in Ezechiele et Apocalypsi a), quam alibi explicavimus. Hinc Apoc. VIII, 13. ubi vulgo legitur: Καὶ εἶδον, καὶ ἤκουσα ἑνὸς ἀγγέλου πετωμένου ἐν μεσουρα-νήματι, pro ἑνὸς ἀγγέλου Hispanicum MSS. optimae notae, et ex eo Complutensis et Plantiniana editio habent ἑνὸς ἀετοῦ. Ita etiam Aretas Commentario in Apocalypsin capite vigesimo quinto. Neque aliter legerunt Syrus et Aethiops. Editio denique vetus, Audivi vocem unius aquilae, volantis per medium coeli, dicentis, etc. Et utraque lectio videtur posse admitti: ut una doceat, angelum Iohanni apparuisse; altera, angelum illum habuisse aquilae speciem, quae angelo maxime convenit, propter velocitatem, robur, longaevitatem, τὸ ὀξυδερκὲς, τὸ ὑψιπετὲς, et si quid aliud aquila commune habet cum natura angelica. col. 184

Second, that angels are signified by eagles is shown by the description of the four living creatures in Ezekiel and the Apocalypse a), which we have explained elsewhere. Hence in Rev 8:13, where the common reading is Καὶ εἶδον, καὶ ἤκουσα ἑνὸς ἀγγέλου πετωμένου ἐν μεσουρανήματι [Kai eidon, kai ēkousa henos angelou petōmenou en mesouranēmati], instead of ἑνὸς ἀγγέλου [henos angelou] a Spanish manuscript of the highest quality, and following it the Complutensian and Plantin editions, have ἑνὸς ἀετοῦ [henos aetou]. So also Arethas in his Commentary on the Apocalypse, chapter 25. Nor did the Syriac and the Ethiopic read otherwise. Finally the old version has: I heard the voice of one eagle, flying through the midst of heaven, saying, etc. Both readings seem admissible: the one teaches that an angel appeared to John; the other, that this angel had the form of an eagle, which suits an angel exceedingly well because of its swiftness, strength and longevity, its τὸ ὀξυδερκὲς [to oxyderkes], its τὸ ὑψιπετὲς [to hypsipetes], and whatever else the eagle has in common with the angelic nature. col. 184 ¶

Tertio, aquilarum nomine fideles significari videntur in Christi verbis Matth. XXIV, 28. Ubi enim est cadaver, ibi congregabuntur aquilae. Ex multis enim hujus loci interpretationibus maxime placet illa Bezae, qua hic et praecedens versus cum versu vigesimo sexto sic connectitur. Si qui insusurraverint, hic, aut illic, quasi latitantem, Christum quaerendum, ne credite; palam enim sese conspiciendum praebebit Christus, ut fulgur, ab oriente usque ad occidentem emicans, et terrarum universum orbem irradians. Et, sicut aquilae ad cadaver statim advolant mira sagacitate: ita piis omnibus aeque demum erit concurrendum, ubi vivificum mortis Christi nidorem percipient. Nec frustra aquilae vocantur, qui aggregantur Christo, cum nemo cum illo uniatur, nisi qui ejus carne et sanguine vescitur. Proinde Hieronymus per πτῶμα, seu cadaver, passionem Christi vult intelligi. Et Chrysostomus in epistolam primam ad Corinthios Homilia vigesima octava Christi corpus πτῶμα vocari dicit propter mortem. Et, ad eucharisticam Christi communionem haec transferens, fideles addit aquilas appellari, ut ostendat, ad alta eum oportere contendere, qui ad hoc corpus accedit, et nihil cum terra debere ei esse commune, neque ad inferiora trahi et repere; sed ad superiora semper volare, et in solem justitiae intueri, mentisque oculum acutissimum habere. Ἀετῶν γὰρ, οὐ κολοιῶν αὕτη ἡ τράπεζα· Aquilarum enim, non graculorum est haec mensa. Luc. XVII, 37. idem Christi dictum repetens Evangelista pro τὸ πτῶμα habet τὸ σῶμα. Sed res eodem recidit. Nam II, 769 τὸ σῶμα pro cadavere Homerus, Xenophon, Plutarchus, Porphyrius et Graecorum alii passim usurpant.

Third, the faithful seem to be signified by the name of eagles in the words of Christ, Matt 24:28. For wheresoever the carcass is, there will the eagles be gathered together. Of the many interpretations of this passage, the one I like best is that of Beza, by which this verse and the preceding one are connected with verse 26 as follows. If any should whisper that Christ is to be sought here or there, as though he were in hiding, do not believe it; for Christ will show himself openly, like lightning flashing from the east to the west and illuminating the whole world. And just as eagles fly at once to a carcass with wonderful keenness of scent, so in the end all the godly will likewise have to run together when they perceive the life-giving savour of the death of Christ. And not without reason are those who gather to Christ called eagles, since no one is united with him except one who feeds on his flesh and blood. Accordingly Jerome takes πτῶμα [ptōma], or carcass, to mean the passion of Christ. And Chrysostom, on the First Epistle to the Corinthians, Homily 28, says that the body of Christ is called πτῶμα [ptōma] on account of his death. Applying this to the eucharistic communion of Christ, he adds that the faithful are called eagles, to show that he who approaches this body must strive towards the heights, and must have nothing in common with the earth, nor be drawn down to lower things and creep; but must always fly to higher things, gaze upon the sun of righteousness, and have the eye of the mind most sharp. Ἀετῶν γὰρ, οὐ κολοιῶν αὕτη ἡ τράπεζα [Aetōn gar, ou koloiōn hautē hē trapeza]· For this table is for eagles, not for jackdaws. In Luke 17:37, where the Evangelist repeats the same saying of Christ, he has τὸ σῶμα [to sōma] instead of τὸ πτῶμα [to ptōma]. But it comes to the same thing. For II, 769 Homer, Xenophon, Plutarch, Porphyry and other Greek writers commonly use τὸ σῶμα [to sōma] for a carcass. ¶

Quarto, aquilae in Scriptura quandoque sunt magni reges. Sic Ezech. XVII, 3. Aquila magna, magnis alis, longis pennis, plena plumis, varia, est Babyloniae rex, ut exponit idem propheta versu duodecimo. Rursus versu septimo, Aquila magna, magnis alis et multis plumis est Aegypti rex, eodem interprete versu decimo quinto. Et ratio similitudinis nemini non est obvia. Cum aquila robore polleat, pernicitate excellat, acri sit visu, animo intrepido, et toto habitu corporis regiam quandam majestatem prae se ferat. Unde est, quod in aves imperium obtinere creditur, ut hujus libri initio b) multis probatum. Et quod Persici reges aquilam auream, pennis extensis, pro regali signo habebant, quod alibi docuimus ex Xenophonte, Curtio et Philostrato. Neque vero est absimile, priscos Assyriae reges hoc idem habuisse signum, atque eo Scripturam alludere, quoties aquilarum nomine illos designat. Ut in verbis Ezechielis, nuper citatis, Aquila magna, etc. Et Dan. VII, 4. ubi de prima bestia, quae Ba-bylonicum imperium adumbrabat, Et alas habebat aquilae. Et Os. VIII, 1. Buccina faucibus tuis (admota dicito), Quasi aquila (involat) super domum Domini. Ubi Chaldaeus paraphrastes, O propheta, faucibus tuis, tanquam tuba, clamita, et dic, Ecce! sicut aquila, quae sese motitat, sic ascendet rex (Assyrius, Nebucadnetsar) cum exercitu suo, et castra metabitur contra domum sanctuarii Domini. Et Ierem. XLVIII, 40. Ut aquila involavit, et in Moabitas extendet alas suas. Et Ierem. XLIX, 22. Ascendet ut aquila, et involabit, et extendet alas suas in Botsram. Nempe, ut aquila, cum impetu in praedam obviam irruebat, et mira celeritate bella conficiebat. Et aquilam illam merito magnam vocat Ezechiel; quia Nebucadnetsar rex erat potentissimus, et magnas illi alas tribuit, id est, magnos exercitus. Ut Es. VIII, 8. Implebunt alae illius latitudinem terrae tuae. Alae, id est, copiae. Atque haec quidem Aegyptio regi cum Assyrio communia fuerunt. Itaque etiam Aegyptius vocatur aquila magna et magnis alis. Sed Assyrio pennae longiores et plurium colorum, id est, ditio major et in plures po- col. 185 pulos. Ac illi proinde, tanquam propriam, pennarum longitudinem et varietatem adscribit Ezechiel. Obiter nota, ut Persas olim et Romanos duces, ita etiam Graecos aquilam habuisse pro signo militari, si Senecae credimus in Thebaide, cum dicit de septem ducibus ad Thebas versu 389.

Fourth, eagles in Scripture are sometimes great kings. Thus in Ezek 17:3 A great eagle, with great wings, long pinions, full of feathers, of various colours, is the king of Babylon, as the same prophet explains in verse twelve. Again, in verse seven, A great eagle, with great wings and many feathers is the king of Egypt, on the interpretation of the same prophet in verse fifteen. And the reason for the likeness is obvious to everyone. For the eagle is powerful in strength, excels in swiftness, is keen of sight and fearless of spirit, and in the whole bearing of its body displays a certain royal majesty. Hence it is believed to hold dominion over the birds, as was proved at length at the beginning of this book b). And the Persian kings had a golden eagle with outspread wings as their royal standard, as we have shown elsewhere from Xenophon, Curtius and Philostratus. Nor is it unlikely that the ancient kings of Assyria had this same standard, and that Scripture alludes to it whenever it designates them by the name of eagles: as in the words of Ezekiel just cited, A great eagle, etc.; and Dan 7:4, where it is said of the first beast, which foreshadowed the Babylonian empire, And it had the wings of an eagle; and Hos 8:1 The trumpet to your throat (say, set it there), As an eagle (he swoops) upon the house of the Lord. Here the Chaldee paraphrast has: O prophet, cry out with your throat as with a trumpet, and say, Behold! like an eagle that moves itself to and fro, so shall the king (of Assyria, Nebuchadnezzar) go up with his army, and encamp against the house of the sanctuary of the Lord. And Jer 48:40 As an eagle he has swooped, and he shall spread out his wings over the Moabites. And Jer 49:22 He shall go up as an eagle, and swoop, and spread out his wings over Botsra. That is, like an eagle he rushed with force upon the prey before him, and brought his wars to an end with marvellous speed. And Ezekiel rightly calls that eagle great, because Nebuchadnezzar was a most powerful king; and he assigns him great wings, that is, great armies. As in Isa 8:8 His wings shall fill the breadth of your land. Wings, that is, forces. These things, indeed, the Egyptian king had in common with the Assyrian. So the Egyptian too is called a great eagle and with great wings. But the Assyrian had longer pinions and of more colours, that is, a greater dominion, extending over more col. 185 peoples. And accordingly Ezekiel ascribes the length and variety of the pinions to him as his own. Note in passing that, like the Persian and Roman commanders of old, the Greeks also had the eagle as a military standard, if we believe Seneca in the Thebaid, where he says of the seven commanders at Thebes, in verse 389: ¶

— — — aera jam bellum cient Aquilaque pugnam signifer mota vocat. II, 770 Quinto, Chaldaeos et Assyrios, qui Nebucadnetsare duce totam Iudaeam devastarunt, Ierosolymam solo aequarunt, et templum igne consumpserunt, Prophetae passim conferunt cum aquilis, ad escam properantibus et in praedam involantibus. Vide Ier. IV, 13. Thren. IV, 19. Hab. I, 8. Itaque ad eosdem pertinent hae legis minae, Deut. XXVIII, 49. Excitabit contra te Dominus gentem remotam, quae ab extremis terrarum finibus sicut aquila involabit. Et fallitur Baal Hatturim, qui ad Alexandrum refert, futili ratione fretus, nempe quod Prophetae voces ידאה הנשר, id est, involabit aquila, sunt ἰσόψηφοι cum voce עשרה, id est, decem. Alexander autem decem dierum iter uno die conficiebat. Sed post mortem Nebucadnetsaris aquilinae illae Assyriorum alae paullatim evulsae sunt, ut praedixerat Daniel VII, 4. et eorum imperium a Persis est plane subversum.

— — — now the bronze trumpets stir up war, and the eagle, as standard-bearer, set in motion, calls to battle. II, 770 Fifth, the Chaldeans and Assyrians who, under the leadership of Nebuchadnezzar, laid waste all Judaea, levelled Jerusalem to the ground and consumed the temple with fire, are often compared by the Prophets to eagles hastening to their food and swooping upon their prey. See Ier. IV, 13. Lam 4:19. Hab 1:8. Therefore these threats of the law also refer to them, Deut 28:49 The Lord will raise up against you a distant nation, which will swoop like an eagle from the furthest ends of the earth. And Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] is mistaken in referring this to Alexander, relying on a futile reason, namely that the Prophet's words ידאה הנשר, that is, the eagle will swoop, are ἰσόψηφοι [isopsēphoi] with the word עשרה, that is, ten; and Alexander completed a ten days' march in one day. But after the death of Nebuchadnezzar those eagle's wings of the Assyrians were gradually plucked off, as Daniel VII, 4. had foretold, and their empire was utterly overthrown by the Persians. ¶
