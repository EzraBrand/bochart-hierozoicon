III, 138 col. 367 Chapter 2. ¶

De Vipera Pauli, et cur Pharisaei dicantur progenies Viperarum.

On Paul's viper, and why the Pharisees are called a brood of vipers. ¶

Quidam, naufragio facto, cum in Libyam salvus evasisset, mox in littore col. 368 dormiens, a vipera morsus interiisse narratur. De quo habetur non inelegans Statyllii Flacci Epigramma Anthologiae libri 3. cap. 22.

A certain man, after a shipwreck, is said to have escaped safely to Libya, and soon after, while sleeping on the shore, col. 368 to have died from the bite of a viper. On him there is a not inelegant epigram of Statyllius Flaccus in the Anthology, book 3, ch. 22. ¶

Λαίλαπα καὶ μανίην ὀλοῆς προφυγόντα θαλάσσης
Ναυηγὸν, Λιβυκαῖς κείμενον ἐν ψαμάθοις,
Οὐκ ἑκὰς ἠϊόνων, πυμάτῳ βεβαρημένον ὕπνῳ,
Γυμνὸν, ἀπὸ στυγερῆς ὃς κάμε ναυφθορίης,
Ἔκτανε λυγρὸς ἔχις. τί μάτην πρὸς κύματ᾽ ἐμόχθει,
Τὴν ἐπὶ γῆς φεύγων μοῖραν ὀφειλομένην;

Λαίλαπα καὶ μανίην ὀλοῆς προφυγόντα θαλάσσης Ναυηγὸν, Λιβυκαῖς κείμενον ἐν ψαμάθοις, Οὐκ ἑκὰς ἠϊόνων, πυμάτῳ βεβαρημένον ὕπνῳ, Γυμνὸν, ἀπὸ στυγερῆς ὃς κάμε ναυφθορίης, Ἔκτανε λυγρὸς ἔχις. τί μάτην πρὸς κύματ᾽ ἐμόχθει, Τὴν ἐπὶ γῆς φεύγων μοῖραν ὀφειλομένην; [Lailapa kai maniēn oloēs prophygonta thalassēs / Nauēgon, Libykais keimenon en psamathois, / Ouk hekas ēïonōn, pymatō bebarēmenon hypnō, / Gymnon, apo stygerēs hos kame nauphthoriēs, / Ektane lygros echis. ti matēn pros kymat᾽ emochthei, / Tēn epi gēs pheugōn moiran opheilomenēn?] ¶

Turbinem et insaniam perniciosi maris effugientem naufragum, Libycis in arenis jacentem, non procul a littore, ultimo gravatum somno, nudum, gravi naufragio adhuc laborantem, occidit funesta vipera. Quid cum fluctibus frustra laborabat, fatum, in terra debitum, frustra fugiens? Aliquid simile Paulo accidit. Illum enim, in insulam, Libyae vicinam, e naufragio vix elapsum, vipera statim aggressa est; sed eventu valde dispari. Neque enim Paulo, sed viperae fatalis fuit haec aggressio. Historiam ita incipit Lucas Actor. XXVIII, 3. Cum involvisset Paulus sarmentorum multitudinem, et in pyram imposuisset. Alii, cum congregasset, cum collegisset, cum converrisset. Et Syrus, ܫܩܠ, attulit, copiam sarmentorum. Quod sequitur Arabs Erpenianus. Et alter, usus longa paraphrasi, qua tamen Pauli mentem non assequitur, وبينما بولس يدور ويفتش ليجمع حطبا كثيرا Et, dum Paulus circuibat et disquirebat, ut lignum multum colligeret. Basilius in Hexaëmeron Homilia 9. uno verbo, φρυγανιζομένῳ τῷ Παύλῳ· quod Hebraice esset מקשש, ut Num. XV, 32. cujus Foemininum מקששת 1 Reg. XVII, 10. Sed, cum Lucas habeat συστρέψαντος τοῦ Παύλου φρυγάνων πλῆθος, non possumus aliud intelligere, quam Paulum sarmenta convolvisse (id enim proprie συστρέφειν), vel ut in fascem colligeret, vel, ut jam collecta ad pyram admoveret, cum fasci ferendo par non esset. Sic igitur convoluta sarmenta cum in ignem conjiceret, vipera, e calore prorepens, manui ejus se alligavit. Sarmenta proprie superflua luxuriantis vitis ligna sunt. Sed generalius est Graecum φρύγανα, quo hic utitur Lucas. Nam ἀπὸ τοῦ φρύγειν, ab urendo Graece dicuntur φρύγανα, virgulta quaevis arida, quae facile ignem concipiunt, uno verbo cremia. Porro, col. 369 III, 139 ex hujusmodi sarmentis et virgultis viperas, aliosque id genus serpentes, passim erumpere, satis notum est. Inde in Palladii Lausiacis capite vigesimo Macarius, puteum fodiens, πλησίον θρύων καὶ φρυγάνων ἐκεῖ κειμένων, ὑπὸ ἀσπί-δος ἐδήχθη, prope juncos et sarmenta seu virgulta ibi jacentia, ab aspide morsus est. Et Midam, vinitorem, ὑπὸ ἐχίδνης δηχθέντα, a vipera demorsum, jacuisse narrat Lucianus in Philopseude: Ἀναδοῦντι γὰρ αὐτῷ τὰ κλήματα, inquit, καὶ ταῖς χάραξι περιπλέκοντι, προσέρπυσαν τὸ θυρίον δακεῖν τὸν μέγαν δάκτυλον· Ei enim, dum ligaret palmites, et pedamentis circumligaret, inde adrepentem be-stiam maximum pedis digitum momordisse. Damir capite الاسود seu De Serpente nigro, وقال للذي قال انه يموت حل حطبك فحله فاذا فيه حيه اسود Et (Iesus) illi dixit, cui mortem interminatus fuerat, ut lignum suum (id est, fascem ligni) solveret; quo facto, in eo repertus est serpens niger. Thuanus De vita sua libro tertio, A templo proximo cum mane rediretur, et cuncti propere ad ignem, nondum bene accensum, accederent, ex fasciculo, sive ab imbre, sive a cella subterranea, unde allatus erat, madido, serpens prosiliit, qui expositus, et diu consideratus, amphisbaenae speciem praeferebat. Ita ex virgultis excessit Pauli vipera: nec attigit ea convolventem; quia tum torpebat frigore. Hyems enim erat. Hinc Prudentius ubi supra,

A shipwrecked man who had escaped the whirlwind and the madness of the deadly sea, lying on the Libyan sands not far from the shore, weighed down by his last sleep, naked, still suffering from his grievous shipwreck, a baleful viper killed. Why did he toil in vain against the waves, vainly fleeing the fate owed him on land? Something similar happened to Paul. For when he had barely escaped from shipwreck onto an island near Libya, a viper at once attacked him, but with a very different outcome: this attack proved fatal not to Paul but to the viper. Luke begins the story thus, Actor. XXVIII, 3: When Paul had gathered up a great quantity of brushwood and laid it on the fire. Others render: when he had assembled, when he had collected, when he had swept together. And the Syriac has ܫܩܠ [שקל], he brought, a quantity of brushwood. This is followed by Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)]. The other Arabic version uses a long paraphrase, which nevertheless does not capture Paul's meaning: وبينما بولس يدور ويفتش ليجمع حطبا كثيرا [wa-baynamā Būlus yadūru wa-yufattishu li-yajmaʿa ḥaṭaban kathīran] And while Paul was going about and searching in order to gather much wood. Basil, in Homily 9 on the Hexaemeron, says it in one word: φρυγανιζομένῳ τῷ Παύλῳ [phryganizomenō tō Paulō]; in Hebrew this would be מקשש, as in Num 15:32, whose feminine is מקששת in 1 Reg. XVII, 10. But since Luke has συστρέψαντος τοῦ Παύλου φρυγάνων πλῆθος [systrepsantos tou Paulou phryganōn plēthos], we can understand nothing else than that Paul rolled the brushwood together (for that is the proper sense of συστρέφειν [systrephein]), either to gather it into a bundle, or to move what was already gathered to the fire, since he was not equal to carrying the load. So when he was throwing the brushwood thus bundled into the fire, a viper, creeping out because of the heat, fastened itself on his hand. Sarmenta [brushwood] are properly the superfluous wood of a rank-growing vine. But the Greek φρύγανα [phrygana], which Luke uses here, is more general. For φρύγανα [phrygana] are so called in Greek ἀπὸ τοῦ φρύγειν [apo tou phrygein], from burning: any dry twigs that easily catch fire, in one word cremia [kindling]. Further, col. 369 III, 139 it is well enough known that vipers and other serpents of that kind everywhere burst out of brushwood and twigs of this sort. Hence in Palladius's Lausiac History, chapter 20, Macarius, while digging a well, πλησίον θρύων καὶ φρυγάνων ἐκεῖ κειμένων, ὑπὸ ἀσπίδος ἐδήχθη [plēsion thryōn kai phryganōn ekei keimenōn, hypo aspidos edēchthē], was bitten by an asp near rushes and brushwood or twigs lying there. And Lucian in the Philopseudes relates that Midas, a vinedresser, lay ὑπὸ ἐχίδνης δηχθέντα [hypo echidnēs dēchthenta], bitten by a viper: Ἀναδοῦντι γὰρ αὐτῷ τὰ κλήματα, he says, καὶ ταῖς χάραξι περιπλέκοντι, προσέρπυσαν τὸ θυρίον δακεῖν τὸν μέγαν δάκτυλον [Anadounti gar autō ta klēmata ... kai tais charaxi periplekonti, proserpysan to thyrion dakein ton megan daktylon]; For while he was tying up the vine shoots and binding them around the stakes, the creature crept up from there and bit the big toe of his foot. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter الاسود [al-aswad], that is On the black serpent: وقال للذي قال انه يموت حل حطبك فحله فاذا فيه حيه اسود [wa-qāla li-lladhī qāla innahu yamūtu ḥull ḥaṭabaka fa-ḥallahu fa-idhā fīhi ḥayya aswad] And (Jesus) told the man whom he had threatened with death to untie his wood (that is, his bundle of wood); when this was done, a black serpent was found in it. Thuanus, On his own life, book 3: When we were returning in the morning from the nearby church, and all hurried to the fire, which was not yet well lit, a serpent leapt out of a bundle of wood that was wet, either from the rain or from the underground cellar from which it had been brought; when it was laid out and examined for a long time, it had the appearance of an amphisbaena. Thus Paul's viper came out of the twigs; nor did it touch him while he was bundling them, because it was then torpid with cold. For it was winter. Hence Prudentius, in the place cited above: ¶

Paulus, dum fragiles cogere surculos,
Et densare foci congeriem studet,
Incautam cumulis inseruit manum,
Torpebat glacie pigra ubi vipera,
Sarmentis laqueos corporis implicans.

Paul, while he was eager to gather brittle shoots and heap up the pile for the hearth, thrust his unwary hand into the stacks, where a sluggish viper lay torpid with frost, twining the coils of its body in the brushwood. ¶

Sed, cum sarmenta, in quibus latebat vipera, Paulus in pyram conjecisset, illa, sensu caloris vel modico recreata, vel nimio exasperata, Paulum aggressa est. Ut in Gabriae Fabulis,

But when Paul had thrown the brushwood in which the viper lay hidden onto the fire, it, either revived by a moderate sensation of heat or provoked by excessive heat, attacked Paul. As in the Fables of Gabrias: ¶

Ἔθαλπέ τις γεωργὸς ἐν κόλποις ὄφιν,
Ὥρᾳ κρύους· ἐπεὶ δὲ θέρμης ᾔσθετο,
Ἔπληξε τὸν θάλψαντα, κἄκτεινεν τάχος.

Ἔθαλπέ τις γεωργὸς ἐν κόλποις ὄφιν, Ὥρᾳ κρύους· ἐπεὶ δὲ θέρμης ᾔσθετο, Ἔπληξε τὸν θάλψαντα, κἄκτεινεν τάχος. [Ethalpe tis geōrgos en kolpois ophin, / Hōra kryous; epei de thermēs ēstheto, / Eplēxe ton thalpsanta, kakteinen tachos.] ¶

Et apud Phaedrum Fabula septuagesima quinta,

And in Phaedrus, Fable 75: ¶

Gelu rigentem quidam colubram sustulit,
Sinuque fovit, contra se ipsum misericors:
Namque, ut refecta est, necuit hominem protinus.

A certain man picked up a snake stiff with frost and warmed it in his bosom, compassionate against himself: for as soon as it was revived, it straightway killed the man. ¶

Porro Pauli manui haec vipera se illigavit, καθῆψε τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ, nempe ut infenso dente morderet. Vulgatus, Erasmus, Castalio, Beza, reddunt invasit manum ejus; Aethiops, pependit e manu ejus; Syrus et uterque Arabs, momordit manum ejus. In qua ipsa sententia videntur fuisse nonnulli Patrum. III, 140 Ita enim Tertullianus in Scorpiaco, de Paulo: Morsum viperae sprevit. Et Ambrosius in Hexaëmeron libri sexti capite sexto, Paulum momordit vipera. Et Prudentius,

Further, this viper fastened itself to Paul's hand, καθῆψε τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ [kathēpse tēs cheiros autou], namely in order to bite with hostile tooth. The Vulgate translator, Erasmus, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Beza render it attacked his hand; the Ethiopic, it hung from his hand; the Syriac and both Arabic versions, it bit his hand. Some of the Fathers seem to have held this very opinion. III, 140 For thus Tertullian in the Scorpiace, of Paul: He scorned the bite of the viper. And Ambrose in the Hexaemeron, book 6, chapter 6: A viper bit Paul. And Prudentius, ¶

Quae, postquam intepuit fomite fumeo,
Laxavitque ferox colla rigentia,
Iam flecti facilis rettulit ad manum,
Vibrato capite, spicula dentium,
Haerentem digiti vulnere mordicus
Pendentemque gerens Paulus inhorruit.

After it had warmed in the smoky kindling, and fiercely loosened its stiffened neck, now easy to bend, it carried to the hand, with darting head, the barbs of its teeth; and Paul shuddered, bearing it clinging and hanging by its bite from the wound in his finger. ¶

Et Arator libro secundo Historiae Apostolicae,

And Arator, book 2 of the Apostolic History, ¶

— — — — cui vipera fixit
Daemonis arma ferens, surgentibus obvia flammis,
Antiqua feritate manum, gelidique veneni
Vulnus in igne dedit. — — —

— — — — whose hand the viper, bearing the demon's weapons, meeting the rising flames, pierced with its ancient ferocity, and gave a wound of cold poison in the fire. — — — ¶

Oecumenius quoque, Ἡ ἔχις, τῇ τοῦ Ἀποστόλου χειρὶ τοὺς ὀδόντας ἐμβαλοῦσα· Vipera, injectis manui Apostoli dentibus. Ego eundem Deum, qui, misso an-gelo suo, conclusit ora leonum, ne Prophetam morsu laederent, Dan. VI, 22. puto viperam cohibuisse, ne in Apostoli manum noxios dentes defigeret. Quod videntur palam docere haec verba Lucae, ἔπαθεν οὐδὲν κακὸν, nihil mali passus col. 370 est. Nam qui a vipera fuisset morsus, non diceretur fuisse immunis ab omni malo. Neque vero καθάπτειν mordere significat, quod quidem sciam, ut neque pendere, aut invadere, sed illigare, ut simplex ἅπτειν· Xenophon in Cynegetico, Ἐκ τῶν τριμμῶν εἰς τὰ συμφέροντα καθάπτων τοὺς περιδρόμους ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν· Ex tramitibus ad loca commoda humi plagas alligans. Anonymus in Venerem armatam Anthologiae libri quarti capite duodecimo,

Oecumenius likewise: Ἡ ἔχις, τῇ τοῦ Ἀποστόλου χειρὶ τοὺς ὀδόντας ἐμβαλοῦσα· [Hē echis, tē tou Apostolou cheiri tous odontas embalousa] The viper, having driven its teeth into the Apostle's hand. I, however, think that the same God who, sending his angel, shut the mouths of the lions lest they injure the Prophet with their bite, Dan 6:22, restrained the viper so that it did not fix its harmful teeth in the Apostle's hand. This these words of Luke seem plainly to teach: ἔπαθεν οὐδὲν κακὸν [epathen ouden kakon], he suffered no harm col. 370. For one who had been bitten by a viper would not be said to have been free of all harm. Nor indeed does καθάπτειν [kathaptein] mean to bite, so far as I know, nor to hang or to attack, but to fasten, like the simple ἅπτειν [haptein]. Xenophon in the Cynegeticus: Ἐκ τῶν τριμμῶν εἰς τὰ συμφέροντα καθάπτων τοὺς περιδρόμους ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν· [Ek tōn trimmōn eis ta sympheronta kathaptōn tous peridromous epi tēn gēn] Fastening the nets to the ground from the paths to suitable places. An anonymous poet on the armed Venus, Anthology, book 4, chapter 12, ¶

Τίπτε μόθων ἄτλητος, Ἐνυαλίοιο λέλογχας,
Κύπρι; τίς ὁ ψεύσας συγνὰ καθᾶψε μάταν.

Τίπτε μόθων ἄτλητος, Ἐνυαλίοιο λέλογχας, Κύπρι; τίς ὁ ψεύσας συγνὰ καθᾶψε μάταν. [Tipte mothōn atlētos, Enyalioio lelonchas, Kypri; tis ho pseusas sygna kathapse matan.] ¶

Cur, cum pugnas tolerare non possis, Martis arma sortita es, o Venus? Quis mendax molesta haec frustra adalligavit? Ita etiam περικαθάπτειν, circumalligare: ut libro quinto Dioscoridis capite De hydrargyro, Περικαθάπτουσιν ἄμβι-κα περιαλείψαντες πηλῷ, Calicem, luto illitum, circumalligant. Et Nomen καθαπτὸς ligatus. Sic in veteri proverbio apud Hesychium, Καθαπτὰ λύεις, vel, ut alii, κάθαμμα (male in Suida κάθαρμα) id est, Ligata, vel Nodos solvis, quaeque aliis sunt δύσλυτα, insolubilia. Itaque in Lucae verbis καθῆψε τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ, Stephanus supplet ἑαυτὴν, et reddit, alligavit se ejus manui. Quomodo dixit Basilius, eandem historiam referens, Homilia nona in Hexaëmeron, Φρυγανιζομένῳ τῷ Παύλῳ ἐνάψας ὁ ἔχις, supple ἑαυτὸν, Sarmenta colligenti III, 141 Paulo cum vipera se illigasset. Tamen alii pependit reddunt; quia ἀφάπτειν et ἐξανάπτειν est appendere, aut suspendere: et statim sequitur, ὡς δὲ εἶδον οἱ βάρβαροι κρεμάμενον τὸ θηρίον ἐκ τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ, cum barbari bestiam viderunt pendentem e manu ejus. Alii rursus momordit, quasi legerint καθήψατο. Nam καθάπτεσθαί τινος in Passiva voce est, aliquem mordere, non tamen dentibus, sed cavillis et dicteriis. Et vero Chrysostomus in hunc locum pro καθῆ-ψε legit καθήψατο. Quod tamen aliter sumit, nempe pro simplici ἥψατο, id est, invasit. Is enim ita habet in sequenti expositione, Εἶτα ἔχιδνα, ἐκ τῆς θέρμης ἐξελθοῦσα, τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ ἅπτεται· καὶ ὅτι ἥψατο, δῆλον ποιεῖ τοῦ-το ἐκ τῆς ἐπαγωγῆς. Deinde vipera, e calore egressa, manum ipsius invadit: invasisse enim palam facit ex sequentibus. Ita omnino reddendum. Quippe ἅπτεσθαι ἀνθρώπου est hominem invadere, ut ex Platone, Thucydide et Plutarcho probat Stephanus. Unde factum, quod a multis καθῆψε τῆς χειρὸς redditur invasit manum; quasi καθῆψε, vel καθήψατο sit idem, quod ἥψατο. Sed nihil opus quidquam mutare, nec usitato verbo inusitatas notiones affingere, cum apprime id conveniat, ut dicatur vipera Pauli manui se alligasse. Nam id ligare dicitur serpens, quod spiris suis complectitur. Sic apud summum poetam angues duo Laocoontem

Why, since you cannot endure battles, have you been allotted the arms of Mars, O Venus? What liar has vainly fastened these burdensome things on you? Likewise περικαθάπτειν [perikathaptein], to fasten around: as in Dioscorides, book 5, the chapter On quicksilver: Περικαθάπτουσιν ἄμβικα περιαλείψαντες πηλῷ [Perikathaptousin ambika perialeipsantes pēlō], They fasten around a cup smeared with clay. And the noun καθαπτὸς [kathaptos], fastened. So in an old proverb in Hesychius: Καθαπτὰ λύεις [Kathapta lyeis], or, as others have it, κάθαμμα [kathamma] (wrongly κάθαρμα [katharma] in Suidas), that is, You untie things fastened, or knots, things which to others are δύσλυτα [dysluta], insoluble. Accordingly, in Luke's words καθῆψε τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ [kathēpse tēs cheiros autou], Stephanus supplies ἑαυτὴν [heautēn] and renders it fastened itself to his hand. So Basil put it, relating the same story in the ninth Homily on the Hexaemeron: Φρυγανιζομένῳ τῷ Παύλῳ ἐνάψας ὁ ἔχις [Phryganizomenō tō Paulō enapsas ho echis], supply ἑαυτὸν [heauton], when the viper had fastened itself on Paul as he gathered brushwood III, 141. Yet others render it hung, because ἀφάπτειν [aphaptein] and ἐξανάπτειν [exanaptein] mean to hang on or to suspend: and there follows at once ὡς δὲ εἶδον οἱ βάρβαροι κρεμάμενον τὸ θηρίον ἐκ τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ [hōs de eidon hoi barbaroi kremamenon to thērion ek tēs cheiros autou], when the barbarians saw the beast hanging from his hand. Others again render it bit, as if they had read καθήψατο [kathēpsato]. For καθάπτεσθαί τινος [kathaptesthai tinos] in the passive voice means to bite someone, yet not with teeth but with jibes and taunts. And indeed Chrysostom on this passage reads καθήψατο [kathēpsato] for καθῆψε [kathēpse]. But he takes it otherwise, namely for the simple ἥψατο [hēpsato], that is, it attacked. For he has this in the following exposition: Εἶτα ἔχιδνα, ἐκ τῆς θέρμης ἐξελθοῦσα, τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ ἅπτεται· καὶ ὅτι ἥψατο, δῆλον ποιεῖ τοῦτο ἐκ τῆς ἐπαγωγῆς. [Eita echidna, ek tēs thermēs exelthousa, tēs cheiros autou haptetai; kai hoti hēpsato, dēlon poiei touto ek tēs epagōgēs.] Then the viper, coming out from the heat, attacks his hand: and that it attacked he makes plain from what follows. So it must certainly be rendered. For ἅπτεσθαι ἀνθρώπου [haptesthai anthrōpou] is to attack a man, as Stephanus proves from Plato, Thucydides and Plutarch. And in Dionysius of Halicarnassus the letter Σῖγμα [Sigma] is θηριώδης [thēriōdēs], because it imitates the hissing of serpents. So too the same word חיה is for the Hebrews any wild beast, and for the Arabs a serpent. And among the Chaldeans the words חיוא and חויא differ little: the former means beast in general, the latter serpent. Thus in Luke, then, τὸ θηρίον [to thērion] is a serpent, and indeed a viper; and when the barbarians saw it hanging from the hand of Paul, to which it had fastened itself, they concluded that he was a murderer, that is, guilty either of homicide or of some other grave crime. The species stands for the genus. O duri cervice, et incircumcisi corde et auribus, vos semper Spiritui Sancto obsistitis. Quales fuerunt patres vestri, tales et vos estis. Et multo ante David Psalm. LXXVIII, 8. Ne sint ut patres eorum, generatio contumax et rebellis, etc. Nec multum repugnabo, si quis viperarum nomine daemones intelligat, qui cur per serpentes significentur, neminem latet. Daemonis porro filii censentur, quotquot in Deum sunt rebelles. Proinde magum, Elymam, Paulus vocat filium diaboli Act. XIII, 8. Et Christus Iudaeis dicit Ioh. VIII, 44. Vos ex patre, diabolo, estis, et desideria patris vestri vultis facere. Aliud δεινῆς ἐχίδνης θρέμμα in Trachiniis Sophoclis versu 1106. Ibi enim ἐχίδνη non est vipera, sed fabulosa Echidna, ex qua natus Cerberus. De illo enim ibi agitur.

Many ask why the Jews, or the Pharisees, are repeatedly a) called γεννήματα ἐχιδνῶν [gennēmata echidnōn], brood of vipers. Most think that this alludes to the tradition that the viper is born by gnawing through its mother's womb, because the Jews showed themselves ungrateful toward God and the Prophets, whom they ought to have regarded as fathers. See, besides those who have written expressly on Matthew and Luke, Isidore of Pelusium, Epistle 105, and the Interpretations of the Parables of the Gospel ascribed to Athanasius, Question 20. To me it does not seem likely that a fabulous matter is alluded to, or that God and the Prophets are in this way compared with vipers. I therefore side with those who understand here the very wicked sons of very wicked parents. That is, κακοῦ κόρακος κακὸν ὠὸν [kakou korakos kakon ōon], A bad crow lays a bad egg. With this in view the martyr Stephen addresses the Jews thus, Acts 7:51. O stiff-necked, and uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit. As your fathers were, so are you also. And long before, David, Ps 78:8: That they might not be like their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation, etc. Nor shall I much object if someone understands by the name of vipers the demons; why these are signified by serpents is known to everyone. Moreover, all who rebel against God are reckoned sons of the devil. Accordingly Paul calls the magician Elymas a son of the devil, Acts 13:8. And Christ says to the Jews, John 8:44. You are of your father, the devil, and you want to do the desires of your father. The δεινῆς ἐχίδνης θρέμμα [deinēs echidnēs thremma] in Sophocles' Trachiniae, line 1106, is something else. For there ἐχίδνη [echidnē] is not a viper but the fabulous Echidna, from whom Cerberus was born; for it is he who is spoken of there. ¶
