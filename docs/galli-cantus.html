II, 688 col. 119 Chapter 17.

De Galli cantu.

On the Crowing of the Cock.

Ex veteris Testamenti tot locis, quibus de gallinaceo genere agi volunt, nullum esse satis certum, liquido constitit hactenus. At res aliter se habet de Novo, ubi aperte loquitur Christus de galli cantu et de gallinae affectu erga pullos, quos sub alis suis congregat. Ad galli cantum haec pertinent Marc. XIII, 35. Vigilate igitur. Nescitis enim quando Dominus domus veniet, vespere, aut media nocte, aut galli cantu, aut mane. Galli cantum Evangelista uno verbo appellat ἀλεκτοροφωνίαν, ut alii ἀλεκτρυοφωνίαν, et Romani gallicinium. Glossae Philoxeni, Ἀλεκτρυοφωνία, Gallicinium, galli cantus. Hebraei id ipsum tempus vocant קריאת הגבר, clamorem, seu cantum galli. Sic in Talmudico col. 120 tractatu Ioma cap. 1. fol. 20. a. בכל יום תורמים את המזבח בקריאת הגבר או סמוך לו בין לפניו בין לאחריו, Quotidie cineres ex altari demunt ad galli cantum, aut proximo tempore, sive praecedat, aut subsequatur. Et fol. 21. a. היוצא לדרך קודם קריאת הגבר דמו בראשו, Quisquis ante galli cantum ad iter se accingit, sanguis ejus est super caput ejus: id est, suae mortis reus est, si quid accidat secus. Et in Semoth Rabba capite primo, ubi de Israelitarum servitute in Aegypto, והיו הנוגשים הולכים לבתי השוטרים בהשכמה להוציאן למלאכתן לקריאת הגבר, Et ibant exactores in praefectorum domos summo mane, ut eos ad operas suas educerent ad galli cantum. Et aliquanto post, עמד לשעת קריאת הגבר והוציאו מביתו, Adstabat hora galli cantus, ut eum educeret e domo sua. Galli cantus proprie medium tempus est inter μεσονύκτιον et mane, ut definit Evangelista: Media nocte, inquit, aut gallicinio, aut mane. Unde est, quod Censorinus capite decimo nono diei partes sic enumerat: Sunt etiam plura noctis et diei tempora subnotata, propriisque discreta nominibus, quae apud veteres poetas passim scripta inveniuntur. Ea omnia ordine suo exponam. Incipio a nocte media, quod tempus principium et postremum est diei Romani. Tempus, quod huic proximum est, vocatur de media nocte. Sequitur gallicinium, cum galli canere incipiunt; deinde conticinium, cum conticuere: tunc ante lucem, et sic diluculum vocatur. Mane, cum lux videtur solis, etc. Neque aliter Macrobius Saturnalium lib. 1. cap. 3. Primum tempus diei dicitur mediae noctis inclinatio, deinde II, 689 gallicinium, inde conticinium, cum et galli conticescunt, et homines etiam tum quiescunt, deinde diluculum, id est, cum incipit dignosci dies. Inde mane, dum dies clarus, etc. Et Glossae veteres, Conticinium, ὁ μεταξὺ ἀλεκτρυοφωνίας χρό-νος, tempus, quod est inter gallicinia. Nullo sensu: sed legendum ὁ μετὰ τὰς ἀλεκτρυοφωνίας χρόνος, tempus, quod est post gallicinia. Quae Plurali numero dicuntur, quia plura sunt. Gallus enim a media nocte canere incipit. Deinde medio inter μεσονύκτιον et auroram spatio rursus canit. Itaque hunc cantum multi secundum vocant. Aristophanes in Ἐκκλησιαζούσαις, pagina 735.

It has so far been clearly established that, of the many places in the Old Testament in which some claim that the fowl kind is treated, none is sufficiently certain. But the matter stands otherwise with the New, where Christ speaks openly of the crowing of the cock and of the hen's affection for her chicks, which she gathers under her wings. To the crowing of the cock belongs Mark 13:35 "Watch therefore, for you do not know when the master of the house will come, in the evening, or at midnight, or at the crowing of the cock, or in the morning." The Evangelist calls the crowing of the cock by a single word, ἀλεκτοροφωνία [alektorophōnia], as others say ἀλεκτρυοφωνία [alektryophōnia], and the Romans gallicinium [cockcrow]. The Glosses of Philoxenus: Ἀλεκτρυοφωνία [Alektryophōnia], gallicinium, galli cantus [cockcrow, the crowing of the cock]. The Hebrews call this very time קריאת הגבר, the cry, or crowing of the cock. Thus in the Talmudic col. 120 tractate Yoma, ch. 1, fol. 20a: בכל יום תורמים את המזבח בקריאת הגבר או סמוך לו בין לפניו בין לאחריו, "Every day they remove the ashes from the altar at the crowing of the cock, or at the time nearest to it, whether before or after." And fol. 21a: היוצא לדרך קודם קריאת הגבר דמו בראשו, "Whoever sets out on a journey before the crowing of the cock, his blood is upon his own head": that is, he is answerable for his own death if anything goes wrong. And in Shemot Rabbah, chapter 1, concerning the servitude of the Israelites in Egypt: והיו הנוגשים הולכים לבתי השוטרים בהשכמה להוציאן למלאכתן לקריאת הגבר, "And the taskmasters went to the houses of the officers very early in the morning, to bring them out to their work at the crowing of the cock." And a little later: עמד לשעת קריאת הגבר והוציאו מביתו, "He stood by at the hour of the crowing of the cock, to bring him out of his house." The crowing of the cock is properly the time midway between μεσονύκτιον [mesonyktion] and morning, as the Evangelist defines it: "At midnight," he says, "or at cockcrow, or in the morning." Hence Censorinus, in chapter 19, enumerates the parts of the day thus: "There are also several times of night and day marked out and distinguished by their own names, which are found written here and there in the old poets. I shall set them all out in order. I begin from midnight, which is the beginning and the end of the Roman day. The time next to this is called 'after midnight'. Then follows cockcrow, when the cocks begin to crow; then conticinium, when they have fallen silent; then 'before light', and so it is called diluculum [dawn]. Morning, when the light of the sun is seen," etc. Nor otherwise Macrobius, Saturnalia book 1, ch. 3: "The first time of the day is called the declining of midnight, then II, 689 cockcrow, then conticinium, when both the cocks fall silent and men too are still at rest; then diluculum, that is, when the day begins to be discerned; then morning, when the day is bright," etc. And the old Glosses: Conticinium, ὁ μεταξὺ ἀλεκτρυοφωνίας χρόνος [ho metaxy alektryophōnias chronos], "the time which is between the cockcrows." This makes no sense; rather one should read ὁ μετὰ τὰς ἀλεκτρυοφωνίας χρόνος [ho meta tas alektryophōnias chronos], "the time which is after the cockcrows." These are spoken of in the plural because there are several. For the cock begins to crow from midnight. Then, in the space midway between μεσονύκτιον [mesonyktion] and dawn, it crows again. Therefore many call this crowing the second. Aristophanes in the Ἐκκλησιάζουσαι [Ekklēsiazousai], page 735:

Οὐδ᾽ εἰ μὰ Δία τοτ᾽ ἦλθες, ὅτε τὸ δεύτερον Ἀλεκτρυὼν ἐφθέγγετο. — — —

Οὐδ᾽ εἰ μὰ Δία τοτ᾽ ἦλθες, ὅτε τὸ δεύτερον Ἀλεκτρυὼν ἐφθέγγετο. — — — [Oud᾽ ei ma Dia tot᾽ ēlthes, hote to deuteron Alektryōn ephthengeto.]

Neque per Iovem si tunc venisses, cum gallus secundo canebat. Heliodorus lib. 5. Ἀλεκτρυόνες ἤδη τὸ δεύτερον ᾖδον, Galli jam secundo canebant. Unde adagium vetus apud Paroemiographos, de quo multa Erasmus: Πρὶν ἢ τὸ δεύτερον ἀλε-κτρυὼν ἐφθέγγετο, Prius quam gallus secundo canat, id est, summo mane. Synesius Epist. 4. Ἧς (νυκτὸς) ἤδη περὶ δευτέραν οὔσης ὀρνίθων ᾠδήν· Cum nox jam esset circa secundum gallorum cantum. Iuvenalis Satyrae nonae versu 106.

"Not even, by Jove, if you had come when the cock was crowing for the second time." Heliodorus, book 5: Ἀλεκτρυόνες ἤδη τὸ δεύτερον ᾖδον [Alektryones ēdē to deuteron ēdon], "The cocks were already crowing for the second time." Hence the old proverb among the Paroemiographers, on which Erasmus [=Desiderius Erasmus, Adagia] says much: Πρὶν ἢ τὸ δεύτερον ἀλεκτρυὼν ἐφθέγγετο [Prin ē to deuteron alektryōn ephthengeto], "Before the cock crows a second time," that is, very early in the morning. Synesius, Epist. 4: Ἧς (νυκτὸς) ἤδη περὶ δευτέραν οὔσης ὀρνίθων ᾠδήν [Hēs (nyktos) ēdē peri deuteran ousēs ornithōn ōdēn]; "When the night was already about the second crowing of the cocks." Juvenal, Satire 9, line 106:

Quod tamen ad cantum galli facit ille secundi, Proximus ante diem caupo sciet. — —

"Yet what he does at the crowing of the second cock, the nearest innkeeper will know before daybreak." — —

Ammianus lib. 22. Casium montem ascendit, unde secundis galliciniis videtur primo solis exortus. Alii vigilia quarta solem e Casio monte conspici scribunt. Mela libri primi cap. 10. Ut ex summo vertice a quarta vigilia ortum solis ostendat. Plinius lib. 10. cap. 22. Casius, cujus excelsa altitudo quarta vigilia orientem per tenebras solem aspicit. Solinus, Cujus e vertice vigilia adhuc quarta conspicitur globus solis. Martianus Capella lib. 6. Casius, cujus altitudo quarta vigilia solem per tenebras videt. Itaque secundum illud gallicinium et quarta vigilia idem sunt. Galli nempe, auctore Plinio, cum sole eunt cubitum, quartaque castrensi vigilia ad curas laboremque revocant. Sed, quamvis profunda nocte galli bis canant, tamen cum aliquid factum legitur gallicinii tempore absque numeri adjectione, semper posterius intelligitur, quod sic appellatur κατ᾽ ἐξοχὴν, aut quia tum galli acrius atque alacrius canunt: aut quia posterior ille cantus hominibus col. 121 est utilior, cum ad diurnos labores eos excitet: aut denique quia prioris gallicinii tempus alio designatur nomine: vocatur enim μεσονύκτιον, media nox, medinoctium. Itaque non solum Marcus Evangelista, sed et Censorinus et Macrobius gallicinium medium ponunt inter mediam noctem et mane, ut jam supra patuit. Et Isidorus (Orig. lib. 5. cap. 31.) gallicinium dictum esse scribit propter gallos, lucis praenuncios. Unde solum posterius ab illo intelligi constat. Heliodori quoque verbis lib. 1. Aethiopicῶν, καθ᾽ ὃν καιρὸν ἀλεκτρυόνες ᾄδουσιν, quo II, 690 tempore galli canunt, id ipsum significari docent, quae statim sequuntur, illos canere αἰσθήσει φυσικῇ τῆς τοῦ ἡλίου καθ᾽ ἡμᾶς περιστροφῆς ἐπὶ τὴν τοῦ Θεοῦ πρόσρησιν κινουμένους, naturali sensu solis ad nos conversionis ad Dei salutationem incitatos. Sed et in Polluce inter noctis partes extremas censetur tempus περὶ ἀλεκτρυόνων ᾠδὰς, et ἀλεκτρυόνων ᾀδόντων. Et Horatii Satyra 1.

Ammianus, book 22: He climbed Mount Casius, from which at the second cockcrow the first rising of the sun is seen. Others write that the sun is seen from Mount Casius at the fourth watch. Mela, book 1, ch. 10: So that from its highest summit it shows the rising of the sun from the fourth watch. Pliny, book 10, ch. 22: Casius, whose lofty height beholds the sun rising through the darkness at the fourth watch. Solinus: From whose summit the orb of the sun is seen while it is still the fourth watch. Martianus Capella, book 6: Casius, whose height sees the sun through the darkness at the fourth watch. Thus that second cockcrow and the fourth watch are the same. For cocks, on the authority of Pliny, go to bed with the sun, and at the fourth military watch call men back to their cares and labour. But although cocks crow twice in the depth of night, nevertheless, when something is read to have been done at the time of cockcrow without the addition of a number, the later one is always understood. It is so called κατ᾽ ἐξοχὴν [kat᾽ exochēn], either because the cocks then crow more keenly and briskly; or because that later crowing is more useful to men, since it rouses them to the labours of the day; or finally because the time of the first cockcrow is designated by another name: for it is called μεσονύκτιον [mesonyktion], midnight, mid-night. col. 121 Therefore not only Mark the Evangelist, but also Censorinus and Macrobius place cockcrow midway between midnight and morning, as has already appeared above. And Isidore (Orig. book 5, ch. 31) writes that cockcrow is so called on account of the cocks, the heralds of the light. Hence it is clear that only the later one is meant by him. In the words of Heliodorus too, book 1 of the Aethiopica, καθ᾽ ὃν καιρὸν ἀλεκτρυόνες ᾄδουσιν [kath᾽ hon kairon alektryones adousin], at the time II, 690 when the cocks crow, the words that immediately follow show that the same thing is meant: that they crow αἰσθήσει φυσικῇ τῆς τοῦ ἡλίου καθ᾽ ἡμᾶς περιστροφῆς ἐπὶ τὴν τοῦ Θεοῦ πρόσρησιν κινουμένους [aisthēsei physikē tēs tou hēliou kath᾽ hēmas peristrophēs epi tēn tou Theou prosrēsin kinoumenous], stirred by a natural sense of the sun's turning towards us to salute God. And in Pollux too, among the last parts of the night is reckoned the time περὶ ἀλεκτρυόνων ᾠδὰς [peri alektryonōn ōdas] and ἀλεκτρυόνων ᾀδόντων [alektryonōn adontōn]. And Horace, Satire 1:

Agricolam laudat juris legumque peritus, Sub galli cantum consultor ubi ostia pulsat.

The man skilled in law and statutes praises the farmer's lot, when towards cockcrow a client knocks at his door.

Quod Matthaeus obscure dicit, „Prius quam gallus cantet“, interpretatus est Marcus, quod prius quam bis cantet. Galli enim de more habent, uno ductu saepe canere, deinde tanquam dormire, et post aliquod tempus iterum cantum incipere. Quod igitur dixit Matthaeus, scilicet prius quam gallus cantet, id est, prius quam impleat clamorem primi cantus, me ter negabis (1).

That is, at earliest dawn. Nor does the cockcrow of the night mean anything else in these words of Apuleius at the beginning of book 8: At the cockcrow of the night a certain young man came from the nearest city. And in the Vulgate, Tob 8:11: About the crowing of the cocks Raguel ordered his servants to be summoned; and they went with him to dig a grave. About the crowing of the cocks, that is, at the time when the domestic cocks crow. That the time before daybreak is meant is shown by what is found in verses 13 and 14: And when they had prepared the pit, Raguel returned to his wife and said to her, Send one of your maidservants, and let her see whether he (Tobias) is dead, so that I may bury him before the day grows light. The Hebrew has נקברהו טרם אור הבוקר ואיש לא ידע, We shall bury him before the morning light, so that no one may know. Since, therefore, the same time is sometimes called simply cockcrow, and sometimes more explicitly the second cockcrow, men learned in every field rightly think that by this observation Mark can easily be reconciled with the other Evangelists in specifying the time at which Peter was to deny Christ three times. For according to Mark, Christ foretold to Peter that he would deny him three times πρὶν ἢ δὶς ἀλέκτορα φωνῆσαι [prin ē dis alektora phōnēsai], before the cock had crowed twice. And indeed, after it had crowed following the first denial, after the third ἐκ δευτέρου ἀλέκτωρ ἐφώνησε [ek deuterou alektōr ephōnēse], the cock crowed again. So Mark has it in ch. 14, verses 30, 68, 69, 70, 71. But in the others the cock does not crow before the third denial. For thus Christ says in Matt 26:34 Ἐν ταύτῃ τῇ νυκτὶ, πρὶν ἀλέκτορα φωνῆσαι, τρὶς ἀπαρνήσῃ με· [En tautē tē nykti, prin alektora phōnēsai, tris aparnēsē me] This night, before the cock crows, you will deny me three times. And Luke 22:34 Οὐ μὴ φωνήσει σήμερον ἀλέκτωρ, πρὶν ἢ τρὶς ἀπαρνήσῃ μὴ εἰδέναι με· [Ou mē phōnēsei sēmeron alektōr, prin ē tris aparnēsē mē eidenai me] Today the cock will not crow before you deny three times that you know me. And John 13:38 Οὐ μὴ ἀλέκτωρ φωνήσει, ἕως οὗ ἀπαρνήσῃ με τρίς· [Ou mē alektōr phōnēsei, heōs hou aparnēsē me tris] The cock will not crow before you deny me three times. These seem ἀσύστατα [asystata]. But, they say, they are reconciled without any difficulty if you suppose that Peter first denied about midnight, and that the cock then crowed for the first time; and that he denied the third time about the fourth watch, or about the time usually called cockcrow, and that the cock then crowed again. On this assumption, these words of Christ, The cock will not crow, or Before the cock crows, will be the same as if he had said, Before the time of cockcrow, or The cock will not crow that customary crowing from which the time at which it crows is called ἀλεκτοροφωνία [alektorophōnia], before you have denied me three times. Meanwhile I have no doubt that Mark, who had been a disciple of Peter, reported the very words of Christ, just as he had learned them from Peter. II, 691 But it sufficed for the others to record what was most important, namely that Christ had not only foreseen and foretold that threefold denial of Peter, but had also specified the time, and that Peter would deny Christ three times before cockcrow. Although it is plain that this is to be understood of the second cockcrow, since this alone is called simply cockcrow, Mark nevertheless expressed it explicitly, and col. 122 added moreover that Peter's first denial preceded the first cockcrow. But, you will say, between the first and second cockcrow a fourth part of the night usually intervenes; and that span would then have been nearly three hours (for in Judaea, at the season of the year when Christ was delivered to death, the nights are more than eleven hours long); whereas between Peter's first and last denial scarcely half of this span can have elapsed. This is clear from Lucae XXII, 56. 57. 58. For there, when Peter had denied to the maidservant that he knew Christ, which was the first denial, μετὰ βραχὺ [meta brachy], a little later, another, seeing him, said, You too are one of them: but Peter said, Man, I am not; which was the second denial. Καὶ διαστάσης ὡσεὶ ὥϱας μιᾶς [Kai diastasēs hōsei hōras mias], And after about one hour had passed, when another asserted the same thing, Peter denied a third time, and immediately the cock crowed. I answer: 1. The Evangelists reported most things summarily; and while Peter, being accused, tried to clear himself, the matter could have been drawn out at greater length, and, with many words exchanged back and forth, much time could have been spent in the dispute itself. 2. From Luke's words μετὰ βραχὺ [meta brachy] it does not follow that there was no interval of time, or a very short one, between Peter's first and second denial. For John, in chapter 18, from verse 17 to 25, inserts many things as having taken place in that interval. And the third denial, which Luke separates from the second by an interval of about an hour, is nevertheless said in Matthew and Mark to have occurred μετὰ μικρὸν [meta mikron], a little later. Therefore that μετὰ μικρὸν [meta mikron], or, what is the same, μετὰ βραχὺ [meta brachy], can be extended more widely than is thought. 3. Something may be added to Luke's hour, since he did not say precisely that one hour intervened, but ὡσεὶ ὥρας μιᾶς [hōsei hōras mias], about one hour. 4. Although a fourth part of the night usually intervenes between the first and second cockcrow, this is not invariable. For it often happens that cocks crow at random, and not at fixed hours. Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] writes in Exercitatio 239 against Cardano that he experienced this both formerly on night watch and later in his nocturnal studies. A cock, therefore, could have anticipated the usual time of cockcrow by one hour after Peter's third denial. Another objection may be raised: when Peter denied Christ, the scribes, priests and elders were in Caiaphas's house to inquire into Christ's life; and Peter meanwhile, having entered the courtyard, waited to see how the matter would finally turn out and what would be decided about Christ. But it is not likely that that assembly lasted so far into the night. For who would believe that so many men from among the leaders of the Jewish people spent almost the whole night without sleep in order to decide this case, when the matter could just as conveniently have been put off to another time? But this objection is raised in vain. For it is certain that it happened so. This will be clear to those who consider II, 692 how many things were done that night before Peter's third denial. Ὀψίας γενομένης [Opsias genomenēs] a), When evening had already come, that is, at the very setting of the sun, Christ celebrated the Passover with the Apostles. He washed their feet b). He preached on the occasion c). He instituted the eucharistic supper d). He restrained with the gravest words the disciples, who were contending among themselves over the primacy e). After this he is denied by Peter a second col. 123 time r), and, after an hour had passed, a third time s). No fair judge of the facts will fail to recognize at once that all these things could not have taken place before the dead of night. And it was indeed altogether extraordinary that a man's life should be dealt with at those hours. But, besides the feast that was close at hand, they were driven to it by their savage rage against Christ and by a more than Vatinian hatred with which they were borne against him. Closest to the interpretation we have given comes that of Theophylact on Mark: Ὅπερ δὲ εἶπεν ἀσαφῶς ὁ Ματθαῖος, ὅτι πρὶν ἀλέκτορα φωνῆσαι ἡρμήνευσεν ὁ Μάρκος, ὅτι πρὶν φωνῆσαι δίς. Εἰώθασι γὰρ οἱ ἀλέκτορες κατὰ μίαν ἀγωγὴν πολλάκις φωνεῖν, εἶτα οἷον ὑπνώττειν, καὶ πάλιν μετά τινα καιρὸν ἑτέραν ἀρχὴν ποιεῖσθαι τοῦ φωνεῖν· ὃ οὖν εἶπεν ὁ Ματθαῖος, τοῦτό ἐστιν, ὅτι, πρὶν ἀλέκτορα φωνῆσαι, τουτέστι, πληρῶσαι τὰς κραυγὰς τῆς πρώτης ἀγωγῆς, ἀπαρνήσῃ με τρίς. [Hoper de eipen asaphōs ho Matthaios, hoti prin alektora phōnēsai hērmēneusen ho Markos, hoti prin phōnēsai dis. For cocks are accustomed often to crow in one stretch, then, as it were, to sleep, and after some time to begin crowing again. II, 693 In galli cantu sunt duo mirabilia: Unum, quod tantulum animal tam contenta voce canit, ut longissime audiatur. Alterum, quod statis horis canit, at-que illo ipso tempore, quo reliquae aves somno indulgent. Prius mire depraedicant Talmudici in tractatu Ioma fol. 20. b. ubi dicit non nemo de gallo sacro Hierosolymis, והיה קולו נשמע משלוש פרסאות מעשה באגריפס המלך שהיה בא בדרך ושמע קולו בשלוש פרסאות וכשבא לביתו שיגר לו מתנות, Vox ejus audiebatur a tribus parasangis. Exemplo sit rex Agrippa, qui, cum iter faceret, illum audivit a tribus parasangis, et cum domum venisset, misit illi munera. Quod leve est prae eo, quod ibidem habet alius, ונשמע קולו ביריחו, Ierichuntem usque vox ejus audiebatur. Nam, ut asserit Rabbi Iochanan, מירושלם ליריחו עשרה פרסי, Ex urbe Ierusalem ad Ierichuntem decem sunt parasangae. Quae quidem fabulosa sunt: sed veriora, quae Cardanus, De subtilitate lib. 10. Gallus, si rarum esset animal, non solum forma, sed cantu admiratione dignum esset. Exultat voce, proculque ea exauditur: et nocte etiam ad mille et amplius passus. De ejusdem nocturno cantu statis horis sic Alkazuinius, ومن عجايب مُعْرِفَتُهُ ساعات الليل فان الليل اذا كان خمسة عشر ساعه يقسط اصواته عليها كما كان يقسطه عليها وهو تسع ساعات وذلك بالهام من الله II, 694 E mirabilibus (naturae ejus illud est), quod novit horas noctis: adeoque, cum nox est horarum quindecim, pro ejus ratione dispensat cantus suos; quomodo etiam dispensat, cum solum est horarum novem, idque per Dei inspirationem. Et Da- mir, واعظم ما فيه من العجايب معرفه الاوقات col. 124 الليلية فيقسط اصواته عليها تقسيطا لا يكاد يغادر منه شيا سوا طال او قصر ويوالي صياحه قبل الفجر وبعده فسبحان من هداه لذلك In eo maxime mirabile illud est, quod novit tempora noctis, et juxta ea tam aequa lance cantus suos dispenset, ut ex iis vix quidquam demat, sive longiora sint, aut bre- viora. Et cantus ejus perpetuo praecedat et sequatur auroram. Laus illi sit, qui eum ad id instituit. Ex Chronicis Espahan Mahometem suo loco docebimus, rei tantae causas exquirentem, ad fabulas confugere. Fingit enim, Deum habere gallum album, cujus alae smaragdis, hyacinthis et margaritis distinctae sint, et ab ortu ad occasum expansae; et caput sub throno gloriae; et pedes in summo aëre: illumque adeo quotidie mane precum tempus indicere, atque audiri ab omnibus coeli et terrae incolis, praeter homines et daemones. Et propterea, inquit, يجيبه ديوك الارض respondent illi galli terrae. Quae partim ex Hebraeis petita esse probavimus. Sed et e Graecis Callisthenes aliquid divinum agnoscit in galli cantu. Unde Cicero, De Divinatione lib. 2. Quid in mente venit Callistheni dicere, Deos gallis signum dedisse cantandi? Tamen apud eundem Democritus optimis verbis causam explicat, cur ante lucem galli canant. Depulso enim de pectore, et in omne corpus diviso, et modificato cibo, cantus edere quiete satiatos. Et nostro saeculo vir doctissimus, Praecipue circa noctis canit meridiem, vel proxime ante solis exortum. Nimirum partim ubi sol dimidiam itineris nocturni partem confecerit, partim ex quo ad nostrum pertigerit finitorem, vel circiter, gallo sensus solvuntur. Quippe animal hoc vi naturae, sive a temperamenti proprietate, sic a sole afficitur, ut tum expergefiat. Gaudens igitur, tum quiete suaviter percepta, tum quia sentiat non sic ut ante pectus vel stomachum gravari cibo (ut qui per totum jam corpus sit digestus) laetitiam cantu et alarum plausu testatur. Simul etiam, quia nocturna aëris constitutione calidus ac tenuis galli sanguis halitus ac vapores in musculis excitavit: eo alas complodens, et gutture cantillans vapores studet una cum pituita frigore noctis collecta dissipare, haud secus ac animalium alia vel pandiculatione vel oscitatione flatus propellunt. In quibus hoc solum probo, quod hoc animal dicitur a sole affici. Sed cur a sole afficiatur, id ipsum est, quod quaeritur, et quidem frustra, quia pendet ex occulta naturae proprietate. Nam, quae de gaudio, ex cibi concoctione percepto, afferuntur, si vera essent, cum in gallo, qui valet, concoctio tam cita sit, II, 695 aut citior etiam hyeme, quam aestate, quia tum intensior est stomachi calor, per quem cibi concoquuntur, hyemale gallicinium non esset aestivo tardius. At res se habet multo aliter. Matutinus enim galli cantus hic fit hyeme tribus aut quatuor horis serius, quam aestate. Et in reliquis, quae hic addunt, par est ratio. Itaque amplius disquirendum est.

II, 693 In the crowing of the cock there are two marvels. One is that so small an animal crows with so strained a voice that it is heard from very far away. The other is that it crows at fixed hours, and at the very time when the other birds give themselves to sleep. The former is wonderfully extolled by the Talmudists in the tractate Yoma, fol. 20b, where someone says of the sacred cock at Jerusalem: והיה קולו נשמע משלוש פרסאות מעשה באגריפס המלך שהיה בא בדרך ושמע קולו בשלוש פרסאות וכשבא לביתו שיגר לו מתנות, Its voice was heard from three parasangs. Let King Agrippa serve as an example: while he was on a journey, he heard it from three parasangs, and when he had come home, he sent it gifts. This is slight compared with what another says in the same place: ונשמע קולו ביריחו, its voice was heard as far as Jericho. For, as Rabbi Yochanan asserts, מירושלם ליריחו עשרה פרסי, From the city of Jerusalem to Jericho there are ten parasangs. These things are indeed fabulous; but truer is what Cardano says, De subtilitate book 10: The cock, if it were a rare animal, would be worthy of admiration not only for its form but for its crowing. It exults with its voice, and it is heard far off: at night even a thousand paces and more. Of its nocturnal crowing at fixed hours Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] says: ومن عجايب معرفته ساعات الليل فان الليل اذا كان خمسة عشر ساعه يقسط اصواته عليها كما كان يقسطه عليها وهو تسع ساعات وذلك بالهام من الله [wa-min ʿajāyib maʿrifatuhu sāʿāt al-layl fa-inna al-layl idhā kāna khamsata ʿashar sāʿa yuqassiṭ aṣwātahu ʿalayhā kamā kāna yuqassiṭuhu ʿalayhā wa-huwa tisʿ sāʿāt wa-dhālika bi-ilhām min Allāh] II, 694 Among the marvels (of its nature is this), that it knows the hours of the night: so that, when the night is of fifteen hours, it apportions its crowings in proportion to it; just as it also apportions them when it is of only nine hours, and this by the inspiration of God. And Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: واعظم ما فيه من العجايب معرفه الاوقات col. 124 الليلية فيقسط اصواته عليها تقسيطا لا يكاد يغادر منه شيا سوا طال او قصر ويوالي صياحه قبل الفجر وبعده فسبحان من هداه لذلك [wa-aʿẓam mā fīhi min al-ʿajāyib maʿrifat al-awqāt al-layliyya fa-yuqassiṭ aṣwātahu ʿalayhā taqsīṭan lā yakādu yughādir minhu shayʾan sawāʾ ṭāla aw qaṣura wa-yuwālī ṣiyāḥahu qabl al-fajr wa-baʿdahu fa-subḥān man hadāhu li-dhālika] The most marvellous thing in it is that it knows the times of the night, and according to them distributes its crowings with so even a balance that it omits hardly anything of them, whether they be longer or shorter. And its crowing continually precedes and follows the dawn. Praise be to Him who taught it this. From the Chronicles of Isfahan we shall show in its place that Muhammad, seeking the causes of so great a thing, took refuge in fables. For he imagines that God has a white cock whose wings are adorned with emeralds, jacinths and pearls and spread from east to west, whose head is beneath the throne of glory and whose feet are in the highest air; and that it thus announces the time of prayer every morning and is heard by all the inhabitants of heaven and earth except men and demons. And therefore, he says, يجيبه ديوك الارض [yujībuhu duyūk al-arḍ] the cocks of the earth answer it. We have shown that this was partly taken from the Hebrews. But among the Greeks too Callisthenes recognizes something divine in the crowing of the cock. Hence Cicero, De Divinatione book 2: What came into Callisthenes' mind, to say that the gods gave cocks the signal to crow? Yet in the same author Democritus explains in excellent words the cause why cocks crow before daylight. For when the food has been driven out of the breast, distributed through the whole body and assimilated, they utter their crowings, satisfied with rest. And in our own age a very learned man writes: It crows chiefly about the midnight, or just before sunrise. That is, partly when the sun has completed half of its nocturnal course, partly from the time when it has reached, or nearly reached, our horizon, the cock's senses are released. For this animal, by a force of nature, or from a property of its temperament, is so affected by the sun that it then wakes up. Rejoicing, therefore, both in the rest it has sweetly enjoyed and because it feels that its breast or stomach is not weighed down by food as before (since this has now been digested through the whole body), it attests its joy by crowing and by clapping its wings. At the same time also, because the cock's warm and thin blood, through the nocturnal condition of the air, has raised exhalations and vapours in the muscles, it therefore claps its wings and, singing with its throat, strives to dispel the vapours together with the phlegm gathered by the cold of the night, just as other animals expel wind by stretching or yawning. Of this I approve only the statement that this animal is affected by the sun. But why it is affected by the sun is the very thing in question, and indeed sought in vain, since it depends on a hidden property of nature. For as to what is alleged about joy felt from the digestion of food: if it were true, then, since in a healthy cock digestion is as quick II, 695 or even quicker in winter than in summer, because the heat of the stomach by which food is digested is then more intense, the winter cockcrow would not be later than the summer one. But the matter is quite otherwise. For the morning crowing of the cock here takes place in winter three or four hours later than in summer. And the same reasoning applies to the other things they add here. So the question must be investigated further.

Hence some interpreters have come to doubt the cockcrow mentioned by the writers of the Gospels. For my part, I would count that Talmudic rule among the fictitious prohibitions which the petty subtlety of the Jewish masters drew out of the simple laws of Moses, but which no one heeded outside the shade of the schools. Certainly, if one wishes to argue the matter from the Talmud, many more passages from the same Talmud can be set against that passage of Bava Kama in which cocks and cockcrow are mentioned, some of which Bochart has cited above. And in the tractate Berachoth we read of a cock that, because it had pierced an infant's skull at the spot where it is most tender and killed the infant, was punished by stoning by sentence of the Sanhedrin. See Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], Lexic. Chald. p. 2654. But if anyone is unwilling to accept this, let him follow the opinion of Reland [=Adriaan Reland, Dissertationes miscellaneae (1706–08)] (in the oration de Galli cantu Hierosolymis audito, Rotterdam 1709, 8vo), that Pilate, or the other Romans living in the city, being either wholly indifferent to or ignorant of Jewish institutions, kept cocks or hens, either for food or for the sake of auspices. For the opinions of others, listed and assessed, see Wolf, Curae Philologg. on Matt 26:34, vol. 1, p. 378 ff. One may also compare Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis, Supplementa ad lexica hebraica (1784–92)], Einleit. in das N. T. vol. 1, p. 71. 72.
