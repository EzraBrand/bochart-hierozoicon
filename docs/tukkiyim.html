De Pavonibus.

On Peacocks. ¶

Semel singulis tri- col. 136 enniis ibat (Solomonis) classis in Tharsis, et afferebat aurum et argentum, dentes elephantinos, simias et pavones. Itaque cur hos sequi maluerit Deodatius ὁ μακαρίτης, nullus capio. A recepta versione recedere parum tutum est, nisi graves causae subsint, quales hic nullas video. There is a notable passage on the peacock in 1 Reg. X, 22. and 2 Chr 9:21: Once every three col. 136 years the fleet (of Solomon) went to Tarshish, and brought gold and silver, elephants' teeth, apes and peacocks. The Hebrew name of the peacocks, תכיים thoucijim, or תוכיים thuccijim, the Greek translators left ἀνεϱμήνευτον [anermēneuton] [untranslated], because they did not understand it. But the Chaldee, the Syriac, the Arabic and the Latin translators explain it as peacocks, and so do all the Hebrews of any repute: R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], R. Levi, Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]. For those who, in Kimchi's Lexicon, render it גאטו מימון gato maimon, that is, long-tailed monkey, are obscure and unknown. So too are those who render it parrot, as Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] notes in the margin R1. Therefore I cannot at all understand why the late Diodati preferred to follow them. It is hardly safe to depart from the received version unless there are weighty reasons, and I see none here. On the origin of the word, Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] says in his Thesaurus: The word seems foreign, for its etymology is unknown. Perhaps it is from striking, by onomatopoeia. The French, to be sure, express onomatopoeically the sound of someone knocking at a door with the words toc, toc; hence toquer means to strike, as does toccare in Italian. But since such things are largely arbitrary: among the Hebrews תוך תוך thoc thoc is said of the sound of a boiling pot. Thus in Berachoth ch. 6, fol. 39a: דיתיב אבי תפי ועביד תוך תוך, That which is placed in the place of setting (of pots on the fire), and makes thoc thoc. But however you understand it, this has nothing to do with the peacock. Nor can any reason be devised why it should be named from striking. I therefore hold a different view: namely, that peacocks are called תוכיים thuccijim by an inversion of the word, for כותיים cuthijim, as if you were to say Cuthaeans, or Persians. For Cuth (var. Chuth) is in Persia. Josephus, Antiquities, book 9, near the end: Οἱ δὲ μετοικισθέντες εἰς Σαμάρειαν Χουθαῖοι (ταύτῃ γὰρ ἐχρῶντο μέχρι δεῦρο τῇ προσηγορίᾳ, διὰ τὸ ἐκ τῆς Χουθᾶς καλουμένης χώρας μεταχθῆναι, αὕτη δ᾽ ἐστὶν ἐν τῇ Περσίδι, καὶ ποταμὸς τοῦ τ᾽ ἔχων ὄνομα) [Hoi de metoikisthentes eis Samareian Chouthaioi (tautē gar echrōnto mechri deuro tē prosēgoria, dia to ek tēs Chouthas kaloumenēs chōras metachthēnai, hautē d᾽ estin en tē Persidi, kai potamos tou t᾽ echōn onoma)]. The Cuthaeans deported into Samaria (for so they were then called, because they had been transferred from the region of Chutha, which is in Persia, where there is also a river of the same II, 710 name). Nor is it anything new for birds to be named after places. Thus the pheasant is named from the Phasis; African and Numidian birds from Numidia and Africa; Seleucides from Seleucia; Garamantides from the Garamantes. And, not to seek examples further afield, the very peacock we are discussing is often called the Median or Persian bird by the ancients. Aristophanes in the Birds: ¶

For Babylonia rears a great many peacocks, variegated with colours of every kind. For although the true homeland of peacocks was India, as we prove elsewhere, they were nevertheless called Median and Persian because they were first carried from India into Persia and Media, and from there gradually dispersed into Judaea, Egypt, Greece, Latium and other regions of the world. Indeed, the very peacocks which Solomon's fleet brought into Judaea may have been obtained from Persia. For it is not likely that on that three-year voyage, by which they made their way to Taprobane (as we show in its place), they always kept a straight course. Rather, by various detours they sought out whatever served their purpose: also in the Persian Gulf, where lay the most celebrated islands of the Phoenicians, Tyrus or Tylus, and Aradus, not far distant from Persia. The name תוכיים thuccijim could also have been given to peacocks after Solomon's time, once they began to be brought overland from Persia and Babylon into Judaea. ¶

Now, that they were called תוכיים with the letters inverted, instead of כותיים, is not without precedent, whether this happened through the carelessness of copyists or, as I believe more likely, was done by deliberate II, 711 design, so that the name of the bird might be distinguished from the name of the people. Thus you may see the same wood called אלמוגים almugin, 1 Reg. X, 11. and אלגומים algumim, 2 Chr 2:7. The same city in which Joshua was buried is called תמנת־סרח Thimnath-Serach, Josh 24:30 and תמנת־חרס Thimnath-Cheres, Judg 2:9. The same son of Dan is called חושים Chusim, Gen 46:23 and שוחם Sucham, Num 26:42. The same man is רחום Rechum, Neh 12:3 and חרם Charim, in the same place, verse 15. Another is שמלי Samlai, Esd. II, 46. and שלמי Salmai, Neh 7:48. So the sheep is called now כבס chebes, now כשב cheseb. And the Ethiopians sometimes invert Hebrew words entirely. Thus רחם, to have pity, is for them ምሕረ፡ [məḥəra]; קלס, to mock, for them ሰለቀ፡ [salaqa]; זנק, to leap, for them ቀነጸ፡ [qanaṣa]. Therefore, on the same pattern, peacocks could have been called תוכיים instead of כותיים. However, I propose this, I do not decide it, and I leave everyone free to judge for himself. ¶

If the peacock were unknown, we would ask what moved Solomon to fetch from so far away, along with treasures of gold and silver, birds of no great use. Or why the sacred writers thought it worth recording? But these doubts are resolved by the appearance of this exceptional bird, which seems made for the very purpose of feasting the eyes of beholders with a delightful spectacle. Accordingly Varro asserts that nature gave it the prize for beauty among birds. And after the Greeks, who vied with one another in describing it, Tertullian paints it thus, De Pallio ch. 3: And yet the peacock too has a garment of feathers, and indeed one of the finest: deeper than any purple where the neck blooms, more gilded than any border where the back gleams, more flowing than any trailing robe where the tail lies. Many-coloured, and diverse-coloured, and changing-coloured. Never itself, always other; and yet always itself when other. In short, changed as often as it is moved. The Arabs too have toiled in the same arena, among them Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: تري في وسط كل ريش دايرة من الذهب مخلطة بالزرقة والصفرة والخضرة وغيرها من الاوان التي [tarī fī wasaṭ kull rīsh dāyira min al-dhahab mukhallaṭa bi-l-zurqa wa-l-ṣufra wa-l-khuḍra wa-ghayrihā min al-awān allatī] col. 138 تلايم بعضها بعضا لينشا من تركيبها [talāyam baʿḍuhā baʿḍan li-yansha min tarkībihā] You will see in the middle of all its feathers a golden circle, mixed with blue, yellow and green, and other colours which harmonize with one another, so that from their combination the highest beauty arises. He adds that gold, laid over any colours whatever, is nothing compared with this splendour, although it is scarcely dug out of the earth and only refined with much labour; whereas so beautiful a bird emerges from the egg of its own accord, without human effort. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] has much more, which I have preferred to pass over, lest I weary the reader on a subject most hackneyed of all. Glory be to him who created the peacock, a bird of exceptional beauty, which in the variety of its colours resembles a bride. Scales are set into its feathers. In its dwelling many suns rise. On its crown a little tree a) is planted. It struts in pomp as though in a violet garment. In Arabic, as though in a violet. But there violet is taken for a garment of violet, or purple colour, which stands out most among the other colours in the neck and tail of the peacock. Hence Tertullian above: Deeper than any purple dye, where the neck blooms. And Achilles Tatius, book 1: Ὁ δὲ τοῦ ταῶ λειμὼν εὐανθέστερος· πεφύτευται γὰρ αὐτῷ καὶ χρυσὸς ἐν τοῖς πτεροῖς, κύκλῳ δὲ τὸ ἁλουργὲς τὸν χρυσὸν περιθέει τὸν ἴσον κύκλον [Ho de tou taō leimōn euanthesteros; pephyteutai gar autō kai chrysos en tois pterois, kyklō de to halourges ton chryson peritheei ton ison kyklon]. The meadow of the peacock (than any meadow) is more flowery: for in its feathers gold also is set, and purple surrounds the gold in an equal circle. This is a monstrous opinion. For who has ever heard tell of peacock bones being carved or engraved, or used for any work in the manner of ivory? Yet one objection can be raised, namely that the Hebrew הבנים hobnim is a plural, whereas the word for ebony is only singular. It is indeed inflected and spelled in various ways. For one says haec ebenus, or evenus, or hebenus, and hoc ebenum, or evenum, II, 718 or hebenum. And in Greek ἡ ἔβενος [hē ebenos], or ἔβελος [ebelos], as in the Suda; or ἐβένη [ebenē], as in Theophrastus. But in the plural, ἐβένους [ebenous] in Greek was said only by Symmachus; and in Latin ebenos or ebena only, as far as I remember, by Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] and Mercerus in their rendering of this passage. Junius answers that it is named in the plural by the Hebrews because there are two kinds of ebony. That is indeed what Theophrastus teaches, Hist. book 4, ch. 5, where he treats the trees of India: Ἴδιον δὲ καὶ ἡ ἐβένη τῆς χώρας ταύτης. Ταύτης δὲ δύο γένη τὸ μὲν εὔξυλον, καὶ καλόν· τὸ δὲ φαῦλον. σπάνιον δὲ τὸ καλὸν, θάτερον δὲ πολύ. Τὴν δὲ χρόαν οὐ θησαυριζομένη λαμβάνει τὴν εὔχρουν, ἀλλ᾽ εὐθὺς τῇ φύσει ἐστὶ δὲ τὸ δένδρον θαμνῶδες, ὥσπερ ὁ κύτισος [Idion de kai hē ebenē tēs chōras tautēs. Tautēs de dyo genē to men euxylon, kai kalon; to de phaulon. spanion de to kalon, thateron de poly. Tēn de chroan ou thēsaurizomenē lambanei tēn euchroun, all᾽ euthys tē physei estin de to dendron thamnōdes, hōsper ho kytisos]. Ebony too is peculiar to this region. Of it there are two kinds: one of excellent wood, and good; the other bad. The good one is rare, but the other common. Nor does ebony acquire its fine colour by being stored away, but has it at once from nature. Moreover it is like a shrub, as the cytisus is. That this is the true meaning of Theophrastus Salmasius proves at length in his Exercitations on Solinus, and not the one that Pliny, book 12, chapter 4, ascribes to him in these words: There are two kinds of it. The rarer is the better one, tree-like, with a trunk col. 143 free of knots, its wood of a black lustre, and pleasing at once even without craftsmanship. The other is shrubby, like the cytisus, and spread over all India. Good ebony is not called εὔξυλος [euxylos] because it is tree-like, that is, large like a tree, but from the excellence of its wood. Nor is being θαμνώδης [thamnōdēs] peculiar to the bad kind; it is common to both. And there θαμνῶδες [thamnōdes] does not mean fruticosum [shrubby], that is, dense with many twigs, but behaving like a shrub, as all bushes do. Dioscorides also makes two kinds of ebony, but distinguishes them by place: Ἔβενος κρατίστη ἡ Αἰθιοπικὴ, καὶ μέλαινα, καὶ μὴ ἔχουσα κτηδόνας [Ebenos kratistē hē Aithiopikē, kai melaina, kai mē echousa ktēdonas]; The best ebony is the Ethiopian, black, and without veins running through it like the teeth of a comb. The Arabic translator calls them طبقات [ṭabaqāt]. And a little later: Ἔστι δέ τις καὶ Ἰνδικὴ, ἔχουσα διαφύσεις λευκὰς, καὶ κιῤῥὰς, καὶ σπίλους ὁμοίως πυκνούς· πλὴν βελτίων ἡ πρώτη [Esti de tis kai Indikē, echousa diaphyseis leukas, kai kirrhas, kai spilous homoiōs pyknous; plēn beltiōn hē prōtē]. There is also another, Indian, with white and reddish lines running through it, and frequent spots as well. But the former is better. So also Isidore: Ebony grows in India and Ethiopia; when cut it hardens into stone; its wood is black, and its bark smooth like that of the laurel. But the Indian is spotted, with small white and tawny markings. The Ethiopian, however, which is held to be superior, is spotted nowhere, but black, smooth and horn-like. And Serapion [=Serapion, Liber de simplicibus medicinis (the Arabic-Latin pharmacopoeia)]: It has two species, namely Absi and Inda. And Absi is a black wood, without lines, etc. In the Indian, however, there are white lines, and of a reddish colour, etc. This is taken from Dioscorides. Absi is حبشي [ḥabashī] Habasi, that is, Ethiopian. Common to all of these is that they recognize only two species of ebony. To which the Hebrew הבנים hobnim does not correspond, because it is a plural, not a dual noun. For the dual would be hobnajim. So something else must be sought. Fuller indeed refers this plural to the palangae of ebony, which were brought from India and Ethiopia in great II, 719 numbers. Herodotus and Arrian in the Periplus call them φάλαγγας [phalangas]; Pliny palangae or phalangae, the spelling varying; that is, round poles, such as are placed under ships, or on which several men carry the same load. Pollux, book 7, ch. 33, §. 9: Τὰ δὲ τῶν νεῶν ξύλα, οἷς ὑποβληθεῖσιν ἐφέλκονται αἱ νῆες, φάλαγγες, καὶ φαλάγγια [Ta de tōn neōn xyla, hois hypoblētheisin ephelkontai hai nēes, phalanges, kai phalangia]. Hesychius: Φαλάγγια· στρόγγυλα ξύλα, καὶ σύμμετρα [Phalangia; strongyla xyla, kai symmetra]. Nonius: Palangae is the name for round poles which are put under ships when they are drawn down to the sea, or when they are hauled up onto the shore. The Glosses: Phalangarii, Οἱ τοῖς δοκοῖς ἀποκρεμῶντες βάρη, καὶ πλείονες ἅμα βαστάζοντες [Hoi tois dokois apokremōntes barē, kai pleiones hama bastazontes]. Again Nonius: We call palangarii those who carry some load on poles. The origin of the word seems to be Hebrew. For in Hebrew פלך pelec, or in archaic form phelec, is baculus [staff], 2 Sam 3:29. And in Arabic فلك [falaka] phalaca means to be round. Hence the round ebony staves which the Tyrian merchants called פלכין pelakin or phelakin could have been called φάλαγγες [phalanges], phalanges, or palanges. Callixenus, in book 5 of Athenaeus, names ἐβένου κορμοὺς [ebenou kormous], logs of ebony. Apuleius, in the first Apology, staves of ebony, though some prefer to read loculos [little boxes] there. These, then, whether staves or κορμοὺς [kormous], or these palangae of ebony, Fuller thinks are signified by the plural הבנים hobnim. But learned men seem to weary themselves here in vain, since the Hebrews scarcely name precious woods of this kind otherwise than with a plural word. Thus the tree from which the ark was made is called once in the singular שטה sitta, Isa 41:19. But in the plural שטים sittim more than twenty times in Exodus. And another tree, from which the beams and supports in the temple of Solomon were made, and the harps and lutes of the singers, is called only by a plural word, אלגומים algumim or אלמוגים almugim, 1 Reg. X, 11. 12. and 2 Chr 9:10–11. And in the Chaldee paraphrases of Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Jerusalem, Gen 50:1: Joseph laid his father בערס דסנדפין, on a couch of sindaphin, that is, as they hold, on a bed made of the most excellent kind of wood, so called with a plural word. col. 144 And I once suspected that it was so named after two villages of Egypt called سندف [Sndf] Sendapha. In his lignum شيز Siz, vel شيزي Siza, a loco sic appellatum: et lignum ساسم sasem, vel سمسم semsem, et in Plurali سماسم semasim eodem nomine dictum, quo sesamum, quamvis a sesamo diversissimum. Quo haec pertinent in Alcamus, الشيز والشيزي خشب اسود للقصاع وهو الابنوس والساسم او خشب الجوز وناحية بارض بادر بيجان Siz, vel Siza, nigrum lignum ad patinas conficiendas. Aut est ebenus, aut sasim, aut li-gnum nucis, et regio quaedam in terra Adarbigian (id est, Atropatia Strabonis et II, 720 Ptolemaei Atropatena), ex qua nempe hoc lignum affertur, et nomen sumit. Idem alibi, الساسم شجر اسود او الابنوس او الشيز او شجر يعمل منه القسي Sasim; arbor nigra, aut ebenus, aut siz, aut arbor, ex qua fiunt arcus. Damir in capite De سمسمة simsima, quod formicae rubrae genus est, ut Chaldaeis שומשמנא, dicit hoc idem lignum سمسم semsem vocari, et Plurali سماسم semasim, quod probat ex his pseudo-prophetae verbis de الجهنميين, id est, de iis, qui in gehenna torquentur, تخرجون من النار بعد ان يكونوا فيها فيخرجون كانها عيدان السماسم Exibunt ex igne post aliquam in eo moram; exibunt, inquam, tanquam ligna semasim, id est, pariter nigri propter adustionem ex igne. In quae verba commentatores multa afferunt absurdissima. Sed peritiores agnoscunt, ligna semasim esse ebenina ligna, aut ebeno atrore similia, ita, ut exusta videantur. Unde lux his Dioscoridis in capite De ebeno, Ἔνιοι δὲ τὰ σησάμινα, ἢ ἀκάνθινα ξύλα ἐμφερῆ ὄντα, ἀντὶ ἐβένου πωλοῦσι· Quae Marcellus Virgilius ita reddidit: Vendunt pro ebeno qui- dam spinae Indicae, aut mori, fallente similitudine, materiam. Putat nempe, pro σησάμινα legendum συκάμινα, quia sesamum nihil habet commune cum ebeno. Sed neque sycaminus, seu sycomorus, nisi quod utrumque lignum est. Cave igitur, ne quidquam mutes. Aliud enim hic sesamina, quam vulgo. Nempe ligna illius arboris, quae Arabice sasim et semsem appellatur, et in Plurali sema- sim. Itaque Dioscoridis Arabs interpres hic recte habet ومن الناس من ياخذ اعضان خشب بعض اصناف الشوك والخشب الذي يقال له سيساما فيبيعه بدل الابنوس لانه col. 145 شبيه به Sunt, qui sumunt lignum cujusdam spinae speciei, et lignum, quod sesamum vocant, et pro ebeno vendunt, quia illi simile est. Quid quod praeter Dioscoridem etiam Arrianus in Periplo meminit φαλάγγων σησαμίνων, καὶ ἐβενίνων, palangarum sesaminarum et ebeninarum, quae ex Indiae urbe Baryga- sis in Persidem afferuntur. Itaque haec lectio certa est et indubitata.

It should be noted in passing that the books of the Arabs mention certain woods so similar to ebony that some take them for ebony. Among these is the wood شيز [shīz] Siz, or شيزي [shīzī] Siza, so called from a place; and the wood ساسم [sāsam] sasem, or سمسم [simsim] semsem, in the plural سماسم [samāsim] semasim, called by the same name as sesame, although it is entirely different from sesame. To this belong these words in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الشيز والشيزي خشب اسود للقصاع وهو الابنوس والساسم او خشب الجوز وناحية بارض بادر بيجان [al-shīz wa-l-shīzī khashab aswad li-l-qiṣāʿ wa-huwa al-abanūs wa-l-sāsam aw khashab al-jawz wa-nāḥiya bi-arḍ bādr bījān] Siz, or Siza, a black wood for making dishes. It is either ebony, or sasim, or the wood of the walnut; and also a certain region in the land of Adarbigian (that is, the Atropatia of Strabo and II, 720 the Atropatena of Ptolemy), from which, namely, this wood is brought and takes its name. The same author elsewhere: الساسم شجر اسود او الابنوس او الشيز او شجر يعمل منه القسي [al-sāsam shajar aswad aw al-abanūs aw al-shīz aw shajar yuʿmal minhu al-qisī] Sasim: a black tree, or ebony, or siz, or a tree from which bows are made. Damir, in the chapter On سمسمة [simsima] simsima, which is a kind of red ant, like שומשמנא among the Chaldeans, says that this same wood is called سمسم [simsim] semsem, in the plural سماسم [samāsim] semasim. He proves this from these words of the pseudo-prophet about الجهنميين [al-jahannamiyyīn], that is, about those who are tormented in Gehenna: تخرجون من النار بعد ان يكونوا فيها فيخرجون كانها عيدان السماسم [takhrujūn min al-nār baʿd an yakūnū fīhā fa-yakhrujūn ka-annahā ʿīdān al-samāsim] They will come out of the fire after some stay in it; they will come out, I say, like the woods semasim, that is, equally black from being scorched by the fire. On these words the commentators offer many quite absurd things. But the more learned recognize that the semasim woods are ebony woods, or woods resembling ebony in blackness, so that they seem burnt. This throws light on these words of Dioscorides in the chapter On ebony: Ἔνιοι δὲ τὰ σησάμινα, ἢ ἀκάνθινα ξύλα ἐμφερῆ ὄντα, ἀντὶ ἐβένου πωλοῦσι· [Enioi de ta sēsamina, ē akanthina xyla empherē onta, anti ebenou pōlousi]. Marcellus Virgilius rendered this as follows: Some sell in place of ebony the timber of the Indian thorn, or of the mulberry, the resemblance deceiving. He thinks, namely, that σησάμινα [sēsamina] should be read συκάμινα [sykamina], because sesame has nothing in common with ebony. But neither has the sycamine or sycomore, except that both are wood. Beware, therefore, of changing anything. For sesamina here means something other than in common usage: namely, the wood of that tree which is called sasim and semsem in Arabic, and in the plural semasim. Accordingly the Arabic translator of Dioscorides rightly has here: ومن الناس من ياخذ اعضان خشب بعض اصناف الشوك والخشب الذي يقال له سيساما فيبيعه بدل الابنوس لانه col. 145 شبيه به [wa-min al-nās man yaʾkhudh aʿḍān khashab baʿḍ aṣnāf al-shawk wa-l-khashab alladhī yuqāl lahu sīsāmā fa-yabīʿuhu badal al-abanūs li-annahu shabīh bihi] There are some who take the wood of a certain kind of thorn, and the wood which they call sesamum, and sell it in place of ebony, because it resembles it. What is more, besides Dioscorides, Arrian too in the Periplus mentions φαλάγγων σησαμίνων, καὶ ἐβενίνων [phalangōn sēsaminōn, kai ebeninōn], beams of sesamine and of ebony, which are brought from the Indian city of Barygaza to Persia. This reading is therefore certain and beyond doubt. ¶
