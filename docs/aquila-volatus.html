Aquilae alae, volatus, nidus, visus, victus.

The eagle's wings, flight, nest, sight and food. ¶

Alas aquilae Scriptura commendat à magnitudine, Ezechielis XVII, 3. Aquila grandis, magnis alis, longis pennis, etc. Et ibi Graeci, Ὁ ἀετὸς ὁ μέ- col. 170 γας, ὁ μεγάλου πτέρυγος, ὁ μακρὸς τῇ ἐκτάσει. Quia et magnas habet alas aquila, et eas inter volandum quam maxime extendit. Unde τανύπτερος poetis, quasi alis extensam dicas. Hesiodus Theogoniae versu 523.

Scripture commends the eagle's wings for their size, Ezechielis XVII, 3. A great eagle with great wings, long-winged, etc. And there the Greeks have Ὁ ἀετὸς ὁ μέγας, ὁ μεγάλου πτέρυγος, ὁ μακρὸς τῇ ἐκτάσει [Ho aetos ho megas, ho megalou pterygos, ho makros tē ektasei]. col. 170 For the eagle both has great wings and spreads them as widely as possible in flight. Hence the poets call it τανύπτερος [tanypteros], as if one were to say with outstretched wings. Hesiod, Theogony line 523: ¶

Καὶ οἱ ἐπ᾽ αἰετὸν ὦρσε τανύπτερον,

Καὶ οἱ ἐπ᾽ αἰετὸν ὦρσε τανύπτερον, [Kai hoi ep᾽ aieton ōrse tanypteron,] ¶

Et illi excitavit aquilam, alis extensam. Et Pindarus Pyth. 5.

And he roused against him an eagle with outstretched wings. And Pindar, Pyth. 5: ¶

Τανύπτερος ἐν ὄρνιξιν αἰετὸς,

Τανύπτερος ἐν ὄρνιξιν αἰετὸς, [Tanypteros en ornixin aietos,] ¶

Aquila in avibus alis maxime extensa. Quod ita describit summus Vates libro ultimo Iliados versu 317.

The eagle, among birds the one with the most outstretched wings. This the greatest of poets describes thus in the last book of the Iliad, line 317: ¶

Ὅσση δ᾽ ὑψορόφοιο θύρη θαλάμοιο τέτυκται Ἀνέρος ἀφνειοῖο εὐκληῒς ἀραρυῖα, Τόσσ᾽ ἄρα τοῦ ἑκάτερθεν ἔσαν πτερά.

Ὅσση δ᾽ ὑψορόφοιο θύρη θαλάμοιο τέτυκται Ἀνέρος ἀφνειοῖο εὐκληῒς ἀραρυῖα, Τόσσ᾽ ἄρα τοῦ ἑκάτερθεν ἔσαν πτερά. [Hossē d᾽ hypsorophoio thyrē thalamoio tetyktai Aneros aphneioio euklēis araryia, Toss᾽ ara tou hekaterthen esan ptera.] ¶

Quanta autem est janua alti cubiculi viri divitis, bene serata, affabrefacta, tantae utrimque erant hujus alae. Et apud Gesnerum pag. 206. Georgius Fabricius nidi aquilae meminit, inter Misenam et Bresam, Germaniae oppida, anno 1500. reperti, ac trium in eo pullorum, quorum unius alae, per transversum extensae, septem ulnarum mensuram aequabant.

As great as is the door of a rich man's lofty chamber, well bolted and skilfully wrought, so great were its wings on either side. And in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], p. 206, Georg Fabricius mentions an eagle's nest found in the year 1500 between Meissen and Bresa, towns of Germany, with three chicks in it, the wings of one of which, stretched out crosswise, measured seven ells. ¶

His alis aquila rapidissime fertur et summo cum impetu, maxime cum in praedam ruit. Hinc Apulejus lib. 1. Floridorum aquilam ait in praedam super- ne sese ruere fulminis vice. Et apud Ciceronem lib. 2. De Divinatione cum ad interpretem retulisset cursor, aquilam se in somnis visum esse factum. Vicisti, ait ille, ista enim ave volat nulla vehementius. Proinde draconem, alioqui audax animal et intrepidum, alarum aquilae solo fragore fugari, veteres tradunt. Aelianus Hist. lib. 2. cap. 26. Καὶ τόν γε τῶν πτερῶν αὐτοῦ ῥοῖζον, καὶ τὸ τῶν θηρίων ἀτρεπτότατον, ὅμως κατέπτηξεν ὁ δράκων ἀκούσας μόνον καὶ παρα-χρῆμα κατέδυ, καὶ ἀσμένως ἠφανίσθη. Phile cap. 1.

With these wings the eagle is borne along most swiftly and with the greatest force, especially when it swoops on its prey. Hence Apuleius, Florida book 1, says the eagle hurls itself down from above upon its prey like a thunderbolt. And in Cicero, On Divination book 2, when a runner reported to an interpreter that he had dreamed he had become an eagle, the latter said: You have won, for no bird flies more vehemently than that one. Accordingly the ancients relate that the serpent, otherwise a bold and fearless animal, is put to flight by the mere rushing sound of the eagle's wings. Aelian, Hist. book 2, ch. 26: Καὶ τόν γε τῶν πτερῶν αὐτοῦ ῥοῖζον, καὶ τὸ τῶν θηρίων ἀτρεπτότατον, ὅμως κατέπτηξεν ὁ δράκων ἀκούσας μόνον καὶ παραχρῆμα κατέδυ, καὶ ἀσμένως ἠφανίσθη [Kai ton ge tōn pterōn autou rhoizon, kai to tōn thēriōn atreptotaton, homōs kateptēxen ho drakōn akousas monon kai parachrēma katedy, kai asmenōs ēphanisthē]. Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)], ch. 1: ¶

Τούτου δὲ καὶ τὸν ῥοῖζον ἀκούσας δράκων, Ἔπτηξέ τε παραχρῆμα, καὶ συνεστάλη, Καὶ τοὺς μυχοὺς ὑπῆλθε τοὺς ἀποκρύφους.

Τούτου δὲ καὶ τὸν ῥοῖζον ἀκούσας δράκων, Ἔπτηξέ τε παραχρῆμα, καὶ συνεστάλη, Καὶ τοὺς μυχοὺς ὑπῆλθε τοὺς ἀποκρύφους. [Toutou de kai ton rhoizon akousas drakōn, Eptēxe te parachrēma, kai synestalē, Kai tous mychous hypēlthe tous apokryphous.] ¶

Hinc est, quod aquilonem ventum appellari vult Festus a vehementissimo volatu ad instar aquilae. Et aquilam ipsam Graece ἀετὸν dici plerique putant ἀπὸ τοῦ II, 751 ἀΐσσειν, quod est, cum impetu ferri et irruere. Nos ab עיט ajit deduximus, quo nomine Hebraei avem rapacem vocant. Sed res fere eodem redit. Nam עיט ab העיט factum, id est, involando et irruendo. Haec eo pertinent, ut doceamus, non frustra esse, quod Scriptura toties eorum, qui ad pugnam ruunt, velocitatem et impetum confert cum aquilis, adeoque cum aquilis, in praedam involantibus. Ita Deut. XXVIII, 49. Excitabit Dominus contra te gentem remotam, ab extremis terrae finibus, כאשר ידאה הנשר, ut involat aquila. Graeci, ὡσεὶ ὅρμημα ἀετοῦ· et Hieronymus, in similitudinem aquilae, volantis cum impetu. Ita Iliad. φ΄. versu 252. Achilles, in Trojanos irruens, a Poeta describitur, αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων, aquilae impetus habens. Οἴματα enim Scholiasti sunt ὁρμήματα. Hesychio, Οἴματα· ἀετοῦ ὁρμήματα. Proinde idem poeta de Hectore Iliad. χ΄. versu 308.

Hence Festus holds that the wind aquilo [north wind] is so called from its most vehement flight, like that of an eagle. And most think that the eagle itself is called ἀετός [aetos] in Greek from ἀΐσσειν [aïssein], II, 751 that is, to rush with force and to swoop. We have derived it from עיט ajit, the name by which the Hebrews call a bird of prey. But it comes to much the same thing, for עיט is formed from העיט, that is, from flying at and swooping. All this serves to show that it is not without reason that Scripture so often compares the speed and force of those who rush into battle with eagles, and indeed with eagles swooping on their prey. Thus Deut 28:49. The Lord will raise against thee a nation from afar, from the farthest ends of the earth, כאשר ידאה הנשר, as the eagle flies. The Greeks: ὡσεὶ ὅρμημα ἀετοῦ [hōsei hormēma aetou]; and Jerome: like an eagle flying with force. So in Iliad 21, line 252, Achilles, rushing upon the Trojans, is described by the Poet as αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou oimat᾽ echōn], having the onrush of an eagle. For to the Scholiast οἴματα [oimata] are ὁρμήματα [hormēmata]; to Hesychius, Οἴματα· ἀετοῦ ὁρμήματα [Oimata: aetou hormēmata]. Likewise the same poet of Hector, Iliad 22, line 308: ¶

Οἴμησεν δὲ ἀλεὶς, ὥς τ᾽ αἰετὸς ὑψιπετήεις,

Οἴμησεν δὲ ἀλεὶς, ὥς τ᾽ αἰετὸς ὑψιπετήεις, [Oimēsen de aleis, hōs t᾽ aietos hypsipetēeis,] ¶

Irruit autem cum impetu conversus, ut aquila altivolans. Perperam igitur Philetas pro αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων legi voluit αἰετοῦ ὄμματ᾽ ἔχων, aquilae oculos habens. Neque enim hic agitur de aquilae oculis, sed de irruentis impetu. Eodem pertinet, quod Saulem et Ionathanem, viros pugnacissimos, David fuisse asserit aquilis velociores, 2 Sam. I, 23. Et quod de Chaldaeis scribit Ieremias IV, 13. Equos habent aquilis velociores. Et de illorum rege Ier. XLVIII, 40. Ut aquila involabit, et in Moabum extendet alas suas. Et rursus Ier. XLIX, 22. Ascendet ut aquila, et involabit, et extendet alas suas in Bosram. Et Thren. IV, 19. Leviores sunt coeli aquilis persecutores nostri.

Gathering himself, he swooped with force, like a high-flying eagle. Wrongly, therefore, did Philetas want to read αἰετοῦ ὄμματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou ommat᾽ echōn], having the eyes of an eagle, instead of αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou oimat᾽ echōn]. For the subject here is not the eagle's eyes but the force of its swoop. To the same point belongs David's statement that Saul and Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan], most warlike men, were swifter than eagles, 2 Sam 1:23. And what Jeremiah writes of the Chaldeans, IV, 13. Their horses are swifter than eagles. And of their king, Ier. XLVIII, 40. He shall fly as an eagle, and shall spread his wings over Moab. And again Ier. XLIX, 22. He shall come up as an eagle, and fly, and spread his wings over Bozrah. And Lam 4:19. Our persecutors are swifter than the eagles of heaven. ¶

Eadem similitudine, sed in diverso argumento, utitur Iob. IX, 26. Prae- col. 171 terierunt (dies mei) ceu naves אבה ebe, ut aquila volat ad escam. In quibus verbis, ut hoc obiter dicam, Ebe suspicor esse fluvii nomen cum Selomone et aliis: quia hodieque duo sunt amnes ابى Ebe, vel Ebbe nomine, in illis locis: quorum unus est prope Cupham, alter in Babyloniae regione VVasit, quae Graecorum est Mesene. Vide Clar. Golii Lexicon pag. 2. Posset et aliud afferri. Arabibus ابى aba Verbum est ad iter accingi. Itaque אניות אבה, naves ebe, dici queant naves veloces, ad iter accinctae, aut actuariae, si mavis, quae sunt expeditiores (1). Itaque tam hujusmodi navibus, quam avibus, quas diximus, id II, 752 est, aquilis ad escam properantibus, comparantur rapide decurrentis aevi nostri spatia, non solum, quia par utrobique est celeritas; sed et quia temporis, semel elapsi, nihil remanet, ut aquila in aëre, et navis in mari, nullum sui vestigium relinquunt: quod observat Solomon Prov. XXX, 19. Unde est, quod Sap. V, 10. 11. 12. utraque similitudine eleganter utitur libri auctor, quisquis est, ut doceat, nos vix dum natos statim ita deficere, ut vitae ante actae nihil supersit.

Job uses the same simile, but in a different context, Job 9:26. They have passed by col. 171 (my days) like ships אבה ebe, as the eagle flies to its food. In these words, to say this in passing, I suspect with Selomo and others that Ebe is the name of a river, because even today there are two rivers named ابى [Abī] Ebe or Ebbe in those regions: one of them is near Kufa, the other in the district of Babylonia called Wasit, which is the Mesene of the Greeks. See the Lexicon of the distinguished Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)], p. 2. Something else could also be adduced. In Arabic ابى [abā] aba is a verb meaning to gird oneself for a journey. So אניות אבה, ships ebe, could be called swift ships, girded for a journey, or actuariae [light vessels], if you prefer, which are the more nimble R1. Thus the spans of our swiftly running life are compared both with ships of this kind and with the birds we have mentioned, that II, 752 is, with eagles hastening to their food. This is not only because the speed is equal in both cases, but also because nothing remains of time once it has slipped away, just as the eagle in the air and the ship in the sea leave no trace of themselves, as Solomon observes, Prov 30:19. Hence in Wis 5:10–11. 12. the author of the book, whoever he is, elegantly uses both similes to teach that we, scarcely born, at once so fail that nothing is left of the life we have lived. ¶

Iam aliud in aquilarum volatu est observandum, eum non modo velocem, sed et constantem esse. Neque enim aquila cito fatiscit, sed, ubi res postulat, diutissime volat, et in loca remotiora fertur. Unde est, quod poetae fingunt, aquilas duas, a Iove emissas, unam ab extremo ortu, alteram ab extremo occasu, a volatu non destitisse, donec Delphis convenissent, aut in vicino Parnasso. Vide Pindarum Pyth. 4. et ibi Scholiastem, Strabonem lib. 9. Plutarchum De Orac. et Claudiani Prologum in Panegyricum Manlii Theodosii. Quale est Arabum commentum apud Damirem, وهو اشد الطير طيرانا واقوي جناحا حتي انه ليطير ما بين المشرق والمغرب في يوم واحد Ea est omnium avium vehementissimi volatus, et alas habet robustissimas: ita, ut, quidquid est inter ortum et occasum, una die pervolet. Proinde aquilinas alas Scriptura iis accommodat, qui procul amoventur. Sic Apoc. XII, 14. mulieri, quae est ecclesia, dantur duae alae aquilae magnae, quibus in desertum volet a conspectu serpentis. Et Psalm. CXXXIX, 9. Syrus interpres habet, Si efferam alas meam ܐܝܟ ܕܢܫܪܐ, sicut aquila, et habitem in extremo mari. Et Arabs, Si sublatus fuerim duabus alis كالنسر sicut aquila, ut incidam in fines maris. Aethiops quoque, Si sumpserim pennas meas ከመ ፡ ንስር ፡ sicut aquila, etc. Tamen Hebraea non habent כנפי נשר, alas aquilae: sed כנפי שחר, alas aurorae. Quod sequuntur Graeci, Chaldaeus, Vulgatus, Hieronymus et Hebraei omnes. Huc etiam pertinet, quod Proverb. XXIII, 5. fluxae opes leguntur aquilae alas assumere, quibus in coelum avolent, id est, velocissime elabantur, et procul a te amandentur, ut non sint amplius tui juris. Proinde in Luciani Timone divitiarum Deus Plutus claudicare se dicit, nec nisi lente progredi, cum a Iove ad aliquem mittitur, ut illius opes augeat. Ὁπόταν δὲ ἀπαλλάττεσθαι δέῃ, inquit, πτηνὸν ὄψει πολὺ τῶν ὀρνέων ὠκύτερον· Sed cum discedendum est, alatum videbis avibus longe celeriorem. Itaque cum dicat Deus Exod. XIX, 4. se, tanquam alis aquilarum, Israelitas, ex Aegypto exeuntes, sustulisse, Iudaei inde colligunt, eos cxx millia una hora confecisse a Rameses Succotham. Ita habet R. Selomo in Exod. XII, 37. מרעמסס סכתה מאה ועשרים מיל היו ובאו שם לפי שעה שנ״ ואשא אתכם על כנפי col. 172 II, 753 נשרים, A Rameses Succotham sunt itineris centum viginti millia, quae unius horae spatio confecerunt: prout dicitur, „Et eos tollam super alas aquilarum“.

Now another point must be observed in the flight of eagles: it is not only swift but also sustained. For the eagle does not tire quickly, but, when the situation demands, flies for a very long time and travels to quite distant places. Hence the poets imagine that two eagles, sent out by Jupiter, one from the farthest east and the other from the farthest west, did not cease from flight until they met at Delphi, or on neighbouring Parnassus. See Pindar, Pyth. 4, and the Scholiast there, Strabo, book 9, Plutarch, De Orac., and Claudian's Prologue to the Panegyric of Manlius Theodorus. Of the same kind is the fiction of the Arabs in Damiri: وهو اشد الطير طيرانا واقوي جناحا حتي انه ليطير ما بين المشرق والمغرب في يوم واحد [wa-huwa ashadd al-ṭayr ṭayarānan wa-aqwā janāḥan ḥattā annahu la-yaṭīru mā bayna al-mashriq wa-al-maghrib fī yawm wāḥid] It is of all birds the most vigorous in flight and has the strongest wings, so that it flies across all that lies between east and west in a single day. Accordingly Scripture applies eagle's wings to those who are carried far away. Thus in Rev 12:14 the woman, who is the church, is given two wings of a great eagle, with which to fly into the desert out of the serpent's sight. And in Ps 139:9 the Syriac translator has: If I raise my wings ܐܝܟ ܕܢܫܪܐ [איך דנשרא], like an eagle, and dwell in the uttermost sea. And the Arabic: If I were lifted up on two wings كالنسر [ka-al-nasr] like an eagle, so as to come to the ends of the sea. The Ethiopic also: If I take my wings ከመ [kama] ፡ ንስር [nəsər] ፡ like an eagle, etc. Yet the Hebrew does not have כנפי נשר, wings of an eagle, but כנפי שחר, wings of the dawn. This is followed by the Greeks, the Chaldee, the Vulgate translator, Jerome and all the Hebrews. Here also belongs the statement in Proverb. XXIII, 5. that fleeting riches take on the wings of an eagle, with which they fly off into heaven, that is, they slip away very swiftly and are removed far from you, so that they are no longer in your possession. Hence in Lucian's Timon Plutus, the god of riches, says that he limps and advances only slowly when he is sent by Jupiter to someone to increase his wealth. Ὁπόταν δὲ ἀπαλλάττεσθαι δέῃ [Hopotan de apallattesthai deē], he says, πτηνὸν ὄψει πολὺ τῶν ὀρνέων ὠκύτερον [ptēnon opsei poly tōn orneōn ōkyteron]· But when I must depart, you will see me winged, far swifter than birds. So, since God says in Exod 19:4 that he carried the Israelites, as they went out of Egypt, as on eagles' wings, the Jews infer from this that they covered 120 miles in one hour from Rameses to Succoth. So R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Exod 12:37: מרעמסס סכתה מאה ועשרים מיל היו ובאו שם לפי שעה שנ״ ואשא אתכם על כנפי col. 172 II, 753 נשרים, From Rameses to Succoth is a journey of one hundred and twenty miles, which they covered in the space of one hour, as it is said: “And I will carry them on eagles' wings”. ¶

Nor should this be omitted: there is no bird that rises higher than the eagle R2. Hence in the oracle given concerning the Athenians, ¶

Αἰετὸς ἐν νεφέλῃσι γενήσεαι, — —

Αἰετὸς ἐν νεφέλῃσι γενήσεαι, — — [Aietos en nephelēsi genēseai, — —] ¶

Aquila in nubibus fies, Scholiastes Aristophanis in Avibus significari ait illos τοσοῦτον αὐξηθήσεσθαι, ὅσον αἰετὸς τῶν ἄλλων ὀρνέων ἐν ταῖς νεφέλαις προὔχει, tantum augendos, quantum aquila in nubibus supra reliquas aves eminet. Aben Ezra quoque in Iob. XXIX, 30. אין עוף שיגבה יותר ממנו, Nulla est avis, quae sublimius se attollat. Et Kimchius in Ierem. XLIX, 16. eandem volare asserit גבוה מכל העופות, omnibus avibus celsius. Et Apulejus lib. 1. Floridorum, Aquila enimvero cum se nubium tenus altissime sublimavit, evecta alis, totum istud spatium, quo pluitur et ningitur, ultra quod cacumen nec fulmini, nec fulguri locus est, in ipso, ut ita dixerim, solo aetheris et fastigio hyemis. Et Horus lib. 2. cap. 53. ὑψηλότερον πάντων τῶν πετεινῶν ἵπταται, omnium avium altissime volat. Unde est, quod a poetis dicitur ὑψιπετὴς et ὑψιπετήεις a), altivolans. Quin ob id ipsum, quod in sublime volat, si Aristoteli credimus Histor. lib. 9. cap. 32. θεῖον οἱ ἄνθρωποί φασιν εἶναι μόνον τῶν ὀρνέων, homines solam avium divinam esse perhibent. Saltem hinc ortae fabulae de aquila in sinu Iovis, et fulminum administra. Et quod ejus pennas alii ad ignem elementarem, alii ad vicini solis radios exuri somniarunt. Ut de aquila taceam, e rogo Caesarum emitti solita, tanquam principis animam in coelum delatura. Ita enim credebatur. Et rogus consumptus est, ex quo evolavit aquila, ejus animam in coelum ferens. Herodianus lib. 4. Proinde in veterum nummis ab una parte videre est imperatoris effigiem, ab altera aquilam, cui sedeat imperator, cum hac inscriptione: CONSECRATIO S. C. Et, si rex, aut princeps aquila vehi se somniet, id illis mortem portendere, scribit Artemidorus lib. 2.

You will be an eagle in the clouds: the Scholiast on Aristophanes, Birds, says this means that they τοσοῦτον αὐξηθήσεσθαι, ὅσον αἰετὸς τῶν ἄλλων ὀρνέων ἐν ταῖς νεφέλαις προὔχει [tosouton auxēthēsesthai, hoson aietos tōn allōn orneōn en tais nephelais prouchei], will be increased as much as the eagle in the clouds stands out above the other birds. And Gregory of Nyssa in the book On the Soul: Πολλὰ τῶν ζώων ἐστὶ μὴ πίνοντα, ὡς ἐπί τινων ἀετῶν ἱστόρηται· [Polla tōn zōōn esti mē pinonta, hōs epi tinōn aetōn historētai] There are many animals that abstain from drink, as is related of certain eagles. Ἀλλ᾽ εἰσι δίψης καὶ κρύους ἀνώτεροι, [All' eisi dipsēs kai kryous anōteroi] And they know not the force of thirst and cold. ¶

Et ipse Suidas in Γαμψώνυχος haec habet: Οἷός ἐστιν ἀετὸς, ἱέραξ, κεγχρηΐς, ἄλλα τε τὰ μὴ πίνοντα· Qualis est aquila, accipiter, tinnunculus, aliaque animalia, quae non bibunt. Et Apostolius Adagio 62. Οὐ γὰρ ἁλίσκεται ἐν νεφέ-λαις ὁ ἀετὸς, καὶ δίψους ἀμείνων ἐστί. Non enim capitur in nubibus aquila, neque siti vincitur. Sed his in Iobo nihil contrarium. Is enim aquilas non scribit aquam bibere, sed sorbere sanguinem. Quod illi ipsi scribunt, qui potum aquilae denegant. Sic auctore Oppiano in Ixeuticis, apud Gesnerum, Sanguis animalium, quae ceperint, illis sufficit, sed aquam omnino non gustant. Ut Horus Apollo de accipitre, Ὕδωρ οὐ πίνει τὸ καθόλου, ἀλλ᾽ αἷμα· Aquam omnino non bibit, sed sanguinem. Et Demetrius in Hieracosophio, Πρῶτον μὲν τὸν φάρυγγα τοῦ ἁλόντος ὄρνιθος τέμνε, καὶ δίδου πίειν τῷ ἱέρακι θερμοῦ ἀναβλύζοντος αἵ-ματος· Primum quidem avis captae gulam incidito, ac profluentem sanguinem calidum accipitri praebe bibendum. Et Aelianus Historiae lib. 10. cap. 14. Σαρκῶν δὲ ἥδεται βορᾷ, καὶ πίνει αἷμα, καὶ τὰ νεόττια ἐκτρέφει· Carnium esu gaudet, et sanguinem bibit, iisdemque suos alit pullos. Hesychius etiam de vulture in Τόργιον, Τόργος· εἶδος γυπὸς αἱματοῤῥόφους· Torgos, vulturis genus, sanguinem sorbentis.

And Suidas himself, under Γαμψώνυχος [Gampsōnychos], has this: Οἷός ἐστιν ἀετὸς, ἱέραξ, κεγχρηΐς, ἄλλα τε τὰ μὴ πίνοντα· [Hoios estin aetos, hierax, kenchrēis, alla te ta mē pinonta] Such as the eagle, the hawk, the kestrel, and other animals that do not drink. And Apostolius, Adage 62: Οὐ γὰρ ἁλίσκεται ἐν νεφέλαις ὁ ἀετὸς, καὶ δίψους ἀμείνων ἐστί. [Ou gar haliksetai en nephelais ho aetos, kai dipsous ameinōn esti] For the eagle is not caught in the clouds, nor is it overcome by thirst. But there is nothing contrary to these in Job. For he does not write that eagles drink water, but that they suck up blood; which the very authors who deny the eagle drink themselves write. Thus, on the authority of Oppian in the Ixeutica, cited by Gesner: The blood of the animals they have caught suffices them, but water they do not taste at all. As Horapollo says of the hawk: Ὕδωρ οὐ πίνει τὸ καθόλου, ἀλλ᾽ αἷμα· [Hydōr ou pinei to katholou, all' haima] It does not drink water at all, but blood. And Demetrius in the Hieracosophion: Πρῶτον μὲν τὸν φάρυγγα τοῦ ἁλόντος ὄρνιθος τέμνε, καὶ δίδου πίειν τῷ ἱέρακι θερμοῦ ἀναβλύζοντος αἵματος· [Prōton men ton pharynga tou halontos ornithos temne, kai didou piein tō hieraki thermou anablyzontos haimatos] First cut the throat of the captured bird, and give the hawk the warm, gushing blood to drink. And Aelian, History book 10, ch. 14: Σαρκῶν δὲ ἥδεται βορᾷ, καὶ πίνει αἷμα, καὶ τὰ νεόττια ἐκτρέφει· [Sarkōn de hēdetai bora, kai pinei haima, kai ta neottia ektrephei] It delights in eating flesh, and drinks blood, and with these it rears its chicks. Hesychius also, of the vulture, under Τόργιον [Torgion]: Τόργος· εἶδος γυπὸς αἱματοῤῥόφους· [Torgos; eidos gypos haimatorrophous] Torgos, a kind of vulture that sucks blood. ¶

Unum in Iobo superest de aquila, Ubi sunt confossi, ibi est. Confossi, id est, confossorum, vel occisorum cadavera. Itaque ad hunc locum palam alludunt Christi verba Matth. XXIV, 28. et Luc. XVII, 37. Ubicunque est cadaver, ibi congregantur aquilae. Quod idem asserunt Arabes. Damir supra de aquila, Cadaver videt e quadringentis parasangis. Item, واذا وقع علي جيفة وعليها عقبان تاخرت ولم تاكل ما دام تاكل منها Et cum incidit in cadaver, super quod sunt ossifragae, recedit, nec reliquias eorum comedit. Item, اذا وقع علي جيفة وامتلا منها II, 758 لم يستطع الطيران حتي يثب وثبات يرفع بها نفسها طبقة بعد طبقة في الهوا Et cum in cadaver incidit, et eo repleta est, volare non potest, donec saltuatim progressa, per gra- dus in aërem se sustulerit. Sed proprie ad percnopterum, seu gypaeetum, id pertinet, degeneris aquilae genus vulturina specie, qua sola aquilarum exanima fert corpora, ut ex Aristotele b) habet Plinius c). A percnoptero percnon, quae et morphnos, multum differre, ex eodem Plinio constat. Illi enim percnos, seu morphnos, est aquila tertii generis, et percnopteros quarti. Et utriusque descriptiones non parum inter se discrepant. Sed Suidas, hoc cum nesciret, percno, seu morphno, quod idem est, hoc tribuit, quod percnopteri est proprium. Verba sunt: Μόρφνος· εἶδος ἀετοῦ· μόνοι δὲ οὗτοι τῶν ἀετῶν οὐ κυνη-γητοῦσιν, ἀλλὰ νεκροῖς σώμασι τρέφονται· Morphnos, aquilae species; ex aqui- col. 176 lis autem istae solae non venantur, sed aluntur cadaveribus. Pari errore Theo- phylactus aquilas, de quibus Christus, habuit pro vulturibus. Ὥσπερ, inquit, ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή· Ut ad cadaver aquilae, id est, vultures, statim congregantur. Et Tzetzes Chil. 5. cap. 9. negat, aquilarum genus ullo modo cadavera attingere, verba sunt:

One thing about the eagle remains in Job: Where the slain are, there is she. The slain, that is, the bodies of the slain or of the killed. Accordingly, the words of Christ in Matt 24:28 and Luke 17:37 plainly allude to this passage: Wheresoever the carcass is, there the eagles are gathered together. The Arabs assert the same. Damir, above, on the eagle: It sees a carcass from four hundred parasangs. Again: واذا وقع علي جيفة وعليها عقبان تاخرت ولم تاكل ما دام تاكل منها [wa-idhā waqaʿa ʿalī jīfa wa-ʿalayhā ʿiqbān taʾakhkharat wa-lam taʾkul mā dām taʾkul minhā] And when it comes upon a carcass on which there are ossifrages, it withdraws and does not eat their leavings. Again: اذا وقع علي جيفة وامتلا منها II, 758 لم يستطع الطيران حتي يثب وثبات يرفع بها نفسها طبقة بعد طبقة في الهوا [idhā waqaʿa ʿalī jīfa wa-imtalā minhā lam yastaṭiʿ al-ṭayarān ḥattā yathib wathabāt yarfaʿ bihā nafsahā ṭabaqa baʿd ṭabaqa fī al-hawā] And when it comes upon a carcass and has filled itself with it, it cannot fly until, advancing by leaps, it has raised itself into the air by degrees. But this properly belongs to the percnopterus, or gypaeetus, a degenerate kind of eagle of vulturine appearance, which alone of the eagles carries off dead bodies, as Pliny c) has it from Aristotle b). That the percnos, which is also the morphnos, differs greatly from the percnopterus is clear from the same Pliny. For in him the percnos, or morphnos, is the eagle of the third kind, and the percnopterus of the fourth. And the descriptions of the two differ considerably from each other. But Suidas, not knowing this, attributed to the percnos, or morphnos (which is the same), what is proper to the percnopterus. His words are: Μόρφνος· εἶδος ἀετοῦ· μόνοι δὲ οὗτοι τῶν ἀετῶν οὐ κυνηγητοῦσιν, ἀλλὰ νεκροῖς σώμασι τρέφονται· [Morphnos; eidos aetou; monoi de houtoi tōn aetōn ou kynēgētousin, alla nekrois sōmasi trephontai] Morphnos, a species of eagle; of the eagles these alone do not hunt, but feed on carcasses. col. 176 By a like error Theophylact took the eagles of which Christ speaks for vultures. Ὥσπερ, he says, ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή· [Hōsper epi nekron sōma synagontai oxeōs hoi aetoi, hoi gypes dēladē] As the eagles, that is, the vultures, gather at once to a carcass. And Tzetzes, Chil. 5, ch. 9, denies that the eagle kind touches carcasses in any way; his words are: ¶

Λέοντες θῆρες, σὺν αὐτοῖς καὶ ἀετῶν τὸ γένος, Νεκρῶν οὐκ ἀπογεύονται τὸ σύνολον σωμάτων·

Λέοντες θῆρες, σὺν αὐτοῖς καὶ ἀετῶν τὸ γένος, Νεκρῶν οὐκ ἀπογεύονται τὸ σύνολον σωμάτων· [Leontes thēres, syn autois kai aetōn to genos, Nekrōn ouk apogeuontai to synolon sōmatōn] ¶

Leones ferae, et cum ipsis aquilarum genus, mortua corpora plane non gustant. Cui effato tam generali nisi addantur exceptiones, non constabit sua veritas, ut jam allata abunde probant. Quae firmantur insuper ex verbis Solomonis, Proverb. XXX, 17. Oculum, qui patrem deridet matrisque disciplinam spernit, hunc effodiunt corvi vallis, et aquilae pulli comedent. Nempe in parentes rebellio tum crimen erat lethale, ut patet ex Exod. XXI, 15. 17. Levit. XX, 9. et Deuter. XXI, 18. Et damnatorum corpora manebant insepulta, ferisque et avibus carnivoris exposita. Nec frustra est, quod oculi meminit Solomon, cum in toto corpore pars nulla sit rapacium avium injuriis tam obvia. Unde est, quod Epi- ctetus, rationem explicans veteris adagii, Κρεῖττον εἶναι ἐς κόρακας ἀπελθεῖν, ἢ ἐς κόλακας· Satius esse, ire ad corvos, quam ad adulatores. Οἱ μὲν κόρακες, inquit, τῶν τελευτηκότων τοὺς ὀφθαλμοὺς λυμαίνονται, ὅταν αὐτῶν οὐδέν ἐστι χρεία· οἱ δὲ κόλακες τῶν ζώντων τὰς ψυχὰς διαφθείρουσι, καὶ τὰ τούτων ὄμματα τυφλώττουσι· Corvi quidem mortuorum oculos tum demum vastant, cum nullius sunt usus: sed adulatores vivorum animas corrumpunt et oculos excaecant (4).

Lions, the beasts, and with them the eagle kind, do not taste dead bodies at all. Unless exceptions are added to so general a statement, its truth will not stand, as what has already been adduced amply proves. These are further confirmed by the words of Solomon, Proverb. XXX, 17: The eye that mocks his father and despises the instruction of his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it. For rebellion against parents was then a capital crime, as is clear from Exod 21:15 17. Lev 20:9 and Deuter. XXI, 18. And the bodies of the condemned remained unburied, exposed to wild beasts and carnivorous birds. Nor is it without reason that Solomon mentions the eye, since in the whole body no part is so exposed to the attacks of birds of prey. Hence Epictetus, explaining the sense of the old adage Κρεῖττον εἶναι ἐς κόρακας ἀπελθεῖν, ἢ ἐς κόλακας· [Kreitton einai es korakas apelthein, ē es kolakas] It is better to go to the ravens than to flatterers, says: Οἱ μὲν κόρακες τῶν τελευτηκότων τοὺς ὀφθαλμοὺς λυμαίνονται, ὅταν αὐτῶν οὐδέν ἐστι χρεία· οἱ δὲ κόλακες τῶν ζώντων τὰς ψυχὰς διαφθείρουσι, καὶ τὰ τούτων ὄμματα τυφλώττουσι· [Hoi men korakes tōn teleutēkotōn tous ophthalmous lymainontai, hotan autōn ouden esti chreia; hoi de kolakes tōn zōntōn tas psychas diaphtheirousi, kai ta toutōn ommata typhlōttousi] Ravens ravage the eyes of the dead only when they are of no further use; but flatterers corrupt the souls of the living and blind their eyes R4. ¶
