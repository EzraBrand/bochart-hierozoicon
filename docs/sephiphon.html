III, 205 Chapter 12. col. 416 ¶

Probatur, שפיפון sephiphon, Gen. XLIX, 17. esse Cerasten et Haemorrhoum.

It is proved that שפיפון sephiphon, Gen 49:17, is the cerastes and the haemorrhous. ¶

Gen. XLIX, 17. cur שפיפון sephiphon, Graeci ἐγκαθήμενον, et Samaritani ࠊࠌࠉࠍ · insidiantem verterint, nullus capio. Sed alios scio multo rectius statuere, שפיפון esse serpentis genus. Id enim aperte clamant Iacobi verba, quae sic habent: DAN erit serpens super viam, שפיפון sephiphon super semitam, qui mordet calces equi, et cadit sessor ejus retrorsum. Itaque sesiphon esse serpentem, plane constat. Tantum de specie ambigitur. Et Hebraei quidem de sola vocabuli origine solliciti sunt: quod Aben Ezra deducit a Verbo שוף, a conterendo; quia serpenti dictum Gen. III, 15. אתה תשופנו עקב, Tu conteres ei calcaneum. Kimchius a Verbo שפה, quod etiam conterendi significationem habet Iob. XXXIII, 21. Ita, ut שפיפון fiat a שפה, secunda geminata, quomodo הגיגי ex הגה. Sed Selomo et Pomarius dici malunt a נשף, ab afflando, vel sibilando. Haec Hebraei de vocis origine, de propria significatione οὐδὲ γρύ· Alios vero si consulas, ipsa te diversitas dubium tenebit, et ambiguum. Ionathan explicat רישי חיוין, praecipuos serpentum: Onkelos פתנה, aspidem: Ierosolymitanus, et Syrus ܡܥܪܘܢܐ, basiliscum: Arabs uterque معرون, ignotum per ignotius: Hieronymus cerasten: Iunius acontiam: Schindlerus, in ארח, viperam: Castalio, Itali, Galli et Angli colubrum.

At Gen 49:17, I cannot at all understand why the Greeks rendered שפיפון sephiphon as ἐγκαθήμενον [enkathēmenon], and the Samaritans as ࠊࠌࠉࠍ [כמין] · lying in ambush. But I know that others hold, much more correctly, that שפיפון is a kind of serpent. For this is openly proclaimed by the words of Jacob, which run thus: DAN shall be a serpent by the way, a שפיפון sephiphon by the path, that bites the horse's heels, so that his rider falls backward. Thus it is quite certain that the sesiphon is a serpent. Only the species is in doubt. The Hebrews, indeed, concern themselves solely with the origin of the word. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] derives it from the verb שוף, to bruise, because it was said to the serpent in Gen 3:15. אתה תשופנו עקב, You shall bruise his heel. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] derives it from the verb שפה, which also has the meaning to bruise in Job 33:21. So שפיפון would be formed from שפה with the second radical doubled, as הגיגי from הגה. But Selomo and Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] prefer to derive it from נשף, to blow, or to hiss. This is what the Hebrews say on the origin of the word; on its proper meaning, οὐδὲ γρύ [oude gry]. If you consult others, however, their very diversity will leave you doubtful and uncertain. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] explains it as רישי חיוין, the chief of serpents; Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] as פתנה, an asp; the Jerusalem Targum and the Syriac as ܡܥܪܘܢܐ [מערונא], a basilisk; both Arabic versions as معرون [maʿrūn], explaining the unknown by the more unknown; Jerome as cerastes; Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] as acontias; Schindler, under ארח, as viper; Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] and the Italian, French and English versions as snake. ¶

In his tenebris nonnihil lucis mutuamur ex Arabibus, apud quos سف sipphon, vel supphon est serpens, albo et nigro distinctus. Alcamus, السف الارقم من الحيات Sipphon (vel supphon) in serpentibus is, qui voca- col. 417 tur arkamo (varius). Et alibi, الارقم اخبث الحيات واطلبها للناس او ما فيه سواد وبياض Arkamo, serpens perniciosissimus, et homini infensissimus: aut qui partim niger, partim albus est. Hinc conjectura non levis, sipphon esse haemorrhoum et haemorrhoidem, de quibus Aëtius: Animalia haec colore quidem sunt arenoso, etc. Per corpus vero notis nigris et al-bis variegata sunt. Avicenna itidem a), ذكرت القدما ان هاتين الحيتين رمليتا الابدان وعلي ابدانها نقط سود وبيض Referunt veteres, hos serpentes corpora habere arenosa, eaque adeo punctis III, 206 nigris et albis esse adspersa. Itaque si שפיפון sephiphon, ex Arabismo explicatur, videbitur esse haemorrhois, viperae species, quae morsu sanguinem elicit, ita, ut, quidquid animae est, per cruorem evocetur, ut habetur in Solino.

In this darkness we borrow some light from the Arabs, among whom سف sipphon, or supphon, is a serpent marked with white and black. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: السف الارقم من الحيات [al-siff al-arqam min al-ḥayyāt], Sipphon (or supphon) is, among serpents, the one called col. 417 arkamo (variegated). And elsewhere: الارقم اخبث الحيات واطلبها للناس او ما فيه سواد وبياض [al-arqam akhbath al-ḥayyāt wa-aṭlabuhā li-l-nās aw mā fīhi sawād wa-bayāḍ], Arkamo, the most pernicious of serpents and the most hostile to man; or one that is partly black, partly white. Hence there is a weighty conjecture that the sipphon is the haemorrhous and haemorrhois, of which Aëtius says: These animals are sandy in colour, etc. But over the body they are variegated with black and white marks. Likewise Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] a): ذكرت القدما ان هاتين الحيتين رمليتا الابدان وعلي ابدانها نقط سود وبيض [dhakarat al-qudamā an hātayn al-ḥayyatayn ramliyyatā al-abdān wa-ʿalī abdānihā nuqaṭ sūd wa-bīḍ] The ancients report that these serpents have sandy bodies, and that these are sprinkled with III, 206 black and white spots. So if שפיפון sephiphon is explained from Arabic usage, it will seem to be the haemorrhois, a species of viper whose bite draws out blood, so that whatever life there is is summoned out through the blood, as we read in Solinus. ¶

Mihi tamen impense arridet Vulgata versio, quae cerasten exponit; quia natura cerastis plane videtur exprimi Iacobi verbis, „Dan erit serpens super viam, שפיפון עלי ארח, sephiphon supra semitam “; vel, ut alii, super orbitam. Ita enim cerasten latere, dicit Nicander Theriacῶn versu 262.

Yet the Vulgate version, which renders it cerastes, greatly appeals to me, because the nature of the cerastes seems plainly expressed in Jacob's words, "Dan shall be a serpent by the way, שפיפון עלי ארח, a sephiphon by the path"; or, as others render it, on the wheel-track. For that is where the cerastes lurks, as Nicander says in the Theriaca, line 262. ¶

— — — ἐν δ᾽ ἀμάθοισιν
Ἦ καὶ ἁματροχιῇσι παρὰ στίβον· — —

— — — ἐν δ᾽ ἀμάθοισιν Ἠ καὶ ἁματροχιῇσι παρὰ στίβον· — — [en d᾽ amathoisin ē kai hamatrochiēsi para stibon] ¶

In arenis, aut etiam in rotarum orbitis prope viam, ut inde viatoribus insidietur. Et Iacobus addit, sephiphon equi calces ita mordere, ut sessor cadat retrorsum, equo labante scilicet, cum in suffragines serpere incipit virus. In poplitibus enim, atque inguine, potissimum mali vim se exerere, tradit idem Nicander:

In the sand, or even in the wheel-ruts beside the road, so as to lie in wait for travellers from there. And Jacob adds that the sephiphon bites the horse's heels so that the rider falls backward, namely when the horse stumbles as the poison begins to creep into its hocks. For the same Nicander relates that the force of the evil shows itself chiefly in the hollows of the knees and in the groin: ¶

Διπλοῖς δ᾽ ἐν βουβῶσιν, καὶ ἰγνύσιν ἀσκελὲς αὐτὸς Μόχθος ἐπιτρέφεται· — — — Mox gravis exercet labor inguina, et acriter urget Poplitem utrumque dolor. — — —

Διπλοῖς δ᾽ ἐν βουβῶσιν, καὶ ἰγνύσιν ἀσκελὲς αὐτὸς Μόχθος ἐπιτρέφεται· — — — [Diplois d᾽ en boubōsin, kai ignysin askeles autos Mochthos epitrephetai] Soon heavy suffering afflicts the groin, and pain sharply presses both knee-hollows. — — — ¶

Neque hominibus solum, sed et brutis lethifer est ille morsus. Dicit enim Cal- lias in Aeliano libri decimi sexti capite vigesimo octavo, Τοὺς κεράστας ὄφεις δει-νοὺς εἶναι τὸ δῆγμα, ἀναιρεῖν γὰρ τὰ ζῶα ἄλογα, καὶ ἀνθρώπους· Cerastas serpentes esse morsu graves, quique tam bruta, quam homines interimant. Hoc gravissimum, quod cerastis insidias devitare facile non est: quia, cum sit arenae concolor, calcatur ab imprudentibus, quos vicissim ille remordet. Diodo- rus libro tertio b), Τὴν δὲ χρόαν ἄμμῳ παραπλησίαν ἔχουσι· διόπερ ἐξωμοιω-μένων αὐτῶν κατὰ τὴν πρόσοψιν τοῖς ὑποκειμένοις ἐδάφεσι, ὀλίγοι μὲν ἐπιγινώ-σκουσιν· οἱ πολλοὶ δ᾽ ἀγνοοῦντες πατοῦσι, καὶ κινδύνοις περιπίπτουσιν ἀπροσδο-κήτοις. Arenam colore referunt: proinde cum subjecto solo sint colore similes, il- los pauci dignoscunt; et plerique imprudentes calcant, atque in improvisa pericula sic incidunt. Unde factum, ut olim Aethiopiae partem, Aegypto subjectam, hi serpentes prorsus desolaverint, ut ibidem habet Diodorus.

And this bite is deadly not only to men but also to beasts. For Callias in Aelian, book 16, chapter 28, says: Τοὺς κεράστας ὄφεις δεινοὺς εἶναι τὸ δῆγμα, ἀναιρεῖν γὰρ τὰ ζῶα ἄλογα, καὶ ἀνθρώπους· [Tous kerastas opheis deinous einai to dēgma, anairein gar ta zōa aloga, kai anthrōpous] The cerastes serpents are dangerous in their bite, and kill both beasts and men. What is most serious is that the ambush of the cerastes is not easily avoided: since it is the colour of the sand, it is trodden on by the unwary, whom it bites in return. Diodorus, book 3 b): Τὴν δὲ χρόαν ἄμμῳ παραπλησίαν ἔχουσι· διόπερ ἐξωμοιωμένων αὐτῶν κατὰ τὴν πρόσοψιν τοῖς ὑποκειμένοις ἐδάφεσι, ὀλίγοι μὲν ἐπιγινώσκουσιν· οἱ πολλοὶ δ᾽ ἀγνοοῦντες πατοῦσι, καὶ κινδύνοις περιπίπτουσιν ἀπροσδοκήτοις. [Tēn de chroan ammō paraplēsian echousi; dioper exōmoiōmenōn autōn kata tēn prosopsin tois hypokeimenois edaphesi, oligoi men epiginōskousin; hoi polloi d᾽ agnoountes patousi, kai kindynois peripiptousin aprosdokētois.] They resemble sand in colour; therefore, since they are like the ground beneath them in colour, few recognize them, and most tread on them unawares and so fall into unforeseen dangers. Hence it came about that these serpents once entirely depopulated a part of Ethiopia bordering on Egypt, as Diodorus relates in the same place. ¶

Iam, si auctoritate certatur, Vulgata convenit utrique Arabi, in quibus معرون est cerastes, ut Avicennae مقرنة. In illis linguis Koph et Ajin non raro permutantur. Hinc Chaldaeis et Syris, terra dicitur ܐܪܥܐ III, 207 et ܐܬܢܢ: fumare, ܩܘܦܣ et ܟܦܣ: frigus, ܩܘܦܣܐ et ܟܦܣܐ. Et Ionathani רקם est idem locus, qui Hebraeis עצמון Num. XXXIV, 4. Non aliter apud Arabes cornu tam est عرن, quam قرن. Giggeius ex Alcamus, عران col. 418 البقر cornua boum, عرنة cornuum radix, عرون limax cochlea, nempe c) ob cornicula duo, quibus praetentat iter, cum oculorum usu careat. Pari ratione cerastes ut مقرن ita et معرون, id est, cornutus, dicitur, vel qui cornicula cochleae similia habet (1). Aelianus, Ὑπὲρ τοῦ μετώπου κέρατα ἔχει δύο, καὶ ἔοικε τοῖς κοχλίου τὰ κέρατα, οὐ μέν ἐστι ὡς ἐκεῖνα ἁπαλά· Super frontem duo habet cornua, cochleae cornibus similia, quamvis non aèque mollia. Plinius tamen libri undecimi capite trigesimo septimo, tam cerastae, quam cochleae corporea dicit esse cornua, id est, carnea. Proinde Bellonius non appellat cornua, sed parvas eminentias instar granorum hordei, quae cornicula referunt (2). Unde falli non parum eos d) apparet, qui scribunt, ex cerastis cornibus cultellorum manubria fieri ad mensas imperatorum, quia sudore produnt venenum appositum. Quis enim manubria cultris ex cornu tantillo fecerit, eoque molli? Ne dicam vero, non esse consentaneum, ut venenosi animalis corniculis vis sit ἀλεξιφάρμακος. Sed cerastis cornibus perperam tribuunt barbari scriptores, quod de cornu كركدان carcaddan, sive monocerotis, alicubi legerant. De illo scilicet Geographus Nubiensis Clim. 1. part. 8. ملوك الهند III, 208 تصنع من قرن هذه الذابة انصبة السكاكين للموايد فانا وضع الطعام بين ايديهم وكان فيه سم عرق ذلك النصاب فيعلم بذلك ان الطعام مسموم Reges Indiae ex hujus animalis cornu cultrorum manubria sibi conficiunt ad mensarum usum. Cumque apponitur eis cibus, si fuerit venenatus, sudore madefiunt ea manubria. Unde deprehenditur cibus esse veneno infectus. An tale quid habebat in animo Epiphanius, cum in Pneumatomachos scribens meminit κεράστου δεινοῦ μονοκέρωτος, cerastae gravis monocerotis? Non puto, sed serpentem cerasten ex illius sententia esse monoceroten. Idem enim sic habet de Basilidianorum haeresi: Ὡς κεράστου τρόπον ἐν ἄμμῳ κεχωσμένη, διὰ δὲ τοῦ κέρα-τος ἐν ἀέρι προκύπτουσα, καὶ τὸν ὄλεθρον τοῖς περιτυγχάνουσιν αὐτὴν ἐργαζο-μένη· Cerastis instar in arena defossa, cornu in aërem exerto, iis, qui in eam incidunt, exitiosa.

Now, if the matter is decided by authority, the Vulgate agrees with both Arabic versions, in which معرون [maʿrūn] is the cerastes, like Avicenna's مقرنة [muqarrana]. In those languages Koph and Ajin are not rarely interchanged. Hence among the Chaldeans and Syrians earth is called ܐܪܥܐ [ארעא] III, 207 and ܐܬܢܢ [אתנן]; to smoke, ܩܘܦܣ [קופס] and ܟܦܣ [כפס]; cold, ܩܘܦܣܐ [קופסא] and ܟܦܣܐ [כפסא]. And in Jonathan רקם is the same place that the Hebrews call עצמון, Num. XXXIV, 4. In the same way among the Arabs horn is both عرن [ʿarn] and قرن [qarn]. Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)], from Alcamus: عران البقر [ʿirān al-baqar] col. 418 the horns of oxen, عرنة [ʿirna] the root of the horns, عرون [ʿarūn] the slug, the snail, namely c) because of the two little horns with which it feels its way, since it lacks the use of eyes. By the same reasoning the cerastes is called both مقرن [muqarran] and معرون [maʿrūn], that is, horned, or having little horns like those of a snail R1. Aelian: Ὑπὲρ τοῦ μετώπου κέρατα ἔχει δύο, καὶ ἔοικε τοῖς κοχλίου τὰ κέρατα, οὐ μέν ἐστι ὡς ἐκεῖνα ἁπαλά· [Hyper tou metōpou kerata echei dyo, kai eoike tois kochliou ta kerata, ou men esti hōs ekeina hapala] Above its forehead it has two horns, similar to the horns of a snail, though not equally soft. Pliny, however, in book 11, chapter 37, says that the horns of both the cerastes and the snail are corporeal, that is, fleshy. Accordingly Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] does not call them horns, but small protuberances like grains of barley, which resemble little horns R2. Hence it is evident that those d) are not a little mistaken who write that knife handles for the tables of emperors are made from the horns of cerastes, because they betray poison placed nearby by sweating. For who would make knife handles out of so tiny a horn, and a soft one at that? Not to mention that it is not reasonable that the little horns of a poisonous animal should have an ἀλεξιφάρμακος [alexipharmakos] power. But the barbarous writers wrongly attribute to the horns of the cerastes what they had read somewhere about the horn of the كركدان [karkadān] carcaddan, or monoceros. Of it, namely, the Nubian Geographer, Clim. [=climate (section) of al-Idrīsī's geography, in the Latin Geographia Nubiensis] 1, section 8: ملوك الهند III, 208 تصنع من قرن هذه الذابة انصبة السكاكين للموايد فانا وضع الطعام بين ايديهم وكان فيه سم عرق ذلك النصاب فيعلم بذلك ان الطعام مسموم [mulūk al-hind taṣnaʿ min qarn hādhihi al-dhāba anṣibat al-sakākīn lil-mawāyid fa-anā wuḍiʿa al-ṭaʿām bayna aydīhim wa-kāna fīhi samm ʿariqa dhālika al-niṣāb fa-yuʿlam bi-dhālika anna al-ṭaʿām masmūm] The kings of India have knife handles made for themselves from the horn of this animal for use at table. And when food is set before them, if it is poisoned, those handles become moist with sweat. From this it is detected that the food is infected with poison. Did Epiphanius have something of this kind in mind when, writing against the Pneumatomachi, he mentions κεράστου δεινοῦ μονοκέρωτος [kerastou deinou monokerōtos], of the dread one-horned cerastes? I do not think so; rather, in his opinion the serpent cerastes is one-horned. For he says the same about the heresy of the Basilidians: Ὡς κεράστου τρόπον ἐν ἄμμῳ κεχωσμένη, διὰ δὲ τοῦ κέρατος ἐν ἀέρι προκύπτουσα, καὶ τὸν ὄλεθρον τοῖς περιτυγχάνουσιν αὐτὴν ἐργαζομένη· [Hōs kerastou tropon en ammō kechōsmenē, dia de tou keratos en aeri prokyptousa, kai ton olethron tois peritynchanousin autēn ergazomenē] Buried in the sand like the cerastes, with its horn thrust up into the air, deadly to those who come upon it. ¶

Ut redeamus ad rem, hactenus id incertum, sephiphon sitne cerastes, an haemorrhous, cum utrique interpretationi multa faveant. Cerasten exponi diximus ab Hieronymo et utroque Arabe, atque hanc interpretationem cum Patriarchae verbis apprime convenire. Sipphon tamen voce simili ab Arabibus ita describi, ut videatur potius esse haemorrhous. Quid, si sephiphon ad utrumque commune nomen esse dicamus? Omnino sic videtur. Atque id in dubium vix revocabit, qui sciet, quam sint similes cerastes et haemorrhous. Uterque est viperae species. Arenosus utrique color, sed maculosus haemorrhoo. Eadem utrique mensura. Avicenna de haemorrhoo et haemorrhoide, واطوالها اطوال المقرنه Longitudo illorum eadem, quae cerastae. Neque haemorrhoo deesse cornua, sic testatur Aelianus libri decimi quinti capite decimo tertio, Φρίττει δὲ τὴν κεφαλὴν οἱονεὶ κέρασί τισιν· Tanquam quibusdam cornibus caput inhorrescit. Et multo ante Nicander,

To return to the matter: it has so far remained uncertain whether the sephiphon is the cerastes or the haemorrhous, since much favors each interpretation. We have said that Jerome and both Arabic versions render it cerastes, and that this interpretation agrees excellently with the words of the Patriarch. Yet the sipphon, a similar word, is so described by the Arabs that it seems rather to be the haemorrhous. What if we say that sephiphon is a name common to both? That certainly seems to be so. And hardly anyone will call this into doubt who knows how similar the cerastes and the haemorrhous are. Each is a species of viper. Both are sand-colored, but the haemorrhous is spotted. Both are of the same size. Avicenna on the haemorrhous and the haemorrhois: واطوالها اطوال المقرنه [wa-aṭwāluhā aṭwāl al-muqarrana] Their length is the same as that of the cerastes. Nor does the haemorrhous lack horns, as Aelian testifies in book 15, chapter 13: Φρίττει δὲ τὴν κεφαλὴν οἱονεὶ κέρασί τισιν· [Phrittei de tēn kephalēn hoionei kerasi tisin] Its head bristles as if with certain horns. And long before, Nicander: ¶

Τοῦ μὲν ὑπὲρ νιφόεντα κεράατα δοιὰ μετώπῳ col. 419 Ἔγκειται· — — — — Bina super frontem nive candidiora videntur Cornua. — — — — —

Τοῦ μὲν ὑπὲρ νιφόεντα κεράατα δοιὰ μετώπῳ col. 419 Ἔγκειται· — — — — [Tou men hyper niphoenta keraata doia metōpō enkeitai] Above its forehead two horns are seen, whiter than snow. — — — — — ¶

Ita, ut cerastae, sive cornuti serpentis, species sit haemorrhous. Hoc praecipuum, quod, cum caeteri serpentes recta proserpant, soli, cerastes et haemorrhous, dubio gressu feruntur, et ambiguo, et in utrumque latus titubantes, ut ita dicam, ebrii, vel claudicantis in morem. Hinc de ceraste Aëtius, Per obliquum non recta proreptat. Et Isidorus, Est flexuosus plus quam alii serpentes. Sed et de haemorrhoo Aelianus, Λοξὸν δὲ οἶμον πρόεισιν, Obliqua via progreditur. Et eum hoc ipso Nicander cum ceraste conferens, III, 209 Δοχμὰ δ᾽ ἐπισκάζει ὀλίγον δέμας, οἷα κεράστης· Claudicat obliquans corpus breve, more cerastae.

Thus the haemorrhous is a species of cerastes, or horned serpent. This is especially notable: whereas the other serpents creep straight forward, the cerastes and the haemorrhous alone move with an uncertain and wavering gait, staggering to either side, drunk, so to speak, or like one who limps. Hence Aëtius on the cerastes: It creeps obliquely, not straight. And Isidore: It is more sinuous than other serpents. And Aelian on the haemorrhous too: Λοξὸν δὲ οἶμον πρόεισιν [Loxon de oimon proeisin], It advances by an oblique path. And Nicander, comparing it with the cerastes in this very respect: III, 209 Δοχμὰ δ᾽ ἐπισκάζει ὀλίγον δέμας, οἷα κεράστης· [Dochma d’ episkazei oligon demas, hoia kerastēs] It limps, bending its short body sideways, like the cerastes. ¶

Causa est in dorsi spinis, quae non osseae, ut in reliquis, sed cartilagineae sunt, et in utramque partem ita flexiles, ut in incessu nulla possit esse firmitas. Quo faciunt haec Lucani,

The cause lies in the vertebrae of the back, which are not bony, as in the rest, but cartilaginous, and so flexible in either direction that there can be no steadiness in their movement. To this point are these words of Lucan: ¶

— — spinaque vagi torquente cerastae.

— — and the cerastes, wandering with twisting spine. ¶

Proinde cerasten auctor est Isidorus tam esse flexuosum, ut spinam non ha-bere videatur. Et Festus Avienus in Orbis terrae descriptione,

Accordingly Isidore states that the cerastes is so sinuous that it seems to have no spine. And Festus Avienus in the Description of the World: ¶

Sic virosarum prolixa volumina sese
Sponte cerastarum facili sub viscere curvent.

Thus the long coils of the poisonous cerastes bend themselves of their own accord beneath their supple body. ¶

Sed causam poetae ad Helenam referunt, quae serpentum homogeneorum cerastae et haemorrhoi spinas confregerit, ut sic Canopi, naucleri sui, manibus parentaret, cerastae scilicet (vel haemorrhoi) morsu in Aegypto interempti. Hanc fabellam Nicander, postquam poëtico sale aspersit, claudit hoc epiphonemate:

But the poets trace the cause to Helen, who broke the spines of the kindred serpents, the cerastes and the haemorrhous, so as to make an offering to the shade of Canopus, her pilot, who had been killed in Egypt by the bite of a cerastes (or haemorrhous). Nicander, after seasoning this little tale with poetic wit, closes it with this epiphonema: ¶

Ἔξοθεν αἱμοῤῥόοι, σκολιοπλανέες τε κεράσται, Οἷοι χωλεύουσι· — — — — Fertur claudus ab hinc haemorrhous, atque cerastes, Obliquam sulcare viam. — — —

Ἔξοθεν αἱμοῤῥόοι, σκολιοπλανέες τε κεράσται, Οἷοι χωλεύουσι· — — — — [Exothen haimorrhooi, skolioplanees te kerastai, Hoioi chōleuousi] From then on the haemorrhous, and the cerastes, go lame, it is said, ploughing a crooked path. — — — ¶

Quibus similia legere est Canonis Hist. 8. et Aeliani libri decimi quinti capite decimo tertio. In his notandum inprimis, quod haemorrhoos et cerastas, ratione gressus obliqui, solos e serpentibus χωλεύειν et ἐπισκάζειν, id est, claudicare, dicit Nicander. Quia inde Hebraei nominis origo manifesta. Hebraice nimirum apud magistros שפף saphaph est claudicare, et שפוף saphuph est claudus. Utrumque ea notione profert Boderianus ex libro Beresith Rabba. Sed et Talmudici שפי sephi, et שפיפן sephiphon, pro claudo usurpant; hac tamen differentia, quod ab illis שפי dicitur, qui uno pede; שפיפן, qui utroque pede claudicat, Graece uno verbo ἀμφιγυήεις, quod est Vulcani epithetum in Homero et Hesiodo. Hinc in tractatu Sota pag. 10. a. אמר ר׳ יוחנן בלעם חיגר ברגלו אחת שנאמר וילך שפי שמשון חיגר בשתי רגליו היה שנאמר שפיפן עלי ארח, Dixit R. Iochanan, Balaam uno pede claudus fuit, cum de eo dicatur Num. XXIII, 3. „Et ivit שפי, claudus“. Sed Samson utroque pede claudus fuit; unde est, quod de illo dicitur Gen. XLIX, 17. „Sephiphon super semitam“. Quae, licet perabsurda, tamen docent id, quod volumus, nomen שפיפון scilicet, ex Hebraeorum sententia, a claudicatione esse factum. Itaque sephiphon est serpens claudus, quales nulli, quod sciam, praeter cerasten et haemorrhoum. Proinde utrumque hoc nomine significari valde verisimile est.

Similar things may be read in Conon, Hist. 8, and in Aelian, book 15, chapter 13. In these it should be noted above all that Nicander says that the haemorrhous and the cerastes, on account of their crooked gait, are the only serpents that χωλεύειν [chōleuein] and ἐπισκάζειν [episkazein], that is, limp. For from this the origin of the Hebrew name is evident. In Hebrew, namely among the masters, שפף saphaph is to limp, and שפוף saphuph is lame. Boderianus [=Guy Le Fèvre de la Boderie] cites both in this sense from the book Beresith Rabba. The Talmudists too use שפי sephi and שפיפן sephiphon for lame, yet with this difference: they call שפי one who limps on one foot, and שפיפן one who limps on both feet, in Greek in one word ἀμφιγυήεις [amphigyēeis], which is the epithet of Vulcan in Homer and Hesiod. Hence in the tractate Sota, p. 10a: אמר ר׳ יוחנן בלעם חיגר ברגלו אחת שנאמר וילך שפי שמשון חיגר בשתי רגליו היה שנאמר שפיפן עלי ארח, R. Iochanan said: Balaam was lame in one foot, since it is said of him Num 23:3, "And he went שפי, lame." But Samson was lame in both feet; hence it is said of him Gen 49:17, "Sephiphon upon the path." These things, though utterly absurd, nevertheless teach what we want: namely, that the name שפיפון, in the opinion of the Hebrews, was formed from limping. So the sephiphon is a lame serpent, and there are none such, as far as I know, except the cerastes and the haemorrhous. It is therefore very probable that both are signified by this name. ¶

III, 210 Samsonem quod attinet, quia non solum Danita fuit, sed et Danitarum col. 420 clarissimus, ad eum referunt, quae hic habentur, Hebraei omnes. De Talmu- dicis id jam patuit. Et disertis verbis Hierosolymitana paraphrasis, והוא שמשון בר מנוח, Is est Samson, Manoae filius. Ionathan quoque et Onkelos, eum li-cet non nominent, tamen aperte designant: sed hic inprimis; cujus verba sunt, Erit vir, qui eligetur, et surget de domo Dan. Ejus timor irruet super populos: et idem Philistaeos fortiter percutiet. Ut basiliscus morabitur circa viam, et ut aspis insidiabitur circa semitam. Interficiet robustos in castris Philistaeorum, equi- tes cum peditibus: enervabit equos et quadrigas (id est, quadrigarum equos, per figuram ἓν διὰ δυοῖν) et eorum ascensores retrorsum dejiciet. Quod de Samsone cur dicatur, non constat satis, cum nusquam tradant Sacri Scriptores, illi rem fuisse cum equitibus, aut quadrigis Philistaeorum. Neque vero necesse est ad solum Samsonem referre, quae dicuntur de tota tribu: aut fractos equitum globos hic sibi fingere. Nec enim alia de causa Danitae cum ceraste conferuntur, qui, in via latens, equorum mordet calcaneos, quam quia potius astu, quam aperto Marte rem gerebant. Cujus rei exemplum habemus Iud. XVIII, 27. ubi Danitae Laisitas incautos adoriuntur. Quod in justo bello licitum. Brasidas apud Thucydidem libro quinto, Τὰ κλέμματα ταῦτα καλλίστην δόξαν ἔχει, ἃ τὸν πολέμιον μάλιστ᾽ ἄν τις ἀπατήσας, τοὺς φίλους μέγιστ᾽ ἂν ὠφελήσειεν· Haec belli furta pulcherrimam consequuntur gloriam, quibus hostes quis maxime fallens, amicos plurimum juvat. Et Choroebus libro secundo Aeneidos,

III, 210 As for Samson: since he was not only a Danite but also the most illustrious of the Danites, col. 420 all the Hebrews refer to him what is said here. This has already been shown for the Talmudists. And the Jerusalem paraphrase says in so many words: והוא שמשון בר מנוח, He is Samson, son of Manoah. Jonathan and Onkelos too, though they do not name him, nevertheless clearly point to him, especially the former, whose words are: There shall be a man who will be chosen, and he will arise from the house of Dan. The fear of him shall fall upon the peoples, and he shall mightily smite the Philistines. Like a basilisk he shall lurk by the way, and like an asp he shall lie in wait by the path. He shall kill the mighty in the camp of the Philistines, horsemen together with foot soldiers; he shall hamstring horses and chariots (that is, the horses of the chariots, by the figure ἓν διὰ δυοῖν [hen dia dyoin]) and throw their riders backward. Why this is said of Samson is not clear enough, since the Sacred Writers nowhere relate that he had to do with the horsemen or chariots of the Philistines. Nor indeed is it necessary to refer to Samson alone what is said of the whole tribe, or to imagine here broken squadrons of horsemen. For the Danites are compared with the cerastes, which lurks in the way and bites the heels of horses, for no other reason than that they conducted their affairs by cunning rather than by open warfare. Of this we have an example in Iud. XVIII, 27., where the Danites attack the unsuspecting people of Laish. This is permitted in a just war. Brasidas in Thucydides, book 5: Τὰ κλέμματα ταῦτα καλλίστην δόξαν ἔχει, ἃ τὸν πολέμιον μάλιστ᾽ ἄν τις ἀπατήσας, τοὺς φίλους μέγιστ᾽ ἂν ὠφελήσειεν· [Ta klemmata tauta kallistēn doxan echei, ha ton polemion malist' an tis apatēsas, tous philous megist' an ōphelēseien] These stratagems of war win the finest glory, by which one, most deceiving the enemy, most helps one's friends. And Coroebus in book 2 of the Aeneid: ¶

Mutemus clypeos, Danaumque insignia nobis
Aptemus. Dolus, an virtus, quis in hoste requirat?

Let us exchange shields, and fit the insignia of the Greeks to ourselves. Trickery or valour, who would ask in an enemy? ¶

Et apud Silium libro quinto Corvinus,

And Corvinus in Silius, book 5: ¶

Bellandum est astu: levior laus vindice dextra.

War must be waged by cunning: lighter is the praise won by the avenging right hand. ¶

Ita malo, quam quod vulgo legunt, indice dextra, aut in duce dextra. Proinde Vegetius libri tertii De arte militari capite nono laudat duces, qui non aperto Marte praelium, in quo est commune periculum, sed ex occulto semper attentant: ut, integris suis, quantum possunt, viribus, hostes interimant certe, vel terreant. Et e nostris Augustinus in Iosuam capite decimo, Cum justum bellum susceperit (homo justus), utrum aperta pugna, utrum insidiis vincat, nihil ad justitiam in- terest. Tamen hic quaedam distinguenda, quae, quia non sunt hujus loci, petenda ex Cl. Grotio, libri tertii De jure belli capitis primi sectione sexta, etc.

This is how I prefer to read it, rather than the common reading indice dextra or in duce dextra. Accordingly Vegetius, De arte militari book 3, chapter 9, praises commanders who attempt battle, in which the danger is shared, not in open combat but always from concealment, so that, with their own forces as far as possible intact, they may surely destroy the enemy, or at least terrify them. And among our own writers Augustine, on Joshua, chapter 10: When he has undertaken a just war (the just man), whether he conquers in open battle or by ambush makes no difference to justice. Yet certain distinctions must be drawn here; since they do not belong to this place, they are to be sought from the illustrious Grotius, De jure belli book 3, chapter 1, section 6, etc. ¶

Mirum est, quosdam veterum eadem haec Iacobi verba, et ad Samsonem cum Iudaeis, et ad Anti-Christum referre, quasi per colubrum et cerasten, qui insidiatur in via, uterque hic significetur. Neque enim iisdem verbis describi potest Samson, Christi typus, ac ecclesiae liberator; et Anti-Christus, Christi III, 211 hostis, et ecclesiae subversor. Praeterea huic interpretationi maxime repugnat, quod in Graeca et Latina versione, qua usi sunt hi doctores, Iacobi verba optative concipiuntur; Γενηθήτω Δὰν ὄφις ἐφ᾽ ὁδοῦ, Fiat Dan coluber in via. Quippe non est verisimile, Iacobum optasse, ut ex stirpe sua oriatur Anti-Christus. Recte igitur ad hunc locum Bellarminus libri tertii De Romano pontifice capite duodecimo, Videtur sane Iacob bene precari filio suo, cum haec dicit; et proinde non malum, sed bonum praedicere.

It is surprising that some of the ancients refer these same words of Jacob both to Samson, with the Jews, and to the Antichrist, as though both were signified by the snake and the cerastes that lies in wait on the path. For Samson, a type of Christ and the deliverer of the church, cannot be described in the same words as the Antichrist, the enemy of Christ III, 211 and the subverter of the church. Moreover, this interpretation is most strongly contradicted by the fact that in the Greek and Latin versions, which these doctors used, Jacob's words are framed as a wish: Γενηθήτω Δὰν ὄφις ἐφ᾽ ὁδοῦ [Genēthētō Dan ophis eph᾽ hodou], Let Dan be a snake on the path. For it is not plausible that Jacob wished the Antichrist to arise from his own stock. Rightly, therefore, does Bellarmine say on this passage, De Romano pontifice book 3, chapter 12: Jacob certainly seems to be blessing his son when he says this, and therefore to be foretelling not evil but good. ¶
