II, 454 col. 1035 Chapter 36. ¶

Probatur, קפוד kippod esse Hericium et Hystricem.

It is proved that קפוד kippod is the hedgehog and the porcupine. ¶

Tria elementa certant de animali קפוד kippod: quod alii terrae adscribunt, alii aquae, alii aëri cum volucribus. In primo ordine pono Graecos et Hieronymum; quibus קפוד kippod est ἐχῖνος hericius, nempe terrestris, qui aestate in sepibus, hyeme in truncis arborum, aut in terrae scrobibus degit. Alii aquaticum animal esse inferunt ex his Esa. XIV, 23. de Babylone, Ponam eam in possessionem hereditariam τοῦ kippod et stagna aquarum. Sed horum agmen aut bi-partitum est, aut tripartitum etiam. Pars testudinem esse vult, pars castorem, pars lutram. Nituntur omnes etymologia vocis. Nempe Esa. XXXVIII, 12. קפד est praecidere, ubi textor stamen praecidit, tela tota jam praetexta. H[?]s autem animalibus praecidendi mira vis. De castore res notissima. Animal est horrendi morsus (inquit Plinius libri octavi capite trigesimo), arbores juxta flumina, ut ferro caedit. Sed et lutram Varro libro quarto De lingua Latina di[?]i vult ἀπὸ τοῦ λύειν, quod succidere dicitur arborum radices in ripa, atque eas dissolvere. Eandem in testudine vim eo mirere magis, quod, cum illi dentes non sint, rostri tamen acie comminuit omnia. Rostri scilicet margines acuti sunt, superna parte inferiorem claudente pyxidum modo. Ita Plinius lib. 11. cap. 37. et lib. 9. cap. 10. Cui simile, quod de marina testudine legitur in Porta coeli fo[?]. col. 1036 34. a. ויש לה כח גדול בפיה ובשיניה ואם תקח אבן בפיה תשבור אותה בחוק גדול לחלקים קטנים, Ipsi est robur ingens in ore et dentibus. Et, si o[?] lapidem accipit, viribus magnis conterit eum in minutissimas partes. Huc accedi[?], quod Arabice, si utrique Kimchio fides, testudinis nomen est קנפאר, quod a[?]b Hebraeo קפוד non multum differt. Sed ab iis objicitur, qui in tertia sentent[?]a sunt, nempe קפוד kippod esse avis speciem: alibi ab Esaia et Sophonia kipp[?]d nominari cum avibus. Esaiae locus sic habet capitis trigesimi quarti capite und[?]-cimo, Possidebunt eum pelecanus et kippod. Nec multo aliter Sophonias capit[?]s secundi versu decimo quarto, Etiam pelecanus, etiam kippod in superliminarib[?]s ejus pernoctabunt. Rursus ab his dubitatur, quod avis genus sit kippod: ali[?]is noctuam explicantibus; aliis vulturem; aliis merulam; aliis butaurum, sive ard[?]-am stellarem; aliis cypselum, qui et apus; aliis plancum, aut aquilam anatariam[?]; et quid non? Et plerasque illas interpretationes ita inter se confert doctissim[?]s Fullerus Miscellaneorum libri primi capite decimo octavo, ut nullam rejiciat pra[?]-ter illam, quae sola vera est. Antiquissimam illam dico, qua קפוד kippod h[?]-ricius exponitur: cui firmandae multa faciunt.

Three elements contend over the animal קפוד kippod: some assign it to the earth, others to the water, others to the air along with the birds. In the first rank I place the Greeks and Jerome, for whom קפוד kippod is ἐχῖνος [echinos], the hedgehog, namely the land hedgehog, which lives in hedges in summer and in winter in tree trunks or in holes in the ground. Others infer that it is an aquatic animal from Isa 14:23, on Babylon: I will make it a hereditary possession of the kippod and pools of water. But the ranks of these are divided in two, or even in three. Some hold it to be the tortoise, some the beaver, some the otter. All rely on the etymology of the word. For in Isa 38:12. קפד means to cut off, where the weaver cuts off the warp when the whole web has been woven. And these animals have a remarkable power of cutting. With the beaver the matter is very well known. It is an animal with a fearsome bite (says Pliny, book 8, chapter 30); it cuts down trees by rivers as if with iron. But Varro too, in book 4 On the Latin Language, would have the otter [lutra] named from λύειν [lyein], because it is said to undercut the roots of trees on the bank and to loosen them. The same power in the tortoise is all the more remarkable because, though it has no teeth, it nevertheless crushes everything with the edge of its beak. That is, the edges of the beak are sharp, the upper part closing over the lower in the manner of a box. So Pliny, book 11, ch. 37, and book 9, ch. 10. Similar to this is what is read of the sea turtle in Porta coeli, fol. col. 1036 34a: ויש לה כח גדול בפיה ובשיניה ואם תקח אבן בפיה תשבור אותה בחוק גדול לחלקים קטנים, It has enormous strength in its mouth and teeth. And if it takes a stone into its mouth, it crushes it with great force into the tiniest pieces. Add to this that in Arabic, if both Kimchis are to be believed, the name of the tortoise is קנפאר, which does not differ much from the Hebrew קפוד. But those who hold the third opinion, namely that קפוד kippod is a species of bird, object that elsewhere kippod is named by Isaiah and Zephaniah together with birds. The passage of Isaiah, chapter 34, verse 11, reads thus: The pelican and the kippod shall possess it. And Zephaniah, chapter 2, verse 14, not very differently: Both the pelican and the kippod shall lodge on its lintels. Again, these are in doubt as to what kind of bird kippod is: some explain it as the owl; others as the vulture; others as the blackbird; others as the bittern, or starry heron; others as the cypselus, which is also the apus; others as the plancus, or duck-eagle[?]; and what not? And the most learned Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], in his Miscellanea, book 1, chapter 18, compares most of those interpretations with one another in such a way that he rejects none except the one which alone is true. I mean that most ancient one, by which קפוד kippod is explained as the hedgehog; and many things serve to confirm it. ¶

Primo, Graecorum auctoritas et Hieronymi, qui constanter ita exponu[?]t. Ab iis autem recedere tutum non puto, nisi graves causae subsint, quales h[?]c II, 455 nullas video. 2. Graecis Hebraeos addo, apud quorum doctissimos pridem invaluit haec sententia, קפוד esse hericium. Hispanica voce R. Nathan explicat ריצ"ו rizo, vel erizo; Italica Poniarius ריצ"יו riccio, Gallica Rabbi Selomo הריצ"ון herisson, Germanica Elias איג"יל Igel. Et in Talmude קפוד kippod, vel קופד kuphad, quod idem est in Chaldaica inflexione, saepe ponitur inter terrestres be-stias quadrupedes. Ut in Berachoth capite nono folio quinquagesimo septimo b. כל מיני חיות יפות לחלום חוץ מן הפיל והקוף והקפוד, Omnes ferarum species bonae sunt ad somnium (id est, boni ominis in somnis visae) praeter elephantem, simiam et kippod, id est, hericium. Nugatur enim R. Selomo, qui exponit cercopithecum. Et in tractatu Kelaim cap. octavo folio trigesimo secundo a. והקופד וחולדת הסניים, Et kuphad (hericius) et mustela ruborum. Et in Bava Kama capite septimo, אין מגדלין חתולים וקופדים, Non alunt feles, et kuphadim (hericios). Et in Sabbatho capite quinto folio quinquagesimo septimo b. ואין העגל יוצא בגימון ולא פרא בעור הקופד, Vitulus (die Sabbathi) non exit cum gemon, nec vacca cum pelle kuphad. גימון gemon Hebraeis puto esse κημὸν, camum, et vocem inde deduci; quamvis hoc vocabulo non designent camum quemvis, sed quo vitulus prohibetur, ne lactet. Aruch definit לוח בפיו שלא יוכל לשתות החלב, Tabulam, quae ori ejus inditur, ne possit lac exsugere. Graece πύσσαχον dixeris. Hesychius, Πύσσαχος· ξύλον καμπύλον τοῖς μόσχοις περὶ τοὺς μυκτῆρας τιθέμενον, ὃ κωλύει θηλάζειν· Lignum curvum vitulis circa nares appositum, quo prohibentur, ne lacteant. Et pellis kuphad (id est, hericii) vaccae mammis alligabatur eodem fine, id est, ne vel vitulus, vel aliud animal ipsam exsugeret. Rabbi Selomo in hunc locum, קופד שרץ הוא שנימיו חדים כמחט וקורין לו הריצון וקושרין עורו בדדי הפרה שלא יניקוה שרצים, Kuphad reptile est (ita Hebraei vocant minima quadrupedum), cujus pili in acus col. 1037 modum acuti sunt, vulgo vocatur herisson. Hujus pellis vaccae mammis alligatur, ne reptilia eas exsugant. Neque enim a solo vitulo lacti cavendum, sed et a serpentibus, e quorum numero boae aluntur primo bubuli lactis succo, unde nomen traxere. Ita Plinius libri octavi capite decimo quarto. Et, qui hunc sequitur, Solinus: Captat (boa) greges bubulos, et quae plurimo lacte rigua bos est, ejus se uberibus innectit, suctuque continuo saginata, etc. 4. Arabice denique قنفذ kunphud, Plurali قنافذ kanaphido, est hericius. Barbari interpretes scribunt caused, ceufud, ceufund, etc. Res notior est, quam ut probatione egeat. Tantum lectorem monebo, nomine قنفذ kunphud, praeter herinaceum, etiam hystricem contineri. Id clarum est ex Avicenna, qui inter species kunphud unum dicit esse montanum, spinas habentem missiles, cujus proprium nomen est دلدل duldul, id est, hystrix. Hinc in Damire hericii foemina vocatur أم دلدل mater τοῦ duldul, id est, hystricis. Et duldul, seu hystrix, ibidem definitur II, 456 عظيم القنافد, hericius major. Et juxta Algiahid, الفرق بين الدلدل والقنافد كالفرق بين البقر والجوامس والبخاتي من الابل العراب Duldul, et kanaphido (id est, hystrix, et hericii proprie dicti) non aliter differunt, quam a bubalis boves, et cameli Ba-ctriani ab Arabicis. Elmacinus denique Saracenicae Historiae libri primi capite quinto videtur per القنفد alkunphud hystricem potius quam hericium significasse, cum de Aisiae camelo scribit, صار كالقنفد من السهام Factus est ut alkunphud prae telis, id est, prae telorum copia, quibus transfixus est. Sed hac in parte habuerunt Arabes, quos sequerentur, Plinium et Oppianum: quorum ille libri octavi capite trigesimo quinto hystrices expresse ponit in herinaceorum genere; hic libri secundi Cynegeticῶν versu 603. echinorum terrestrium duo genera faciens, alios parvos et imbelles, alios μεγέθει πολὺ μείζονας, longe majores, et circumquaque horrentes — ἀρειοτέρῃσιν ἀκωκαῖς, majoribus cuspidibus, per majores illos echinos videtur hystrices intelligere. Sed et Suidae ἡ ὕστριξ hystrix est ὁ ἀκανθόχοιρος, ὁ χερσαῖος ἐχῖνος, porcus spinosus, hericius terrestris.

First, the authority of the Greeks and of Jerome, who consistently explain it so. I do not think it safe to depart from them unless there are weighty reasons, and here II, 455 I see none. 2. To the Greeks I add the Hebrews, among whose most learned men this opinion has long prevailed, that קפוד is the hedgehog. R. Nathan explains it with the Spanish word ריצ"ו rizo, or erizo; Poniarius with the Italian ריצ"יו riccio; Rabbi Selomo with the French הריצ"ון herisson; Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] with the German איג"יל Igel. And in the Talmud קפוד kippod, or קופד kuphad, which is the same in the Chaldee inflection, is often placed among land quadrupeds. Thus in Berachoth, chapter 9, folio 57b: כל מיני חיות יפות לחלום חוץ מן הפיל והקוף והקפוד, All species of wild beasts are good for a dream (that is, of good omen when seen in sleep), except the elephant, the ape and the kippod, that is, the hedgehog. For R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] talks nonsense when he explains it as the long-tailed monkey. And in the tractate Kelaim, ch. 8, folio 32a: והקופד וחולדת הסניים, And the kuphad (hedgehog) and the bramble weasel. And in Bava Kama, chapter 7: אין מגדלין חתולים וקופדים, They do not raise cats and kuphadim (hedgehogs). And in Sabbath, chapter 5, folio 57b: ואין העגל יוצא בגימון ולא פרא בעור הקופד, A calf may not go out (on the Sabbath) with a gemon, nor a cow with the skin of a kuphad. I think the Hebrew גימון gemon is κημὸν [kēmon], a muzzle, and that the word is derived from it; although by this term they do not mean any muzzle whatever, but the one by which a calf is prevented from sucking. The Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] defines it: לוח בפיו שלא יוכל לשתות החלב, A board that is put in its mouth so that it cannot suck the milk. In Greek you would call it πύσσαχον [pyssachon]. Hesychius: Πύσσαχος· ξύλον καμπύλον τοῖς μόσχοις περὶ τοὺς μυκτῆρας τιθέμενον, ὃ κωλύει θηλάζειν· [Pyssachos; xylon kampylon tois moschois peri tous myktēras tithemenon, ho kōlyei thēlazein] A curved piece of wood placed around the nostrils of calves, by which they are prevented from sucking. And the skin of a kuphad (that is, of a hedgehog) was tied to the cow's udder for the same purpose, that is, so that neither the calf nor any other animal might suck it. Rabbi Selomo on this passage: קופד שרץ הוא שנימיו חדים כמחט וקורין לו הריצון וקושרין עורו בדדי הפרה שלא יניקוה שרצים, Kuphad is a creeping thing (so the Hebrews call the smallest quadrupeds) whose hairs are sharp like col. 1037 needles; it is commonly called herisson. Its skin is tied to the cow's udder so that creeping things may not suck it. For the milk had to be guarded not from the calf alone, but also from serpents, among which boas are fed at first on the juice of cow's milk, whence they took their name. So Pliny, book 8, chapter 14. And Solinus, who follows him: It (the boa) lies in wait for herds of cattle, and fastens itself to the udder of whichever cow is most abundant in milk, and fattened by continual sucking, etc. 4. Finally, in Arabic قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud, plural قنافذ [qanāfidh] kanaphido, is the hedgehog. Barbarous translators write caused, ceufud, ceufund, etc. The matter is too well known to need proof. I will only advise the reader that the name قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud includes, besides the hedgehog, also the porcupine. This is clear from Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], who says that among the species of kunphud one is the mountain kind, having spines that can be shot, whose proper name is دلدل [duldul] duldul, that is, the porcupine. Hence in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] the female hedgehog is called أم دلدل [umm duldul] mother τοῦ [tou] duldul, that is, of the porcupine. And duldul, or porcupine, is defined in the same place II, 456 as عظيم القنافد [ʿaẓīm al-qanāfid], the larger hedgehog. And according to Algiahid: الفرق بين الدلدل والقنافد كالفرق بين البقر والجوامس والبخاتي من الابل العراب [al-farq bayna al-duldul wa-al-qanāfid ka-al-farq bayna al-baqar wa-al-jawāmis wa-al-bakhātī min al-ibil al-ʿirāb] Duldul and kanaphido (that is, the porcupine and hedgehogs properly so called) differ no otherwise than oxen from buffaloes, and Bactrian camels from Arabian ones. Finally Elmacinus, in the Saracen History, book 1, chapter 5, seems to have meant by القنفد [al-qunfud] alkunphud the porcupine rather than the hedgehog, when he writes of Aisha's camel: صار كالقنفد من السهام [ṣāra ka-al-qunfud min al-sihām] It became like an alkunphud from the darts, that is, from the multitude of darts with which it was pierced. But in this the Arabs had Pliny and Oppian to follow: the former, in book 8, chapter 35, expressly places porcupines in the hedgehog kind; the latter, in book 2 of the Cynegetica, line 603, making two kinds of land hedgehogs, some small and unwarlike, others μεγέθει πολὺ μείζονας [megethei poly meizonas], far larger, and bristling all round — ἀρειοτέρῃσιν ἀκωκαῖς [areioterēsin akōkais], with stronger points, seems to understand porcupines by those larger hedgehogs. And for Suidas too ἡ ὕστριξ [hē hystrix], the porcupine, is ὁ ἀκανθόχοιρος, ὁ χερσαῖος ἐχῖνος [ho akanthochoiros, ho chersaios echinos], the spiny pig, the land hedgehog. ¶

Iam, ne quis objiciat ex utroque Kimchio Arabum قنفذ kunphud esse testudinem, ajo, illos hac in parte graviter hallucinari. Cum Arabice testudo non dicatur kunphud, sed سلخفاة sulachpha, vel سلخفية sulachphia, quasi cortice, vel putamine latentem dicas: nempe خفا chapha est occultare; et سلخ silch, vel sulch, φολὶς cortex, proprie serpentis, sed et testudinis: ut γῆρας, quam vocem plerique usurpant pro leberide, seu spolio serpentis, sed et nonnulli pro χελωνίω, ut Aelianus Historiae lib. II. cap. 37. et Suidas in Φολίδωτον. At قنفذ kunphud esse hericium, nemo ibit inficias, qui Damirem legerit: ubi قنفذ kunphud cognominatur ابو الشوك abussuchi, id est, pater spinarum: et انقد ankado, id est, decorticator: et ابو سفين abusaphin, id est, pater, qui abradit, vel expolit; quia hujus col. 1038 cute vestes expoliuntur: et العساعس alasaiso, quia noctu vagatur. Id enim proprie est عس assa, ut et سري sara; unde proverbium apud illos in usu, اسري من قنفذ Magis noctivagus quam kunphud. Ibidem multa reperias, quae ad ejus descriptionem pertinent, in quibus haec eximia: القنفذ ان جاع صعد الكرم منكسا فيقطع العناقيد ويرمي بها ثم ينزل فياكل منها ما اطاق واذا كان له فراخ تمرغ في العناقيد لتشتبك في شوكه ويذهب به الي فراخه وهو لا يظهر الا ليلا وهو مولع باكل الافاع II, 457 ولا يتالم لها وانا لذعته الحية اكل الصعتر البري فيبري وله خمسة اسنان في فيه والبرية منها تسفد قايمة وظهر الذكر لاصق بظهر الانثي Alkunphud, cum esurit, ascendit in vitem inversus, et decerpit racemos, quos abjicit. Postea inde descendens ex iis comedit, quantum potest. Si catulos habeat, volutatur in racemis, ut spinis suis implicentur, et cum iis contendit ad catulos suos. Non apparet nisi nocte. Viperarum esum mire appetit, nec ab illis laeditur. A serpente demorsus, origani sylvestris esu sanatur. Quinque dentes habet in ore. Terrestris kunphud coit erectus, ita, ut maris dorsum haereat dorso foeminae. Haec ad hericium pertinere, nemo non videt, quamvis in iis nonnulla sint, quae in Graecis frustra quaeras. Iam, si medicos consulamus, Avicennam puta, Abenbitarem atque illum ipsum Damirem, de quo supra, eos non solum reperiemus easdem virtutes tribuere τῷ kunphud, quas τῷ ἐχίνῳ Graeci scriptores: sed et Dioscoridis et Galeni loca de echino terrestri et marino reddentes, illum القنفذ البري alkunphud albarri, seu terrestrem, hunc القنفذ البحري alkunphud al- bahari, seu marinum, constanter nominare. Ut taceam Prophetarum interpretes, qui Arabicam versionem ex Graeca expresserunt, ubique Graecum ἐχίνους reddidisse القنافذ alkanaphido, quod est Plurale τοῦ kunphud. Exempla vide Esa. XIV, 23. et XXXIV, 11. et Sophon. II, 14. Proinde ex Hebraeis id ul-tro agnoscit R. Nathan, Arabice Kimchio longe doctior, قنفذ kunphud esse hericium. Porro Hebraeorum קפוד kippod, Chaldaeorum קופד kuphad, et Ara- bum قنفذ kunphud idem esse animal, statim agnoscet, quisquis harum linguarum aliquem habebit gustum. Longa vocalis in קופד kuphad supplet defectum puncti Daghes, quod habetur in קפוד kippod. Atque illud ipsum Daghes supplet litera Nun, quae se prodit in Arabico قنفذ kunphud, ut etiam in col. 1039 Aethiopico ቀንፈዝ፡ konphoz ejusdem notionis. Sic חטה chitta, id est, triti- cum, est pro חנטה chinta, ut scribunt Arabes. Lignum, ex quo facta fuit arca testimonii, שטה sitta dicitur, pro שנטה sinta; ut Arabice سنط sent. Ara- nea עכביש accabis, pro ancabis; ut Arabice عنكبوث ancabuths. Et עז ez, id est, capra, Plurali עזים izzim, Arabice est عنز enz. Quod hic in eos observandum, qui vocem קפוד kippod revocant ad Radicem קפד kaphad, ut significet animal, quod peculiari aliqua vi incidendi polleat. Atqui vera Radix non est קפד kaphad, sed inusitata קנפד kanphad: nisi quis putet, compositam esse vocem, Gutturalibus (ut fit) elisis, ex קנה kana, quod est spiculum, et אפד aphad, quod est amicire: quia hoc animal spinis et aculeis tanquam spiculis amictus et circumvallatus est. Aut, si malis, in قنفذ kunphud Nun esse epentheticum, etiam radix קפד kaphad quaedam habet significata, quae huc referre II, 458 possis. Ut cum קפדן kaphdan usurpatur de homine aspero et iracundo. Quo sensu in Sanhedrin capite undecimo propheta Elias dicitur fuisse קפדן. Et in tractatu De Sabbatho cap. 2. fol. 30. b. לעולם יהא אדם ענותן כהלל ואל יהא קפדן כשמאי, Semper sit homo mansuetus sicut Hillel, neque sit kaphdan (seu iracundus) ut Sammai. Quia, ut ait Pomarius, homo asper et iracundus est כמו הקפוד עם קוצים, ut hericius cum spinis. Ita Graeca haec adagia, Ἐχῖνου τρα-χύτερος, et Ἅπας ἐχῖνος τραχὺς, hominem morosum significant et intractabilem. Now, lest anyone object, on the authority of both Kimchis, that the Arabs' قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud is the tortoise, I say that they are gravely mistaken on this point. For in Arabic the tortoise is not called kunphud but سلخفاة [sulaḥfāh] sulachpha, or سلخفية [sulaḥfiya] sulachphia, as if you were to say hiding in a shell or husk: for خفا [khafā] chapha means to hide, and سلخ [salkh] silch, or sulch, is φολὶς [pholis], a covering, properly of a serpent, but also of a tortoise; just as γῆρας [gēras], a word most use for the leberis, or slough of a serpent, but some also for χελωνίω [chelōniō], as Aelian, History book 2, ch. 37, and Suidas under Φολίδωτον [Pholidōton]. But that قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud is the hedgehog no one will deny who has read Damiri, where قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud is surnamed ابو الشوك [abū al-shawk] abussuchi, that is, father of thorns; and انقد [anqad] ankado, that is, the stripper of bark; and ابو سفين [abū safīn] abusaphin, that is, the father who scrapes or polishes, because garments are polished with its col. 1038 skin; and العساعس [al-ʿasāʿis] alasaiso, because it roams at night. For that is properly عس [ʿassa] assa, as also سري [sarā] sara; hence the proverb in use among them, اسري من قنفذ [asrā min qunfudh], More night-roaming than the kunphud. Everyone sees that these things pertain to the hedgehog, although among them there are some that you would seek in vain in the Greeks. Now, if we consult the physicians, say Avicenna, Ibn al-Baitar and that same Damiri mentioned above, we shall find not only that they attribute to the kunphud the same virtues that the Greek writers attribute to the ἐχίνῳ [echinō], but also that, in rendering the passages of Dioscorides and Galen on the land and sea hedgehog, they consistently call the former القنفذ البري [al-qunfudh al-barrī] alkunphud albarri, or land hedgehog, the latter القنفذ البحري [al-qunfudh al-baḥrī] alkunphud albahari, or sea hedgehog. This is to be noted here against those who trace the word קפוד kippod to the root קפד kaphad, so that it would signify an animal endowed with some peculiar power of cutting. But the true root is not קפד kaphad, but the unused קנפד kanphad; unless someone thinks that the word is a compound, with the gutturals elided (as happens), of קנה kana, which is a dart, and אפד aphad, which is to clothe, because this animal is clothed and fenced about with spines and prickles as with darts. Or, if you prefer that the Nun in قنفذ [qunfudh] kunphud is epenthetic, even the root קפד kaphad has certain meanings which II, 458 you could refer here. Because, as the Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] says, a harsh and irascible man is כמו הקפוד עם קוצים, like a hedgehog with spines. So these Greek proverbs, Ἐχῖνου τραχύτερος [Echinou trachyteros] and Ἅπας ἐχῖνος τραχὺς [Hapas echinos trachys], signify a morose and intractable man. To the same point belongs the fact that קפד kaphad, or הקפיד hikphid, means to be attentive and anxious, and הקפדה hakkephada means attention and anxiety; because, as is in the Pomarius, when the hedgehog קורץ כל גופו, covers its whole body with spines, its face hiding between its feet, and raises its prickles, with which it defends itself as with a shield and wounds those who wish to lay violent hands on it, פעולת הזאת היא השגחה והרגשה נמרצת, this action is one of providence and the keenest attention. And Aelian, book 1, chapter 31: Τὰς ἄνωθεν τρίχας οἱονεὶ βέλη ἐκπέμπει, καὶ εὐστόχως βάλλει πολλάκις, τὰ νῶτα φρίξασα, καὶ ἐκεῖναί γε πηδῶσιν. ὥσπερ οὖν ἔκ τινος ἀφειμέναι νευρᾶς· [Tas anōthen trichas hoionei belē ekpempei, kai eustochōs ballei pollakis, ta nōta phrixasa, kai ekeinai ge pēdōsin. hōsper oun ek tinos apheimenai neuras] It sends forth its upper hairs like arrows, and often aims them straight as at a target, contracting its back as it bristles. And they leap out as if shot from a bowstring. The Scholiast on Aristophanes on the Peace, and from him Suidas: Ἔστι δὲ ὕστριξ II, 461 θηρίον τρίχας ἔχον ὑὸς, ἂν ἐν τῷ διώκεσθαι ἐπακοντίζει κατὰ τῶν διωκόντων· [Esti de hystrix thērion trichas echon hyos, an en tō diōkesthai epakontizei kata tōn diōkontōn] The porcupine is a wild beast bristling with swine's bristles (whence its name), which, when someone pursues it, it shoots out at its pursuers. Porta coeli p. 19 d: גם יש מין מחוירי הבר שיש להם שערות גסים כמו חצים וזה לסבת גסות העור, There is a species of wild swine whose hairs are thick like arrows, namely because of the thickness and hardness of the skin. Avicenna, book 2 of the Canon, in the chapter قنفذ [qunfudh] Kunphud, والجبلي هو الدلدل ذو الشوك السهمي [wa-l-jabalī huwa al-duldul dhū al-shawk al-sahmī] And the mountain one (kunphud) is the duldul (porcupine), which has arrow-like spines, that is, missiles. Damir in the chapter دلدل [duldul] Duldul: واذا راي ما يكرهه انقبض فيخرج منه شوك كالمسال يخرج من اصابه والشوك الذي علي ظهره بحو الذراع [wa-idhā rāy mā yakrahuhu inqabaḍa fa-yakhruju minhu shawk ka-l-masāl yakhruju min aṣābahu wa-l-shawk alladhī ʿalī ẓahrihi bḥw al-dhirāʿ] And when it sees anything hostile, it contracts itself. And spines like needles come out of it, by which those pressing on it are wounded. And the spines that are on its back are about a cubit long. Marco Polo, book 1, chapter 33, on Scassem, a city of Tartary: In that region there are many spiny swine (porcupines), which, when they are caught, often injure men and dogs with their spines. For the dogs, set on the swine by the hunters, so provoke and stir up those beasts that, running together (contracting) their backs, on which the spines rest, they shake them violently, and with a certain violence discharge the very quills and hurl them at the nearer men and dogs. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium]: It strikes whomever it wishes with a sure and aimed blow, with a wound sometimes almost incurable, being narrow and deep, dangerous indeed if a nerve is pierced. So that Oppian not without reason sings, ¶

Ὑστρίγγων οὔπω τι πέλει κατὰ δάσκιον ὕλην Ῥίγιον εἰσιδέειν, ὅτ' ἀργαλεώτερον ἄλλο,

Which Stephanus condensed thus in his Notes on Callimachus: ¶

Hystrice nil silvis visu magis horret opacis.

Nothing in the shady woods is more dreadful to behold than the porcupine. ¶

De hoc Prophetas egisse eo sit verissimilius, quod ab Arabibus disco rem, ignotam hactenus, hystricem animal esse in Syria frequens. Damir in capite دلدل Duldul, seu De Hystrice, هو كثير ببلاد الشام والعراق وبلاد المغارب Abundat in regione Syriae et Irak (Mesopotamiae et Babyloniae), et col. 1042 in regione Megareb, id est, Africa occidentali. Quod ipsum de hericio sic docet: وهو صنفان قنفد يكون بارض مصر قدر الفار وقنفد يكون بارض الشام وعراق في قدر الكلب القلطي والفرق بينهما كالفرق بين الفار والجرذ Hericius duarum est specierum. Unus enim est in terra Aegypti, soricis magnitudine; et alter in terris Syriae et Irak (Mesopotamiae et Babyloniae), Melitensis canis magnitudine, ita, ut tam differant, quam differunt sorex et mus. II, 462 Qui aquaticum animal קפוד kippod esse colligunt ex his Esaiae de Babylone, Ponam eam in possessionem τοῦ kippod, et stagna aquarum, ne sic quidem quidquam afferunt, quod sententiae nostrae officiat. Cum in aquatilibus etiam sit echini species, qui Avicennae, Damiri, Abenbitari قنفد بحري, aliis ἐχῖνος θαλάσσιος, echinus marinus dicitur, aut vero ποτάμιος, fluviatilis. Ha-beri enim et fluviatiles echinos, Paulus Aegineta prodidit. Quibus scatere potuit Euphrates ad Babylonem. Ad eas saltem insulas, quae Euphratis ori objectae sunt, insignis magnitudinis echinos reperiri, ex Nearcho discimus. Strabo, Λέ-γει δὲ (ὁ Νέαρχος) ἐν ταῖς πρὸ τοῦ Εὐφράτου νήσοις φύεσθαι παγούρων καὶ ἐχί-νων μεγέθη· Sed nos Esaiae locum multo aliter interpretamur, nempe hoc modo: Ponam eam in possessionem τοῦ kippod (id est, terrestris echini), etiam stagna aquarum. Id est, Non modo desolabitur urbs, sed et vicina stagna sic exarescent, ut tam apta futura sit in iis, quam in urbe echino terrestri sedes, qui locis aridis maxime delectatur. Sic Sophon. II, 13. poni dicitur Ninive לשממה וציה, in desolationem et siccitatem, atque ita demum fieri, ut kippod in ea diversetur. Et Esa. XXXIV, 11. antequam Idumaea tradatur in possessionem τοῦ kippod, mutantur in picem torrentes, et pulvis in sulphurem.

That the Prophets spoke of this animal may be the more probable because I learn from the Arabs something hitherto unknown, that the porcupine is an animal common in Syria. Damir, in the chapter دلدل [duldul] Duldul, or On the Porcupine: هو كثير ببلاد الشام والعراق وبلاد المغارب [huwa kathīr bi-bilād al-Shām wa-l-ʿIrāq wa-bilād al-Maghārib] It abounds in the region of Syria and Iraq (Mesopotamia and Babylonia), and col. 1042 in the region of Megareb, that is, western Africa. He teaches the same about the hedgehog, thus: وهو صنفان قنفد يكون بارض مصر قدر الفار وقنفد يكون بارض الشام وعراق في قدر الكلب القلطي والفرق بينهما كالفرق بين الفار والجرذ [wa-huwa ṣinfān qunfud yakūn bi-arḍ Miṣr qadr al-fār wa-qunfud yakūn bi-arḍ al-Shām wa-ʿIrāq fī qadr al-kalb al-qalṭī wa-l-farq baynahumā ka-l-farq bayn al-fār wa-l-jurdh] The hedgehog is of two species. One is in the land of Egypt, of the size of a shrew; the other in the lands of Syria and Iraq (Mesopotamia and Babylonia), of the size of a Maltese dog, so that they differ as much as the shrew and the mouse differ. II, 462 Those who infer that the kippod kippod קפוד is an aquatic animal from these words of Isaiah about Babylon, I will make it a possession of the kippod, and pools of water, do not even so adduce anything that stands in the way of our view. For among aquatic creatures too there is a species of hedgehog, which by Avicenna, Damir and Ibn al-Baitar is called قنفد بحري [qunfud baḥrī], by others ἐχῖνος θαλάσσιος [echinos thalassios], sea hedgehog, or else ποτάμιος [potamios], river hedgehog. For Paul of Aegina reported that there are also river hedgehogs. The Euphrates near Babylon could have teemed with them. At least we learn from Nearchus that hedgehogs of remarkable size are found near the islands lying off the mouth of the Euphrates. Strabo: Λέγει δὲ (ὁ Νέαρχος) ἐν ταῖς πρὸ τοῦ Εὐφράτου νήσοις φύεσθαι παγούρων καὶ ἐχίνων μεγέθη· [Legei de (ho Nearchos) en tais pro tou Euphratou nēsois phyesthai pagourōn kai echinōn megethē]. But we interpret the passage of Isaiah very differently, namely in this way: I will make it a possession of the kippod (that is, of the land hedgehog), even the pools of water. That is, not only will the city be laid waste, but the neighbouring pools too will dry up, so that they will be as fit a dwelling as the city for the land hedgehog, which delights above all in dry places. Thus in Zeph 2:13 Nineveh is said to be made לשממה וציה, a desolation and dryness, and only then does it come about that the kippod lodges in it. And in Isa 34:11, before Idumaea is handed over as a possession of the kippod, the streams are turned into pitch and the dust into brimstone. ¶

Ut קפוד kippod probetur esse avis, parum in eo momenti est, quod Es. XXXIV, 11. cum avibus nominatur. Statim enim versu decimo tertio junguntur simul dracones et struthionss, quamvis animalia toto genere diversa. Et Sophonias greges, bestias, pelecanum et kippod simul in unum fascem congerit. Quod ipsum magis afficit lectorem, cum in descriptione vastitatis ea, quae ad horrorem pertinent, ita confusaneo ordine proponuntur. Cum inter multas aves leporum meminit Sophocles in hoc versu in Amyco Satyrico,

To prove that the kippod kippod קפוד is a bird, it carries little weight that in Es. XXXIV, 11. it is named together with birds. For right in verse thirteen dragons and ostriches are joined together, although they are animals of entirely different kinds. And Zephaniah gathers flocks, beasts, the pelican and the kippod together into one bundle. This affects the reader all the more, since in a description of devastation the things that inspire horror are set forth in such a jumbled order. When Sophocles, in this line of the satyr play Amycus, mentions hares among many birds, ¶

Γέρανοι, κορῶναι, γλαῦκες ἴκτινοι, λαγοὶ,

Γέρανοι, κορῶναι, γλαῦκες ἴκτινοι, λαγοὶ, [Geranoi, korōnai, glaukes iktinoi, lagoi,] ¶

Grues, cornices, noctuae, milvi, lepores: quis inde concluserit, leporem esse avis genus? Scio, non neminem pro λαγοὶ reposuisse λάροι, quod avis aquaticae genus est. Sed frustra. Athenaeus enim libri noni capite decimo quarto hunc versum citat, ut probet, λαγοὶ tam Atticum esse, quam λαγώ. Itaque retinendum λαγοί. Contra, in Luciani Syria Dea, inter multas quadrupedes aquilas sic nominari videas: Ἐν δὲ τῇ αὐλῇ ἄφετοι νέμονται βόες μεγάλοι, καὶ ἵπποι, καὶ αἐτοὶ, καὶ ἄρκτοι, καὶ λέοντες· In atrio autem liberi pascuntur boves magni, et equi, et aquilae, et ursi, et leones. Unde inepte colligatur, ibi aquilas non esse aves, sed genus quadrupedis.

Cranes, crows, owls, kites, hares: who would conclude from this that the hare is a kind of bird? I know that someone has substituted λάροι [laroi], a kind of water bird, for λαγοὶ [lagoi]. But in vain. For Athenaeus, book 9, chapter 14, cites this line to prove that λαγοὶ [lagoi] is as much Attic as λαγώ [lagō]. So λαγοί [lagoi] must be retained. Conversely, in Lucian's Syrian Goddess you may see eagles named among many quadrupeds thus: Ἐν δὲ τῇ αὐλῇ ἄφετοι νέμονται βόες μεγάλοι, καὶ ἵπποι, καὶ αἐτοὶ, καὶ ἄρκτοι, καὶ λέοντες· [En de tē aulē aphetoi nemontai boes megaloi, kai hippoi, kai aetoi, kai arktoi, kai leontes] In the court, moreover, there graze freely great oxen, and horses, and eagles, and bears, and lions. From which it would be absurd to infer that the eagles there are not birds but a kind of quadruped. ¶
