II, 645 col. 89 CAP. XIII.

II, 645 col. 89 Chapter 13.

Quid sit πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ, perdix venator in cavea, Sirach. XI, 31. al. 37.

What is πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ [perdix thēreutēs en kartallō], the hunter partridge in a cage, Sir 11:31 var. 37.

Postquam de spurio perdice actum, de genuino pauca dicemus ad celebrem Sirachidis locum Eccles. XI, 31. al. 37. Πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ, οὕτως καρδία ὑπερηφάνου, καὶ ὡς ὁ κατάσκοπος ἐπιβλέπει πτῶσιν· Ut perdix venator in cavea, ita cor superbi, qui ut speculator observat casum. Sensus est, hominem superbum, altero in ruinam impulso, sic in illum insultare, quomodo perdix venator in cavea in sui generis aves, quas in laqueum induxit. Ibi enim πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς est perdix cicur, qui feros perdices suis artibus in caveam aut laqueos allicit. Aristoteles Historiae lib. 9. cap. 8. Καὶ τῶν περδίκων δ᾽ οἱ τι-θασσοὶ τοὺς ἀγρίους πέρδικας ὀχεύουσι, καὶ ἐπικορίζουσι (lege ἐπικοῤῥίζουσι) καὶ ὑβρίζουσιν. Ἐπὶ δὲ τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρδικα ὠθεῖται τῶν ἀγρίων ὁ ἡγεμὼν, ἀν-τιάσας ὡς μαχούμενος, τούτου δ᾽ ἁλόντος ἐν ταῖς πηκταῖς, πάλιν προσέρχεται ἄλλος, ἀντιάσας τὸν αὐτὸν τρόπον. Cicures quoque perdices feros subagitant, et verberant, et contumelia afficiunt. Et in venatorem perdicem impetum facit agrestium dux, contra congrediens ut pugnaturus. Eo vero capto in viminea cavea, denuo contra procedit alter eodem modo. Vides utrobique τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρ- col. 90 δικα, perdicem venatorem, et caveam vimineam. Id enim proprie κάρταλλος in Syrachide, ut in Philosopho αἱ πήκται. Hesychius Κάρταλον (lege Κάρταλλον)· τὸ πλεκτὸν ἀγγεῖον (malim πηκτὸν, ut in Aristotele) ἐν τοῖς ὀψαρτυτικοῖς κλωβός. Recte omnino. Cavea enim hujusmodi vocatur etiam κλωβὸς, ut Hebraice כלוב club, et Syriace ܩܠܘܒܝܐ klubja. Sic Ierem. V, 27. ככלוב מלא עוף, ut cluub (cavea) scatens avibus. Ubi Syrus, ܐܝܟ ܩܠܘܒܝܐ, ut klubja (quamvis Graeci, ὡς παγὶς ἐφεσταμένη, et Arabs مثل فخ منصوب II, 646 ܡܨܝܕܬܐ ut laqueus tensus, et Hieronymus, sicut decipula). Agitur enim de cavea, in quam alliciuntur aves, quod patet ex tota serie. Ita enim habet versus proxime praecedens: Quia inventi sunt in populo meo insidiantes quasi aucupes, laqueos ponentes ad capiendos homines. Et hic ipse, Sicut club, scatens avibus, ita domus eorum plenae sunt fraude: id est, rebus, per fraudem partis et comparatis. Itaque quod fugit Lexicographos, ut in Hebraeo Ieremiae textu כלוב club, ita etiam in Graecis scriptoribus κλουβὸς, aut κλωβὸς, vel pro dolosa hac cavea, vel pro cavea quavis etiam alibi occurrit. Oppianus in Ixeuticis [III, 14.] de aegitho, Capitur κλωβῷ, cavea, cui alius, pridem captus, ad alliciendum voce includitur. Poeta vetus apud Suidam, in Ἀμφιῤῥῶγας· Κλωβοὺς τ᾽ ἀμφιῤῥῶγας, caveas undique diruptas. Philostorgius lib. 10. cap. 11. (in manuscripto meo, quod edito exemplari saepe est emendatius) de Aegyptio pumilione, Ὁ δὲ Αἰγύπτιος οὕτω κατεβραχύνετο, ὥστε μηδ᾽ ἀχαρίστως τοὺς ἐν τοῖς κλουβοῖς πέρδικας ἐκμιμεῖσθαι, καὶ συναθύρειν αὐτῷ πρὸς ἔριν ἐκείνους· Aegyptius vero ita contrahebatur, ut perdices in clubis (seu caveis) inclusos non injucunde imitaretur, qui et ipsi cum eo per lusum contendebant. Et eundem Nicephorus lib. 12. cap. 37. fuisse dicit παραπλήσιον πέρδικι, καὶ μὴ ἀχάριστον ἐν κλωβῷ τὴν ἐκείνου προσομιλίαν. Et Cedrenus editionis Parisiensis pag. 592. B. Ζῶον ἦν τι πτῆνον ἐν κλωβῷ κατὰ τὰ βασίλεια αἰωρούμενον. Avis quaedam in regia erat in clobo (id est, cavea) appensa. Glossae Philoxeni, Κλωβὸς, cavea. Itaque frustra vir doctissimus in Philostorgio pro κλουβοῖς rescribi vult κλοιοῖς, vel κλυβοῖς. Et Nicephori interpres voces ἐν κλωβῷ perridicule reddidit, In globo scilicet hominum. Caveam capiendis avibus Talmudici in tractatu Kilaim פלצור paltsur vocant, et Romani transennam. Plautus in Bacchidibus Actus quarti Scena Nimio,

Having dealt with the spurious partridge, we shall say a little about the genuine one, with reference to the well-known passage of Sirach, Sir 11:31 var. 37: Πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ, οὕτως καρδία ὑπερηφάνου, καὶ ὡς ὁ κατάσκοπος ἐπιβλέπει πτῶσιν [Perdix thēreutēs en kartallō, houtōs kardia hyperēphanou, kai hōs ho kataskopos epiblepei ptōsin]; Like a hunter partridge in a cage, so is the heart of the proud man, who like a spy watches for a fall. The sense is that a proud man, when another has been driven to ruin, exults over him in the same way as the hunter partridge in a cage exults over the birds of its own kind that it has led into the snare. For there πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς [perdix thēreutēs] is a tame partridge, which by its arts lures wild partridges into the cage or the snares. Aristotle, History book 9, ch. 8: Καὶ τῶν περδίκων δ᾽ οἱ τιθασσοὶ τοὺς ἀγρίους πέρδικας ὀχεύουσι, καὶ ἐπικορίζουσι [kai tōn perdikōn d᾽ hoi tithassoi tous agrious perdikas ocheuousi, kai epikorizousi] (read ἐπικοῤῥίζουσι [epikorrizousi]) καὶ ὑβρίζουσιν. Ἐπὶ δὲ τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρδικα ὠθεῖται τῶν ἀγρίων ὁ ἡγεμὼν, ἀντιάσας ὡς μαχούμενος, τούτου δ᾽ ἁλόντος ἐν ταῖς πηκταῖς, πάλιν προσέρχεται ἄλλος, ἀντιάσας τὸν αὐτὸν τρόπον [kai hybrizousin. Epi de ton thēreutēn perdika ōtheitai tōn agriōn ho hēgemōn, antiasas hōs machoumenos, toutou d᾽ halontos en tais pēktais, palin proserchetai allos, antiasas ton auton tropon]. Tame partridges also tread the wild partridges, and beat them, and treat them with insult. And the leader of the wild ones rushes at the hunter partridge, advancing against it as if to fight. When he has been caught in the wicker cage, another in turn comes forward against it in the same way. You see in both places τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρ- col. 90 δικα [ton thēreutēn perdika], the hunter partridge, and the wicker cage. For that is properly κάρταλλος [kartallos] in Sirach, as αἱ πήκται [hai pēktai] is in the Philosopher. Hesychius: Κάρταλον [Kartalon] (read Κάρταλλον [Kartallon]): τὸ πλεκτὸν ἀγγεῖον [to plekton angeion] (I would prefer πηκτὸν [pēkton], as in Aristotle) ἐν τοῖς ὀψαρτυτικοῖς κλωβός [en tois opsartytikois klōbos]. Quite correctly. For a cage of this kind is also called κλωβὸς [klōbos], as in Hebrew כלוב club, and in Syriac ܩܠܘܒܝܐ [קלוביא] klubja. Thus Ierem. V, 27: ככלוב מלא עוף, like a cluub (cage) teeming with birds. Here the Syriac has ܐܝܟ ܩܠܘܒܝܐ [איך קלוביא], like a klubja (although the Greeks have ὡς παγὶς ἐφεσταμένη [hōs pagis ephestamenē], and the Arabic مثل فخ منصوب [mithl fakhkh manṣūb] II, 646 ܡܨܝܕܬܐ [מצידתא] like a set snare, and Jerome like a trap). For the subject is a cage into which birds are lured, as is clear from the whole context. For the verse immediately preceding reads: For among my people are found those who lie in wait like fowlers, setting snares to catch men. And this verse itself: Like a club teeming with birds, so their houses are full of deceit, that is, of things gained and acquired by deceit. Thus, a point that has escaped the lexicographers: just as כלוב club occurs in the Hebrew text of Jeremiah, so also in Greek writers κλουβὸς [kloubos] or κλωβὸς [klōbos] occurs elsewhere too, either for this deceitful cage or for any cage at all. Oppian in the Ixeutica [III, 14.], on the aegithus: It is caught κλωβῷ [klōbō], in a cage, in which another bird, caught earlier, is shut up to lure it by its voice. An old poet in Suidas, under Ἀμφιῤῥῶγας [Amphirrhōgas]: Κλωβοὺς τ᾽ ἀμφιῤῥῶγας [Klōbous t᾽ amphirrhōgas], cages broken on all sides. Philostorgius book 10, ch. 11 (in my manuscript, which is often more correct than the printed copy), on the Egyptian dwarf: Ὁ δὲ Αἰγύπτιος οὕτω κατεβραχύνετο, ὥστε μηδ᾽ ἀχαρίστως τοὺς ἐν τοῖς κλουβοῖς πέρδικας ἐκμιμεῖσθαι, καὶ συναθύρειν αὐτῷ πρὸς ἔριν ἐκείνους [Ho de Aigyptios houtō katebrachyneto, hōste mēd᾽ acharistōs tous en tois kloubois perdikas ekmimeisthai, kai synathyrein autō pros erin ekeinous]; The Egyptian was so stunted that he imitated, not unpleasingly, the partridges shut up in clubs (or cages), which themselves contended with him in play. And Nicephorus, book 12, ch. 37, says that the same man was παραπλήσιον πέρδικι, καὶ μὴ ἀχάριστον ἐν κλωβῷ τὴν ἐκείνου προσομιλίαν [paraplēsion perdiki, kai mē acharislon en klōbō tēn ekeinou prosomilian]. And Cedrenus, Paris edition, p. 592 B: Ζῶον ἦν τι πτῆνον ἐν κλωβῷ κατὰ τὰ βασίλεια αἰωρούμενον [Zōon ēn ti ptēnon en klōbō kata ta basileia aiōroumenon]. There was a certain bird in the palace, hung up in a clobus (that is, a cage). The Glosses of Philoxenus: Κλωβὸς [Klōbos], cavea [cage]. Therefore a very learned man wishes in vain to change κλουβοῖς [klouhois] in Philostorgius to κλοιοῖς [kloiois] or κλυβοῖς [klybois]. And the translator of Nicephorus rendered the words ἐν κλωβῷ [en klōbō] quite absurdly: In a globe, namely of men. The Talmudists, in the tractate Kilaim, call a cage for catching birds פלצור paltsur, and the Romans transenna. Plautus in the Bacchides, act 4, the scene Nimio:

Nunc ab transenna hic turdus lumbricum petit. Pendebit hodie pulchre, ita intendi tenus.

Now this thrush is going for the worm from the trap. He will hang there nicely today, so well have I set the snare.

Et in Persa Act. 4. Scena Cui homini,

And in the Persa, act 4, the scene Cui homini:

Hunc ego hominem hodie in transennam doctis ducam dolis.

Today I shall lead this man into the trap with clever tricks.

Et in Rudente Act. 4. Scena Quam mox,

And in the Rudens, act 4, the scene Quam mox:

O Gripe, Gripe, in aetate hominum plurimae Fiant transennae, ubi capiuntur dolis (1).

O Gripus, Gripus, in men's lifetime there come to be very many traps, in which they are caught by tricks R1.

II, 647 Porro de perdice venatore etiam alii meminerunt. Nisi quod pro venatore Athenaeus lib. 9. θηρεύοντα πέρδικα, venantem perdicem vocat. Plinius a), aucupis indicem, aut illicem potius, ut Turnebus b) et Scaliger c) legendum monent. Aelianus d) eodem sensu τὸν παλεύοντα πέρδικα. Eustathius Antiochenus τὸν πέρδικα συμπράττοντα τοῖς θηρευταῖς πρὸς τὴν ἄγραν. Theodoretus in Catena in Ieremiam πέρδικα θηρατικὸν, tales addens e Babylone haberi. Nempe id aucupii genus in Oriente jam olim fuit in usu. Proinde in Xenophonte lib. 1. Cyropaediae Cambyses Cyrum filium sic alloquitur: Ὄρνιθες δὲ ἐπεπαί-δευτό σοι, ὥς τέ σοι μὲν τὰ συμφέροντα ὑπερητεῖν, τὰς δὲ ὁμοφύλους ὄρνιθας ἐξαπατᾷν, Aves a te ita edoctae fuerant, ut et tibi utilia subministrarent, et aves ejusdem generis deciperent. Qui plura de hoc argumento volet, eos consulat, quos citavimus. Et Albertum Magnum ubi de cubeth e), id est, perdice foemina, col. 91 Arabice القبج quam cavea inclusam ait mares ad se allicere. Quamvis Damir id ipsum mari his verbis tribuit, في طبع الذكر ان يخدع امثاله بقرقرته ولهذا اتخذه الصيادون في اشراكهم ليكثر القرقرة فيجتمع الحجل فيقعن معه De maris natura hoc est, ut aves sibi similes cantu suo alliciat: proinde venatores illum secum assumunt, ut frequenti cantu suo perdices foeminas congreget, quae ad eum volent. Sed ad id utrumque sexum et vario successu adhiberi, pluribus docent Philosophus, Aelianus et Athenaeus ubi supra. Arabica versio in Sirachide, pro القبج في القفص perdix in cavea, nullo sensu habet الصنج في الرقص sistrum, vel cymbalum in tripudio. Quod factum esse per scribarum imperitiam, docet literarum similitudo. Pagnino, Castalioni, Ariae, Camerario, Iansenio, aliis πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς est capta perdix, quod esset πέρδιξ θη-ρευθεὶς. Pari errore in Syro habetur ܐܝܟ ܩܩܒܢܐ ܕܨܝܕܐ ut perdix venatione capta; et in Vulgata, sicut perdix inducitur in caveam.

II, 647 Moreover, others also mention the hunter partridge. Except that instead of "hunter" Athenaeus, book 9, calls it θηρεύοντα πέρδικα [thēreuonta perdika], a hunting partridge. Pliny a) calls it the fowler's informer, or rather his decoy [illicem], as Turnebus b) and Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] c) advise reading. Aelian d), in the same sense, says τὸν παλεύοντα πέρδικα [ton paleuonta perdika]. Eustathius of Antioch says τὸν πέρδικα συμπράττοντα τοῖς θηρευταῖς πρὸς τὴν ἄγραν [ton perdika sympratonta tois thēreutais pros tēn agran]. Theodoret, in the Catena on Jeremiah, says πέρδικα θηρατικὸν [perdika thēratikon], adding that such birds are obtained from Babylon. Indeed, this kind of fowling was already in use in the East long ago. Accordingly, in Xenophon, book 1 of the Cyropaedia, Cambyses addresses his son Cyrus thus: Ὄρνιθες δὲ ἐπεπαίδευτό σοι, ὥς τέ σοι μὲν τὰ συμφέροντα ὑπερητεῖν, τὰς δὲ ὁμοφύλους ὄρνιθας ἐξαπατᾷν [Ornithes de epepaideuto soi, hōs te soi men ta sympheronta hypērētein, tas de homophylous ornithas exapatan], "You had trained birds so that they would both serve you in what was useful and deceive birds of the same kind." Whoever wants more on this subject should consult those we have cited. Also Albertus Magnus, where he treats of the cubeth e), that is, the female partridge, col. 91 in Arabic القبج [al-qabj], which he says, when shut in a cage, lures the males to itself. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], however, attributes this very thing to the male in these words: في طبع الذكر ان يخدع امثاله بقرقرته ولهذا اتخذه الصيادون في اشراكهم ليكثر القرقرة فيجتمع الحجل فيقعن معه [fī ṭabʿ al-dhakar an yakhdaʿa amthālahu bi-qarqaratihi wa-li-hādhā ittakhadhahu al-ṣayyādūn fī ashrākihim li-yukthira al-qarqara fa-yajtamiʿa al-ḥajal fa-yaqaʿna maʿahu] "It is the nature of the male to lure birds like itself by its call; therefore hunters take it with them, so that by its frequent calling it may gather the female partridges, which fly to it." But that both sexes are used for this, with varying success, is shown at length by the Philosopher, Aelian and Athenaeus in the places cited above. The Arabic version of Sirach, instead of القبج في القفص [al-qabj fī al-qafaṣ] a partridge in a cage, has, without any sense, الصنج في الرقص [al-ṣanj fī al-raqṣ] a sistrum, or a cymbal in the dance. That this arose through the ignorance of scribes is shown by the similarity of the letters. For Pagnino, Castalio, Arias, Camerarius, Jansenius and others, πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς [perdix thēreutēs] is a captured partridge, as though it were πέρδιξ θηρευθεὶς [perdix thēreutheis]. By the same error the Syriac has ܐܝܟ ܩܩܒܢܐ ܕܨܝܕܐ [איך קקבנא דצידא] like a partridge taken in hunting; and the Vulgate, "as a partridge is led into the cage."
