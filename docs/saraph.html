III, 211 CHAPTER XIII. col. 421 ¶

Probatur, שרף saraph esse Hydrum et Chersydrum; a quibus morsi Israelitae aenei serpentis aspectu sanati sunt.

It is proved that שרף saraph is the hydrus and chersydrus; those bitten by which, the Israelites, were healed by the sight of the bronze serpent. ¶

Etiam שרף saraph dignus est commentatione nostra, cum et Christi typus fuerit Num. XXI, 9. Ioh. III, 14. et nobilissimos angelorum habeat cognomines Es. VI, 2. Accedit, quod serpentum solus volare legitur Es. XIV, 29. (al. XV, 2.) et XXX, 6. Ubi מעופף ad verum volatum pertinet, non ad saltum, ut Hebraei perperam scribunt. Duo loca memorant Sacri Scriptores, in quibus alitur potissimum, Arabiam desertam scilicet, Deut. VIII, 15. et Aegyptum, Es. XXX, 6. Nomen ab adustione factum: seu propter colorem igneum, quem expresserit aeneus serpens; ita Vatablus: vel, quia flatu et morsu adurit. R. Selomo, in Numerorum XXI. את הנחשים השרפים ששורפים את האדם בארס שיניהם, Serpentes, seraphim dicti, quia hominem urebant veneno dentium suorum. Et in Es. XXX. de serpente saraph, וזרק להב מפיו, Flammam ex ore suo spargit. Et in Lexico Kimchius, נקראים כן לפי שהם שורפים בהבל פיהם, Ita vocantur, quia urunt halitu oris sui. Non absimili de causa ii angeli dicti שרפים seraphim, (id est, Dionysio a) et Cyrillo b) interpretibus, ἐμπρησταὶ, ἤτοι θερμαίνοντες) quorum unus Esaiae, prophetae, labia mundavit, carbone ex altari sumto. Sed et Graeci πρηστῆρας et καύσωνας quosdam serpentes etiam ab ardore appellant. Quam causam habuere nuperi quidam interpretes, cur col. 422 שרף saraph Hebraeum presterem et causonem redderent, vocibus, quae Hebraicae respondent ad amussim, si originem respexeris. Nam שרף Verbum idem esse, quod ἐμπρῆσαι, vel κατακαῦσαι, statim animadvertet, quisquis cum fonte conferet Graecorum versiones. Sed originationibus ita mordicus haerendum III, 212 non est, ut res interim negligatur. Cum diversissimis serpentibus id commune sit, ut flatu vel morsu adurant, quidni desumtum inde nomen ad varias serpentum species pertinere potuerit? Quid, quod ipsi Graeci nesciunt, quod serpentis genus sit prester? Presterem enim alii confundunt cum dipsade; ut Dioscorides et Aegineta. Alii diversum faciunt; ut Lucanus et Solinus. Et quid hic senserit Aelianus, nemo facile dixerit, cum alibi Dioscoridi, alibi Lucano videatur accedere. Itaque rem potius involvunt, quam explicant, qui שרף saraph interpretantur voce tam dubia et ambigua.

The שרף saraph also deserves our discussion, since it was a type of Christ, Num 21:9. John 3:14, and since it shares its name with the noblest of the angels, Isa 6:2. Moreover, it alone among serpents is said to fly, Isa 14:29 (var. XV, 2.) and XXX, 6. There מעופף refers to true flight, not to leaping, as the Hebrews wrongly write. The Sacred Writers mention two places in which it chiefly breeds, namely Arabia Deserta, Deut 8:15, and Egypt, Isa 30:6. The name is derived from burning: either because of its fiery colour, which the bronze serpent would have reproduced (so Vatablus), or because it burns with its breath and bite. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], on Numbers XXI: את הנחשים השרפים ששורפים את האדם בארס שיניהם, The serpents were called seraphim because they burned a man with the poison of their teeth. And on Es. XXX, of the serpent saraph: וזרק להב מפיו, It scatters flame from its mouth. And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon: נקראים כן לפי שהם שורפים בהבל פיהם, They are so called because they burn with the breath of their mouth. For a not dissimilar reason those angels were called שרפים seraphim (that is, as Dionysius a) and Cyril b) interpret it, ἐμπρησταὶ, ἤτοι θερμαίνοντες [emprēstai, ētoi thermainontes]), one of whom cleansed the lips of the prophet Isaiah with a coal taken from the altar. The Greeks, too, name certain serpents πρηστῆρας [prēstēras] and καύσωνας [kausōnas], likewise from burning. This was the reason why certain recent interpreters col. 422 rendered the Hebrew שרף saraph as prester and causon, words which correspond exactly to the Hebrew if one considers their origin. For anyone who compares the versions of the Greeks with the source will at once notice that the verb שרף is the same as ἐμπρῆσαι [emprēsai] or κατακαῦσαι [katakausai]. But one must not cling so stubbornly to etymologies III, 212 that the thing itself is meanwhile neglected. Since it is common to very different serpents that they burn with breath or bite, why could a name taken from this not apply to various species of serpents? What of the fact that the Greeks themselves do not know what kind of serpent the prester is? For some confuse the prester with the dipsas, as Dioscorides and Aegineta do; others make it distinct, as Lucan and Solinus do. And what Aelian thought here nobody could easily say, since in one place he seems to side with Dioscorides, in another with Lucan. Therefore those who interpret שרף saraph by so doubtful and ambiguous a word rather obscure the matter than explain it. ¶

Mihi, omnia expendenti, maxime fit verissimile, שרף saraph esse hydrum, vel chersydrum. Hi duo parum inter se differunt. Qui enim hydrus hyeme fuerat, dum aqua erat in lacunis, postquam ea defecit aestu, in sicco degere cogitur, atque ita fit chersydrus, id est, hydrus ἐν χέρσῳ, in sicco degens. Tum vero infestante siti in calidis regionibus virus habet multo acrius et nocentius. Unde Poeta c),

To me, weighing everything, it seems most probable that שרף saraph is the hydrus or chersydrus. These two differ little from each other. For what had been a hydrus in winter, while there was water in the pools, is forced to live on dry land after the water has failed in the heat, and so becomes a chersydrus, that is, a hydrus living ἐν χέρσῳ [en chersō], on dry land. Then indeed, tormented by thirst in hot regions, it has a far sharper and more harmful poison. Hence the Poet c): ¶

Postquam exhausta palus, terraeque ardore dehiscunt,
Exsilit in siccum, et, flammantia lumina torquens,
Saevit agris, asperque siti, atque exterritus aestu.

After the marsh is drained and the earth gapes with heat, it leaps out onto the dry ground and, rolling its flaming eyes, rages in the fields, fierce with thirst and maddened by the heat. ¶

Cum vero terrestris sit, maxime merum et nocentius id acquirit. Itaque tum morsu suo exurit, quidquid attingit. Nicander de morsis a chersydro, Theriacῶn versu 363.

In moist places, sated with moist food, it does not have pure poison; but when it is terrestrial, it acquires it in its most undiluted and more harmful form. Nec modus est, sorbent avidae praecordia flammae, Coeruleusque fluit toto de corpore sudor, Ambustique sonant nervi, pulmonibus errat Ignis edax imis, perque omnes pascitur artus.

Countless pains overpower the men, ravaging them as with fires; and at once burning pustules spread over the limbs. For that is properly πρηδόνες [prēdones]. Aëtius: In those bitten there occurs swelling, continuous burning pain, etc. Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] d): تاخذ اللسعة بوجع شديد وتلتهب [taʾkhudh al-lasʿa bi-wajaʿ shadīd wa-taltahib] The bite begins with violent pain, and becomes inflamed. Others in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] report that on a rod brought to its mouth blisters are raised, or pustules of the colour of bile; and that a sword, from the point to the hilt, is stained a foul blue colour, as if seized by a violent flame; likewise that this poison has a fiery force, and therefore always seeks the upper parts. The poets imagine that Hercules was consumed by this fire when he had put on a garment dipped in the poison of the Lernaean Hydra. Ovid, Metamorphoses, book 9, Fable 3: III, 213 The very blood hisses, as a glowing plate once dipped in a cold pool, and boils with the burning poison. There is no limit: the greedy flames suck up his vitals, dark-blue sweat flows from his whole body, his scorched sinews crackle, the devouring fire roams in the depths of his lungs and feeds through all his limbs. ¶

Although these things are exaggerated, as is the custom of poets, they nevertheless show abundantly that the name שרף saraph, that is, ἐμπρηστοῦ [emprēstou], suits this evil most of all. ¶

Unum video posse objici, hydros in aquosis locis nasci et educari: qualibus caruisse desertum Israelitarum vero consentaneum est, in illa parte inprimis, in qua morsi sunt a serpentibus. Cum ob id ipsum hi serpentes in illos immissi sint, quod propter aquae inopiam in Deum et Mosem obmurmurassent, Num. XXI, 5. 6. Sed huic objectioni potest occurri multifariam. 1. Neque enim, si siti fatiscentibus Israelitis poculentae defuerunt aquae, continuo sequitur, nullas fuisse in deserto palustres et lutulentas aquas, in quibus hydri laterent. Certe Nicander auctor est, hydros ut plurimum degere ἐπὶ βροχθώδει λίμνῃ, id III, 216 est, ἀνύδρῳ, ἢ ξηρᾷ, ἢ ὀλίγον ὕδωρ ἐχούσῃ, in lacu inaquoso, aut arido, aut parum aquae habente, ut interpretatur Scholiastes. 2. Praeterea hydros notum est (atque id jam ante p) probavimus), aqua deficiente non continuo mori, sed fieri chersydros, atque hoc ipso vere seraphim, id est, ardentes et exurentes. Idem hic contigisse probabile est: et tum fuisse serpentes illos morsus periculosioris; quia etiam e paludibus aquas aestus absumpserat. Non vanam esse hanc conjecturam, temporis ratio probat. Addit enim Nicander, hydros fieri chersydros, et vias obsidere circa Caniculares dies:

I see one possible objection: that hydri are born and reared in watery places, and it is reasonable to suppose that the desert of the Israelites lacked such places, above all in the part where they were bitten by the serpents, since these serpents were sent against them for the very reason that they had murmured against God and Moses for lack of water, Num 21:5–6. But this objection can be met in several ways. 1. Even if the Israelites, faint with thirst, lacked drinkable water, it does not at once follow that there were no marshy and muddy waters in the desert in which hydri might lurk. Inimicos illos meos, qui noluerunt me regnare supra se, huc adducite, et coram me jugulate.

III, 220 From these things it is clear that the sign expressed the thing signified exceedingly well; yet the reality rises many degrees above the figure. For the bronze serpent, although a symbol of God's grace, could of itself do nothing. For he who turned to it was healed not by the thing that he saw, but by you, the Saviour of all, as the author of the book of Wisdom XVI, 7 truly says. But Christ has life hidden in himself, Coloss. III, 3, and gives life to whomever he wills, John 5:21. The sight of the serpent brought healing to the Israelites alone; faith in Christ brings it to all, for in him there is neither Jew nor Greek, but you are all one in Christ Jesus, Gal. III, 28. The bronze serpent rescued those who looked at it from the death of the body in such a way that they still remained subject to death; through Christ we are freed for ever from the death of the soul as well as of the body, as he himself teaches, John 11:26. Whoever lives and believes in me shall never die. The power of the bronze serpent lasted for a day or two; but Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today, and for ever, Heb 13:8. Therefore it was impious to worship the bronze serpent, and Hezekiah rightly broke in pieces so outstanding a monument of divine power when he noticed that the Israelites were burning incense to it, 2 Regum XVIII, 4. But all honour the Son, just as they honour the Father, John 5:23. And all the angels worship him, Heb 1:6. And God has highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, on earth and under the earth, Phil. II, 9. 10. Accordingly, whereas the bronze serpent which the Israelites worshipped was broken in pieces, on the contrary those will be broken in pieces who refuse to Christ the worship due to him. Ps 2:9. You shall break them with a rod of iron, and dash them in pieces like a potter's vessel. And Dan. II, 44, of the kingdom of Christ: It shall crush and consume all those kingdoms, but it shall itself stand for ever. And in the parable, Luc. XIX, 27: Those enemies of mine, who did not want me to reign over them, bring them here and slay them before me. ¶

In quo praecise loco fuerit erectus serpens aeneus, Moses non dicit: sed Israelitas, inde profectos, narrat castra posuisse in Oboth, Num. XXI, 10. Unde sequitur, locum illum fuisse פינון Phinon, vel פונון Phunon, de quo Gen. XXXVI, 41. Num. XXXVIII, 42. 43. et 1 Paral. I, 52. Nam ex Phunone profecti castra metati sunt in Oboth, Num. XXXIII, 43. Is porro fuit Idumaeae locus, fodinis aeris valde insignis, ut constat ex Patribus, qui Phaeno, et Phennen, et Fenon, et Metallo-fenon scribunt; atque inde Phaenesia, vel Phennesia metalla. Sic apud Eusebium lib. 8. Historiae cap. 13. Sylvano ecclesiarum circa Gazam episcopo, et aliis undequadraginta, caput amputatum est κατὰ τὰ ἐν Φαινοῖ χαλκοῦ μέταλλα, ad aeris metalla, quae in Phaeno. Et in Athanasii Epistola Ad Solitarios narratur Eutychius, εἰς μέταλλον ἀποσταλῆναι, καὶ μέταλλον οὐχ ἁπλῶς, ἀλλ᾽ εἰς τὸ τῆς III, 221 metallum fuisse missus, neque solum ad metallum, sed et ad metal- lum Phaeno. Et apud Epiphanium in Meletianis, Meletius, et alii multi in exilium acti sunt, ὑπερορισθέντες ἐν τοῖς Φαινήσιος μετάλλοις, ad Phaenesia metalla relegati. Theodoretus Historiae lib. 4. cap. 19. Τοῖς Φεννεσίοις καὶ Προκοννησίοις παρεδίδοντο μετάλλοις. Ad Phennesia et Proconnesia metalla damnabantur. Nec multo post de quodam Damasi diacono, Τοῖς κατὰ Φέννης παρεδόθη μετάλλοις· ἔστι δὲ ταῦτα τοῦ χαλκοῦ· Ad Phennes metal- la damnatus est; quae sunt aeris metalla. Ita etiam Nicephorus Callistus lib. XI, 28. Sed disertissime Hieronymus in Locis; FENON, castra filiorum Israe-lis in deserto. Fuit autem quondam civitas Principum Edom, nunc viculus in deserto, ubi aeris metalla damnatorum suppliciis effodiuntur, inter civitatem Pe- col. 428 tram et Zoaram. Item, DEDAN est in quarto milliari a Metallo-feno con- tra Aquilonem. Eodem refero haec Aristeae in Historia de Septuaginta In- terpretibus, Ἐλέγετο δὲ καὶ ἐκ τῶν παρακειμένων ὀρέων τῆς Ἀραβίας μέ-ταλλα χαλκᾶ καὶ σιδηρᾶ συνίστασθαι πρότερον· ἐκλέλειπται δὲ ταῦτα, καθ᾽ ὃν ἐπεκράτησαν χρόνον Πέρσαι. Narrabatur etiam, ex Arabiae monti- bus (Iudaeae) vicinis prius metalla aeris et ferri fuisse eruta: sed ea defecisse (id est, neglecta fuisse, ut constat ex sequentibus), quo tempore Persae rerum potiebantur. In his fodinis sumptum videtur aes, ex quo conflatus fuit serpens aeneus. Neque id fortasse sine mysterio. Sic enim significari potuit corpus, quod assumpsit Christus, non aliunde fuisse allatum, sed ibi natum, et formatum, ubi in carne manifestatus est.

Moses does not say in exactly what place the bronze serpent was set up; but he relates that the Israelites, setting out from there, encamped in Oboth, Num 21:10. From this it follows that the place was פינון Phinon, or פונון Phunon, of which see Gen 36:41. Num 38:42–43. and 1 Chr 1:52. For setting out from Phunon they encamped in Oboth, Num 33:43. This, moreover, was a place in Idumaea very famous for its copper mines, as is clear from the Fathers, who write Phaeno, Phennen, Fenon and Metallo-fenon; and hence Phaenesia or Phennesia metalla [the Phaenesian or Phennesian mines]. Thus in Eusebius, book 8 of the History, ch. 13, Sylvanus, bishop of the churches around Gaza, and thirty-nine others were beheaded κατὰ τὰ ἐν Φαινοῖ χαλκοῦ μέταλλα [kata ta en Phainoi chalkou metalla], at the copper mines which are in Phaeno. And in Athanasius' Letter To the Solitaries it is related that Eutychius εἰς μέταλλον ἀποσταλῆναι, καὶ μέταλλον οὐχ ἁπλῶς, ἀλλ᾽ εἰς τὸ τῆς [eis metallon apostalēnai, kai metallon ouch haplōs, all᾽ eis to tēs] III, 221 was sent to the mine, and not merely to a mine, but to the mine of Phaeno. And in Epiphanius, on the Meletians: Meletius and many others were driven into exile, ὑπερορισθέντες ἐν τοῖς Φαινήσιος μετάλλοις [hyperoristhentes en tois Phainēsios metallois], banished to the Phaenesian mines. Theodoret, History book 4, ch. 19: Τοῖς Φεννεσίοις καὶ Προκοννησίοις παρεδίδοντο μετάλλοις [Tois Phennesiois kai Prokonnēsiois paredidonto metallois]. They were condemned to the Phennesian and Proconnesian mines. And not much later, of a certain deacon of Damasus: Τοῖς κατὰ Φέννης παρεδόθη μετάλλοις· ἔστι δὲ ταῦτα τοῦ χαλκοῦ· [Tois kata Phennēs paredothē metallois; esti de tauta tou chalkou;] He was condemned to the mines at Phennes, which are copper mines. So also Nicephorus Callistus, book 11, 28. But most explicitly Jerome in the Places: FENON, a camp of the children of Israel in the desert. It was once a city of the Princes of Edom, now a small village in the desert, where copper is dug out of the mines by condemned convicts as their punishment, between the city of Pe- col. 428 tra and Zoara. Likewise: DEDAN is at the fourth milestone from Metallo-fenon towards the north. To the same I refer these words of Aristeas in the History of the Seventy Translators: Ἐλέγετο δὲ καὶ ἐκ τῶν παρακειμένων ὀρέων τῆς Ἀραβίας μέταλλα χαλκᾶ καὶ σιδηρᾶ συνίστασθαι πρότερον· ἐκλέλειπται δὲ ταῦτα, καθ᾽ ὃν ἐπεκράτησαν χρόνον Πέρσαι [Elegeto de kai ek tōn parakeimenōn oreōn tēs Arabias metalla chalka kai sidēra synistasthai proteron; ekleleiptai de tauta, kath᾽ hon epekratēsan chronon Persai]. It was also said that formerly copper and iron ores were dug from the mountains of Arabia neighbouring (Judaea), but that these had failed (that is, had been neglected, as is clear from what follows) at the time when the Persians held power. From these mines, it seems, was taken the bronze from which the bronze serpent was cast. And perhaps this was not without a mystery. For in this way it could be signified that the body which Christ assumed was not brought from elsewhere, but was born and formed in the very place where he was manifested in the flesh. ¶
