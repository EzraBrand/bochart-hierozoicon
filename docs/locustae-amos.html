III, 320 col. 483 Chapter VI. ¶

Quae de Locustis habeat Amos, propheta.

What the prophet Amos has about locusts. ¶

Ad objectionem, supra allatam, de locustis Ioelis neminem alium meminisse, aliter respondet Theodoretus, nempe praeter Ioelem etiam prophetam Amos meminisse καὶ ἀκρίδος, καὶ βρούχου, καὶ πυρώσεως, καὶ ἰκτέρου, καὶ συκῶν, καὶ ἐλαιῶν, καὶ ἀμπέλων, ὑπὸ καμπῶν διαφθειρομένων, καὶ πρὸς τούτοις αὐχμοῦ, καὶ ἀνομβρίας, et locustae, et bruchi, et combustionis, et auruginis, ficuum item, et olearum, et vitium, ab erucis consumptarum, siccitatis insuper, et pluviarum inopiae. Alludit nimirum ad duo loca, quorum prior est Amos IV, 7. 8. 9. Ego prohibui a vobis imbrem, cum adhuc tres menses ad messem (superessent). Et demisi pluviam super civitatem unam; super alteram vero civitatem non demisi pluviam. Pars una compluta est, et pars, super quam non pluit, exaruit. Et vagabantur duae vel tres civitates ad unam, ut biberent aquas, neque saturabantur, etc. Percussi vos uredine et rubigine (Graece ἐν πυρώσει, καὶ ἐν ἰκτέρῳ, combustione et auругine). Multitudinem hortorum vestrorum, et vinearum vestrarum, et ficus vestras, et oleas vestras comedit הגזם gazam (id est, locusta, arboribus infesta, non κάμπη, eruca, ut in Graecis, et Vulgato, nec tamen ad me conversi estis, dicit Dominus. Alter est, Amos VII, 1. 2. 3. Sic ostendit mihi Dominus Deus. Is nempe formabat גובי (gobai) locustas, cum primum succrevit הלקש, foenum cordum. Erat autem foenum cordum post foenisecia regis. Fuit autem, cum absolvissent comedere herbam terrae, ut dicerem, Domine Deus, condona, obsecro. Quomodo stabit Iacobus: nam parvus est. Poenituit (igitur) Dominum hujus rei. Non erit, inquit Dominus. Quae duo loca Theodoretus cum Ioele conferens, putavit, utrumque Prophetam eandem calamitatem, unum ut futuram, alterum ut praeteritam, descripsisse. Sed quam vere, non satis scio, cum Ioel et Amos non parum in multis differant. In illo summa est ubique siccitas.

To the objection raised above, that no one else mentions the locusts of Joel, Theodoret answers differently: namely, that besides Joel the prophet Amos also mentions καὶ ἀκρίδος, καὶ βρούχου, καὶ πυρώσεως, καὶ ἰκτέρου, καὶ συκῶν, καὶ ἐλαιῶν, καὶ ἀμπέλων, ὑπὸ καμπῶν διαφθειρομένων, καὶ πρὸς τούτοις αὐχμοῦ, καὶ ἀνομβρίας [kai akridos, kai brouchou, kai pyrōseōs, kai ikterou, kai sykōn, kai elaiōn, kai ampelōn, hypo kampōn diaphtheiromenōn, kai pros toutois auchmou, kai anombrias], both the locust and the bruchus, and burning and jaundice, and also the figs, olives and vines consumed by caterpillars, and moreover drought and lack of rain. He is evidently alluding to two passages. The first is Amos 4:7–8. 9. I withheld the rain from you when there were yet three months (remaining) to the harvest. And I sent rain upon one city, but upon another city I did not send rain. One part was rained upon, and the part on which it did not rain withered. And two or three cities wandered to one city to drink water, and were not satisfied, etc. I struck you with mildew and blight (in Greek ἐν πυρώσει, καὶ ἐν ἰκτέρῳ [en pyrōsei, kai en ikterō], with burning and jaundice). The multitude of your gardens and of your vineyards, and your figs and your olives, the הגזם gazam devoured (that is, the locust that attacks trees, not κάμπη [kampē], the caterpillar, as in the Greek and the Vulgate), yet you have not returned to me, says the Lord. The second is Amos 7:1–2. 3. Thus the Lord God showed me. He was forming גובי (gobai) locusts when the הלקש, the aftermath hay, first began to grow. And the aftermath hay was after the king's mowings. And it came to pass, when they had finished eating the grass of the land, that I said: Lord God, forgive, I beseech you. How shall Jacob stand? For he is small. The Lord (therefore) repented of this. It shall not be, says the Lord. Theodoret, comparing these two passages with Joel, thought that both prophets described the same calamity, the one as future, the other as past. But how truly, I do not quite know, since Joel and Amos differ not a little in many respects. In the former there is extreme drought everywhere. ¶

Vide Ioelem capitis primi versibus 18. 19. 20. At Amosi capite quarto Deus pluviam alibi dimittit, alibi non dimittit: et, parte una compluta, altera exarescit. Ibidem solus gazam, in Ioele praeter gazam tres aliae locustarum spe- col. 484 cies Iudaeam depopulantur, nec solum arborum proventum absumunt, ut in Amoso, sed et ipsas arbores corrumpunt ac tabefaciunt, Ioel. I, 12. Et populi, quibus Amos ἀμεταμέλειαν objicit, in Ioele ad Deum convertuntur, jejunioque et precibus ejus misericordiam implorant. Rursus Amosi capite septimo locustas non format Deus, nisi post foenisecia, cum foenum cordum jam succrevisset. Itaque, jam peracta messe, solis herbis sunt noxiae. At in Ioele vastatur frumentum et hordeum, et perit messis agri. Amos denique, solus pro Israele intercedens, malum ingruens statim avertit. Sed in Ioele sacerdotes, et e populo senes, juvenes, ipsi pueri, adhuc ab ubere pendentes, in domum Dei clangore tubae convocantur, ut communibus suffragiis Deum exorent. Neque is facile flectitur, sed postquam continuasset hoc malum in plures annos. Parum igitur verisimile est, Amosum ut completa illa ipsa narrare, quae praedixerat Ioel, cum in tam multis diffideant.

See Joel, chapter 1, verses 18. 19. 20. But in chapter 4 of Amos God sends rain in one place and not in another, and while one part is rained upon, the other withers. There the gazam alone appears; in Joel, besides the gazam, three other kinds of locusts lay waste Judaea col. 484, and they not only consume the produce of the trees, as in Amos, but also damage and wither the trees themselves, Joel 1:12. And the peoples whom Amos reproaches for impenitence turn to God in Joel, and implore his mercy with fasting and prayers. Again, in chapter 7 of Amos God does not form the locusts until after the mowings, when the aftermath hay had already grown up. So, the harvest being already completed, they harm only the grass. But in Joel the wheat and barley are laid waste, and the harvest of the field perishes. Finally Amos, interceding alone for Israel, at once averts the approaching evil. But in Joel the priests, and from among the people the old men, the young men, even the children still hanging at the breast, are summoned to the house of God by the blast of the trumpet, to entreat God with common supplications. Nor is he easily moved, but only after this evil had continued for several years. It is therefore hardly probable that Amos narrates as fulfilled the very things that Joel had foretold, since they disagree in so many points. ¶

Sed et Amos duas clades refert, suo tempore per locustam illatas: unam capite quarto, quae praecessit messem, et cum siccitate, uredine et rubigine conjuncta fuit. Nempe in siccitate locustas uberius provenire, jam b) docuimus. Et easdem in Aegyptum impellit ventus קדים kadim, Exod. X, 13. A quo ipso vento שדפון, uredo, et ירקון, rubigo. Unde est, quod utrumque malum ἀνεμοφθορία graece dicitur. Et in Graeca paraphrasi modo pro ירקון occurrit; ut Agg. II, 18. modo etiam pro שדפון, ut Deut. XXVIII, 22. et 2 Par. VI, 28. Quod sequuntur Aquila, Symmachus et Theodotion in eo ipso loco, in quo versamur, id est, Amos IV, 8. ubi seniores ἐν πυρώσει. Et Gen. XLI, 6. 27. spicae שדופות קדים, id est, uredine percussae a vento kadim, in Graeca editione uno verbo vocantur ἀνεμόφθοροι, Ab eodem igitur vento kadim, a quo siccitas, uredo, et rubigo, locustae quoque, in Iudaeam adductae, hortos et vineas, id est, hortorum et vinearum proventum, ficusque et oleas consumpserunt, id est, omnes fructus arborum. Nam praecipuae nominantur, et hortorum nomine reliquae continentur. Hic enim horti sunt paradisi, ut Syrus interpres recte reddidit: id est, agri consepti, atque omnis generis plantis diligenter III, 322 consiti. Hic porro cum neque pascuis, neque segetibus infestus fuerit gazam, sed solos arborum fructus legatur consumpsisse, huic locustae speciei potuit id esse proprium, ut non aliunde vesceretur, quam ex arborum proventu. Atque ipsum nomen id suadet. Nam, ut suo loco notatum, apud Talmudicos, Arabes et Aethiopes Verbum גזם est abscindere et amputare, non quidvis, sed ramos arborum.

But Amos also reports two disasters brought on by the locust in his time. The first is in chapter four; it preceded the harvest and was joined with drought, mildew and blight. That locusts come forth more abundantly in drought we have already b) shown. And the wind קדים kadim drives them into Egypt, Exod 10:13. From this same wind come שדפון, uredo [mildew], and ירקון, rubigo [blight]. Hence both evils are called ἀνεμοφθορία [anemophthoria] in Greek. In the Greek paraphrase this word occurs sometimes for ירקון, as in Agg. II, 18., and sometimes also for שדפון, as in Deut 28:22 and 2 Chr 6:28 Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion follow this in the very passage we are dealing with, that is, Amos 4:8, where the Elders have ἐν πυρώσει [en pyrōsei]. And in Gen 41:6 27. the ears שדופות קדים, that is, struck with mildew by the wind kadim, are called in the Greek edition by a single word, ἀνεμόφθοροι [anemophthoroi]. By the same wind kadim, then, from which come drought, mildew and blight, the locusts too were brought into Judaea and consumed the gardens and vineyards, that is, the produce of the gardens and vineyards, and the figs and olives, that is, all the fruits of the trees. For the chief ones are named, and the rest are included under the name of gardens. Here gardens are paradises, as the Syriac translator rightly rendered it: that is, enclosed fields carefully III, 322 planted with plants of every kind. Further, since the gazam here harmed neither pastures nor crops, but is said to have consumed only the fruits of the trees, it may have been peculiar to this species of locust that it fed on nothing other than the produce of trees. And the name itself suggests this. For, as noted in its place, among the Talmudists, Arabs and Ethiopians the verb גזם means to cut off and to lop, not just anything, but the branches of trees. ¶

Haec de prima locustarum in Iudaeam irruptione Amos IV, 9. Quae cum Israelitas ad poenitentiam non provocasset, Hortos vestros, etc. comedit גזם gazam (inquit Dominus) neque tamen ad me conversi estis: alias locustas immisit Deus, de quibus ita Propheta capitis septimi versu primo, Sic ostendit mihi Dominus. Et, ecce! formabat locustas, cum surcrevisset foenum cordum: et foenum erat post regis foenisecia. Foenum cordum, Hebraeis לקש, solet in Italia autumno provenire. Columella de agnis libri octavi capite tertio, Aluntur commodissime autumnali foeno, quod cordum vocatur. Quo ipso anni tempore locustas parere, nos docent Aristoteles et Plinius. Ille Historiae libri quinti capite vigesimo octavo, Τίκτουσι δὲ λήγοντος τοῦ θέρους, Pariunt aestatis exitu (non veris, ut habet Gaza). Hic libri undecimi capite vigesimo nono, Hae pariunt, etc. autumni tempore. Et naturalis quidem est ille partus, ut reliquorum animalium. Quod ibidem Philosophus multis verbis docet, quorum summa est, foeminas parituras in terram defigere τὸν πρὸς τῇ κέρκῳ αὐλὸν, cauliculum, quod in cauda habent, et circa finem aestatis universas eodem loco foetus deponere, ut quasi favus videantur. Nec summae telluri committi, sed rimis et fissuris, quibus terra dehiscit. Ubi cum tota hyeme latuerunt, ineunte aestate inde emer- col. 485 gunt nigri vermes ovorum specie, rupta quadam terra tenui, qua tanquam membranula ambiuntur. Ex quibus Plinius haec solum, Hae pariunt in terram, demisso spinae caule, ova condensa autumni tempore. Ea durant hyeme sub terra: subsequenti autem exitu veris emittunt parvas, nigrantes, et sine cruribus et pennis. Et paullo post, alios sequutus; Alii duplicem earum foetum geminumque exitum tradunt. Vergiliarum exortu parere: deinde ad Canis ortum obire, et alias renasci. Quidam Arcturi occasu renasci. Nempe, prout varia sunt climata, locustis quoque varium est nascendi tempus et denascendi. So much for the first irruption of the locusts into Judaea, Amos 4:9. When this had not moved the Israelites to repentance (The gazam גזם ate your gardens, etc., says the Lord, yet you have not returned to me), God sent other locusts, of which the Prophet speaks thus in chapter 7, verse 1: Thus the Lord showed me. And behold! he was forming locusts when the second-crop hay had sprung up; and the hay was after the king's mowings. Second-crop hay, in Hebrew לקש, usually comes up in Italy in autumn. Columella, on lambs, book 8, chapter 3: They are most conveniently fed on autumn hay, which is called cordum. Aristotle and Pliny teach us that locusts lay their eggs at that very season of the year. The former, History book 5, chapter 28: Τίκτουσι δὲ λήγοντος τοῦ θέρους [Tiktousi de lēgontos tou therous], They lay at the end of summer (not of spring, as Gaza has it). The latter, book 11, chapter 29: These lay, etc., in the autumn season. And that birth is indeed natural, like that of the other animals. The Philosopher teaches this at length in the same place; the sum of it is that the females about to lay fix in the ground τὸν πρὸς τῇ κέρκῳ αὐλὸν [ton pros tē kerkō aulon], the little stalk which they have at the tail, and that towards the end of summer they all deposit their young in the same place, so that they look like a honeycomb. Nor are these committed to the surface of the earth, but to the cracks and fissures with which the ground gapes. When they have lain hidden there all winter, at the beginning of summer they emerge col. 485 as black worms in the form of eggs, after a thin layer of earth, by which they are enclosed as by a little membrane, has burst. Of this Pliny has only the following: These lay in the ground, letting down the stalk of their spine, eggs packed together in the autumn season. These last through the winter under the ground; and at the end of the following spring they release small, blackish young without legs and wings. And a little later, following others: Others report a double brood and a twofold emergence: that they lay at the rising of the Pleiades, then die at the rising of the Dog Star, and others are born again. Some say they are born again at the setting of Arcturus. That is, as climates vary, so too the time of birth and of death varies for locusts. On this same subject you may read much among the Arabs. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: وهو اذا اراد ان يبيض التمس لبيضه المواضع الصلدة والصخور الصلبة التي لا تعمل فيها المعاول فيضربها بذنبه فتفرج له ثم يلقي بيضه في ذلك الصدع فيكون له الافحوص ويكون حاضنا لها ومربيا [wa-huwa idhā arāda an yabīḍa iltamasa li-bayḍihi al-mawāḍiʿ al-ṣalda wa-al-ṣukhūr al-ṣulba allatī lā taʿmalu fīhā al-maʿāwil fa-yaḍribuhā bi-dhanabihi fa-tanfariju lahu thumma yulqī bayḍahu fī dhālika al-ṣadʿ fa-yakūnu lahu al-ufḥūṣ wa-yakūnu ḥāḍinan lahā wa-murabbiyan] When the locust is about to lay, it chooses rough places for its eggs, and very hard rocks which iron has not touched, and III, 323 beats them with its tail until they open into a crack. Then it lays its eggs in that crack, and this serves it as a nest in which it warms and rears its young. But Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] has it otherwise: وانا رعت ايام الربيع طلبت ارض طيبة التربة رخوة ونزلت هناك وحفرت باذنابها حفرا وباضت فيها كل واحد ماية بيضة وطارت [wa-anā raʿat ayyām al-rabīʿ ṭalabat arḍ ṭayyibat al-turba rikhwa wa-nazalat hunāka wa-ḥafarat bi-adhnābihā ḥufaran wa-bāḍat fīhā kull wāḥid māyat bayḍa wa-ṭārat] When they graze in springtime, they seek the ground of fertile and soft land, into which they descend; and with their tails they dig pits, in which, when each has laid a hundred eggs, they fly away from there. Close to this number of eggs comes Ibno Omar, when he tells the tale that locusts fell into the hands of Muhammad, on whose wings was written: نحن جند الله الاكبر لنا تسعة وتسعون بيضة ولو تمت لنا الماية لاكلنا الدنيا بما فيها [naḥnu jund Allāh al-akbar lanā tisʿa wa-tisʿūn bayḍa wa-law tammat lanā al-māya la-akalnā al-dunyā bi-mā fīhā] We are the army of the great God. We have ninety-nine eggs; and if the hundred were completed, we would consume the whole world and whatever is in it. There follows in Alkazuinius: ثم اذا اتي ايام الربيع واعتدل الزمان شققت البيض المدفون فظهرت مثل الدبيب الصغار علي وجه الارض فاكلت زرعها حتي قويت ثم نهضت الي بيض اخر وباضت كما فعلت في العام الاول [thumma idhā atā ayyām al-rabīʿ wa-iʿtadala al-zamān shuqqiqat al-bayḍ al-madfūn fa-ẓaharat mithl al-dabīb al-ṣighār ʿalā wajh al-arḍ fa-akalat zarʿahā ḥattā qawiyat thumma nahaḍat ilā bayḍ ākhar wa-bāḍat kamā faʿalat fī al-ʿām al-awwal] Then col. 486 in springtime, when the season is milder, that buried egg splits, and from it there emerges a kind of small creeping thing on the face of the earth, which eats its crops, until, grown stronger with age, it proceeds to another laying and lays eggs, as had been done the preceding year. In the Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], سرا [srā] sara, and قصم [qṣm] kasam, and مكن [mkn] machin is the egg of the locust; مزكوت [mzkwt] mazcuth is a pregnant locust; متح [mtḥ] mathaha, or متخ [mtkh] mathacha, سخت [skht] sachatha, زر [zr] zarra, and قلز [qlz] kallaza are so many words used of the locust when غرزت ذنبها في الارض لتبيض [gharazat dhanabahā fī al-arḍ li-tabīḍa] it fixes its tail in the ground in order to lay eggs. The locust itself is called صفرا [ṣafrā] saphra when it has laid its eggs, because it is then yellow. The young itself, newly hatched, is دبا [dabā] daba, and دبر [dbr] dabar, and قمص [qmṣ] kamas. Although it is sufficiently clear from this that locusts are formed by Nature, the Prophet nevertheless attributes the formation of the locusts to God. He was forming, he says, locusts, when the second-crop hay first came up. And he uses the verb יצר, which Moses employs where the formation of man and of the other animals is treated in the creation account, Gen 2:7–8. 19. Not as if God had created locusts anew after that first production of all things; nor to attribute something peculiar to locusts, as do the petty theologians of the Muslims, who tell the tale that God fashioned locusts من الطين التي فضلت من III, 324 خلق ادم عليه السلام [min al-ṭīn allatī faḍalat min khalq Ādam ʿalayhi al-salām] from the (better) clay which was left over from the creation of Adam, on whom be peace. Rather, the formation of locusts is attributed to God when they are fertile beyond the usual and multiply immeasurably. For this does not happen without the special providence of God, who uses them as a punishment for sins. Thus this is one of the threats of the divine Law, Deut 28:38. You shall cast much seed into the field, and gather little; for the locust shall consume it. And in verse 42: All your trees and the fruits of your land the locust shall possess (as it were) by right of inheritance. Accordingly God speaks thus in 2 Chr 7:13. If I command the locust to consume the land. Such is what we read of the Egyptians in Ps 78:46. He (God) gave their produce to the bruchus, and their labour to the locust. And Ps 105:34 He (God) spoke, and the locust came, and the bruchus without number. Hence not only the Hebrews but also the Arabs call locusts the army of God, as was observed above c) on Joel 2:25. And they tell the tale that, as their Prophet sat with Abdalla, a locust fell onto their table with this inscription on its back and wings: انا الله لا اله الا انا رب الجراد وزارقها ان شيت بعثتها رزقا القوم وان شيت بعثتها بلا علي القوم [anā Allāh lā ilāha illā anā rabb al-jarād wa-zāriquhā in shiʾtu baʿathtuhā rizqan al-qawm wa-in shiʾtu baʿathtuhā balāʾ ʿalā al-qawm] I am God, and there is none besides me, the lord of the locusts, who feed them and, when I please, send them forth to be food for peoples; but also, when I please, send them forth to be a plague upon them. Hence an anonymous poet on locusts: ¶

فسبحان من يهلك القوي الاكول بالصعيف الماكول

فسبحان من يهلك القوي الاكول بالصعيف الماكول [fa-subḥāna man yuhliku al-qawī al-akūl bi-al-ṣaʿīf al-makūl] ¶

Laus illi sit, qui perdit robustum cibum (id est, segetes, unde panis) per infirmum col. 487 comedonem (id est, per locustam). Neque aliud sibi volunt haec Plinii, Deorum irae pestis ea intelligitur. Et vota, et sacrificia, etiam ab ethnicis, diis suis oblata, ad hoc malum avertendum. Sic Casii, montis, incolas, cum fruges eorum locustae vastabant, Seleucidum avium adventum a Iove precibus impetrasse, a quibus absumerentur, testatur Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo septimo. Quod idem ab Apolline in Sarpedonio templo petiisse Ciliciae Seleucienses, legas apud Zosimum libro primo. Et Athenis Apollo Parnopius dictus est a locustis pulsis, ut habetur in Pausaniae Atticis: quomodo in Oëta Hercules Cornopion, auctore Strabone libro decimo tertio.

Praise be to Him who destroys the strong food (that is, the crops, from which bread comes) through the weak col. 487 eater (that is, through the locust). Nor do these words of Pliny mean anything else: this plague is understood to be a sign of the wrath of the gods. And vows and sacrifices were offered, even by the pagans, to their gods to avert this evil. Thus Pliny, book 10, chapter 27, testifies that the inhabitants of Mount Casius, when locusts were laying waste their crops, obtained from Jupiter by their prayers the arrival of the Seleucid birds, by which the locusts were consumed. That the people of Seleucia in Cilicia sought the same from Apollo in the Sarpedonian temple, you may read in Zosimus, book 1. And at Athens Apollo was called Parnopius from the locusts he drove away, as is found in Pausanias' Attica; just as on Oeta Hercules was called Cornopion, on the authority of Strabo, book 13. ¶

Notanda haec in Arnobium, qui hujusmodi animalcula negavit esse Dei opus, et professus est se nescire, a quo condita fuerint. Non, inquit, si negemus, III, 325 muscas, scarabaeos, et cimices, nitedulas, curculiones, et tineas, omnipoten- tis esse opus regis, sequaciter postulandum a nobis est, ut, quis ea fecerit institue- ritque, dicamus. Possimus enim nulla cum reprehensione nescire, quis et illis ori- ginem dederit, et obtinere non esse a Deo superiore prolata tam supervacua, tam vana, tam ad nullas pertinentia rationes. Haec ille libro secundo ex Marcioni- tarum lacunis hausta, qui apud Tertullianum adversus Marcionem libro primo ita loquuntur: Sufficit unicum hoc opus Deo nostro, quod hominem liberavit sum- ma et praecipua bonitate sua, et omnibus locustis anteponenda: id est, omnium locustarum creationi, quam Deo affingitis. Hinc Hieronymus in Prooemio in Epistolam ad Philemonem, Quod si non putant eorum esse parva, quorum et ma- gna sunt; alterum mihi conditorem, juxta Valentinum, Marcionem et Apellem, formicae, vermium, culicum, locustarum: alterum coeli, terrae, maris et angelo- rum debent introducere.

These points should be noted against Arnobius, who denied that such little creatures are the work of God and professed that he did not know by whom they had been made. If we deny, he says, that III, 325 flies, beetles and bugs, dormice, weevils and moths are the work of the almighty King, it does not follow that we must be required to say who made and formed them. For we may without any reproach not know who gave them their origin, and yet maintain that things so superfluous, so empty, so unrelated to any purpose were not brought forth by the supreme God. This he wrote in book 2, drawn from the puddles of the Marcionites, who in Tertullian, Against Marcion, book 1, speak thus: This one work suffices for our God, that he has freed man by his supreme and pre-eminent goodness, which is to be preferred to all the locusts: that is, to the creation of all the locusts, which you ascribe to God. Hence Jerome, in the Prologue to the Epistle to Philemon: But if they do not think that the small things belong to him to whom the great things also belong, then, following Valentinus, Marcion and Apelles, they must introduce one creator of the ant, the worms, the gnats and the locusts, and another of heaven, earth, the sea and the angels. ¶

In Propheta sequitur, Cum autem absolvissent comedere herbam terrae, dixi, Domine Deus, condona, obsecro, etc.] Agitur de locustis, quae, post messem creatae, solis herbis vescebantur. Tum enim deerant segetes. Nec volatu poterant in arbores se attollere, cum alas nondum haberent, nec nisi humi reptitarent. Hujusmodi locustas bruchos a multis appellari, observavimus d), atque ex illis primo attelabos fieri, et postmodum locustas proprie dictas. Priusquam igitur hi bruchi, in majus aucti, plura damna inferrent, pro populo intercedit Propheta, et precibus suis obtinet, ut amoveatur haec pestis.

In the Prophet there follows: And when they had finished eating the grass of the land, I said, O Lord God, forgive, I beseech you, etc.] The subject is locusts which, produced after the harvest, fed on grass alone; for at that time the crops were gone. Nor could they raise themselves into the trees by flight, since they did not yet have wings and only crept on the ground. We have observed d) that locusts of this kind are called bruchi by many, and that from them first attelabi come into being, and afterwards locusts properly so called. Therefore, before these bruchi, grown larger, could inflict more damage, the Prophet intercedes for the people and by his prayers obtains the removal of this plague. ¶
