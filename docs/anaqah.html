II, 487 col. 1063 CHAPTER II. ¶

Probatur, אנקה anaka Levit. XI, 30. esse Stellionis genus, ab acerbo stridore sic appellati.

It is proved that אנקה anaka of Lev 11:30 is a kind of stellio, so called from its harsh shrill cry. ¶

Iam sequentur Levit. XI, 30. reptilium quinque species, quorum nomina ex- col. 1064 plicare non est facile, cum alibi nusquam reperiantur. Nempe אנקה anaka, כח coach, לטאה letaa, חמט chomet, תנשמת thinsemeth. Horum אנקה anaka Graecis et Hieronymo est μυγάλη mygale, seu mus araneus. Μυγάλην dici volunt, quia ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς, ex mure et mustela nascitur. Nicandri Scholia- stes, Ἀμύντας δή φησι τὴν μυγάλην ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς γίνεσθαι. Quod est absurdissimum, cum et mustela sit mure multo major, et illi tam infensa, quam felis, ut supra a) observavimus. Itaque ut mustela cum mure coëat, atque inde nascatur mus araneus, plane vero est absimile. Melius igitur Aetius Tetrabibli libri quarti Sermone primo, capite decimo quarto: Mus araneus colore quidem mustelae similis est, magnitudine autem muri, unde et mygales obtinuit nomen. Quamvis mus araneus muris habeat speciem potius, quam magnitudinem. Omnino enim est mure minor. Romanis mus araneus a veneno dici videtur, quod illi commune est cum aranea. Et Hebraice אנקה anaka dici credita est ratione vocis, quam habet acutissimam. Ita, ut pro gemitu et ejulatu possit haberi. Alioqui enim אנקה anaka Hebraeis gemitum, aut clamorem sonat. Si idem sensit Onkelos, in ejus Paraphrasi, pro ילא jala (qua voce ibi redditur אנקה anaka) rescribendum est פלא pala, vel phala. Arabes enim murem araneum sic appellant. Avicenna libro quarto Fen. 6. Tract. 3. capite ultimo in titulo, فصل في عضة موغالي وهو الغلا Caput de morsu mygales, quae (Arabibus) est alphala. In Latina versione legitur alcale, quod esset الغلا. Alterutrum semidoctos mutasse putaverim in ילא jala. Cujus nominis occurrunt in libris Talmudicis animalia duo diversissima, nempe sanguisuga et hericius. Sed quorum neutrum hic habet locum. Neutrum enim Hebraice אנקה anaka; sed hericius קפוד kippod; et hirudo עלוקה aluka dicitur, vel עלקה alka potius. Praeterea hic agitur de iis reptilibus, quae super terram repunt, e quo numero non est hirudo. Haec de prima et antiquissima interpretatione vocis אנקה anaka.

Next, in Lev 11:30, follow five species of creeping things whose names are not easy to ex- col. 1064 plain, since they are found nowhere else. They are אנקה anaka, כח coach, לטאה letaa, חמט chomet, תנשמת thinsemeth. Of these, אנקה anaka is for the Greeks and for Jerome μυγάλη [mygalē] mygale, that is, the mus araneus [shrew]. They hold that it is called Μυγάλην [Mygalēn] because it is born ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς [ek myos kai galēs], from a mouse and a weasel. The scholiast on Nicander says: Ἀμύντας δή φησι τὴν μυγάλην ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς γίνεσθαι [Amyntas dē phēsi tēn mygalēn ek myos kai galēs ginesthai]. This is utterly absurd, since the weasel is much larger than the mouse and as hostile to it as the cat, as we observed above a). That a weasel should mate with a mouse and a shrew be born of it is therefore wholly improbable. Aetius puts it better in the Tetrabiblos, book 4, sermon 1, chapter 14: The shrew is like the weasel in colour, but like the mouse in size; hence it also obtained the name mygale. Yet the shrew has the appearance of a mouse rather than its size, for it is altogether smaller than a mouse. The Romans seem to call it mus araneus [spider mouse] from its venom, which it shares with the spider. And in Hebrew it has been thought to be called אנקה anaka by reason of its voice, which is extremely shrill, so that it can be taken for a groan and a wail; for otherwise אנקה anaka means groaning or outcry to the Hebrews. If Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] understood the same, then in his Paraphrase, instead of ילא jala (the word by which אנקה anaka is rendered there), one must read פלא pala, or phala. For the Arabs call the shrew so. Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], book 4, Fen 6, tract. 3, last chapter, in the title: فصل في عضة موغالي وهو الغلا [faṣl fī ʿaḍḍat mūghālī wa-huwa al-ghalā] Chapter on the bite of the mygale, which (for the Arabs) is alphala. In the Latin version one reads alcale, which would be الغلا [al-ghalā]. I should think that half-learned men changed one or the other into ילא jala. Under this name two very different animals occur in the Talmudic books, namely the leech and the hedgehog. But neither of them has a place here. For neither is called אנקה anaka in Hebrew; rather the hedgehog is called קפוד kippod, and the leech עלוקה aluka, or rather עלקה alka. Moreover, the subject here is those creeping things that creep upon the earth, and the leech is not of that number. So much for the first and most ancient interpretation of the word אנקה anaka. ¶

Secunda Ionathanis est, et Ierosolymitani Interpretis: quorum unus hic habet מינקת חויא, et alter פלית חויא, vel פיילת חויא. Ita legendum, non חיוא, in Lexico Baal-Aruch. Lexicographi neutrum intelligunt. Est enim II, 488 alienum, quod פליית pro locusta hic sumunt, ut פליא ביארי, capite nono tractatus Talmudici De Sabbatho. Aliud enim פליא ביארי, aliud פליית חויא. Nempe id ipsum, quod Arabibus فالية الافاعي phaliath alaphai, id est, scrutatrix serpentum, aut viperarum, (quomodo appellari supra b)) nos docuerunt pictum genus scarabaeorum, quod cum scorpionibus et serpentibus degere solet in latibulo crocodili terrestris) id ipsum, inquam, est Chaldaeum פליית חויא. Illis enim חויא est serpens, et פליית paljath, seu phaljath, palam deductum est ex Arabico فالية phaliath, quod scrutatricem reddimus; quia فلي phala est scrutari. Sic فلي الراس caput scrutatus est, pediculos in eo col. 1065 quaesiturus, فلي الشعر carmen scrutatus est, id est, exploravit et examinavit, ut in Poetae mentem penetraret. Et merito serpentes scrutari dicitur hoc scarabaeorum genus, quod in eodem latibulo cum iis jugiter degit, adeoque serpentes, vel venientes, vel discedsntes, perpetuo comitatur: ita, ut eos, vel adventu suo, vel recessu suo abesse significet. Unde proverbium, apud Arabes receptum, اتكلم فالية الافاعي An alloquutus es phaliath alaphai, scrutatricem serpentum? Quod usurpatur لاول شر ينتظر بعده شر منه de priore malo, post quod aliud expectatur, ex eo oboriturum: sed, quia فلي phala verbum non solum scrutari significat, sed et nutrire et educare; Ionathan, pro פליית חויא, habet מינקת חויא, i. e. nutricem serpentis. Porro, cur voce אנקה anaka hoc scarabaei genus designari voluerint Chaldaei duo interpretes, causa mihi nulla apparet praeter ipsum vocum ordinem. Nempe, ut vox צב tsab crocodilum terrestrem significat, quod multis c) probavimus, ita sequens אנקה anaka פליית חויא, id est, serpentis scrutatricem significare visa est, quae crocodili terrestris perpetua est assecla, et in eodem cum illo latibulo degere solet. Sed haec qualiscunque ratio non est apud me magni momenti, ut neque ipsa versio, quae videtur ea niti. Quod paene omiseram, haec animalcula nonnullis Arabum non sunt scarabaei, sed بنات وردان benath vvardan, quomodo Dioscoridis σίλφας, seu blattas appellat Abenbitar.

The second is that of Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and of the Jerusalem Targum: the one of them has here מינקת חויא, and the other פלית חויא, or פיילת חויא. So it should be read, not חיוא, in the Lexicon of Baal-Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome]. The lexicographers understand neither. For it is II, 488 beside the point that they take פליית here for a locust, as in פליא ביארי in chapter 9 of the Talmudic tractate On the Sabbath. For פליא ביארי is one thing, פליית חויא another. Namely, the very thing that the Arabs call فالية الافاعي [fāliyat al-afāʿī] phaliath alaphai, that is, the searcher of serpents, or of vipers (they taught us above b) that this name is given to a painted kind of beetle which usually lives with scorpions and serpents in the lair of the land crocodile), that very thing, I say, is the Chaldee פליית חויא. For to them חויא is serpent, and פליית paljath, or phaljath, is plainly derived from the Arabic فالية [fāliya] phaliath, which we render searcher, because فلي [falā] phala means to search. Thus فلي الراس [falā al-raʾs] he searched the head, to look for lice in it; col. 1065 فلي الشعر [falā al-shiʿr] he searched the poem, that is, he explored and examined it in order to penetrate the poet's mind. And this kind of beetle is rightly said to search the serpents, since it lives continually with them in the same lair, and so constantly accompanies the serpents, whether coming or going, in such a way that by its arrival or its departure it signals whether they are near or away. Hence the proverb current among the Arabs: اتكلم فالية الافاعي [a-takallama fāliyat al-afāʿī] Have you spoken to the phaliath alaphai, the searcher of serpents? It is used لاول شر ينتظر بعده شر منه [li-awwal sharr yuntaẓar baʿdahu sharr minhu] of a first evil after which another is expected to arise from it. But since the verb فلي [falā] phala means not only to search but also to nourish and to rear, Jonathan has, instead of פליית חויא, מינקת חויא, i.e. the nurse of the serpent. Further, why the two Chaldee translators wished this kind of beetle to be designated by the word אנקה anaka, I see no reason except the very order of the words. Namely, as the word צב tsab signifies the land crocodile, as we have proved at length c), so the following אנקה anaka seemed to signify פליית חויא, that is, the searcher of the serpent, which is the constant companion of the land crocodile and usually lives in the same lair with it. But this reason, such as it is, carries little weight with me, as does the version itself, which seems to rest on it. Something I had almost omitted: for some of the Arabs these little creatures are not beetles but بنات وردان [banāt wardān] benath vvardan, which is what Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār] calls the σίλφας [silphas] of Dioscorides, or blattae [cockroaches]. ¶

Tertio loco eos numero, quibus אנקה anaka est lacertae genus, qui nec pauci sunt, nec contemnendi. In Syro enim sic habetur, ܐܡܩܬܐ amaktha, vel amkatha, quod lacertam exposuit Maronitarum doctissimus Gabriel Sionita ὁ μακαρίτης. In Samaritano [?] semamitha, id est, stellio, ut suo loco palam fiet. In Arabe Erpeniano الورل alvvarlo, id est, lacertae species, quae barbaris est guaril. In Arabe, Parisiis edito, الورك alvvarco, quod idem II, 489 est. Nam nescio, quo fato factum est, ut in Avicenna et aliis multo saepius scribatur الورك alvvarco, quamvis vera scriptura sit الورل alvvarlo: quod docent omnes Arabum Lexicographi, et barbarum nomen guaril inde deductum. Ut taceam criticam observationem, quae sic habet apud Damirem capite ورل Guaril, لا تلتقي الراء مع اللام الا في اربع مواضع الورل وهو الحيوان المذكور وارل اسم جبل والغرلة وهو القلفة وحرل وهو ضرب من الحجارة. Re non occurrit cum Lam (id est, R cum L) nisi quatuor locis. 1. In alvvarlo animalis nomine, de quo supra. 2. In Aral, montis nomine, de quo supra. 3. In alghorla, quod praeputium sonat (ut Hebraeum ערלה). 4. In charal, quod lapidis est genus. Hi sunt igitur quatuor veteres, Syrus, Samaritanus et Arabes duo, quibus anaka est lacertae species. Quibus cur calculum addam, causas habeo non leves. 1. Quod praecesserat צב tsab, id est, crocodilus terrestris: et statim adduntur col. 1066 aliae quatuor lacertorum species, ut constabit ex sequentibus. Proinde videtur anaka esse in eodem genere. Nam mygale, vel aliud quodvis animal, inter tot lacertorum species, minus opportuno loco esset insertum. 2. Ex Hebraeo אנקה anaka plane ortum est Syrum ܐܡܩܬܐ amaktha, vel amkatha. Nam haec duo vocabula (praeter terminationem, quae in illo Hebraea, in hoc Syra est) nulla ex parte differunt: nisi quod Hebraicum NUN Syri mutarunt in MEM, qua mutatione in illis linguis nihil est frequentius. Sic Chaldaeis, aut Syris fortis dicitur ܚܣܝܢ chasin, et ܚܣܝܡ chasim: Ratio ܛܥܢܐ taana, et ܛܥܡܐ taama: Nympha ܢܝܢܦܐ ninphe, et ܢܝܡܦܐ nimphe: Fustis ܩܘܪܢܣܐ kurnesa, et ܩܘܪܡܙܐ kurmeza: Terebinthus, vel arbor, quae fert pistacium, ܒܘܛܡܐ butem, ut Hebraice בוטן boten. Et Arabice pollex ابهام ibham dicitur pro Hebraeo בהן bohen: ut pinguescere, دسم dasama, pro Hebraeo דשן dasen: et canere رنم ranima pro Hebraeo רנן ranan. Aethiopice ዘመወ፡ zamavva, scortatus est, pro Hebraeo זנה zana: እድሜ፡ edem, tempus pro Chaldaeo עדן iddan: አደመ፡ adam, jucundus, suavis, ex Hebraeo עדן adan, deliciari. Tale Aegyptium ΜΕΤΩΠΟΝ, unguenti genus ex Hebraeo נטף nataph, aut Chaldaeo נטופא netopha: ΜΕΙΣΙ serpens ex Hebraeo נחש naas. Ut Aegyptia urbs Memphis a Prophetis modo נוף Noph, modo מוף Moph appellatur. Pari exemplo hic Syrum ܐܡܩܬܐ amaktha deducitur ex Hebraeo אנקה anaka, N et M permutatis. Itaque, quidquid sit Syrum amaktha, id ipsum esse Hebraeum anaka, vero valde consentaneum est. Certum autem est, ܐܡܩܬܐ lacertae genus esse, adeoque stellionem, Prov. XXX, 28. ubi habetur in Syra II, 490 Paraphrasi pro Hebraeo שממית semamith. Ut in Chaldaea per literarum μετά-θεσιν אקמתא akmetha, quod idem est. Itaque ut ܐܡܩܬܐ amaktha, vel amkatha, Syrum; et אקמתא akmetha Chaldaeum est stellio: ita etiam Hebraeum אנקה anaka. Unde est, quod etiam a Samaritano אנקה anaka redditur * [?] semamitha, id est, stellio: et ab utroque Arabe الورل alvvarlo, vel الورك alvvarco, lacertae genus, ad stellionem accedentis, quamque adeo pro stellione multi habent, quia est formae similis. Proinde sic de illo Alkazuinius, هو العظيم من شكل الوزغ وسوام ابرص Hic est magnus, ejusdem formae cum guezga et savvamabras, id est, cum stellionibus minoribus et majoribus. Quae ex Avicenna sumta sunt. Et Al-camus, الورل دابة كالضب والعظيم من اشكال الوزغ Alvvarlo (vulgo guaril), animal ut dhab (seu crocodilus terrestris). Et magnus est in iis, qui formam habent guezgae, seu stellionis. 3. Hebraeis אנקה anaka gemitum et acutum clamorem sonat, qualis fit in magno luctu. Unde stellionem dici posse si quis neget, quasi sit mutum animal, eum refellent haec Plinii de Graeciae stellione libri 29. capite 4. Hunc Graeci coloten vocant, et ascalaboten, et galeoten. In Italia non nascitur. Est enim hic plenus lentigine, stridoris acerbi, et * vescitur. Quae omnia á nostris stellionibus aliena sunt. Nota, stridoris acerbi. Graece dixeris τρισμοῦ τραχέος, unde merito אנקה anaka dictus. At-que ut apud Hebraeos ex Foeminino Singulari saepe fit Plurale Masculinum, ut col. 1067 יעלים ex יעלה, et יענים ex יענה: ita ex אנקה anaka Plurale potuit esse אנקים. Et in secundo Thargum Esther capitis primi versu secundo, cum inter diversa animalia, quae singula coram Solomone propriam vocem edunt, describuntur אנקין בכיין anakin flentes, aut ejulantes, fortasse id accipiendum est, non (ut vulgo sumitur) de aquilarum clangore, sed de stellionum clamore, et acerbo stridore, quem ad ejulatum accedere testatur ipsa vox אנקה anaka. Atque ob id ipsum Arabes, cum hoc saltem scirent, anakam esse lacerti genus, reddiderunt الورل alvvarlo, qui et ipse vocem habet, quamvis fortasse minus sonoram. Hinc Alcamus, ضغث الورل صوت Dhagathsa alvvarlo, id est, clamavit. Quod e nostris, qui Arabica Lexica ediderunt, non intellexerunt satis. Alius enim reddidit, Femur fragorem reddidit: alius, Sonuit coxa. Quia legerunt الورك alvvarco, quod ירך, i. e. coxam sonat. Sed meminisse debuerant, illud ipsum الورك passim occurrere pro الورل, ut jam supra d) notavimus. Ne dicam in exemplari τοῦ Alcamus, quo sum usus, optime scribi الورل alvvarlo. Itaque, quod nostros homines latuerat hactenus, hoc pro II, 491 comperto maneat, in lacertarum genere quasdam esse vocales, atque in his stellionem esse, qui acutissime stridet. Sed de stellione plura dicemus in explicatione vocis שממית semamith.

In third place I count those for whom אנקה anaka is a kind of lizard; they are neither few nor to be despised. For in the Syriac we read ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] amaktha, or amkatha, which the most learned of the Maronites, the late Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)] (ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs]), rendered lacerta [lizard]. In the Samaritan it is [?] semamitha, that is, stellio, as will become clear in its proper place. In Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] it is الورل [al-waral] alvvarlo, that is, a species of lizard which the barbarous writers call guaril. In the Arabic edition published at Paris it is الورك [al-warak] alvvarco, which is the same thing II, 489. For by what chance I do not know, in Avicenna and others الورك [al-warak] alvvarco is written far more often, although the true spelling is الورل [al-waral] alvvarlo. This is taught by all the Arabic lexicographers, and the barbarous name guaril is derived from it. Not to mention the critical observation which is found in Damiri, in the chapter ورل [waral] Guaril: لا تلتقي الراء مع اللام الا في اربع مواضع الورل وهو الحيوان المذكور وارل اسم جبل والغرلة وهو القلفة وحرل وهو ضرب من الحجارة [lā taltaqī al-rāʾ maʿa al-lām illā fī arbaʿ mawāḍiʿ al-waral wa-huwa al-ḥayawān al-madhkūr wa-aral ism jabal wa-al-ghurla wa-hiya al-qulfa wa-ḥaral wa-huwa ḍarb min al-ḥijāra]. Re does not meet with Lam (that is, R with L) except in four places. 1. In alvvarlo, the name of the animal discussed above. 2. In Aral, the name of a mountain discussed above. 3. In alghorla, which means foreskin (like the Hebrew ערלה). 4. In charal, which is a kind of stone. These, then, are the four ancient versions, the Syriac, the Samaritan and the two Arabic, for which anaka is a species of lizard. I have weighty reasons for casting my vote with them. 1. צב tsab, that is, the land crocodile, had preceded, and four other species of lizards are added immediately afterwards col. 1066, as will be evident from what follows. Accordingly anaka seems to belong to the same genus. For the mygale, or any other animal, would be inserted rather inappropriately among so many species of lizards. 2. The Syriac ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] amaktha, or amkatha, plainly arose from the Hebrew אנקה anaka. For these two words (apart from the ending, which is Hebrew in the one and Syriac in the other) differ in no respect, except that the Syrians changed the Hebrew NUN into MEM, a change than which nothing is more frequent in those languages. Thus among the Chaldeans or Syrians strong is ܚܣܝܢ [חסין] chasin and ܚܣܝܡ [חסים] chasim; reason, ܛܥܢܐ [טענא] taana and ܛܥܡܐ [טעמא] taama; nymph, ܢܝܢܦܐ [נינפא] ninphe and ܢܝܡܦܐ [נימפא] nimphe; cudgel, ܩܘܪܢܣܐ [קורנסא] kurnesa and ܩܘܪܡܙܐ [קורמזא] kurmeza; the terebinth, or the tree that bears the pistachio, ܒܘܛܡܐ [בוטמא] butem, as in Hebrew בוטן boten. And in Arabic thumb is ابهام [ibhām] ibham for the Hebrew בהן bohen; likewise to grow fat, دسم [dasama] dasama, for the Hebrew דשן dasen; and to sing, رنم [ranima] ranima, for the Hebrew רנן ranan. In Ethiopic ዘመወ፡ [zamawa] zamavva, he committed fornication, for the Hebrew זנה zana; እድሜ፡ [ʾədəmē] edem, time, for the Chaldee עדן iddan; አደመ፡ [ʾadama] adam, pleasant, sweet, from the Hebrew עדן adan, to take delight. Of the same kind is the Egyptian ΜΕΤΩΠΟΝ [metōpon], a kind of ointment, from the Hebrew נטף nataph or the Chaldee נטופא netopha; and ΜΕΙΣΙ [meisi], serpent, from the Hebrew נחש naas. So too the Egyptian city of Memphis is called by the Prophets now נוף Noph, now מוף Moph. By a like example the Syriac ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] amaktha is here derived from the Hebrew אנקה anaka, with N and M interchanged. Therefore, whatever the Syriac amaktha may be, it is highly probable that the Hebrew anaka is the very same. Now it is certain that ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] is a kind of lizard, and indeed the stellio, Prov. XXX, 28, where it stands in the Syriac II, 490 Paraphrase for the Hebrew שממית semamith; just as in the Chaldee, by metathesis (μετάθεσιν [metathesin]) of the letters, אקמתא akmetha, which is the same. Therefore, as the Syriac ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] amaktha, or amkatha, and the Chaldee אקמתא akmetha is the stellio, so also is the Hebrew אנקה anaka. Hence it is that the Samaritan too renders אנקה anaka by * [?] semamitha, that is, stellio; and both Arabic versions by الورل [al-waral] alvvarlo, or الورك [al-warak] alvvarco, a kind of lizard close to the stellio, which many therefore take for the stellio, because it is similar in form. Accordingly Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] says of it: هو العظيم من شكل الوزغ وسوام ابرص [huwa al-ʿaẓīm min shakl al-wazagh wa-sawām abraṣ] It is a large one, of the same form as the guezga and savvamabras, that is, as the smaller and larger stellios. This is taken from Avicenna. And Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: الورل دابة كالضب والعظيم من اشكال الوزغ [al-waral dābba ka-al-ḍabb wa-al-ʿaẓīm min ashkāl al-wazagh] Alvvarlo (commonly guaril), an animal like the dhab (or land crocodile). And it is the large one among those which have the form of the guezga, or stellio. 3. In Hebrew אנקה anaka means a groan and a shrill cry, such as is uttered in great mourning. If anyone should deny that the stellio can be so called, as though it were a mute animal, he will be refuted by these words of Pliny on the stellio of Greece, book 29, chapter 4: The Greeks call it colotes, and ascalabotes, and galeotes. It does not occur in Italy. For it is covered with spots, has a harsh shriek, and feeds on *. All of which is foreign to our stellios. Note: a harsh shriek. In Greek you would say τρισμοῦ τραχέος [trismou tracheos], whence it is rightly called אנקה anaka. And as among the Hebrews a masculine plural is often formed from a feminine singular, as col. 1067 יעלים from יעלה, and יענים from יענה, so from אנקה anaka the plural could be אנקים. And in the second Targum of Esther, chapter 1, verse 2, where among the various animals which each utter their own cry before Solomon are described אנקין בכיין anakin weeping, or wailing, this should perhaps be understood not (as it is commonly taken) of the screaming of eagles, but of the cry and harsh shriek of stellios, which the very word אנקה anaka attests approaches wailing. And for this very reason the Arabs, since they knew at least this much, that the anaka is a kind of lizard, rendered it الورل [al-waral] alvvarlo, which itself also has a voice, though perhaps less resonant. Hence Alcamus: ضغث الورل صوت [ḍaghatha al-waral ṣawt] Dhagathsa alvvarlo, that is, it cried out. This those of our scholars who have published Arabic lexicons did not sufficiently understand. For one rendered it The thigh made a cracking noise; another, The hip sounded. For they read الورك [al-warak] alvvarco, which means ירך, i.e. hip. But they ought to have remembered that this very الورك [al-warak] occurs everywhere for الورل [al-waral], as we noted above d). Not to mention that in the copy of Alcamus which I used, الورل [al-waral] alvvarlo is quite correctly written. Therefore, what has hitherto escaped our scholars, let this stand as II, 491 established: that among the lizards some are vocal, and among them is the stellio, which shrieks very shrilly. But we shall say more about the stellio in the explanation of the word שממית semamith. ¶

Tribus interpretationibus hujus vocis, quas adduximus, jungi queant tres aliae, in quibus ne gutta quidem apparet bonae mentis. Prima est paraphrastarum Arabum, quos legi manuscriptos, ubi אנקה anaka est عنكبوت an-cabuths, id est, aranea. Quae aranea fortasse nata est ex Graecorum et Hieronymi mygale, seu mure araneo: quasi mus araneus idem sit cum aranea, quae summa fuerit imperitia: aut ex Syrorum ܐܡܟܬܐ amkatha; quia Chaldaeum אקמתא akmetha, huic synonymum, plerique Hebraeorum pro aranea sumunt. At aranea Hebraeis non est אנקה anaka, sed עכביש accabis. Neque Prophetam verissimile est hic araneae meminisse inter lacertarum species.

To the three interpretations of this word that we have adduced, three others may be added, in which not even a drop of good sense appears. The first is that of the Arabic paraphrasts, whom I have read in manuscript, where אנקה anaka is عنكبوت [ʿankabūt] ancabuths, that is, aranea [spider]. This aranea perhaps arose from the mygale of the Greeks and of Jerome, or mus araneus [shrew-mouse]: as though the shrew-mouse were the same as the spider, which would be the height of ignorance; or from the Syriac ܐܡܟܬܐ [אמכתא] amkatha, since most of the Hebrews take the Chaldee אקמתא akmetha, which is synonymous with it, for the spider. But for the Hebrews the spider is not אנקה anaka but עכביש accabis. Nor is it at all likely that the Prophet mentions the spider here among the species of lizards. ¶

Secunda interpretatio est R. Mardochaei in Hebraicis Concordantiis, ubi hoc animal definit שרץ העוף reptile volans, cum Moses illud diserte ponat inter reptilia, quae repunt super terram, Levit. XI, 29. 30.

The second interpretation is that of R. Mardochai in the Hebrew Concordance, where he defines this animal as שרץ העוף, a flying creeping thing, although Moses expressly places it among the creeping things that creep upon the earth, Lev 11:29–30. ¶

Tertia est R. Selomonis, qui Gallica voce reddidit בברא bieure, id est, fibrum, seu castorem, ut est in Pomario: animal ita amphibium, ut sit potius aquaticum. Piscis enim habet caudam, et pedes, ad natandum potius, quam ad incessum comparatos, degitque in fluminum ripis, unde alimentum petat. Dioscorides, Ζῶόν ἐστιν ἀμφίβιον, τὸ πλεῖστον ἐν ὕδασι σὺν ἰχθύσι, καὶ καρκί-νοις τρεφόμενον· Animal est amphibium, plerumque in aquis cum piscibus et cancris vescens. Alkazuinius, القندر بري بحري يكون في الانهار العظيمة من بلاد ايسو يتخذ في البر بيتا الي جانب النهر Alkundur (id est, castor) amphibium animal. Reperitur in magnis amnibus regionis Aisu, et domum sibi deligit ad amnis ripam. Proinde fibrum dici volunt Varro et Festus ab extremis fluminum oris. Nam fibrum dicebant veteres pro extremo. Unde fibrae jecoris, et vestium fimbriae appellatae. Tale igitur animal quis credat, Mosem recensuisse inter re- col. 1068 ptilia terrae? Maxime, cum proportio sit valde dispar. Reptilia enim hic vocat Moses talpas, et mures, et lacertarum genera. Praeterea ratio nulla est, cur a clamore et ejulatu castor dicatur אנקה anaka, cum vox castoris nihil quidquam habeat insigne, quod quidem sciam. Sed hujus sententiae primus auctor, quisquis fuit, promiscue sumsisse videtur castores et castorides: quae marina esse animalia dictitant, tam horrendi ejulatus, ut brevi moriatur, qui audierit. Aelianus Historiae libri noni capite quinquagesimo, Αἱ καστορίδες ζῶόν εἰσι θα-λάττιον, καὶ ἐπὶ ταῖς ἀκταῖς, καὶ ταῖς πέτραις ταῖς προβεβλημέναις, ἀπόΦημόν II, 492 τινα κωκυτὸν μεθιᾶσι, καὶ ὠρύονται βαρύτατα. Τούτου τοίνυν τοῦ ἤχου ὅστις ἂν ὑπακούσῃ, ἄφυκτα οἵ ἐστι, καὶ οὐ μετὰ μικρὸν ἀποθνήσκει· Castorides, marinum animal, ad littora et saxa eminentia infaustum edunt ululatum, et gravissime rugiunt. Ita, ut earum clamorem qui audierit, non effugiat, quin paullo post excedat e vita. Oppianus libro primo Halieuticῶν, vs. 398. seqq.

The third is that of R. Solomon, who rendered it with the French word בברא bieure, that is, fiber or castor [beaver], as it is in the Pomarium: an animal so amphibious that it is rather aquatic. For it has the tail of a fish, and feet fitted for swimming rather than for walking, and it lives on the banks of rivers, from which it seeks its food. Dioscorides: Ζῶόν ἐστιν ἀμφίβιον, τὸ πλεῖστον ἐν ὕδασι σὺν ἰχθύσι, καὶ καρκίνοις τρεφόμενον [Zōon estin amphibion, to pleiston en hydasi syn ichthysi, kai karkinois trephomenon]; It is an amphibious animal, mostly feeding in the waters on fish and crabs. Alkazuini: القندر بري بحري يكون في الانهار العظيمة من بلاد ايسو يتخذ في البر بيتا الي جانب النهر [al-qundur barrī baḥrī yakūn fī al-anhār al-ʿaẓīma min bilād Īsū yattakhidh fī al-barr baytan ilā jānib al-nahr] Alkundur (that is, the beaver) is an amphibious animal. It is found in the great rivers of the region of Aisu, and chooses a home for itself on the river bank. Accordingly Varro and Festus hold that it is called fiber from the outermost edges of rivers. For the ancients said fibrum for extremum [outermost]. Hence the fibrae [lobes] of the liver and the fimbriae [fringes] of garments were so named. Who then would believe that Moses listed such an animal among the creeping things of the col. 1068 earth? Especially since the proportion is very unequal. For here Moses calls moles, mice and kinds of lizards creeping things. Moreover, there is no reason why the beaver should be called אנקה anaka from crying and wailing, since the voice of the beaver has nothing remarkable about it, so far as I know. But the first author of this opinion, whoever he was, seems to have confused castores and castorides: these are said to be marine animals of so horrible a wailing that whoever hears it soon dies. Aelian, History, book 9, chapter 50: Αἱ καστορίδες ζῶόν εἰσι θαλάττιον, καὶ ἐπὶ ταῖς ἀκταῖς, καὶ ταῖς πέτραις ταῖς προβεβλημέναις, ἀπόΦημόν II, 492 τινα κωκυτὸν μεθιᾶσι, καὶ ὠρύονται βαρύτατα. Τούτου τοίνυν τοῦ ἤχου ὅστις ἂν ὑπακούσῃ, ἄφυκτα οἵ ἐστι, καὶ οὐ μετὰ μικρὸν ἀποθνήσκει [Hai kastorides zōon eisi thalattion, kai epi tais aktais, kai tais petrais tais probeblēmenais, apophēmon tina kōkyton methiasi, kai ōryontai barytata. Toutou toinyn tou ēchou hostis an hypakousē, aphykta hoi esti, kai ou meta mikron apothnēskei]; The castorides, a marine animal, utter an ill-omened wail on the shores and the jutting rocks, and roar most heavily. So that whoever hears their cry cannot escape departing from life shortly afterwards. Oppian, Halieutica, book 1, v. 398 ff. ¶

Καστορίδες τ᾽ ὀλοαὶ δυσπενθέες, αἵ τ᾽ ἀλεγεινὴν Ὅσσαν ἐπὶ κροκαλοίσιον ἀπαίσιον ὠρύονται Ἀνδράσιν· ὃς δέ κε γῆρυν ἐν οὔασιν ἀλγινόεσσαν Δέξηται, στυγερῆς τ᾽ ἐνοπῆς κωκυτὸν ἀκούσῃ, Οὐ τηλοῦ θανάτοιο τάχ᾽ ἔσσεται· ἀλλὰ οἱ ἄτην Καὶ μόρον αἰνοτάτη κείνη μαντεύεται αὐδή.

Καστορίδες τ᾽ ὀλοαὶ δυσπενθέες, αἵ τ᾽ ἀλεγεινὴν Ὅσσαν ἐπὶ κροκαλοίσιον ἀπαίσιον ὠρύονται Ἀνδράσιν· ὃς δέ κε γῆρυν ἐν οὔασιν ἀλγινόεσσαν Δέξηται, στυγερῆς τ᾽ ἐνοπῆς κωκυτὸν ἀκούσῃ, Οὐ τηλοῦ θανάτοιο τάχ᾽ ἔσσεται· ἀλλὰ οἱ ἄτην Καὶ μόρον αἰνοτάτη κείνη μαντεύεται αὐδή. [Kastorides t᾽ oloai dyspenthees, hai t᾽ alegeinēn Ossan epi krokaloision apaision ōryontai Andrasin; hos de ke gēryn en ousin alginoessan Dexētai, stygerēs t᾽ enopēs kōkyton akousē, Ou tēlou thanatoio tach᾽ essetai; alla hoi atēn Kai moron ainotatē keinē manteuetai audē.] ¶

Castoridesque perniciosae et gravis luctus, quaeque in littoribus tristem vocem et hominibus infaustam emittunt. Qui vero moestum clamorem auribus perceperit, et quaerulae vocis ejulatum audierit, non procul erit a morte: sed ipsi cladem et mortem praesagit gravissima illa vox. Tzetzes Chiliadis sextae Historia quadragesima septima,

And the deadly castorides of grievous mourning, which on the shores utter a sad voice, ill-omened for men. Whoever receives the doleful cry in his ears and hears the wailing of the plaintive voice will not be far from death: that most grievous voice foretells ruin and death for him. Tzetzes, Chiliad 6, History 47: ¶

Ἡ καστορὶς δ᾽ ὠρύεται, ὃς δ᾽ ἂν ἀκούσῃ ταύτης Χρόνῳ βραχεῖ παρέρχεται τοῦ ταλαιπώρου βίου·

Ἡ καστορὶς δ᾽ ὠρύεται, ὃς δ᾽ ἂν ἀκούσῃ ταύτης Χρόνῳ βραχεῖ παρέρχεται τοῦ ταλαιπώρου βίου· [Hē kastoris d᾽ ōryetai, hos d᾽ an akousē tautēs Chronō brachei parerchetai tou talaipōrou biou;] ¶

Rugit autem castoris, et quisquis illam audierit, ex misera hac vita brevi excedet. Haec de castoridum ejulatu et rugitu reperio. Sed falluntur, qui castoridem pro fibro, aut castore habent, in quibus doctissimus Rittershusius in Notis ad Oppianum. Aliud enim castor, aliud castoris. Ille quadrupes amphibium, haec piscis est. Tzetzes ubi supra,

And the castoris roars, and whoever hears it will shortly depart from this wretched life. This is what I find about the wailing and roaring of the castorides. But those are mistaken who take the castoris for the fiber or castor [beaver], among them the most learned Rittershusius in his Notes on Oppian. For the castor is one thing, the castoris another. The former is an amphibious quadruped, the latter is a fish. Tzetzes, in the place cited above: ¶

Πάντες δ᾽ ἰχθύες ἄφωνοι, πλὴν σκάρου, καὶ καστορίδος·

Πάντες δ᾽ ἰχθύες ἄφωνοι, πλὴν σκάρου, καὶ καστορίδος· [Pantes d᾽ ichthyes aphōnoi, plēn skarou, kai kastoridos;] ¶

Omnes autem pisces muti sunt, exceptis scaro et castoride. Ille in ripis fluminum, haec circa maris littora describitur et censetur ab Oppiano inter ea cete, quae nonnunquam e mari in siccum exeunt: cum contra castor ex terra in amnes feratur. Denique castor est verum animal: castoris fabulosum, ut quidem puto, cum animal in mari nullum noverim, in quod quadret haec descriptio.

But all fishes are mute, except the scarus and the castoris. The former is described on the banks of rivers, the latter around the shores of the sea, and it is counted by Oppian among those sea monsters that sometimes come out of the sea onto dry land; whereas the beaver, on the contrary, goes from land into rivers. Finally, the beaver is a real animal; the castoris is fabulous, as I at least think, since I know of no animal in the sea to which this description fits. ¶

E recentioribus אנקה anaka, Pagnino, Graecos et Hieronymum sequuto, est mygale: et Deodatio mus araneus: Munstero et Genevensibus ericius, tanquam ex Onkelo, cujus Paraphrasis hic corrupta est: Iunio et Buxtorfio attelabus, tanquam ex Hierosolymitano interprete, quem non satis intellexerant: Castalioni fiber ex Rabbi Selomone: Ariae viverra, tanquam ex eodem. Sed ב״ברא Selomonis non debuit legi viverra, quia viverra vox Romana est, sed ב״ברא mere Gallica. II, 493 Gallus enim fuit Rabbi Selomo, nec nisi Gallicis vocibus Hebraeas interpretatur. Tamen ex eodem fonte Angli quoque a ferret, id est, furonem, hic habent, animal, viverrae simile. Sed nihil mirum in devia abiisse tot interpretes, qui neque Syram viderant, neque Samaritanam paraphrasim, neque duas illas Arabicas, ex quibus veram vocis אנקה anaka notionem exsculpsimus.

Among the more recent writers, אנקה anaka is, for Pagnino, who followed the Greeks and Jerome, the mygale; for Deodati the mus araneus [shrew-mouse]; for Münster and the Genevans the ericius [hedgehog], as if on the authority of Onkelos, whose Paraphrase is corrupt here; for Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] the attelabus, as if on the authority of the Jerusalem translator, whom they had not sufficiently understood; for Castellio the fiber [beaver], on the authority of Rabbi Solomon; for Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] the viverra [ferret], as if from the same source. But Solomon's ב״ברא should not have been read as viverra, since viverra is a Roman word, whereas ב״ברא is purely French. II, 493 For Rabbi Solomon was a Frenchman, and he interprets Hebrew words only by French ones. Yet from the same source the English too have here a ferret, that is, furo, an animal similar to the viverra. But it is no wonder that so many translators went astray, since they had seen neither the Syriac nor the Samaritan paraphrase, nor those two Arabic ones, from which we have extracted the true meaning of the word אנקה anaka. ¶
