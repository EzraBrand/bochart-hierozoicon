II, 811 col. 217 CHAPTER XIV. ¶

Probatur, בת יענה bath jaana esse Struthionem foeminam.

It is proved that בת יענה bath jaana is the female ostrich. ¶

Sequitur avis, Hebraice בת יענה bath jaana dicta; quasi filia clamoris. Et sim- col. 218 pliciter יענה jaana. Unde Plurale יענים jeenim, Masculinae terminationis, ut יעלים jeelim, ibices, a Singulari foeminino יעלה jaala. Hujus Prophetae saepe meminerunt inter aves eremicolas. Ita de Babylone dicitur Ierem. L, 39. In ea habitabunt בנות יענה, filiae jaana. Et Es. XIII, 21. Ibi habitabunt filiae jaana. Et de Iudumaea Esa. XXXIV, 13. Erit lustrum draconum, חציר לבנות יענה, pascuum filiarum jaana. Inde Es. XLIII, 20. Me glorificabunt agri be-stiae, dracones, ובנות יענה, et filiae jaana. Immitem esse avem, probant haec Ieremiae Thren. IV, 3. Filia populi mei crudelis facta est כיענים במדבר, ut jeenim in deserto. Et luctuosam haec Michaeae capitis primi versu octavo, Planctum edam ut dracones, et luctum כבנות יענה, ut filiae jaana. Quod ipsum Iob respiciens capitis trigesimi versu vigesimo nono, Frater, inquit, fui draconum, et socius לבנות יענה, filiarum jaana. Haec de bath jaana et jeenim sacri scriptores.

Next comes the bird called in Hebrew בת יענה bath jaana, as if daughter of crying, and simply יענה jaana. col. 218 Hence the plural יענים jeenim, with a masculine ending, as יעלים jeelim, ibexes, from the feminine singular יעלה jaala. The Prophets often mention this bird among the birds that dwell in the desert. Thus it is said of Babylon in Jer 50:39 In it shall dwell בנות יענה, the daughters of jaana. And in Isa 13:21. There shall dwell the daughters of jaana. And of Idumaea, Isa 34:13 It shall be a den of dragons, חציר לבנות יענה, a pasture of the daughters of jaana. Hence Isa 43:20. The beasts of the field shall glorify me, the dragons, ובנות יענה, and the daughters of jaana. That it is a cruel bird is proved by these words of Jeremiah, Lam 4:3 The daughter of my people has become cruel כיענים במדבר, like the jeenim in the desert. And that it is mournful, by these words of Micah, chapter 1, verse 8: I will make a wailing like the dragons, and a mourning כבנות יענה, like the daughters of jaana. With this very passage in view, Job says in chapter 30, verse 29: I was a brother of dragons, and a companion לבנות יענה, of the daughters of jaana. This is what the sacred writers say of bath jaana and jeenim. ¶

Sirenes reddunt Graeci interpretes in Michea, et Ieremia, et Esa. XIII, 21. Ad quem locum Cyrillus: Σειρῆνας δὲ, οἶμαι, φησὶ τὰ εὔστομεῖν εἰδότα πτη-νὰ, ἢ κατά γε τὴν Ἑβραίων ἔκδοσιν, τὴν γλαῦκα· Sirenes, ut puto, appellat aves, canendi peritas, aut, juxta Hebraeorum editionem, noctuam. Et idem in Mich. I, 8. postquam de poetarum Sirenibus egit, Ἡ δέ γε θεόπνευστος γρα-φὴ Σειρῆνας καλεῖ τὰ τῶν στρουθίων λαλίστατά τε, καὶ εὐστομεῖν εἰωθότα, ἢ καὶ αὐτὰς ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε τὰς ἀηδόνας, αἱ τοῖς ἐναλίοις ἐπωάζουσαι κόλποις. εἶτα τῆς καλιᾶς λεφθείσης ὑπὸ κυμάτων, γοερὸν ᾄδουσι μέλος, καὶ καταθρήνουσι τρόπον τινα τὴν τῆς ἑαυτῶν ὠδῖνος ζημίαν. Sacra autem Scriptura Sirenes vocat avicularum disertissimas et maxime canoras, aut interdum ipsas luscinias, quae in finibus maris ovis incubant. Deinde, nido a fluctibus abrepto, lugubre melos fundunt, et partus sui damna quodammodo lamentantur. In quibus verbis mirabere, cur scribat τὰς ἀηδόνας, luscinias, quae cum mari et fluctibus nihil commune habent, in maris finibus ovis incubare, et propter nidum suum, a fluctibus abreptum, lamentari. Quod cum plane alienum, pro τὰς ἀηδόνας meo periculo rescribe τὰς ἀλκυόνας. Sequitur nempe Chrysostomum, qui in haec Iobi capitis trigesimi versu vigesimo nono, Ἀδελφὸς γέγονα Σειρήνων, Sirenum vocem ita exponit: Σειρῆνας λέγει ᾠδικάς τινας ὄρνιθας, ἀλκυόνας, ἢ γλαῦκας· ἄμφω γὰρ θρηνητικά. Sirenes vocat canoras quasdam aves, alcyones puta, II, 812 aut noctuas; utraeque enim sunt luctuosae. Et nescio, quo fato factum, ut, quamvis ἀλκυὼν et ἀηδὼν sint voces, scriptura dissimiles satis, tamen a librariis non raro confundantur. Sic in Aristotele, Historiae animalium libri octavi capite secundo, ubi recentiores editiones recte habent, Τὸ τῶν ἀλκυόνων δὲ γένος πάρυδρόν ἐστι· Alcyonum autem genus ad aquas degit: pro τῶν ἀλκυόνων, perperam legebatur in veteribus editionibus τῶν ἀηδόνων. Et cum apud Suidam, in Ἡμερινὰ ζῶα, marinis avibus adscribuntur ἀλκυὼν, ἀηδὼν, κήυκες, alcyon, luscinia, ceyces, vox ἀηδὼν nihil quidquam est, quam diversa lectio vocis ἀλ-κυὼν, quae ad marginem primo notata est, deinde in textum intrusa. His observatis, Cyrilli verba plana erunt et perspicua: quia Sirenes apud poetas figuram col. 219 avium ex parte habent, et stationem ad maris littus, et miram cantus suavitatem, vult Scripturae interpretes Sirenum nomine alcyones intelligere, littoreas aves, valde canoras. Proinde Chaeremon in Luciani Alcyone de ejus voce, Ὡς ἡδεῖα ταῖς ἀκοαῖς! Quam jucunda est auditu! Et in Imaginibus idem Lucianus quandam, ait, tam suaviter ad cytharam canere, ut, illa aliquid canente, sit ὥρα σιωπᾶν ταῦτα ἀλκυόσι, καὶ τέττιξι, καὶ τοῖς κύκνοις, tempus illa tacendi al-cyonibus, et cicadis, et cygnis. Plutarchus item in libro Utra anim. Ποίας γὰρ ἀηδόνας (παραβάλλειν) ἄξιον τῷ φιλομούσῳ τῆς ἀλκυόνος; Quaenam enim lusciniae cum alcyone cantus ratione conferri queant? Et Oppianus nullam avem suavius canere asserit. Sic Anthologiae libri tertii capite vigesimo quarto, Tymnaeus, ut avem a cantus gratia maxime laudet, dicit, eam alcyonum cantum exaequare:

The Greek translators render it sirens in Micah, in Jeremiah, and in Isa 13:21. On this passage Cyril says: Σειρῆνας δὲ, οἶμαι, φησὶ τὰ εὔστομεῖν εἰδότα πτηνὰ, ἢ κατά γε τὴν Ἑβραίων ἔκδοσιν, τὴν γλαῦκα· [Seirēnas de, oimai, phēsi ta eustomein eidota ptēna, ē kata ge tēn Hebraiōn ekdosin, tēn glauka] By sirens he means, I think, birds skilled in singing, or, according to the Hebrew edition, the little owl. And the same author on Mic 1:8, after discussing the sirens of the poets, says: Ἡ δέ γε θεόπνευστος γραφὴ Σειρῆνας καλεῖ τὰ τῶν στρουθίων λαλίστατά τε, καὶ εὐστομεῖν εἰωθότα, ἢ καὶ αὐτὰς ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε τὰς ἀηδόνας, αἱ τοῖς ἐναλίοις ἐπωάζουσαι κόλποις. εἶτα τῆς καλιᾶς λεφθείσης ὑπὸ κυμάτων, γοερὸν ᾄδουσι μέλος, καὶ καταθρήνουσι τρόπον τινα τὴν τῆς ἑαυτῶν ὠδῖνος ζημίαν. [Hē de ge theopneustos graphē Seirēnas kalei ta tōn strouthiōn lalistata te, kai eustomein eiōthota, ē kai autas esth᾽ hote tas aēdonas, hai tois enaliois epōazousai kolpois. eita tēs kalias lephtheisēs hypo kymatōn, goeron adousi melos, kai katathrēnousi tropon tina tēn tēs heautōn ōdinos zēmian.] But the sacred Scripture calls sirens the most eloquent and most tuneful of small birds, or at times the nightingales themselves, which brood their eggs at the edges of the sea. Then, when the nest has been swept away by the waves, they pour forth a mournful song, and in a way lament the loss of their brood. In these words you will wonder why he writes that τὰς ἀηδόνας [tas aēdonas], nightingales, which have nothing in common with the sea and the waves, brood their eggs at the edges of the sea and lament for their nest swept away by the waves. Since this is plainly out of place, at my own risk read τὰς ἀλκυόνας [tas alkyonas] instead of τὰς ἀηδόνας [tas aēdonas]. For he is following Chrysostom, who, on these words of Job, chapter 30, verse 29, Ἀδελφὸς γέγονα Σειρήνων [Adelphos gegona Seirēnōn], explains the word sirens thus: Σειρῆνας λέγει ᾠδικάς τινας ὄρνιθας, ἀλκυόνας, ἢ γλαῦκας· ἄμφω γὰρ θρηνητικά. [Seirēnas legei ōdikas tinas ornithas, alkyonas, ē glaukas; amphō gar thrēnētika.] By sirens he means certain tuneful birds, namely halcyons, II, 812 or little owls; for both are mournful. And I do not know by what fate it has come about that, although ἀλκυὼν [alkyōn] and ἀηδὼν [aēdōn] are words quite different in spelling, they are nevertheless not rarely confused by copyists. Thus in Aristotle, History of Animals, book 8, chapter 2, where the more recent editions rightly read Τὸ τῶν ἀλκυόνων δὲ γένος πάρυδρόν ἐστι· [To tōn alkyonōn de genos parydron esti] The kind of halcyons lives near the waters: the older editions wrongly read τῶν ἀηδόνων [tōn aēdonōn] instead of τῶν ἀλκυόνων [tōn alkyonōn]. And when in Suidas, under Ἡμερινὰ ζῶα [Hēmerina zōa], ἀλκυὼν, ἀηδὼν, κήυκες [alkyōn, aēdōn, kēykes], halcyon, nightingale, ceyces, are listed among sea birds, the word ἀηδὼν [aēdōn] is nothing but a variant reading of the word ἀλκυὼν [alkyōn], first noted in the margin and then intruded into the text. With these observations made, Cyril's words will be plain and clear: since the sirens in the poets have in part the shape of birds, col. 219 a station on the seashore, and a wonderful sweetness of song, he holds that the interpreters of Scripture mean by the name sirens the halcyons, shore birds that are very tuneful. Accordingly, Chaeremon in Lucian's Halcyon says of its voice: Ὡς ἡδεῖα ταῖς ἀκοαῖς! [Hōs hēdeia tais akoais!] How pleasant it is to hear! And in the Images the same Lucian says that a certain woman sings so sweetly to the cithara that, when she sings something, it is ὥρα σιωπᾶν ταῦτα ἀλκυόσι, καὶ τέττιξι, καὶ τοῖς κύκνοις [hōra siōpan tauta alkyosi, kai tettixi, kai tois kyknois], time for the halcyons, the cicadas and the swans to be silent. Plutarch likewise, in the book Utra anim.: Ποίας γὰρ ἀηδόνας (παραβάλλειν) ἄξιον τῷ φιλομούσῳ τῆς ἀλκυόνος; [Poias gar aēdonas (paraballein) axion tō philomousō tēs alkyonos?] For what nightingales could be compared with the halcyon in respect of song? And Oppian asserts that no bird sings more sweetly. So in the Anthology, book 3, chapter 24, Tymnaeus, to praise a bird as highly as possible for the charm of its song, says that it equals the song of the halcyons: ¶

Ὄρνεον ὦ χάρισιν μεμελημένον, ὦ παρόμοιον Ἀλκυόσιν τὸν σὸν φθόγγον ἰσωσάμενον.

Ὄρνεον ὦ χάρισιν μεμελημένον, ὦ παρόμοιον Ἀλκυόσιν τὸν σὸν φθόγγον ἰσωσάμενον. [Orneon ō charisin memelēmenon, ō paromoion Alkyosin ton son phthongon isōsamenon.] ¶

Addit Cyrillus, alcyones in maris finibus ovis incubare, et propter nidum, a fluctibus abreptum, lugubre melos fundere: ut Valerius Flaccus libro quarto Argonauticῶν,

Cyril adds that halcyons brood their eggs at the edges of the sea and, when the nest is swept away by the waves, pour forth a mournful song; as Valerius Flaccus in book 4 of the Argonautica: ¶

Fluctus ab undisoni ceu forte crepidine saxi Cum rapit alcyonis miserae foetumque, laremque, It super aegra parens, quaeriturque tumentibus undis.

As when, perchance, from the ledge of a wave-resounding rock, the billow snatches away both the young and the home of the wretched halcyon, the stricken parent flies above it and laments on the swelling waves. ¶

Ita enim Scholiastae veteres, ut Homeri ad Iliada nonum, Παρὰ δὲ τοῖς αἰγια-λοῖς τῆς ἀλκυόνος τικτούσης, συμβαίνει ἐπελθόντα τὰ κύματα, τὰ ἔκγονα αὐτῆς κατασύρειν. Ζεὺς δὲ θεασάμενος αὐτὴν κλαίουσαν, etc. Cum ad littora parit alcyon, contingit, ut foetus ejus fluctus abripiant. Lugentem vero Iupiter videns, etc. Et Aristophanis in Avibus, Ἐθρήνει δὲ τῶν ὠῶν αὐτῆς ἐν τῇ θα-λάσσῃ κλωμένων· Lugebat autem propter ova sua, in mari fracta. Et Theocriti in Idyll. 7. Θρηνητικὸν δὲ τὸ ζῶον, καὶ παρὰ τοῖς αἰγιαλοῖς νεοττεῦον, τὸ κῦμα ἀφαιρεῖται τοὺς νεοσσοὺς αὐτοῦ. Luctuosum est animal, quo ad littora nidulante, II, 813 pullos ejus fluctus abripit. Vide etiam Etymologum in Ἀλκυὼν, et Phavorinum in Εὐηνίνη. Sed et poetae vetustiores eandem avem ut θρηνητικὴν describunt, quamvis diversa de causa. Homerus libri noni Iliados versu 559.

For so say the ancient scholiasts, such as that of Homer on the ninth book of the Iliad: Παρὰ δὲ τοῖς αἰγιαλοῖς τῆς ἀλκυόνος τικτούσης, συμβαίνει ἐπελθόντα τὰ κύματα, τὰ ἔκγονα αὐτῆς κατασύρειν. Ζεὺς δὲ θεασάμενος αὐτὴν κλαίουσαν, etc. [Para de tois aigialois tēs alkyonos tiktousēs, symbainei epelthonta ta kymata, ta ekgona autēs katasyrein. Zeus de theasamenos autēn klaiousan], When the halcyon gives birth on the shore, it happens that the waves carry off its young. But Jupiter, seeing her mourning, etc. And the scholiast of Aristophanes on the Birds: Ἐθρήνει δὲ τῶν ὠῶν αὐτῆς ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ κλωμένων [Ethrēnei de tōn ōōn autēs en tē thalassē klōmenōn]; She mourned for her eggs, broken in the sea. And that of Theocritus on Idyll 7: Θρηνητικὸν δὲ τὸ ζῶον, καὶ παρὰ τοῖς αἰγιαλοῖς νεοττεῦον, τὸ κῦμα ἀφαιρεῖται τοὺς νεοσσοὺς αὐτοῦ [Thrēnētikon de to zōon, kai para tois aigialois neotteuon, to kyma aphaireitai tous neossous autou]. It is a mournful creature; when it nests on the shore, II, 813 the wave carries off its chicks. See also the Etymologicum under Ἀλκυὼν [Alkyōn], and Phavorinus under Εὐηνίνη [Euēninē]. But the older poets too describe this same bird as θρηνητικὴν [thrēnētikēn], though for a different reason. Homer, Iliad book 9, line 559: ¶

Μήτηρ ἀλκυόνος πολυπένθεος οἶτον ἔχουσα,

Μήτηρ ἀλκυόνος πολυπένθεος οἶτον ἔχουσα, [Mētēr alkyonos polypentheos oiton echousa,] ¶

Her mother having the grief of the mournful halcyon. Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris, line 1089: ¶

Ὄρνις, ἃ παρὰ τὰς πετρίνας Πόντου δειράδας ἀλκυὼν Ἔλεγον οἶτον ἀείδεις. [Ornis, ha para tas petrinas Pontou deirados alkyōn Elegon oiton aeideis.] ¶

And the moaning tribes of halcyons. The Sibylline oracles, book 7, on Sardinia: ¶

Ἀλκυόνες δ᾽ ἐπὶ σοὶ οἰκτρὸν γόον ἀΐξουσιν,

Ἀλκυόνες δ᾽ ἐπὶ σοὶ οἰκτρὸν γόον ἀΐξουσιν, [Alkyones d᾽ epi soi oiktron goon aixousin,] ¶

Alcyones super te miserabilem lamentationem excitabunt. Lucianus in Alcyone, Θαλαττία τις ὄρνις ἀλκυὼν ὀνομαζομένη, πολύθρηνος, καὶ πολύδακρυς· Marina quaedam avis alcyon dicta, valde luctuosa et lacrymosa. Et paullo post, Γοώδη γοῦν ὡς ἀληθῶς τὸν ἦχον ἀφίησι τὸ ζῶον· Lugubrem profecto sonum emittit illud animal. Item, Ὦ ὄρνι θρήνων μελῳδέ· O avis, lamentationum modulatrix. Cum igitur alcyon sit omnium avium maxime lugubris, si poetarum judicio stamus, inde collegerunt veteres, eam a Michea significari, cum dicit, juxta Graecos interpretes, Luctum edam ut Sirenes; aut Hebraice, ut filiae jaanae. Quod tamen non sufficit, cum caetera non conveniant. Nam marina est avis alcyon, quamque adeo dictam esse volunt παρὰ τὸ ἐν ἁλὶ κύειν, ex eo, quod in mari concipit et parit. Itaque ἁλκυόνα, halcyonem, cum spiritu aspero multi scribunt: in quibus Theocritus in Thalysiis versu 57.

The halcyons will raise a pitiful lamentation over you. Lucian, in the Halcyon: Θαλαττία τις ὄρνις ἀλκυὼν ὀνομαζομένη, πολύθρηνος, καὶ πολύδακρυς [Thalattia tis ornis alkyōn onomazomenē, polythrēnos, kai polydakrys]; A certain sea bird called halcyon, very mournful and tearful. And shortly after: Γοώδη γοῦν ὡς ἀληθῶς τὸν ἦχον ἀφίησι τὸ ζῶον [Goōdē goun hōs alēthōs ton ēchon aphiēsi to zōon]; That creature truly emits a mournful sound. Likewise: Ὦ ὄρνι θρήνων μελῳδέ [Ō orni thrēnōn melōde]; O bird, singer of laments. Since, then, the halcyon is the most mournful of all birds, if we stand by the judgement of the poets, the ancients inferred from this that it is meant by Micah when he says, according to the Greek translators, I will make lamentation like the Sirens; or in Hebrew, like the daughters of jaana. This, however, is not enough, since the other features do not fit. For the halcyon is a sea bird, and they hold that it was so named παρὰ τὸ ἐν ἁλὶ κύειν [para to en hali kyein], because it conceives and gives birth in the sea. Hence many write ἁλκυόνα [halkyona], halcyon, with the rough breathing; among them Theocritus in the Thalysia, line 57: ¶

— χ᾽ ἁλκυόνες στορεσεῦντι Τὰ κύματα — —

— χ᾽ ἁλκυόνες στορεσεῦντι Τὰ κύματα — — [ch᾽ halkyones storeseunti Ta kymata] ¶

Et halcyones fluctus sternent. At filiae jaana in desertis degunt, a mari remotissimis, puta circa Babylonem et Botsram Idumaeae, Ierem. L, 39. Esa. XIII, 21. et XXXIV, 13. Praeterea crudelis et ἄστοργος est bath jaana, Thren. IV, 3. At alcyon, Plutarcho auctore ubi supra, vincit τῷ φιλοτέκνῳ χελιδόνας, prolis amore hirundines; et τὸ φιλάνδρῳ πελειάδας, amore conjugis columbas (ita enim legendum, non φιλανθρώπῳ, ut habent nostrae editiones). Itaque cerylos maritos suos, aetate graves, αἱ ἀλκυόνες περιάγουσιν ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν τῶν κα-λουμένων μεσοπτερυγίων, interscapiliis suis sublatos alcyones circumferunt. Quo pertinent Alcmanis versus apud Antigonum Hist. 27.

And the halcyons will calm the waves. But the daughters of jaana live in deserts very far from the sea, for instance around Babylon and Bozrah of Edom, Jer 50:39. Isa 13:21 and XXXIV, 13. Moreover, the bath jaana is cruel and ἄστοργος [astorgos], Lam 4:3. But the halcyon, on the authority of Plutarch in the place cited above, surpasses τῷ φιλοτέκνῳ χελιδόνας [tō philoteknō chelidonas], swallows in love of offspring; and τὸ φιλάνδρῳ πελειάδας [to philandrō peleiadas], doves in love of the mate (for so it should be read, not φιλανθρώπῳ [philanthrōpō], as our editions have it). Thus αἱ ἀλκυόνες περιάγουσιν ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν τῶν καλουμένων μεσοπτερυγίων [hai alkyones periagousin ep᾽ autōn tōn kaloumenōn mesopterygiōn] their mates, the ceryli, when weighed down by age: the halcyons carry them about, lifted on their backs between the wings. To this belong Alcman's lines in Antigonus, Hist. 27: ¶

— — βάλε δὴ, βάλε κηρύλος εἴην, Ὅς τ᾽ ἐπὶ κύματος ἄνθος ἅμ᾽ ἀλκυόνεσσι πότηται·

— — βάλε δὴ, βάλε κηρύλος εἴην, Ὅς τ᾽ ἐπὶ κύματος ἄνθος ἅμ᾽ ἀλκυόνεσσι πότηται· [bale dē, bale kērylos eiēn, Hos t᾽ epi kymatos anthos ham᾽ alkyonessi potētai] ¶

Utinam cerylus essem (alcyonum mares sic vocant) qui maris superficiem supervolat cum alcyonibus. Caetera ibi vide.

Would that I were a cerylus (so they call the males of the halcyons) that flies over the surface of the sea with the halcyons. See the rest there. ¶

Proinde ad alcyonem nonnisi cunctanter referunt sacri doctores, quae in Graeca versione legerant de Sirenibus, Chrysostomusque et Cyrillus addunt, ab aliis τὴν γλαῦκα, id est noctuam, intelligi; idque κατὰ τὴν Ἑβραίων ἔκδοσιν, juxta Hebraeorum editionem: quasi vox Hebraea בת־יענה bath-jaana, id significet. A qua sententia parum recedunt recentiores, Pagninus scilicet, Arias, Iunius, et Tremellius, Schindlerus, Buxtorfius, et Bibliorum vernaculi interpretes; II, 815 quibus bath-jaana est ulula, vel bubo (1). Et hanc versionem vir eruditus nostro saeculo multis asserit, Fullerus scilicet Miscellaneorum libri sexti capite septimo, Ejusmodi, inquit, alitis genus, quod et θρηνητικὸν sit, et φιλέρημον, recte, opinor, Tremellius ululam vertit, quam Graeci (ut et Latini) a moesto velut ululatu ὀλολυγόνα appellant. Eodem quoque modo a ענה, clamando, sive resonando יענה jaana nuncuparunt, propter vocem scilicet sonoram, praesertim noctis silentio, et saepius ingeminatam. Nisi malimus (quod potius credo) ululae quandam speciem esse, quam strigem vocarunt. Etenim strix ὀλολυγὼν redditur Graece in Glossario antiquo. Eam vero ἐρημαίην, seu solitudinis amantem, nominat Aratus: ejusque voces tristes ac ferales semper existimatas fuisse constat.

Accordingly, the sacred doctors refer only hesitantly to the halcyon what they had read in the Greek version about the Sirens; and Chrysostom and Cyril add that others understand τὴν γλαῦκα [tēn glauka], that is, the little owl, and that this is κατὰ τὴν Ἑβραίων ἔκδοσιν [kata tēn Hebraiōn ekdosin], according to the Hebrews' edition, as if the Hebrew word בת־יענה bath-jaana meant this. From this opinion the more recent scholars depart little, namely Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], Schindlerus, Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], and the translators of the vernacular Bibles; II, 815 for them bath-jaana is the owl or the eagle owl R1. And a learned man of our century defends this rendering at length, namely Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], in book 6, chapter 7 of his Miscellanea: A kind of bird of this sort, which is both θρηνητικὸν [thrēnētikon] and φιλέρημον [philerēmon], Tremellius rightly, I think, renders owl, which the Greeks (as also the Latins) call ὀλολυγόνα [ololygona] from its mournful, as it were, howling. In the same way they named it יענה jaana from ענה, to cry out or resound, namely because of its sonorous voice, especially in the silence of the night, and often repeated. Unless we prefer (which I rather believe) that it is a certain species of owl which they called the strix. For strix is rendered ὀλολυγὼν [ololygōn] in Greek in an ancient Glossary. Aratus calls it ἐρημαίην [erēmaiēn], that is, a lover of solitude; and it is agreed that its cries were always considered sad and deathly. ¶

Haec ille: quae utut speciosa, mihi tamen haudquaquam persuadent; quia video veteres sacrae scripturae interpretes fere unanimi consensu בת־יענה struthionem interpretari. Quod ipsum, quia in quaestione est, probare jam aggredior. Atque ut ab antiquissimis ordiar, LXX. interpretes ut plurimum στρουθὸν reddunt, vel στρουθίον, id est, struthionem. Ita in hoc ipso loco, in quo versamur, Levit. XI, 16. τὸν στρουθὸν habent (2). Et Iob. XXX, 29. ἑταῖρος (γέγο-να) στρουθῶν. Et Esa. XXXIV, 13. αὐλὴ στρουθῶν. Et Esa. XLIII, 20. εὐλο-γήσει με, etc. θυγατέρες στρουθῶν. Et Thren. IV, 3. ὡς στρουθίον ἐν ἐρήμῳ. col. 221 Dices, vocem esse ambiguam: quia στρουθὸς modo pro passere sumitur ex avicularum genere; modo pro struthione, vel, quod idem est, pro strutho-camelo, qui et Latine dicitur passer marinus, in Plauto, Ausonio, Festo, et aliis. Et dubitari posse, utro sensu illi interpretes usurpaverint. Ita est: sed de struthionibus accipiunt veteres prope omnes. Chrysostomus in Iobum, Στρουθοὺς λέγει τοὺς στρουθοκαμήλους, ὡς καὶ Σύμμαχος ἐξέδωκεν. Ita etiam Eusebius, Basilius, Hieronymus, Theodoretus, Procopius in Esaiam, Olympiodorus in Hieremiam, et novissime Suidas in Σειρῆνας· Στρουθὸν λέγει, ὃν ἡμεῖς στρουθοκάμηλον λέγο-μεν. Neque aliter senserunt Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, et Vulgatus interpres: quorum hic struthiones, illi στρουθοκαμήλους constanter reddunt. Solus denique e veteribus, quod meminerim, Novatianus de passere accipiens in libro De cibis Iudaicis capite tertio, Passerem, inquit, cum interdicit, intemperantiam coarguit. Et e recentioribus solus Fullerus eandem sententiam amplectitur, II, 816 hoc scilicet Canone fretus, quem pronuntiat ut ex tripode: Nomen στρουθὸς solum, ac per se positum, nullo ad differentiam designandam addito epitheto, passerem Latine significare. Nempe legerat vir doctus, et in his rebus accuratus supra fidem, struthionem ab Herodoto appellari στρουθὸν κατάγαιον· ab Aristophane, Xenophonte, Athenaeo, et Stratone in Anthologia μέγαν· ab Aristotele Λιβυκόν· ab Heraclide Ἀράβιον· a Luciano χαμαιπετῆ· ab Aeliano χερσαῖον· a Pausania στρουθὸν τῶν ἀπτήνων· ab Herodiano Μαυρούσιον· ab Hesychio ἄγριον. Itaque perpetuum esse putavit, quod plerumque fit. Certum tamen est, etiam sine epitheto στρουθὸν pro struthione non raro sumi. Quis enim dixerit passerum pennas, non struthionum, intelligi a Theophrasto, cum dicit galeas ornari τῶν στρουθῶν πτεροῖς, Historiae plantarum libri quarti capite quinto. Et capite proxime antecedenti, cum in parte Libyae, in qua non pluit, nasci scribit πτῶκα, καὶ δορκάδα, καὶ στρουθὸν, καὶ ἕτερα τῶν θηρίων, leporem, et capream, et struthum (ut ibi habet interpres, saepe ineptus,) et feras alias: quid aliud ibi sit στρουθὸς quam struthio, quem multi feris adscribunt, quia mediae naturae est inter aves et feras? Et στρουθῶν συνωρίδας ὀκτὼ, quas processisse narrat a) in Ptolemaei pompa Callixenus Rhodius, non struthionum bigas, sed passerum fuisse quis putaverit, quomodo in curru Veneris? Et passeris pedem innuere doctissimum Scholiastem, qui Cleocritum ab Aristophane in Avibus traduci ait ὡς στρουθόποδα, τουτέστι μεγαλόπουν. Et de passerum pellibus egisse aut Pollucem eo loco, quo Arabes στρουθῶν δοραῖς in bello contegi narrat b) pro scutis, et thoracibus: aut Strabonem, cum Struthophagos has aves venari scribit c) ταῖς δοραῖς τῶν στρουθῶν σκεπασθέντας, ut hac specie delusae eae accedant incautius? Et in Aethiopicis animalibus, quae vidit Apollonius, cum a Philostrato conjunctim nominantur ἔλαφοι, καὶ δορκάδες, καὶ στρουθοὶ, καὶ ὄνοι, quis passeres intelligat στρουθῶν nomine: eosque cum cervis, capreis, et onagris inter Aethiopiae feras censeri credat? Haec igitur exempla, nisi fallor, abunde docent, Fulleri Canoni, de quo supra, nullam fidem esse adhibendam, et verissimum esse, quod dixi, στρουθὸν, etiam per se sumptum, tam de struthione dici, quam de passere. Quod ipsis etiam Atticis familiare fuisse, testatur Hesychius his verbis, Στρουθός· ὁ καταφερὴς, καὶ λάγνος. Ἀττικοὶ δὲ τὰς στρουθοκαμή-λους. Ut vel Attice loquendo LXX. interpretes potuerint sic usurpare. Si rogaveris, cur non potius usi sint struthocameli nomine, vel aliquo saltem epitheto ad devitandam vocis homonymiam, responsio erit in promptu. Nam qua aetate vivebant, nondum erat in usu struthocameli nomen, quo nescio an quisquam utatur ante Strabonem: nisi quod Diodorus, paucis annis vetustior, illud videtur II, 817 designare, cum libro tertio narrat, quoddam esse in Struthophagis avis genus, μεμιγμένην ἔχων τὴν φύσιν, τῷ χερσαίῳ ζώῳ, δι᾽ ἣν τῆς συνθέτου τετύχηκε col. 222 προσηγορίας, quae mixtam habet naturam cum animali terrestri, ac proinde compositum nomen adepta est. Inde est, quod Galenus, Graecis interpretibus tot saeculis junior, struthiocameli nomen, tanquam novum, et veteribus ignotum, absque praefatione usurpare vix audet. Ita libri tertii De alimentis capite vigesimo, Τὸ μὲν τῶν χηνῶν ὄνομα, inquit, σύνεθές ἐστι καὶ τοῖς παλαιοῖς· τὸ δὲ τῶν στρουθοκαμήλων ἄηθες. ὀνομάζουσι γὰρ αὐτὰς μεγάλας στρουθούς. Anserum quidem nomen etiam veteribus consuetum est; sed struthocamelorum insolens. Eos enim appellant magnos passeres. Et in libro De prob. suc. alim. capite sexto, Ἐν δὲ τοῖς μεγάλοις στρουθοῖς, ἃς νῦν ὀνομάζουσι στρουθοκαμήλους· In magnis passeribus, quos nunc struthocamelos nominant. Sed et στρουθοὺς simpliciter dicere maluerunt, quam στρουθοὺς μεγάλους, aut καταγαίους, etc. cum, quia Hebraeum בת יענה bath jaana, nullum habet adjunctum epithetum, tum, quia non erat opus quidquam addere, cum res ipsa per se doceat, eas aves in Prophetis hoc vocabulo describi, quibus praeter nudum nomen nihil est commune cum passeribus: aves, inquam, solitarias, et in locis desertis horridisque commorantes; cum gregatim volent passeres, cultaque loca et frugifera libentius frequentent.

So he writes. However plausible this may be, it does not persuade me at all, because I see that the ancient interpreters of Sacred Scripture, with almost unanimous agreement, interpret בת־יענה as ostrich. Since this very point is in question, I now undertake to prove it. To begin with the most ancient, the LXX translators mostly render it στρουθὸν [strouthon] or στρουθίον [strouthion], that is, ostrich. Thus in the very passage we are dealing with, Lev 11:16, they have τὸν στρουθὸν [ton strouthon] R2. And at Job 30:29 ἑταῖρος (γέγονα) στρουθῶν [hetairos (gegona) strouthōn]. And at Isa 34:13 αὐλὴ στρουθῶν [aulē strouthōn]. And at Isa 43:20 εὐλογήσει με, etc. θυγατέρες στρουθῶν [eulogēsei me, etc. thygateres strouthōn]. And at Lam 4:3 ὡς στρουθίον ἐν ἐρήμῳ [hōs strouthion en erēmō]. col. 221 You will say that the word is ambiguous, because στρουθὸς [strouthos] is sometimes taken for the sparrow, one of the small birds, and sometimes for the ostrich, or, what is the same, for the strutho-camelus, which in Latin is also called passer marinus [sea sparrow] in Plautus, Ausonius, Festus and others; and that it can be doubted in which sense those interpreters used it. So it is; but almost all the ancients take it of ostriches. Chrysostom on Job: Στρουθοὺς λέγει τοὺς στρουθοκαμήλους, ὡς καὶ Σύμμαχος ἐξέδωκεν [Strouthous legei tous strouthokamēlous, hōs kai Symmachos exedōken]. So too Eusebius, Basil, Jerome, Theodoret and Procopius on Isaiah, Olympiodorus on Jeremiah, and most recently Suidas under Σειρῆνας [Seirēnas]: Στρουθὸν λέγει, ὃν ἡμεῖς στρουθοκάμηλον λέγομεν [Strouthon legei, hon hēmeis strouthokamēlon legomen]. Nor did Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion and the Vulgate translator think otherwise: the last consistently renders struthiones [ostriches], the others στρουθοκαμήλους [strouthokamēlous]. Finally, alone among the ancients, as far as I remember, Novatian takes it of the sparrow, in the book On Jewish Foods, chapter 3: When he forbids the sparrow, he says, he rebukes intemperance. And alone among the moderns Fuller embraces the same view, II, 816 relying, that is, on this rule, which he pronounces as if from the tripod: The noun στρουθὸς [strouthos], placed alone and by itself, with no epithet added to mark a distinction, means sparrow in Latin. No doubt the learned man, who is incredibly precise in such matters, had read that the ostrich is called στρουθὸν κατάγαιον [strouthon katagaion] by Herodotus; μέγαν [megan] by Aristophanes, Xenophon, Athenaeus and Strato in the Anthology; Λιβυκόν [Libykon] by Aristotle; Ἀράβιον [Arabion] by Heraclides; χαμαιπετῆ [chamaipetē] by Lucian; χερσαῖον [chersaion] by Aelian; στρουθὸν τῶν ἀπτήνων [strouthon tōn aptēnōn] by Pausanias; Μαυρούσιον [Maurousion] by Herodian; ἄγριον [agrion] by Hesychius. So he thought that what usually happens always happens. It is certain, however, that στρουθὸν [strouthon] is not rarely taken for the ostrich even without an epithet. For who would say that sparrows' feathers, not ostriches', are meant by Theophrastus when he says that helmets are adorned τῶν στρουθῶν πτεροῖς [tōn strouthōn pterois], History of Plants, book 4, chapter 5? And in the chapter just before, when he writes that in the part of Libya where it does not rain there are born πτῶκα, καὶ δορκάδα, καὶ στρουθὸν, καὶ ἕτερα τῶν θηρίων [ptōka, kai dorkada, kai strouthon, kai hetera tōn thēriōn], the hare, and the roe, and the struthus (as the translator, often inept, has it there) and other wild beasts: what else can στρουθὸς [strouthos] be there than the ostrich, which many count among wild beasts because it is of a nature intermediate between birds and beasts? And the στρουθῶν συνωρίδας ὀκτὼ [strouthōn synōridas oktō] which, as Callixenus of Rhodes relates a), went forth in the procession of Ptolemy: who would think they were pairs of sparrows rather than of ostriches, as in the chariot of Venus? And would the most learned Scholiast mean a sparrow's foot when he says that Cleocritus is mocked by Aristophanes in the Birds ὡς στρουθόποδα, τουτέστι μεγαλόπουν [hōs strouthopoda, toutesti megalopoun]? And did Pollux speak of sparrow skins in the passage where he relates b) that the Arabs covered themselves in war στρουθῶν δοραῖς [strouthōn dorais] instead of shields and breastplates; or Strabo, when he writes c) that the Struthophagi hunt these birds ταῖς δοραῖς τῶν στρουθῶν σκεπασθέντας [tais dorais tōn strouthōn skepasthentas], so that, deceived by this appearance, the birds approach more incautiously? And among the Ethiopian animals that Apollonius saw, when Philostratus names together ἔλαφοι, καὶ δορκάδες, καὶ στρουθοὶ, καὶ ὄνοι [elaphoi, kai dorkades, kai strouthoi, kai onoi], who would understand sparrows by the name στρουθῶν [strouthōn], and believe they are reckoned with deer, roes and wild asses among the wild beasts of Ethiopia? These examples, then, if I am not mistaken, amply show that no trust is to be placed in Fuller's rule mentioned above, and that what I said is perfectly true: στρουθὸν [strouthon], even taken by itself, is said of the ostrich as well as of the sparrow. That this was familiar even to the Attic writers is attested by Hesychius in these words: Στρουθός· ὁ καταφερὴς, καὶ λάγνος. Ἀττικοὶ δὲ τὰς στρουθοκαμήλους [Strouthos; ho katapherēs, kai lagnos. Attikoi de tas strouthokamēlous]. So that even speaking in Attic fashion the LXX translators could use it thus. If you ask why they did not rather use the name struthocamelus, or at least some epithet to avoid the ambiguity of the word, the answer is ready. For in the age in which they lived the name struthocamelus was not yet in use; I do not know whether anyone uses it before Strabo, except that Diodorus, a few years older, seems II, 817 to indicate it when he relates in book 3 that among the Struthophagi there is a kind of bird μεμιγμένην ἔχων τὴν φύσιν, τῷ χερσαίῳ ζώῳ, δι᾽ ἣν τῆς συνθέτου τετύχηκε col. 222 προσηγορίας [memigmenēn echōn tēn physin, tō chersaiō zōō, di᾽ hēn tēs synthetou tetychēke prosēgorias], which has a nature mixed with a land animal, and has therefore obtained a compound name. Hence it is that Galen, so many centuries later than the Greek translators, hardly dares to use the name struthiocamelus without a preface, as new and unknown to the ancients. Thus in book 3 of On Foods, chapter 20, he says: Τὸ μὲν τῶν χηνῶν ὄνομα σύνεθές ἐστι καὶ τοῖς παλαιοῖς· τὸ δὲ τῶν στρουθοκαμήλων ἄηθες. ὀνομάζουσι γὰρ αὐτὰς μεγάλας στρουθούς [To men tōn chēnōn onoma synethes esti kai tois palaiois; to de tōn strouthokamēlōn aēthes. onomazousi gar autas megalas strouthous]. The name of geese was indeed customary even among the ancients, but that of struthocameli is unusual; for they call them great sparrows. And in the book De prob. suc. alim., chapter 6: Ἐν δὲ τοῖς μεγάλοις στρουθοῖς, ἃς νῦν ὀνομάζουσι στρουθοκαμήλους [En de tois megalois strouthois, has nyn onomazousi strouthokamēlous]; In the great sparrows, which they now call struthocameli. But they preferred to say simply στρουθοὺς [strouthous] rather than στρουθοὺς μεγάλους [strouthous megalous] or καταγαίους [katagaious], etc., both because the Hebrew בת יענה bath jaana has no epithet attached, and because there was no need to add anything, since the matter itself shows that this word in the Prophets describes birds which have nothing in common with sparrows except the bare name: birds, I say, that are solitary and dwell in desert and dreadful places; whereas sparrows fly in flocks and more willingly frequent cultivated and fruitful places. ¶

Neque id Fullerum fugit; itaque ne cogatur struthiones hic agnoscere, aliud comminiscitur, nempe in Graeca versione στρουθοὺς esse ululas, aut vero striges. Quod probare conatur duplici argumento, sed utroque prorsus invalido. Prius tale est. In Suida στρουθίζων exponitur τρίζων stridens, quod strigum est proprium: ergo etiam στρουθὸς, unde στρουθίζειν, est strix. Et strigum id esse proprium, probat ex libro sexto Fastorum, ubi sic Ovidius:

Nor did this escape Fuller. So, in order not to be forced to recognize ostriches here, he devises something else, namely that in the Greek version στρουθούς [strouthous] are owls, or rather screech owls [striges]. He tries to prove this with two arguments, but both are entirely invalid. The first is as follows. In Suidas στρουθίζων [strouthizōn] is explained as τρίζων [trizōn], screeching, which is proper to screech owls; therefore στρουθός [strouthos] too, from which στρουθίζειν [strouthizein] comes, is a strix [screech owl]. And that this is proper to screech owls he proves from the sixth book of the Fasti, where Ovid says: ¶

Est illis strigibus nomen: sed nominis hujus Causa, quod horrendum stridere nocte solent.

Their name is striges; but the reason for this name is that they are wont to screech horribly at night. ¶

Ut in Poematio De Philomela, quisquis ejus sit auctor;

As in the little poem On the Nightingale, whoever its author may be: ¶

Strix nocturna sonans, et vespertilio stridunt.

The screech owl sounding by night, and the bat, screech. ¶

Sed frustra id affertur, cum passerum quoque sit τρίζειν. Proinde Iliad. β΄. versu 314. draco passerculos octo cum matre.

But this is adduced in vain, since τρίζειν [trizein] belongs to sparrows as well. Thus in Iliad β΄ [2], line 314, the dragon [devoured] eight little sparrows with their mother: ¶

— — — ἐλεεινὰ κατέσθιε τετριγῶτας,

— — — ἐλεεινὰ κατέσθιε τετριγῶτας [eleeina katesthie tetrigōtas], ¶

voravit miserabiliter stridentes. Cum igitur τρίζειν tam sit passerum, quam strigum, cum Suidas στρουθίζων exponit τρίζων, tam potuit passerum pipitum, quam strigum stridorem respicere. Et vero id sumptum ex Esa. X, 14. ubi pro Hebraeo מצפצף, id est, avicularum more garriens aut minuriens, Graeca versio habet στρουθίζων, quod Theodoretus exponit τρίσαι stridere, et γρύζειν στρουθοῖς II, 818 παραπλησίως passerum instar pipire. Itaque causa non fuit, cur hic Fullerus strigum stridorem et ululatum sibi fingeret. Sed aliud intentat telum, quod validius videbitur. Strix (inquit) in Glossario non solum ὀλολυγὼν transfertur, id est, ulula, sed etiam στρουθός. Ibi enim ita habetur, Strix, Ὀλολυγὼν, στρου-θός. Itaque haec tria, strix, ὀλολυγὼν, et στρουθὸς sunt synonyma. Respondeo, illum Glossae locum palam esse mendosum. Neque enim unquam fando auditum est, voce στρουθὸς vel strigem vel ululam significari. Itaque illud στρουθὸς loco suo motum est, et reponendum inferius, post illa verba, Struthio, Στρου-θοκάμηλος. Haec verborum trajectio in Glossis frequens. Tale in iisdem, Facundus, Εὔγλωττος, γόνιμος· quorum ultimum pertinet ad τὰ Foecundus, vel Foecunda, quae infra sequuntur. Item, Fastus, Ἐπισκύνιον, ἡμερολόγιον· quorum hoc fastos, non fastum significat. Ergo pertinebat ad vocem Fasti, quae praetermissa est. Ibidem, Rigor, Κρύος, ἀπόσκληρον. Quorum τὸ ἀπόσκληρον referendum ad τὸ rigidum, quod sequitur. Et in Hesychio, Ἀλύω· ἀδημονῶ, ἀθυμῶ· Φοίνικες. Absurde: neque enim verbum ἀλύω potest esse Phoenicia vox. Itaque vox Φοίνικες loco suo mota est, et ad id pertinet, quod sequitur, Ἄλφα· βοὸς κεφαλή. Quod ipsum mendo non vacat. Scriben- col. 223 dum enim βοῦς, κεφαλὴ, ut alibi monuimus. Potest etiam aliter restitui Glossarii locus, in quo versamur: nempe si pro στρουθὸς legatur στρίγλος, quod strigis apud Graecos fuisse nomen, probant haec Hesychii, licet corruptissima. Since, then, τρίζειν [trizein] belongs as much to sparrows as to screech owls, when Suidas explains στρουθίζων [strouthizōn] as τρίζων [trizōn], he could have had in view the chirping of sparrows just as well as the screeching of owls. And indeed it is taken from Isa 10:14, where for the Hebrew מצפצף, that is, chattering like little birds or twittering, the Greek version has στρουθίζων [strouthizōn], which Theodoret explains as τρίσαι [trisai], to screech, and γρύζειν στρουθοῖς II, 818 παραπλησίως [gryzein strouthois paraplēsiōs], to chirp like sparrows. So there was no reason for Fuller to imagine here the screeching and hooting of owls. But he aims another weapon, which will seem stronger. Strix, he says, is rendered in the Glossary not only as ὀλολυγών [ololygōn], that is, owl, but also as στρουθός [strouthos]. For there it reads thus: Strix, Ὀλολυγών, στρουθός [Ololygōn, strouthos]. Therefore these three, strix, ὀλολυγών [ololygōn] and στρουθός [strouthos], are synonyms. I reply that this passage of the Glossary is plainly corrupt. For it has never been heard that the word στρουθός [strouthos] means either a strix or an owl. So that στρουθός [strouthos] has been moved from its place and must be put back lower down, after the words Struthio, Στρουθοκάμηλος [Strouthokamēlos]. Such transposition of words is frequent in the Glosses. Thus in the same: Facundus, Εὔγλωττος, γόνιμος [Euglōttos, gonimos]; the last of these belongs to Foecundus or Foecunda, which follow below. Likewise, Fastus, Ἐπισκύνιον, ἡμερολόγιον [Episkynion, hēmerologion]; the latter means fasti [calendar], not fastus [pride]. It therefore belonged to the word Fasti, which has been omitted. In the same place, Rigor, Κρύος, ἀπόσκληρον [Kryos, aposklēron]; of these, ἀπόσκληρον [aposklēron] must be referred to rigidum, which follows. And in Hesychius: Ἀλύω· ἀδημονῶ, ἀθυμῶ· Φοίνικες [Alyō; adēmonō, athymō; Phoinikes]. Absurd: for the verb ἀλύω [alyō] cannot be a Phoenician word. So the word Φοίνικες [Phoinikes] has been moved from its place, and belongs to what follows, Ἄλφα· βοὸς κεφαλή [Alpha; boos kephalē]. And this itself is not free from error. col. 223 For one must write βοῦς, κεφαλή [bous, kephalē], as we have noted elsewhere. The passage of the Glossary under discussion can also be restored otherwise: namely if for στρουθός [strouthos] one reads στρίγλος [striglos], which was the name of the strix among the Greeks, as these words of Hesychius prove, though very corrupt: Στρίγλος· τὰ ἐντὸς τοῦ κέρατος, νυκτίφοιτον. Καλεῖται δὲ καὶ νυκτοβόα, οἱ δὲ νυκτικόρακα [Striglos; ta entos tou keratos, nyktiphoiton. Kaleitai de kai nyktoboa, hoi de nyktikoraka]. I think these should be written thus: Στρίγλος· τὸ ἐν τοῖς τερατείοις νυκτίφοιτον [Striglos; to en tois terateiois nyktiphoiton] etc. Among uncanny things, that which walks by night; it is also called nycti-boa (as it were, crying by night) and by some nycti-corax. ¶

Haec de Graecis interpretibus vocis bath-jaana. Iam ad Chaldaeos, Syros, atque Arabes me confero, qui omnes idem sequuntur. Nam Chaldaei tres, Onkelos, Ionathan et Hierosolymitanus hic habent נעמא naama, vel נעמיתא naamitha. Syrus etiam ܢܥܡܐ naama. Et Arabes, tam editi, quam manuscripti نعام naam, vel نعامة naama. Quibus vocibus struthionem significari, tam certum est, quam quod est certissimum. Quod uno consensu me docent Arabum lexicographi, historici, geographi, medici, philosophi. Sed Damir inprimis et Alkazuinius, qui in Capitibus De naam, vel naama, struthionem describunt multo accuratius, quam Graeci et Romani scriptores. Et Abenbitar, qui in naama describit ad verbum, quae de struthione habet Galenus li-bro tertio De alimentis. Quin rationem nominis iidem aperiunt. Struthioni et camelo multa sunt communia. Proinde pes utriusque dicitur خف choph, et منسم mensim: et utriusque foemina قلوص kalus. Sic vicina sunt نعام naam, et نعم naam: quorum illud struthionem, hoc camelum significat. Unde II, 819 est, quod ظربان dorban, id est, ea mustelae species, quam a foetore barbari scriptores putorium vocant, ab Arabibus dicitur esse مفرق النعم dissipator τοῦ naam; quia camelorum turmas foetore suo dispergit atque dissipat. Et in Plurali انعام anam sunt cameli. Ut in his Mahumetani theologi in Al-camus, الله جعل لكم الانعام لتركبوا Deus vobis fecit Alanama (camelos), quibus veheremini. Itaque etiam struthio vocatur نعام naam; quia est camelo similis. Tale est, quod observat Algiahid, struthionem a Persis appellari استرموك istarmuch, quasi cameli avem dicas, quia videtur ex utroque conflatum esse animal. Hinc Alkazuinius, نعامة حيوان مركب من حلقة الطير والجمل اخذ من البعير العنق والمنسم ومن الطير المنقار والجناح والريش Struthio est animal, ex cameli et avis natura compositum: ex camelo enim collum et pedes sumpsit; ex ave rostrum, alas et plumas. Ergo ut Graece et Latine, ita Persice, Chaldaice, Syriace et Arabice huic avi nomen fecit ἡ μεμιγμέ-νη φύσις τῷ χερσαίῳ ζώῳ. Et cum recentiores Chaldaicae linguae Lexicographi נעמא naama, vel נעמיתא naamitha, ululam aut bubonem interpretantur, gravi errore se obstringunt, in quem nunquam incidissent, si vel minimam habuissent col. 224 Arabicae linguae notitiam. Quamvis eo usque devenire necesse non habuerint. Quippe etiam ex Talmude patet, נעמית naamith esse struthionem. Sic cum in tractatu Kelim, sive De Vasis cap. 17. §. 14. in vasorum censu ponitur ביצת נעמית המצופה, ovum naamith obductum (auro scilicet, vel argento). Certum est, ovum naamith esse struthionis ovum, ex quo vasa fieri, res notissima. Plinius libri decimi capite primo; Ova (struthionum) propter amplitudinem pro quibusdam habita vasis. Et de iisdem Lucianus in Dipsadibus, Σκεύεσι χρῶνται κενώσαντες, καὶ ἐκπώματα ποιοῦνται ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν· Iis evacuatis pro vasis utuntur, et ex iis pocula fiunt. Rursus in tractatu De Sabbatho cap. 18. fol. 128. a. מטלטלין שברי זכוכית מפני שמאכל לנעמיות, Circumferunt (licite die Sabbathi) fragmenta vitri; quia sunt pro cibo struthionibus. Ita omnino vertenda vox לנעמיות, non ululis, aut bubonibus, quos domi alere nemo satagit: ut carnivoras aves esse taceam, cum struthio, quodcunque offertur, ferrum, ossa, lintea, vitrum, lapides, sine delectu, deglutiat. Quod ex Plinio, Aeliano, Damire, Alkazuinio, Leone Africano, Marmolio et aliis probare sit facillimum.

So much for the Greek interpreters of the word bath-jaana. I now turn to the Chaldeans, Syrians, and Arabs, who all follow the same view. For the three Chaldee versions, Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and the Jerusalem Targum, have here נעמא naama, or נעמיתא naamitha. The Syriac also has ܢܥܡܐ [נעמא] naama. And the Arabic versions, both printed and manuscript, have نعام [naʿām] naam, or نعامة [naʿāma] naama. That these words signify the ostrich is as certain as anything can be. This the Arab lexicographers, historians, geographers, physicians and philosophers teach me with one consent. But above all Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt], who in their chapters On the naam, or naama, describe the ostrich far more accurately than the Greek and Roman writers. And Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], who under naama reproduces word for word what Galen has on the ostrich in book 3 On Foods. Indeed, the same writers also explain the reason for the name. The ostrich and the camel have much in common. Accordingly the foot of each is called خف [khuff] choph, and منسم [mansim] mensim: and the female of each قلوص [qalūṣ] kalus. Thus نعام [naʿām] naam, and نعم [naʿam] naam are close to each other: the former signifies the ostrich, the latter the camel. Hence II, 819 it is that ظربان [ẓarbān] dorban, that is, the species of weasel which barbarous writers call putorium [polecat] from its stench, is said by the Arabs to be مفرق النعم [mufarriq al-naʿam] the disperser of the naam, because with its stench it scatters and disperses the herds of camels. And in the plural انعام [anʿām] anam are camels. As in these words of the Muslim theologians in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الله جعل لكم الانعام لتركبوا [Allāh jaʿala lakum al-anʿām li-tarkabū] God has made for you the Alanama (camels), that you might ride on them. Therefore the ostrich too is called نعام [naʿām] naam, because it resembles the camel. Similar is what Algiahid observes, that the ostrich is called by the Persians استرموك [ushtarmurgh] istarmuch, as if you were to say camel-bird, because it seems to be an animal compounded of both. Hence Alkazuinius: نعامة حيوان مركب من حلقة الطير والجمل اخذ من البعير العنق والمنسم ومن الطير المنقار والجناح والريش [naʿāma ḥayawān murakkab min ḥalqat al-ṭayr wa-l-jamal akhadha min al-baʿīr al-ʿunuq wa-l-mansim wa-min al-ṭayr al-minqār wa-l-janāḥ wa-l-rīsh] The ostrich is an animal composed of the nature of the camel and of the bird: from the camel it took its neck and feet; from the bird its beak, wings and feathers. So, as in Greek and Latin, so too in Persian, in Chaldee, in Syriac and in Arabic, the name of this bird was given by ἡ μεμιγμένη φύσις τῷ χερσαίῳ ζώῳ [hē memigmenē physis tō chersaiō zōō]. And when the more recent lexicographers of the Chaldee language interpret נעמא naama, or נעמיתא naamitha, as owl or eagle owl, they commit a grave error, into which they would never have fallen had they possessed even the slightest col. 224 knowledge of the Arabic language. Though they need not have gone so far. For it is clear from the Talmud as well that נעמית naamith is the ostrich. Thus in the tractate Kelim, or On Vessels, ch. 17, §. 14, there is listed among vessels ביצת נעמית המצופה, a naamith egg overlaid (that is, with gold or silver). It is certain that the naamith egg is the egg of the ostrich, from which vessels are made, a very well-known fact. Pliny, book 10, chapter 1: Their eggs (of ostriches) are, because of their size, regarded by some as vessels. And of the same Lucian in The Dipsads: Σκεύεσι χρῶνται κενώσαντες, καὶ ἐκπώματα ποιοῦνται ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν· [Skeuesi chrōntai kenōsantes, kai ekpōmata poiountai ep᾽ autōn] When emptied they use them as vessels, and cups are made of them. Again in the tractate On the Sabbath, ch. 18, fol. 128a: מטלטלין שברי זכוכית מפני שמאכל לנעמיות, They may carry about (lawfully on the Sabbath day) fragments of glass, because they are food for ostriches. That is certainly how the word לנעמיות should be translated, not owls or eagle owls, which nobody bothers to keep at home; to say nothing of their being carnivorous birds, whereas the ostrich swallows without discrimination whatever is offered it: iron, bones, linen, glass, stones. This would be very easy to prove from Pliny, Aelian, Damir, Alkazuinius, Leo Africanus, Marmol and others. ¶

Now I shall add something about the Hebrews, for the most learned of whom יענה jaana is the ostrich. That it is so taken is clear from Maimonides [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] in the treatise On Forbidden Foods, at the beginning of chapter 3. And from the Glossators of the Talmud, who explain נעמיות naamijoth, that is, ostriches, II, 820 by בנות יענה. So also Abraham Peritsol and Chazkuni on Leviticus explain it. And David de Pomis [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary] in his Lexicon says so most plainly, as if from Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra]: יענה לפר׳ אברהם אע״ איוב לט״ הוא עוף אשר ישים ביצותיו בארץ ונקרא סטרו״צו בלעז, jaana, according to R. Abraham Aben Ezra on Iob. XXXIX., is a bird which lays its eggs on the ground, and in the vernacular is called struzzo, that is, in Italian. And the Porta coeli, fol. 22d: ומין אחד מן בח״ שהוא אמצעי בין חיה ועוף והוא בת־יענה שאינו מעופף ואינו הולכת אך יש לה חרודים תחת כנפיה ועוקצתם אותה והוא מדלגת ומאכלה הברזל ואומרים עליה כי בראיה תאסיף ביציה וכתב אבן רשד כי בהלעיטה את הזהב הוא נתך בגופה מחממה יותר מן האש בסגולה שלהם, There is one species of animal intermediate between quadrupeds and birds, namely bath-jaana, which neither flies nor walks, but has spikes under its wings, by which it is pricked, and it leaps. It also feeds on iron, and is said to hatch its eggs by looking at them. Averroës writes that it melts swallowed gold in its body by its heat better than fire does, and this by a certain innate power. Repeating this last point at fol. 23b, the same work has הנעמיות in place of daughters of jaana. Thus for him בת־יענה, daughter of jaana, and נעמית naamith are synonyms, and both mean the ostrich. Nor do we find any of the Hebrews who interprets it otherwise. For Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and others, who hold that יענה jaana is a θρηνητικὴν [thrēnētikēn] bird, nevertheless do not deny that it is the ostrich. ¶

Eodem facit, quod Iudaeis semper id persuasum fuit, struthionis esum lege Dei sibi fuisse prohibitum, quod Graecae, et Chaldaicae legis versiones abunde docent, et Iudaeorum perpetua praxis. Itaque cum in Lampridio narratur Heliogabalus struthiones et camelos in coenis exhibuisse aliquoties, dicens praeceptum Iudaeis, ut ederent, in postremis verbis latet omnino insigne aliquod mendum, cum ea sint oppido falsa. Ubi enim Iudaeis praecepit Deus, ut vel camelos, vel struthiones ederent? Quin utrumque diserte prohibet. Et de camelo res certa. De struthione etiam apud veteres Iudaeos, quorum ne unus quidem per בת יענה bath jaana, aliud quidquam intellexit, quam struthionem. Itaque nihil agunt, qui, pro struthiones et camelos, ex Palatino codice, junctis vocibus, struthio-camelos, id est, struthiones legunt. Neque enim minus ex- col. 225 pressa est struthionum quam camelorum prohibitio. Demus tamen, struthiones in lege non prohiberi, an sequetur, fuisse praeceptum Iudaeis, ut ederent? Quis nescit, quantum haec differant, aliquid concedere tanquam liberum, et idem praecipere tanquam necessarium? Illa certe tantum differunt, quantum differunt libertas ad utrumque ἀδιάφορος, et necessitas ad unum determinata. Verum ut hoc quoque concedamus, struthionum esum non concessum modo, sed et praeceptum fuisse Iudaeis, quae duo sunt falsissima, quid hoc ad Heliogabalum? An hominem ethnicum, impium, et prorsus atheum, credibile est, id in animo habuisse, ut Iudaicis legibus obsequeretur, et ad illarum praescripta II, 821 mores suos componeret? Haec cum sint absurda prorsus, videant eruditiores, an Lampridii verba sic legi queant, Struthiones, et camelos exhibuit in coenis aliquoties, esse dicens praeceptum sui Dei, ut ederent. Camelos et struthiones amicis cum apponebat, quia delicatulis illis tam viles carnes parum sapiebant, fingebat, Dei sui praeceptum esse, ut eas ederent, id est, ad religionem, et cultum Dei sui id pertinere, Heliogabali scilicet, cuius erat sacerdos. Idem Lampridius supra, Fuit autem Heliogabali, vel Iovis, vel Solis Sacerdos. Et paulo post, Prius dictus est Varius, post Heliogabalus a sacerdotio Dei Heliogabali, cui templum Romae in eo loco constituit, in quo prius aedes Horci fuit, quem e Syria secum advexit. Item, Heliogabalum in Palatino monte juxta aedes Imperatorias consecravit, eique templum fecit, studens et Matris typum, et Vestae ignem, et Palladium, et ancilia, et omnia Romanis veneranda in illud transferre templum, et id agens, ne quis Romae Deus, nisi Heliogabalus, coleretur. Dicebat praeterea Iudaeorum et Samaritanorum religiones, et Christianam devotionem illuc transferendam, ut omnium culturarum secretum Heliogabali sacerdotium teneret. Et infra, Signum, quod Palladium esse credebat, abstulit, et auro fictum in sui Dei templo locavit. Et rursus, Ablatumque sanctum nomen in penetrale Dei sui transtulit. Item, Omnes sane Deos sui Dei ministros esse ajebat. Ita hic, Esse dicens praeceptum Dei sui, ut ederent. Ex vocibus Dei sui, vel sui Dei, κατ᾽ ἀναγραμματισμὸν Iudaeis fecisse videtur scribarum imperitia. Et post aliquoties vox esse praetermissa propter τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον. Utut sit, cum in eandem versionem vocis יענה jaana, conveniant Graeci, Latini, Chaldaei, Syri, Arabes, atque Hebraei interpretes, ab his omnibus non video tuto posse recedi, nisi magnae causae subsint, quales hic nullas video. Quidni enim בת יענה filia jaana putetur esse struthio? Excutiantur scripturae loca, quibus de ea fit mentio: quid in iis reperietur, quod struthioni non conveniat?

To the same effect is the fact that the Jews were always convinced that the eating of the ostrich had been forbidden them by the law of God, as the Greek and Chaldee versions of the law amply show, together with the constant practice of the Jews. Therefore, when Lampridius relates that Heliogabalus several times served ostriches and camels at his banquets, saying it was commanded to the Jews that they should eat them, some notable corruption certainly lurks in the last words, since they are utterly false. For where did God command the Jews to eat either camels or ostriches? On the contrary, he expressly forbids both. Concerning the camel the matter is certain. Concerning the ostrich it is also certain among the ancient Jews, not one of whom understood by בת יענה bath jaana anything other than the ostrich. Therefore those who, instead of struthiones et camelos [ostriches and camels], read from the Palatine codex, with the words joined, struthio-camelos, that is, ostriches, achieve nothing. For the prohibition of ostriches is no less explicit col. 225 than that of camels. Yet let us grant that ostriches are not forbidden in the law: will it follow that the Jews were commanded to eat them? Who does not know how great the difference is between conceding something as permitted and commanding the same as necessary? These certainly differ as much as freedom ἀδιάφορος [adiaphoros] toward either alternative differs from necessity determined to one. But even if we also concede that the eating of ostriches was not only permitted but even commanded to the Jews, both of which are utterly false, what has this to do with Heliogabalus? Is it credible that a heathen, impious and altogether godless man had it in mind to obey the Jewish laws and to shape his conduct II, 821 by their prescriptions? Since these things are wholly absurd, let more learned men consider whether the words of Lampridius may be read thus: He several times served ostriches and camels at his banquets, saying it was a command of his God that they should eat them. When he set camels and ostriches before his friends, since such cheap meat had little appeal to those dainty men, he pretended that it was a command of his God that they should eat them, that is, that this belonged to the religion and worship of his God, namely Heliogabalus, whose priest he was. The same Lampridius says above: Now he was a priest of Heliogabalus, that is, of Jupiter or of the Sun. And a little later: He was first called Varius, afterwards Heliogabalus, from his priesthood of the god Heliogabalus, whom he brought with him from Syria and for whom he established a temple at Rome on the spot where formerly the shrine of Orcus had stood. Likewise: He consecrated Heliogabalus on the Palatine hill beside the imperial palace and built him a temple, striving to transfer into that temple the image of the Mother, the fire of Vesta, the Palladium, the shields, and everything held sacred by the Romans, and aiming that no god but Heliogabalus should be worshipped at Rome. He said moreover that the religions of the Jews and Samaritans and the Christian devotion must be transferred there, so that the priesthood of Heliogabalus might hold the secret of every cult. And below: He carried off the image which he believed to be the Palladium, and, having had it cast in gold, placed it in the temple of his God. And again: And the sacred name, carried off, he transferred to the inner sanctuary of his God. Likewise: He declared that all the gods were ministers of his God. So here: Saying it was a command of his God that they should eat them. From the words Dei sui [of his God], or sui Dei, the ignorance of scribes seems to have made, κατ᾽ ἀναγραμματισμὸν [kat᾽ anagrammatismon], Iudaeis [to the Jews]. And after aliquoties [several times] the word esse was omitted because of τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον [to homoioptōton]. However that may be, since the Greek, Latin, Chaldee, Syriac, Arabic and Hebrew interpreters agree in the same rendering of the word יענה jaana, I do not see that one can safely depart from all of them unless there are weighty reasons, and I see none such here. For why should not בת יענה filia jaana [daughter of jaana] be thought to be the ostrich? Let the passages of Scripture in which it is mentioned be examined: what will be found in them that does not suit the ostrich? ¶

Thus Mic 1:8: “I will make a wailing like the dragons, and a mourning like the daughters of jaana.” And Job 30:28–29: “I stood up in the congregation and cried out. I was a brother of dragons and a companion of the sons of jaana.” That is, even in the midst of an assembly of men I could not restrain myself from wailing with as strained a voice as the dragons and the daughters of jaana: the former take their name from mourning, the latter from crying out R4. I reply: however diligently the Greek writers wrote on natural history, many things nevertheless escaped them even regarding the animals born in Greece. What wonder, then, if the same happens with unknown and foreign ones? Certainly, if this argument has any force, it will also follow from col. 228 it that the ostrich is entirely ἄφωνον [aphōnon], since the Greeks wrote nothing whatever about its voice. Yet that it does have a voice is taught both by reason itself and by the records of the Arabs, to whom an Arabian bird ought to be better known than to the Greeks. II, 825 In an Arabic book entitled الكفاية [al-Kifāya], as if one should say Sufficiency: يقال عار الظليم اذا صاح والزمار صياح الانثي [yuqāl ʿāra al-ẓalīm idhā ṣāḥa wa-l-zimār ṣiyāḥ al-unthā] The word arra is used of the male ostrich when it cries out, and zimar is the voice of the female. And the cry itself is called عرار [ʿirār] irar; but زمرت النعام [zamarat al-naʿām] zamarath, that is, the female ostrich cried out. And elsewhere in the same Alcamus: رغا او رغي البعير والضبع والنعام صوت فصاح [raghā aw raghiya al-baʿīr wa-l-ḍabʿ wa-l-naʿām ṣawwata fa-ṣāḥa] Ragha or raghia, of the camel, the hyena and the ostrich: it cried out, it wailed. And again: رغا الصبي بكا اشد البكا [raghā al-ṣabī bakā ashadd al-bukā] Ragha, of a boy, that is, he wept bitterly. Moreover نقع [naqʿ] neke is the voice of the ostrich, and نقنق [naqnaqa] naknaka means the female ostrich clucked, when she laid eggs, or in flight and fear. And lest you think these things rest on their testimony alone, here are the words of Johannes de Laet in his description of America, book 15, chapter 7: In the interior of Brazil many ostriches are found, which the natives call in their language janducocu, etc. They are tall, and cry so loudly that they are heard half a league away R5. Fuller raises another objection: that there was no reason why eating the ostrich should be forbidden in the Law, since the bird was either wholly unknown to the Jews or at least very rare among them, being one that Pliny calls an African or Ethiopian bird, as far removed as possible from Judaea. But this weapon is empty. For even if we granted that the ostrich is born in Ethiopia alone, it would not follow that it was unknown to the Jews, especially in the age of Moses, when they had long been detained in neighbouring Egypt. For in that part of Ethiopia which is nearest to Egypt, Apollonius is said in Philostratus to have found ostriches. But indeed, who does not know that in Arabia too, which borders on Judaea and Syria, there is a great abundance of ostriches? Diodorus teaches this in express words in book 2, in his description of Arabia: Θηρίων τε πλῆθος ἀλκίμων ὁ προσορίζουσα τῇ Συρίᾳ τρέφει [Thēriōn te plēthos alkimōn ho prosorizousa tē Syria trephei]. The part of Arabia which borders on Syria nourishes an abundance of wild beasts of outstanding strength. In this list, after he has II, 826 placed lions, leopards and tigers, he says: Φέρει δὲ καὶ ζῶα διφυῆ, καὶ μεμιγμένα ταῖς ἰδέαις, ὧν αἱ μὲν ὀνομαζόμεναι στρουθοκάμηλοι περιειλήφασι τοῖς τύποις μίγματα χηνῶν, καὶ καμήλων [Pherei de kai zōa diphyē, kai memigmena tais ideais, hōn hai men onomazomenai strouthokamēloi perieilēphasi tois typois migmata chēnōn, kai kamēlōn]; It also produces animals of ambiguous nature and twofold form, of which those called ostriches have the appearance of geese and camels mixed together. Hence Xenophon, travelling through Arabia with Cyrus, met near the Euphrates οὐκ ὀλίγοι στρουθοὶ αἱ μεγάλαι [ouk oligoi strouthoi hai megalai], not a few ostriches, as he himself relates in book 1 of the Anabasis. And in Heraclides στρουθοὶ οἱ Ἀράβιοι [strouthoi hoi Arabioi], Arabian ostriches, are counted among the birds that were served to the king of the Persians, as has already been observed above d). Moreover we have learned from Pollux that the Arabs used ostrich skins in place of breastplates and shields. Accordingly, in the book of the Notitiae the ostrich is among the insignia of Arabia R6. Therefore, if Fuller denies that Ethiopian ostriches were known to the Jews, col. 229 let him at least admit that the Arabian ones could not have been hidden from them. Above all it is absurd that Fuller will have יענה jaana be the strix [screech owl], an animal, forsooth, much better known to the Jews than the ostrich, indeed plainly fabulous, as is clear even from Pliny, book 11, chapter 39. This is why Hesychius, as emended by us, places it ἐν τοῖς τερατείοις [en tois terateiois], among the prodigious animals, such as the Chimaera, the Centaurs, etc. ¶

In Aben Ezra on this passage some answer, שהוא מין שלא ימצאו זכרים כמו הארנבת, that it is a species of animal which has no males, just as the hare has none. Rabbi Moses, son of Maimon, in the book On Forbidden Foods, right at the beginning of chapter 3, understands by the daughter of the ostrich its egg. II, 827 So the Hebrews, but most absurdly. To prove that Firmus was a great glutton, and above all swallowed a great deal of meat, Vopiscus reports that he ate an ostrich in a day. Setting aside these trifles, then, I observe that the Chaldeans sometimes prefix the word son or daughter to the names of birds without any regard to age. Thus in Lev 9:13. בר גזא bar geza, or son of geza, is the griffon in Jonathan and in the Jerusalem translator: בר גזא bar geza, that is, son of strength. For elsewhere the griffon is called עזא from strength, and we have shown elsewhere that the letter Ajin, or Gain, is often changed into Gimel. And in Deut 14:13. בת כנפא, daughter of the wing, is the vulture in Onkelos. That the white hawk is called by the Talmudists בר חוריא bar churja, son of whiteness, we show in the last chapter of this book. And in the Talmudic Tractate Bechoroth ch. 9, fol. 57b, בר יוכני bar jucne, or son of jucne, is a fabulous bird of prodigious size (like the رخ [rukh] ruch of the Arabs), which is so called from its lofty nest. For among the Chaldeans יוכני jucne is the same II, 828 as وكنة [wakna] vvacna among the Arabs, by which they denote not just any nest, but a nest on a mountain or rock. Accordingly we read there: ונפלה ביצת בר יוכני, And the egg of the son of jucne fell; that is, from the summit of a mountain or rock. The same manner of speaking is also in use among the Arabs, who call the duck ابن الما [ibn al-mā] son of water; and the raven ابن داية [ibn dāya] son of the withers, because it gladly settles on the necks of beasts of burden that the yoke or loads have chafed and ulcerated, in order to gnaw away the dead flesh. And the thos [jackal] ابن اوي [ibn āwā] son of avvi, or of barking, because in Syria and Cilicia the fields resound with its barking at night. And the beech marten (in French fouine) ابن مقرض [ibn miqraḍ] son of gnawing; and the common weasel ابن عرس [ibn ʿirs] son of the wedding, from its elegance and neat fur; just as in Italian it is called donnola, as if dominula [little lady], and in French belette, as if you said bellula [pretty little one], and in vulgar Greek νυμφίτζα [nymphitza] or νυφίτζα [nyphitza]. Since this word is plainly barbarous, I cannot guess how it crept into Diodorus, in whom you may read, book 1, p. 52 of the edition of Henri Estienne, that the Egyptians greatly venerate certain animals not only living but also dead, οἷον τοὺς τε αἰλούρους, καὶ τοὺς ἰχνεύμονας, καὶ νυφίτζας, καὶ κύνας [hoion tous te ailourous, kai tous ichneumonas, kai nyphitzas, kai kynas], such as cats, and ichneumons, and weasels, and dogs. The flea, too, is in Arabic ابن طامر [ibn ṭāmir] the leaping son. And the sea creatures of female form which they say are found in the Adriatic Sea near Greece they call بنات الروم [banāt al-Rūm] daughters of the Greeks: as they call بنات وردان [banāt wardān] daughters of vardan those little creatures which Dioscorides names κουβαρίδας [koubaridas], Marcellus Empiricus cutiones, and we cloportes [woodlice]; although others understand σίλφας [silphas], or blattae [cockroaches]. But I think the former is truer, and that vardan was formed from the Greek word κουβάριδες [koubarides], with the first syllable cut off. ¶

Iam, si roges, cur filia dicatur, non filius, et an ad sexum id pertineat, et quorsum Deus foeminae potius, quam maris struthionis esum in Lege prohibuerit; responsio non erit ita obvia: nam filii nomen etiam ad foeminam pertinet, ut patuit in iis, quae nugantur Talmudici de ovo בר יוכני bar jucne, sive filii jucne. At mas in ave, aut quadrupede nunquam foemina dicitur. Neque filiae vocabulum complecti videas totam speciem, nisi in solo vulture, quem Chaldaei בת כנפא, filiam alae vocant certa de causa. Nempe quia fabulantur, in illo genere nullos esse mares. Unde et Graece in Horo dicitur ἡ γὺψ Foeminino genere, ut pluribus docebimus in capite De רחם racham. Sed de struthione simile quidquam nemo scripsit, praeter paucos Hebraeos recentioris aevi. Tamen certum est, quod dicturus sum, ex multis struthionis nominibus, apud Arabes receptis, duo esse maxime in usu, nempe ظليم dtalim, et نعام naam: quorum illud marem, hoc proprie foeminam sonat. Sed Foemininum نعام naam multo esse receptius, et in Historicorum et Geographorum libris vix aliud occurrere. Unde est, quod Syri et Chaldaei struthionem non vocant טלים talim, sed נעמא naama. Nempe in illo genere foemina mari praefertur propter ova, quae sunt magni pretii, neque solum ad esum, sed et ad usum exquiruntur, cum ex illis vasa fieri, eaque eximia, jam supra II, 830 docuerimus. Praeterea adulti mares in manus hominum raro incidunt; quia sunt velocissimi. At foeminae saepius et facilius capiuntur, sive sint ovis graves, aut super pullis incubent. Et nidi quoque apparent eminus, quia in planis locis multa arena aggesta exstruuntur. Proinde ut foemina notior, ita etiam eius nomen multo est usitatius, et fere solum in ore vulgi. Itaque Moses usum foeminae nominatim prohibuit. Ne quis tamen, sola foemina prohibita, concessum esse maris esum sibi fingeret, rem ignotam hactenus jam probare contendam, nempe Mosem, postquam a foemina, tanquam notiore, fecit initium, marem etiam prohibuisse, eumque significari voce תחמס thachmas, quae mox sequitur: adeoque haec verba Mosis, Non comedetis, etc. את בת היענה ואת התחמס, filiam jaana, et thachmas, ita esse vertenda, Non comedetis struthionem foeminam, neque struthionem marem. Eo pertinet caput sequens, quo probatur, voce, hactenus ignota, תחמס thachmas, marem struthionem significari.

These examples make plain what we had undertaken to prove: that certain animals are called sons and daughters without regard to age, for they are so called at any age. And the term son or daughter is then taken otherwise than in common use. For the griffin is not called son of strength because it was born of strength, but because it is strong in strength; as ἡ γὺψ [hē gyps] is strong in flight, whence it is called daughter of the wing; and the duck son of the water, because it delights in waters; and the thos son of avvi, or of barking, because it is an animal much given to barking. For the same reason col. 231 the ostrich is called בת יענה, daughter of jaana, or of crying, not from II, 829 age but from its cry, because it is a noisy animal R7. But as the griffin is called not only בר גזא, son of geza, but also, by abbreviation, גזא geza or גז gez, so the ostrich is sometimes called יענה jaana for בת יענה, daughter of jaana; whence the plural יענים jeenim, Lam 4:3. Now, if you ask why it is called daughter and not son, whether this pertains to sex, and to what purpose God in the Law forbade the eating of the female rather than the male ostrich, the answer will not be so obvious. For the term son also applies to the female, as was evident in the trifles the Talmudists tell about the egg of the בר יוכני bar jucne, or son of jucne. But the male in a bird or quadruped is never called female. Nor will you see the word daughter embrace a whole species, except in the vulture alone, which the Chaldeans call בת כנפא, daughter of the wing, for a definite reason: namely, because they tell the fable that in that kind there are no males. Hence also in Greek, in Horapollo, ἡ γὺψ [hē gyps] is used in the feminine gender, as we shall show more fully in the chapter On רחם racham. But no one has written anything similar about the ostrich, except a few Hebrews of more recent times. Yet what I am about to say is certain: of the many names of the ostrich accepted among the Arabs, two are most in use, namely ظليم [ẓalīm] dtalim and نعام [naʿām] naam, of which the former properly denotes the male, the latter the female. But the feminine نعام [naʿām] naam is far more accepted, and in the books of the historians and geographers scarcely any other occurs. Hence the Syrians and Chaldeans call the ostrich not טלים talim but נעמא naama. In that kind, namely, the female is preferred to the male because of the eggs, which are of great value and are sought not only for eating but also for use, since vessels, and excellent ones, are made from them, as we have already II, 830 shown above. Moreover, adult males rarely fall into human hands, because they are extremely swift. But the females are caught more often and more easily, whether they are heavy with eggs or brooding over their chicks. And their nests are visible from afar, because they are built in open places with much sand heaped up. Accordingly, as the female is better known, so her name too is far more common, and almost the only one in the mouth of the people. Therefore Moses expressly forbade the use of the female. Yet lest anyone, since only the female was forbidden, should imagine that eating the male was permitted to him, I shall now endeavour to prove something hitherto unknown: namely, that Moses, after beginning with the female as the better known, forbade the male as well, and that he is signified by the word תחמס thachmas, which follows immediately. And so these words of Moses, You shall not eat, etc., את בת היענה ואת התחמס, the daughter of jaana and the thachmas, are to be translated thus: You shall not eat the female ostrich, nor the male ostrich. To this pertains the following chapter, in which it is proved that the word תחמס thachmas, hitherto unknown, signifies the male ostrich. ¶
