III, 56 Chapter XXVI. col. 303 ¶

Ex Arabica lingua probatur, רחם racham esse Vulturis speciem.

It is proved from the Arabic language that רחם racham is a species of vulture. ¶

Iam in ea, quam descripsimus, interpretum haesitatione Arabes consulere tutissimum esse quis neget, cum avis رخم racham, vel رخمة rachama, apud eos hodieque sit notissima, et describatur pluribus, quod sequentia palam facient? Damir, الرخمة طاير ابقع يشبه النسر في الخلقة Rachama, avis varii coloris, ad aquilae naturam accedens. Et paullo post, وفي طبع هذا الطاير انه لا يرضي من الجبال الا من بالوحش منها ولا من الاماكن الا بالاسحقها والابعدها من اماكن اعداية ولا من الهضاب الا بصخورها ويقال لها لانوق E natura hujus avis est, quod non amat montes, nisi maxime so- litarios; neque loca alia, nisi maxime dissita, et ab habitatis remotissima; et in lo- cis petrosis nonnisi rupes inaccessas. Alio nomine vocatur anuk. Et infra, وتبيض بيضة واحدة وربما اتامت وهي من ليام الطير وهن ثلاث الغراب والبوم والرخم والعرب تضرب المثل في الامتناع ببيضها فيقولون اعز من بيض الانوق وابعد من بيض الانوق فانه لا يكاد col. 304 يظفر به لان اوكارها في روس الجبال والاماكن الصعبة كما تقدم قال الشاعر ۞

Now, given the hesitation of the interpreters that we have described, who would deny that it is safest to consult the Arabs? For the bird رخم [rakham] racham, or رخمة [rakhama] rachama, is very well known among them even today, and it is described by many, as what follows will make plain. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: الرخمة طاير ابقع يشبه النسر في الخلقة [al-rakhama ṭāyir abqaʿ yushbih al-nasr fī al-khilqa] Rachama, a bird of variegated colour, approaching the nature of the eagle. And a little later: وفي طبع هذا الطاير انه لا يرضي من الجبال الا من بالوحش منها ولا من الاماكن الا بالاسحقها والابعدها من اماكن اعداية ولا من الهضاب الا بصخورها ويقال لها لانوق [wa-fī ṭabʿ hādhā al-ṭāyir annahu lā yarḍī min al-jibāl illā min bi-l-waḥsh minhā wa-lā min al-amākin illā bi-l-asḥaqihā wa-l-abʿadihā min amākin aʿdāyih wa-lā min al-hiḍāb illā bi-ṣukhūrihā wa-yuqāl lahā lānūq] It is the nature of this bird that it does not like mountains unless they are extremely solitary; nor other places, unless they are very far apart and most remote from inhabited places; and among rocky places only inaccessible crags. It is called by another name anuk. And below: وتبيض بيضة واحدة وربما اتامت وهي من ليام الطير وهن ثلاث الغراب والبوم والرخم والعرب تضرب المثل في الامتناع ببيضها فيقولون اعز من بيض الانوق وابعد من بيض الانوق فانه لا يكاد col. 304 يظفر به لان اوكارها في روس الجبال والاماكن الصعبة كما تقدم قال الشاعر ۞ [wa-tabīḍ bayḍa wāḥida wa-rubbamā atāmat wa-hiya min liyām al-ṭayr wa-hunna thalāth al-ghurāb wa-l-būm wa-l-rakham wa-l-ʿarab taḍrib al-mathal fī al-imtināʿ bi-bayḍihā fa-yaqūlūn aʿazz min bayḍ al-anūq wa-abʿad min bayḍ al-anūq fa-innahu lā yakād yuẓfar bihi li-anna awkārahā fī rūs al-jibāl wa-l-amākin al-ṣaʿba kamā taqaddam qāla al-shāʿir] ¶

وكنت اذا استودعت سر ۞ اكتمت كبيض انوق لا ينال لها وكر ۞

وكنت اذا استودعت سر ۞ اكتمت كبيض انوق لا ينال لها وكر ۞ [wa-kuntu idhā istawdaʿtu sirr aktamtu ka-bayḍ anūq lā yunāl lahā wakr] ¶

Et ovum parit unicum, sed et quandoque gemina edit. Est ex avibus liam (quasi ignobiles dixeris). Eae autem tres sunt, corvus, noctua et racham. Proverbium est apud Arabes, quo significatur, quam ova ejus sint inaccessa, „Rarius ovo anuk“, aut „Remotius ovo anuk“: quod vix quisquam assequitur, quia nidificat in vertice montium et in praeruptis locis, ut jam ante dictum est. Unde est, quod poëta dixit, „Et fui, cum seorsim in abditum locum evocatus sum, latens ut ovum „anuk, cujus nidus est inaccessus“. Alkazuinius, رخمه طاير النسر في خلقته يختار لبيضه اطراف الجبال III, 57 الشاهقة ليصعب الوصول الي بيضه يقال اعز من بيض الانوق فاذا جاء اوان بيضها ذهبت الي الهند واتت حجر يقال له ابوطاقيون وهو حجر مدور مثل خرزة اذا حركت تعقعق في جوفه حجر اخر ويترك ذلك الحجر تحتها فتبيض من غير وجع وتطير خلف العساكر لتاكل من جيف Rachama est avis aquila (id est, aquilini generis). E natura ejus est, ut eligat ovis suis summorum montium vertices, ut ad ea difficilior sit accessus. Unde est, quod dicitur, „Rarius ovo anuk (id est, rachamae)“. Quo tempore ova positura est, in Indiam se conferens inde affert lapidem, quem abutacium vocant (imo εὐτόκιον). Is lapis est margaritae instar rotundus. Cum movetur, sonat in ventre ejus lapis alius. Eo lapide sibi supposito, ova ponit sine dolore. Castra sequitur volatu suo, ut vescatur occisorum cadaveribus. Alcamus الانوق العقاب الرخمة وطاير اسود له كالعرف او اسود اصلع الراس اصفر المنقار وهو اعز من بيض الانوق لانها تحرزها فلا يكاد يظفر به لان اوكارها في التلل الصعبة قيل col. 305 فاخلاقها عشر خصال تحصن بيضها وتحمي فرجها وتالف ولدها ولا تمكن من نفسها غير زوجها وتقطع في اول القواطع وترجع في اول الرواجع وتطير في التحسير ولا تغتر بالشكير ولا ترب بالوكور ولا تسقط علي الجفر بالشكير Anuk, aquilae genus, quae et rachama dicitur. Avis nigra, quae habet tanquam jubam. Alii, avis nigra, calvi capitis, flavi rostri, de qua proverbium, „Rarius est ovo anuk“; quia ovo ita prospicit, ut vix possit invadi. Nidificat enim in montibus inaccessis. Huic propria decem tribuuntur. 1. Munit ova sua. 2. Pullos suos tuetur. 3. Proli suae semper adhaeret. 4. Nihil per se constituit absque compare suo. 5. Migrat cum primis migrantibus. 6. Redit cum primis redeuntibus. 7. Volat in pennarum defluvio. 8. Neque propter pennarum paucitatem periclitatur. 9. Neque manet in nido. 10. Neque cadit in coëundi impotentiam: quomodo reliquae aves, cum pennae defluxerunt. E quibus decem propriis prima quatuor etiam in Damire leguntur verbis fere totidem. Andreas Bellunensis, Alrachame est avis magna sicut milvus, plumis corporis albis, et pennis alarum nigris. Edit carnes putridas, et similia. In III, 58 Syria abundat (1). Ad Alcorani Suratam الاخراب Alachrab, quae in Latino interprete est Azoara quadragesima tertia, ubi de Mose agitur, versus finem, dicit Commentator, Abulabas Cordubensis, لم يعرف موضع قبره الا الرخم فلذلك جعله الله اصم ابكم Nemo novit locum sepulchri ejus praeter avem racham, ac proinde illam fecit Deus surdam et mutam. Id omni ex parte fabulosum est, ut pleraque hujusmodi nugatorum. Nam, ut caetera taceam, haec avis est maxime vocalis, ut aquilinum et vulturinum omne genus. Proinde, ut Arabes saepe avibus, veluti nos campanis, quidvis affingunt, Zamachsari scribit, rachamae clangorem haec fere verba sonare, سبحان ربي الاعلي Sobchan rabbi alali, id est, Laus Domino meo altissimo.

And it lays a single egg, but sometimes it also produces twins. It is one of the birds called liam (as if you were to say ignoble). These are three: the raven, the little owl and the racham. There is a proverb among the Arabs signifying how inaccessible its eggs are: “Rarer than the egg of the anuk”, or “More remote than the egg of the anuk”: for hardly anyone reaches it, because it nests on the summit of mountains and in precipitous places, as was said before. Hence the poet said: “And when I was called apart into a hidden place, I was concealed like the egg of the anuk, whose nest is inaccessible”. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: رخمه طاير النسر في خلقته يختار لبيضه اطراف الجبال III, 57 الشاهقة ليصعب الوصول الي بيضه يقال اعز من بيض الانوق فاذا جاء اوان بيضها ذهبت الي الهند واتت حجر يقال له ابوطاقيون وهو حجر مدور مثل خرزة اذا حركت تعقعق في جوفه حجر اخر ويترك ذلك الحجر تحتها فتبيض من غير وجع وتطير خلف العساكر لتاكل من جيف [rakhama ṭāyir al-nasr fī khilqatih yakhtār li-bayḍih aṭrāf al-jibāl al-shāhiqa li-yaṣʿub al-wuṣūl ilā bayḍih yuqāl aʿazz min bayḍ al-anūq fa-idhā jāʾa awān bayḍihā dhahabat ilā al-hind wa-atat ḥajar yuqāl lahu abūṭāqiyūn wa-huwa ḥajar mudawwar mithl kharaza idhā ḥurrikat taʿaqʿaqa fī jawfih ḥajar ākhar wa-yutrak dhālika al-ḥajar taḥtahā fa-tabīḍ min ghayr wajaʿ wa-taṭīr khalf al-ʿasākir li-taʾkul min jiyaf] Rachama is an eagle bird (that is, of the aquiline kind). It is its nature to choose the summits of the highest mountains for its eggs, so that access to them may be more difficult. Hence the saying, “Rarer than the egg of the anuk” (that is, of the rachama). When it is about to lay its eggs, it goes to India and brings from there a stone which they call abutacium (rather, εὐτόκιον [eutokion]). This stone is round like a pearl. When it is moved, another stone sounds inside it. With this stone placed under itself, it lays its eggs without pain. It follows armies in its flight, to feed on the corpses of the slain. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الانوق العقاب الرخمة وطاير اسود له كالعرف او اسود اصلع الراس اصفر المنقار وهو اعز من بيض الانوق لانها تحرزها فلا يكاد يظفر به لان اوكارها في التلل الصعبة قيل col. 305 فاخلاقها عشر خصال تحصن بيضها وتحمي فرجها وتالف ولدها ولا تمكن من نفسها غير زوجها وتقطع في اول القواطع وترجع في اول الرواجع وتطير في التحسير ولا تغتر بالشكير ولا ترب بالوكور ولا تسقط علي الجفر بالشكير [al-anūq al-ʿuqāb al-rakhama wa-ṭāyir aswad lahu ka-l-ʿurf aw aswad aṣlaʿ al-raʾs aṣfar al-minqār wa-huwa aʿazz min bayḍ al-anūq li-annahā taḥruzuhā fa-lā yakād yuẓfar bihi li-anna awkārahā fī al-tilal al-ṣaʿba qīla fa-akhlāquhā ʿashar khiṣāl tuḥaṣṣin bayḍahā wa-taḥmī farkhahā wa-taʾlaf waladahā wa-lā tumakkin min nafsihā ghayr zawjihā wa-taqṭaʿ fī awwal al-qawāṭiʿ wa-tarjiʿ fī awwal al-rawājiʿ wa-taṭīr fī al-taḥsīr wa-lā taghtarr bi-l-shakīr wa-lā tarubb bi-l-wukūr wa-lā tasquṭ ʿalā al-jafr bi-l-shakīr] Anuk, a kind of eagle, which is also called rachama. A black bird, which has something like a mane. Others say: a black bird with a bald head and a yellow beak, about which there is the proverb, “Rarer than the egg of the anuk”; because it guards its egg so well that it can hardly be attacked. For it nests in inaccessible mountains. Ten peculiarities are attributed to it. 1. It protects its eggs. 2. It guards its young. 3. It always stays close to its offspring. 4. It does nothing on its own without its mate. 5. It migrates with the first migrants. 6. It returns with the first to return. 7. It flies during the moulting of its feathers. 8. Nor is it endangered by the scantiness of its feathers. 9. Nor does it remain in the nest. 10. Nor does it fall into an inability to mate: as the other birds do when their feathers have fallen out. Of these ten peculiarities the first four are also found in Damir, in almost the same words. Andreas Bellunensis: Alrachame is a large bird like a kite, with white body plumage and black wing feathers. It eats putrid flesh and the like. In III, 58 Syria it is abundant R1. On the Sura of the Qur'an الاخراب [al-akhrāb] Alachrab, which in the Latin translator is Azoara 43, where Moses is treated, towards the end, the Commentator Abulabas Cordubensis says: لم يعرف موضع قبره الا الرخم فلذلك جعله الله اصم ابكم [lam yaʿrif mawḍiʿ qabrih illā al-rakham fa-li-dhālika jaʿalahu allāh aṣamm abkam] No one knew the place of his tomb except the bird racham, and therefore God made it deaf and dumb. This is entirely fabulous, like most things of such triflers. For, to say nothing of the rest, this bird is extremely vocal, like the whole aquiline and vulturine kind. Accordingly, as the Arabs often attribute any words they like to birds, as we do to bells, Zamachsari writes that the cry of the rachama sounds roughly like these words: سبحان ربي الاعلي [subḥān rabbī al-aʿlī] Sobchan rabbi alali, that is, Praise to my Lord most high. ¶

Iam si liberet addere, quidquid habent Alkazuinius, Ibnolbatrik, Avicenna, Serapion, Sariph, Abenbitar, Alcamus et Arabum alii de hujus avis medicis virtutibus, res in immensum excresceret. Nobis igitur e multis sufficiet addere pauca quaedam, unde constet, quam avem Arabes sic indigetent. 1. Stercus rachamae embryonem excutit. Alkazuinius, ذرقها ان احتملته المراة القت جنينها, Stercus ejus si ferat mulier, embryonem ejicit. Avicenna, قيل ان زبله يسقط الجنين تبخيرا Stercus ejus embryonem suffitu ejicere dicitur. Ecce idem in Dioscoride de stercore vulturis in capite De stercore libro secundo, Γυπὸς ἄφοδος ὑποθυμιαθεῖ-σα ἔμβρυα ἐκτινάσσειν παραδέδοται, Vulturis stercus suffitu foetus pellere dicitur. Et in Samonico capite trigesimo quarto,

Now if I wished to add everything that Alkazuinius, Ibnolbatrik, Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], Serapion [=Serapion, Liber de simplicibus medicinis (the Arabic-Latin pharmacopoeia)], Sariph, Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], Alcamus and other Arabs say about the medicinal virtues of this bird, the matter would grow beyond measure. It will therefore suffice for us to add a few things out of many, from which it may be clear what bird the Arabs call by this name. 1. The dung of the rachama expels the embryo. Alkazuinius: ذرقها ان احتملته المراة القت جنينها [dharquhā in iḥtamalathu al-marʾa alqat janīnahā], If a woman applies its dung, she expels the embryo. Avicenna: قيل ان زبله يسقط الجنين تبخيرا [qīla inna zibluhu yusqiṭu al-janīn tabkhīran], Its dung is said to expel the embryo by fumigation. Behold, the same is found in Dioscorides concerning the dung of the vulture, in the chapter On dung in book 2: Γυπὸς ἄφοδος ὑποθυμιαθεῖσα ἔμβρυα ἐκτινάσσειν παραδέδοται [gypos aphodos hypothymiatheisa embrya ektinassein paradedotai], Vulture's dung, used as a fumigation, is said to drive out the foetus. And in Serenus Sammonicus [=Quintus Serenus Sammonicus, Liber medicinalis], chapter 34: ¶

Quin etiam stercus supponunt vulturis atri, col. 306
Sentiat ut minime partus quam proximus urget.

Moreover, they place beneath the dung of the black vulture, col. 306 so that she may scarcely feel the birth that presses close. ¶

2. Pluma quoque rachamae ad partum prodest. Sariph, وان اخذت ريشه من جناحها اليمن ووضعت بين رجلي المطلغة سهل اولادها Cum sumitur penna ex ala ejus dextera, et ad parturientis pedes ponitur, levat dolores partus. Ita etiam Alcamus in رخم racham. Quod idem de vulturis penna habet Plinius libri vigesimi noni capite quarto, Penna vulturina subjecta pedibus adjuvat parturientes. Et Sextus libri secundi capite secundo, Efficit, ut sine labore partum edant. III, 59 3. Fel rachamae medetur albugini. Alkazuinius, يكتحل بمرارتها وحدها لبياض العين Ex solo vulturis felle fit collyrium ad oculi albuginem. Avicenna, يكتحل بمرارتها لبياض العين بالما البارد Ex felle ejus cum aqua frigida fit collyrium ad oculorum albugines. Vulturino felli vis eadem. Plinius libri vigesimi noni capite sexto, postquam aquilae felle inungi dixit nubeculas, et caligationes, suffusionesque oculorum, Eadem vis et in vulturino felle, etc. Samonicus capite decimo quarto,

2. The feather of the rachama also assists childbirth. Sariph: وان اخذت ريشه من جناحها اليمن ووضعت بين رجلي المطلغة سهل اولادها [wa-in ukhidhat rīsha min janāḥihā al-yumn wa-wuḍiʿat bayna rijlay al-muṭlagha sahhala awlādahā], When a feather is taken from its right wing and placed at the feet of a woman in labour, it relieves the pains of childbirth. So also Alcamus under رخم [rakham] racham. Pliny says the same of the vulture's feather, book 29, chapter 4: A vulture's feather placed under the feet assists women in labour. And Sextus, book 2, chapter 2: It makes them give birth without labour. III, 59 3. The gall of the rachama cures albugo [white opacity of the eye]. Alkazuinius: يكتحل بمرارتها وحدها لبياض العين [yuktaḥalu bi-marāratihā waḥdahā li-bayāḍ al-ʿayn], From the vulture's gall alone an eye-salve is made for albugo of the eye. Avicenna: يكتحل بمرارتها لبياض العين بالما البارد [yuktaḥalu bi-marāratihā li-bayāḍ al-ʿayn bi-l-mā al-bārid], From its gall with cold water an eye-salve is made for albugo of the eyes. The vulture's gall has the same power. Pliny, book 29, chapter 6, after saying that eagle's gall is applied as an ointment for clouding, dimness and cataracts of the eyes: The same power is also in vulture's gall, etc. Serenus Sammonicus, chapter 14: ¶

Si tenebras oculis obducit pigra senectus,
Expressae marathro guttae cum melle liquenti
Detergere malum potuerunt, vel vulturis atri
Fella, chelidoniae fuerint quis gramina mista.

If sluggish old age draws darkness over the eyes, drops pressed from fennel with flowing honey have been able to wipe away the ill, or the gall of the black vulture, if blades of celandine are mixed with it. ¶

Marcellus, Fellēs gallinacei, vel vulturini, quod longe magis prodest, scrupulum, et mellis optimi unciam bene tritam conjunges, atque in pyxide cuprea habebis, et opportune ad inungendum uteris. Hoc nihil potentius caliginem relevat. Galenus Euporistων libri primi capite quadragesimo quarto, Omnem oculorum obscuritatem incipientemque suffusionem curat fel vulturis, cum marrubii succo et melle At-tico. Sextus, Vulturis fel, etc. omnem caliginem discutit.

Marcellus: Combine a scruple of the gall of a cock, or of a vulture, which is far more beneficial, with an ounce of the best honey, well ground, keep it in a copper box, and use it for anointing when needed. Nothing relieves dimness more powerfully than this. Galen, Euporista, book 1, chapter 44: Vulture's gall, with juice of horehound and Attic honey, cures every dimness of the eyes and incipient cataract. Sextus: Vulture's gall, etc. dispels all dimness. ¶

4. Rachamae jecur a daemone obsessis utile. Alcamus, كبده يشوي ويسحق ويسقي المجنون ثلثة ايام كل يوم ثلاث مرات ويبريه Iecinore ejus tosto et contrito, per triduum potatur daemoniacus quotidie tribus vicibus, et convalescit. Ita etiam Damir. Nec multo aliter Abenbitar, كبدها شوي ويسحق ويداف بخل ويسقي من به جنون كل يوم ثلاث مرات ثلاثة ايام متواليه فيبريه Iecur ejus assatur, conteritur, et in aceto maceratur, et illi propinatur, quia a daemone obsessus est, per triduum quotidie tribus vicibus, per tres dies continuos, et sanat eum. Nempe de comitiali morbo, in quo videtur esse θεῖόν τι, aliquid simile tradiderant vetustiores. Praeterea vultures naturam habent vaticinam: quippe cum iis peregrinantur, qui dimicaturi sunt, in spem cadaverum, quae tunc cadent. Apud Plinium tradit Umbricius, aruspicum peritissimus, triduo ante, aut biduo volare eos, ubi cadavera futura sint. Unde Plautus in Truculento Scena Piscis,

Moreover, vultures have a prophetic nature: for they travel abroad with those who are about to fight, in hope of the corpses that will then fall. In Pliny, Umbricius, the most skilled of the haruspices, reports that they fly three or two days beforehand to where there will be corpses. Hence Plautus in the Truculentus, in the scene Piscis: ¶

— — vide, ut jam, quasi volturii, triduo
Prius praedivinant quo die esuri sient.

Whose vulture will this carcass be? col. 310 III, 64 I add one thing: instead of רחם racham, in Deuteronomy we read רחמה rachama, with a feminine ending. For I do not agree with those who hold that the He in רחמה is paragogic, since I know that in Arabic too the feminine رخمة [rakhama] rachama occurs far more often than the masculine رخم [rakham] racham. And, as Damir notes, الها فيها للجنس [al-hā fīhā li-l-jins] in that word the letter He pertains to the genus, lest it be thought to be the He of unity, as commonly among the Arabs. But, what is more surprising, although in Greek one says ὁ γὺψ [ho gyps] in the masculine gender, yet in the Greek version of Horapollo, an Egyptian writer, ἡ γύψ [hē gyps] is always read, evidently so that the Greek word should correspond to the Egyptian רחמה rachama, or to another word analogous to it, which was feminine. This seems to have arisen from a popular error, since most Egyptians were persuaded that all vultures are female, and that they conceive in no other way than by facing the south wind, or the east wind, when it blows. Horus, Hieroglyphica, book 1, chapter 11: Ἄῤῥεν ἐν τούτῳ τῷ γένει τῶν ζώων οὐχ ὑπάρχει [Arrhen en toutō tō genei tōn zōōn ouch hyparchei], In this kind of animal there is no male. And after relating that other birds conceive from the wind eggs fit only for eating, not also for generation, he says: Γυπῶν δὲ οὐχ' ὑπηνέμιον ποιουμένων τὴν ὀχείαν ἡ τῶν ὠῶν γένεσις ζωογονεῖται [Gypōn de ouch hypēnemion poioumenōn tēn ocheian hē tōn ōōn genesis zōogoneitai], But in vultures, although they make a wind-borne union (that is, although they conceive in no other way than from the wind: for instead of οὐχ [ouch] I read κἂν [kan], or εἰ καὶ [ei kai], or something of the kind), a living creature is nevertheless generated from their eggs. Plutarch in the Roman Questions, Question 93: Εἰ δὲ (ὡς Αἰγύπτιοι μυθολογοῦσι) θῆλυ πᾶν τὸ γένος ἐστὶ καὶ κυΐσκονται δεχόμενοι καταπνέοντα τὸν Ἀπηλιώτην· [Ei de (hōs Aigyptioi mythologousi) thēly pan to genos esti kai kyiskontai dechomenoi katapneonta ton Apēliōtēn] If it is true, as the Egyptians relate, that the whole race of vultures is female, and that they conceive by receiving the blast of the east wind. Ammianus, book 17, on the Egyptians: By the vulture they express the word for nature, because natural science records that no males can be found among these birds. Perhaps instead of naturae [nature] we should read matris [mother], as in Homer: Μητέρα δὲ γράφοντες γῦπα ζωγραφοῦσι, ἐπειδὴ ἄῤῥην ἐν τούτῳ τῷ γένει τῶν ζώων οὐχ' ὑπάρχει [Mētera de graphontes gypa zōgraphousi, epeidē arrhēn en toutō tō genei tōn zōōn ouch hyparchei]. Tzetzes too, Chiliad 12, History 439, after relating this very thing much more fully, declares that he drew it ἐξ Αἰγυπτίων [ex Aigyptiōn], from the Egyptians. This fable, then, first arose in Egypt; from there it gradually spread to the other nations, especially after the birth of Christ, since many doctors of the church eagerly embraced it, or at least used it as an argumentum ad hominem, as they call it, against those who denied that Christ could have been born of a virgin. Accordingly, that female vultures conceive and bear without intercourse is written, from the choir of the Fathers, by Tertullian against the Valentinians, chapter 10; Origen against Celsus, book 1; Basil on the Hexaemeron, book 5, chapter 20; Eustathius on the Hexaemeron, page 27; Procopius on Isaiah; Isidore, Etymologies, book 12, chapter 7. To these may be added Theophylact Simocatta, Physical Question 8; Philes, and Pisides in the Iambics (which others attribute to Cyril of Alexandria); Peter Damian, book 2, Letter 18; Tzetzes, as above; the Etymologicum, III, 65 Cyril the lexicographer, the Scholiasts whom we have cited, etc. Nor do I doubt that this opinion, however false and absurd, already prevailed among many from the time of Moses. Far be it from us, however, to think that Moses, swept away by that torrent, ever imagined any such thing about the vulture, even in a dream. I mean only this: that in Deu- col. 311 teronomy he used the feminine word רחמה rachama in order to speak with the common people. But, lest anyone should turn this into a defence of the error, in Exodus he said רחם racham in the masculine. ¶
