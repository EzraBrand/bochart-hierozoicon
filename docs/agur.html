De Grue.

On the Crane. ¶

In anatomia gruis aliquid peculiare observasse se scribit Bellonius libri quarti capite primo De Avibus, quod nimirum canna pulmonis aliter se habet, quam in aliis. Sterno enim, inquit, adhaerens carnem utrimque penetrat. Ac proinde mirum non est, quod vox ejus tam longe auditur. Neque enim ulla est avis tam vocalis atque grus. Hinc Ezechias in eucharistico carmine, postquam a morbo col. 69 convaluit Es. XXXVIII, 14. כסיס עגור כן אצפצף, ut sis et agor pipiebam, id est, ut hirundo et grus; quarum avium una querulam, altera contentissimam habet vocem. Inter sis et agor deest Copulativa Vau; ut Hab. III, 11. שמש ירח עמד זבלה, sol, luna (id est, sol et luna) steterunt in loco suo. Et 1 Par. II, 1. Ruben, Simeon, Levi, pro Ruben, et Simeon, et Levi. Itaque mens Ezechiae haec est, se, gravi morbo afflictum, non modo instar hirundinis leni murmure gemuisse, sed et gruis instar contenta voce ejulasse. Sic Talmudici in tractatu Kiddusim cap. 2. fol. 44. a. וצווח ר״ל ככורכיא, Et clamabat ut grus dominus Lakis. Et Smyrnaeus libro tertio de Nereidum planctu in morte Achillis,

In the anatomy of the crane, Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] writes in book 4, chapter 1 of De Avibus that he observed something peculiar: namely, that the windpipe is arranged otherwise than in other birds. For, adhering to the breastbone, he says, it penetrates the flesh on both sides. And so it is no wonder that its voice is heard so far away. For no bird is as vocal as the crane. Hence Hezekiah, in his song of thanksgiving after he recovered from his illness col. 69, Es. XXXVIII, 14: כסיס עגור כן אצפצף, like sis and agor I chirped, that is, like the swallow and the crane; of these birds the one has a plaintive voice, the other a very strained one. Between sis and agor the copulative Vau is missing, as in Hab 3:11. שמש ירח עמד זבלה, sun, moon (that is, sun and moon) stood still in their place. And 1 Chr 2:1. Reuben, Simeon, Levi, for Reuben, and Simeon, and Levi. Thus Hezekiah's meaning is this: afflicted by grave illness, he not only moaned with a gentle murmur like a swallow, but also wailed with a strained voice like a crane. So the Talmudists in the tractate Kiddushin, ch. 2, fol. 44a: וצווח ר״ל ככורכיא, And Resh Lakish cried out like a crane. And Quintus of Smyrna, in book 3, on the lament of the Nereids at the death of Achilles, ¶

— — — — ταί δ᾽ ἐφέροντο Κλαγγηδὸν κραιπνῇσιν ἐειδόμεναι γεράνοισιν, Ὀσσόμεναι a) μέγα χεῖμα, — —

— — — — ταί δ᾽ ἐφέροντο [tai d’ epheronto] Κλαγγηδὸν κραιπνῇσιν ἐειδόμεναι γεράνοισιν [klangēdon kraipnēsin eeidomenai geranoisin], Ὀσσόμεναι a) μέγα χεῖμα [Ossomenai mega cheima], — — ¶

Illae vero ferebantur cum clangore, citis gruibus similes, quae magnam tempestatem praesentiscunt. Et libro decimo tertio, ubi de Trojae excidio,

But they were borne along with clangor, like swift cranes that sense a great storm in advance. And in book 13, on the destruction of Troy, ¶

— — στοναχὴ δὲ δαϊκταμένων αἰζηῶν Ἔπλετο λευγαλέη, περὶ δ᾽ ἴαχε πάντα μέλαθρα II, 615 Ἄσπετον· οἰμωγὴ δὲ πέλεν στονόεσσα γυναίκων Εἰδομένων γεράνοισιν, ὅτ᾽ αἰετὸν ἀθρήσωσιν. Ὑψόθεν αἴσσοντα δι᾽ αἰθέρος, οὐδ᾽ ἄρα τῇσι Θαρσαλέον στέρνοισι πέλει μένος, ἀλλὰ ἑ μοῦνον Μακρὸν ἀνατρύζουσι, φοβούμεναι ἱερὸν ὄρνιν.

— — στοναχὴ δὲ δαϊκταμένων αἰζηῶν [stonachē de daiktamenōn aizēōn] Ἔπλετο λευγαλέη, περὶ δ᾽ ἴαχε πάντα μέλαθρα [Epleto leugaleē, peri d’ iache panta melathra] II, 615 Ἄσπετον· οἰμωγὴ δὲ πέλεν στονόεσσα γυναίκων [Aspeton; oimōgē de pelen stonoessa gynaikōn] Εἰδομένων γεράνοισιν, ὅτ᾽ αἰετὸν ἀθρήσωσιν [Eidomenōn geranoisin, hot’ aieton athrēsōsin]. Ὑψόθεν αἴσσοντα δι᾽ αἰθέρος, οὐδ᾽ ἄρα τῇσι [Hypsothen aissonta di’ aitheros, oud’ ara tēsi] Θαρσαλέον στέρνοισι πέλει μένος, ἀλλὰ ἑ μοῦνον [Tharsaleon sternoisi pelei menos, alla he mounon] Μακρὸν ἀνατρύζουσι, φοβούμεναι ἱερὸν ὄρνιν [Makron anatryzousi, phoboumenai hieron ornin]. ¶

Et juvenum caesorum gemitus erat miserabilis, omnesque domus horrendum resonabant. Et tristissimus erat mulierum ejulatus, gruibus similium, cum aquilam vident, ex alto per aetherem irruentem. Nec fortis illis in pectore vis residet, sed tantummodo multum strident, sacram Iovis alitem formidantes. Homerus quoque militum barritum cum gruum clangore conferens initio libri tertii Iliados,

And the groaning of the slain young men was pitiful, and all the houses resounded dreadfully. And most mournful was the wailing of the women, like cranes when they see an eagle swooping from on high through the air. Nor does any brave strength remain in their breasts; they only shriek long, dreading the sacred bird of Jove. Homer too, comparing the battle cry of soldiers with the clangor of cranes at the beginning of book 3 of the Iliad, ¶

Τρῶες μὲν κλαγγῇ τ᾽ ἐνοπῇ τ᾽ ἴσαν ὄρνιθες ὥς, Ἠΰτε περ κλαγγὴ γεράνων πέλει οὐρανόθι πρό.

Τρῶες μὲν κλαγγῇ τ᾽ ἐνοπῇ τ᾽ ἴσαν ὄρνιθες ὥς [Trōes men klangē t’ enopē t’ isan ornithes hōs], Ἠΰτε περ κλαγγὴ γεράνων πέλει οὐρανόθι πρό [Ēute per klangē geranōn pelei ouranothi pro]. ¶

Trojani quidem cum clangore et clamore ibant ut aves, qualis est gruum clangor ad coelum usque. Quod Maro imitatus Aeneidos libro decimo,

The Trojans went with clangor and clamor like birds, like the clangor of cranes up to the sky. Which Virgil imitated in book 10 of the Aeneid, ¶

— — clamorem ad sidera tollunt Dardanidae e muris, quales sub nubibus atris Strymoniae dant signa grues, atque aethera tranant Cum sonitu, fugiuntque Notos clamore secundo.

— — the Dardanians raise a shout to the stars from the walls, as beneath black clouds Strymonian cranes give their signals and swim through the air with noise, and flee the South Winds with favoring clamor. ¶

Et Martialis, ut anum Philenidem non loquacem solum, sed et vocalem ac Stentoream fuisse significet, libri noni Epigrammate 30.

And Martial, to indicate that the old woman Philaenis was not only talkative but also loud-voiced and Stentorian, in book 9, Epigram 30: ¶

Heu, quae lingua silet! Non illam mille catastae Vincebant, nec quae turba Serapin amat. Nec matutini cirrata caterva magistri, Nec quae Strymonio de grege ripa sonat.

Alas, what a tongue is silent! A thousand slave-auction platforms could not outdo it, nor the throng that loves Serapis, nor the curly-haired troop of the morning schoolmaster, nor the bank that resounds with the Strymonian flock. ¶

Sed et ad gruum clamorem pertinent, quae sequuntur. Homerus iterum ubi supra,

The following also pertain to the cry of cranes. Homer again, in the place cited above, ¶

Κλαγγῇ ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων,

Κλαγγῇ ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων [Klangē tai ge petontai ep’ Ōkeanoio rhoaōn], ¶

Cum clangore hae volant ad Oceani fluenta. Hesiodus in Operibus,

With clangor they fly toward the streams of Ocean. Hesiod in the Works, ¶

Φράζεσθαι δ᾽ εὖτ᾽ ἂν φωνὴν γεράνου ἐπακούσης, Ὑψόθεν ἐκ νεφέων ἐνιαύσια κεκλαγυίης,

Φράζεσθαι δ᾽ εὖτ᾽ ἂν φωνὴν γεράνου ἐπακούσης [Phrazesthai d’ eut’ an phōnēn geranou epakousēs], Ὑψόθεν ἐκ νεφέων ἐνιαύσια κεκλαγυίης [Hypsothen ek nepheōn eniausia keklagyiēs], ¶

Attende, cum vocem gruis audieris sursum ex nubibus quotannis clangentis. Lucretius libri quarti versu 18.

Take heed when you hear the voice of the crane clangoring yearly from the clouds on high. Lucretius, book 4, verse 18: ¶

Parvus ut est cygni melior canor, ille gruum quam Clamor in aethereis dispersis nubibus austri.

As the small song of the swan is better than the clamor of cranes scattered among the ethereal clouds of the South Wind. ¶

Proinde Eustathius gruem dicit avem esse φωνητικὴν, vocalem. Et notandum, plerasque linguas ipso avis nomine vocem illius utcumque imitari. Ita Hebraeum עגור agor, Chaldaeum כורכיא kurkeja, Arabica كركي kurki (1), et غرنوق gurnuk (2), Graecum γέρανος, Cambro-Britannicum garan, German. cran, Romanum grus sunt totidem voces ὀνοματοποιητικαί. Hinc Isidorus, col. 70 Grues nomen de propria voce sumpserunt. Tali enim voce susurrant. Et gruere est gruum more clangere. Festus, Gruere dicuntur grues, ut sues grunnire. Poëmatium De cantu Philomelae,

Accordingly Eustathius says the crane is a φωνητικὴν [phōnētikēn], vocal, bird. And it should be noted that most languages, in the very name of the bird, imitate its voice in some way. Thus the Hebrew עגור agor, the Chaldee כורכיא kurkeja, the Arabic كركي [kurkī] kurki R1 and غرنوق [gharnūq] gurnuk R2, the Greek γέρανος [geranos], the Cambro-British garan, the German cran, the Roman grus are so many onomatopoeic (ὀνοματοποιητικαί [onomatopoiētikai]) words. Hence Isidore, col. 70 Cranes took their name from their own voice. For they whisper with such a sound. And gruere is to clangor in the manner of cranes. Festus: Cranes are said gruere, as swine grunnire. The little poem On the Song of Philomela: ¶

Grus gruit, inque glomis cygni prope flumina drensant.

The crane cries gru, and the swans in flocks drensant near the rivers. ¶

Glossarium vetus: Gruunt, γερανίζουσι. Interim, ne quis putet, grues semper sonora voce clangere, Damir haec habet de grue, quae excubias agit, dum reliquae dormiunt: والذي يحرس يهتف خافي كانه ينذر بانه جارس Et quae in custodia est nonnisi submissa voce murmurat, ac si sciret, se esse in custodia. Quod ipsum ad Ezechiae locum non absurde referatur, quasi hoc gruis lene murmur cum hirundinis minuritione junxerit.

An old Glossary: Gruunt, γερανίζουσι [geranizousi]. Meanwhile, lest anyone think that cranes always clangor with a resounding voice, Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] says this of the crane that keeps watch while the rest sleep: والذي يحرس يهتف خافي كانه ينذر بانه جارس [wa-lladhī yaḥrus yahtif khāfī ka-annahu yundhir bi-annahu jāris] And the one on guard murmurs only in a low voice, as if it knew that it was on guard. This very thing might not absurdly be referred to the passage of Hezekiah, as though he joined this gentle murmur of the crane with the twittering of the swallow. ¶

II, 617 Nunc de migratione gruum agemus in illud Ierem. VIII, 7. Turtur, et hirundo, et grus custodierunt tempus adventus sui. In genere gruem non solum alio migrare constat, sed et fuga frigoris in remotissima loca proficisci. Symeon Sethi, Τὸ δὲ τοιοῦτον πτηνὸν διέρχεται τὴν οἰκουμένην ἅπασαν, μὴ μένον ἐν ἑνὶ κλίματι· Haec avis omnem terrarum orbem peragrat, in una regione haud quaquam manens. Aristoteles Historiae animalium libri octavi capite duodecimo, Ἐκτοπίζουσιν εἰς τὰ ἔσχατα ἐκ τῶν ἐσχάτων· Ex ultimis in ultima (grues) migrant. Id est, ut ipse interpretatur, ἐκ τῶν Σκυθικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ, ex Scythicis oris ad paludes, quae sunt supra Aegyptum, unde fluit Nilus. Herodotum sequutus, qui, de Nili fontibus et vicino Oceano agens libri secundi capite vigesimo secundo, Γέρανοι δὲ, inquit, φεύγου-σαι τὸν χειμῶνα, τὸν ἐν τῇ Σκυθικῇ χώρῃ γενόμενον, φοιτῶσι εἰς χειμαδίην ἐς τοὺς τόπους τούτους· Grues, Scythicae plagae hyemem fugientes, ad illa loca hyematum se conferunt. Aelianus etiam Historiae animalium libri tertii capite decimo tertio, Εἰς Αἴγυπτόν τε, καὶ Λιβύην ἀπαίρουσι, καὶ Αἰθιόπιαν, ὥσπερ γὰρ οὖν γῆς περίοδον εἰδυῖαι, καὶ φύσεις ἀέρων, καὶ ὡρῶν διαφορὰς στέλλονται αἱ πᾶσαι· Quasi terrae ambitum, et coeli naturam, et temporum varietatem nossent, in Aegyptum, Lybiam et Aethiopiam abeunt, et proficiscuntur omnes. Et Homerus multo ante,

II, 617 Now we shall treat the migration of cranes, with reference to Jer 8:7. The turtledove, and the swallow, and the crane have kept the time of their coming. In general it is agreed that the crane not only migrates elsewhere, but also travels to the most remote places to escape the cold. Symeon Seth [=Symeon Seth (11th c.), Syntagma de alimentorum facultatibus]: Τὸ δὲ τοιοῦτον πτηνὸν διέρχεται τὴν οἰκουμένην ἅπασαν, μὴ μένον ἐν ἑνὶ κλίματι [To de toiouton ptēnon dierchetai tēn oikoumenēn hapasan, mē menon en heni klimati]; This bird traverses the whole world, by no means staying in one region. Aristotle, History of Animals, book 8, chapter 12: Ἐκτοπίζουσιν εἰς τὰ ἔσχατα ἐκ τῶν ἐσχάτων [Ektopizousin eis ta eschata ek tōn eschatōn]; They (the cranes) migrate from the farthest regions to the farthest. That is, as he himself explains, ἐκ τῶν Σκυθικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ [ek tōn Skythikōn eis ta helē ta anō tēs Aigyptou, hothen ho Neilos rhei], from the Scythian shores to the marshes that lie above Egypt, whence the Nile flows. He followed Herodotus, who, treating the sources of the Nile and the neighbouring Ocean in book 2, chapter 22, says: Γέρανοι δὲ φεύγουσαι τὸν χειμῶνα, τὸν ἐν τῇ Σκυθικῇ χώρῃ γενόμενον, φοιτῶσι εἰς χειμαδίην ἐς τοὺς τόπους τούτους [Geranoi de pheugousai ton cheimōna, ton en tē Skythikē chōrē genomenon, phoitōsi eis cheimadiēn es tous topous toutous]; Cranes, fleeing the winter of the Scythian region, betake themselves to those places to spend the winter. Aelian too, History of Animals, book 3, chapter 13: Εἰς Αἴγυπτόν τε, καὶ Λιβύην ἀπαίρουσι, καὶ Αἰθιόπιαν, ὥσπερ γὰρ οὖν γῆς περίοδον εἰδυῖαι, καὶ φύσεις ἀέρων, καὶ ὡρῶν διαφορὰς στέλλονται αἱ πᾶσαι [Eis Aigypton te, kai Libyēn apairousi, kai Aithiopian, hōsper gar oun gēs periodon eiduiai, kai physeis aerōn, kai hōrōn diaphoras stellontai hai pasai]; As though they knew the circuit of the earth, the nature of the sky and the variation of the seasons, they depart for Egypt, Libya and Ethiopia, and all set out. And Homer long before: ¶

— — — ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων,

— — — ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων [tai ge petontai ep᾽ Ōkeanoio rhoaōn], ¶

Ad Oceani fluenta hae volant, nempe australis, ubi Pygmaei; quod docent sequentia. Unde Pompejo Lucullus apud Plutarchum, Εἶτά σοι δοκῶ ἐλάττονα τῶν γεράνων νοῦν ἔχειν, καὶ τῶν πελαργῶν, ὥς τε ταῖς ὥραις μὴ συμμετα-βάλλειν τὰς διαίτας· An tibi videor gruibus et ciconiis minus sapere, ita, ut cum anni tempestatibus stationes non mutem?

These fly to the streams of Ocean, namely the southern Ocean, where the Pygmies are, as what follows shows. Hence Lucullus to Pompey in Plutarch: Εἶτά σοι δοκῶ ἐλάττονα τῶν γεράνων νοῦν ἔχειν, καὶ τῶν πελαργῶν, ὥς τε ταῖς ὥραις μὴ συμμεταβάλλειν τὰς διαίτας [Eita soi dokō elattona tōn geranōn noun echein, kai tōn pelargōn, hōs te tais hōrais mē symmetaballein tas diaitas]; Do I seem to you to have less sense than cranes and storks, so as not to change my abode with the seasons of the year? ¶

Plerasque in Thracia nasci, veteres crediderunt. Aelianus ubi supra, Αἱ γέρανοι γίνονται μὲν ἐν Θράκῃ· Grues quidem in Thracia nascuntur. Et paullo post, Οὐκοῦν φιλοῦσι τὴν χώραν, ἐν ᾗ γεγόνασι· Regionem igitur amant, in qua nati sunt. Inde Thraciae aves dicuntur, et Strymoniae, et Bistoniae, a Strymone, fluvio, et Bistone, lacu Thraciae, unde creduntur proficisci. Virgilius libro decimo Aeneidos,

The ancients believed that most of them are born in Thrace. Aelian, in the place cited above: Αἱ γέρανοι γίνονται μὲν ἐν Θράκῃ [Hai geranoi ginontai men en Thrakē]; Cranes are born in Thrace. And shortly after: Οὐκοῦν φιλοῦσι τὴν χώραν, ἐν ᾗ γεγόνασι [Oukoun philousi tēn chōran, en hē gegonasi]; They therefore love the region in which they were born. Hence they are called birds of Thrace, and Strymonian and Bistonian, from the Strymon, a river, and the Biston, a lake of Thrace, from which they are believed to set out. Virgil, Aeneid, book 10: ¶

— — — quales sub nubibus altis Strymoniae dant signa grues. — —

Antigonus follows the same in the Mirabilia, ch. 46, and so does Alkazuinius with the Auctarium: واذا ارادت النوم ادخل كل واحد راسه تحت جناحها لعلمها ان الجناح احمل للصدمة من الراس لما فيه من العين التي هي اشرف الاعضا والدماغ الذي هو ملاك البدن ونام كل واحد منها قايما علي احدي رجليه حتي لا يكون نومها ثقيل واما قايدها وحارسها فلا ينام ولا يدخل راسه في جناحه ولا يزال ينظر من جميع الجوانب فان احس باحد صاح باعلا صوته فاخبر اصحابه بالعدو [wa-idhā arādat al-nawm adkhala kull wāḥid raʾsahu taḥt janāḥihā li-ʿilmihā anna al-janāḥ aḥmal li-l-ṣadma min al-raʾs li-mā fīhi min al-ʿayn allatī hiya ashraf al-aʿḍā wa-l-dimāgh alladhī huwa milāk al-badan wa-nāma kull wāḥid minhā qāyiman ʿalā iḥdā rijlayhi ḥattā lā yakūn nawmuhā thaqīl wa-ammā qāyiduhā wa-ḥārisuhā fa-lā yanām wa-lā yudkhil raʾsahu fī janāḥihi wa-lā yazāl yanẓur min jamīʿ al-jawānib fa-in aḥassa bi-aḥad ṣāḥa bi-aʿlā ṣawtihi fa-akhbara aṣḥābahu bi-l-ʿaduww] When they wish to sleep, they put their heads II, 625 under their wings, because they know that the wings bear a blow better than the head, in which is the eye, the noblest of the parts, and the brain, on which the rest of the body depends. They sleep, moreover, standing on one foot, so that their sleep may not be heavy. But the one who is leader and guard abstains from sleep and does not hide his head under his wings. Rather, he continually looks out on every side. And if he perceives anyone, he cries out with a loud voice to signal to his companions that an enemy is near. Others add two further points. The first is that cranes keep watch in turn. Horus, book 2, ch. 90: Αὗται γὰρ ἑαυτὰς φυλάττουσι γρηγοροῦσαι κατ᾽ ὄρδινον ἐν πάσῃ τῇ νυκτί [Hautai gar heautas phylattousi grēgorousai kat᾽ ordinon en pasē tē nykti]. They guard themselves, keeping watch in order through the whole night. Solinus: They divide the night watches, so that every tenth one is sleepless. Isidore: At night they divide the watches, and keep the order of the vigils by turns. Cassiodorus: They watch in turn, and guard themselves by common caution. Basil: Τοῦ καιροῦ τῆς φυλακῆς πληρομένου, ἡ μὲν βοήσασα πρὸς ὕπνον ἐτράπετο. ἡ δὲ τὴν διαδοχὴν ὑποδεξαμένη, ἧς ἔτυχεν ἀσφαλείας, col. 76 ἀντέδωκεν ἐν τῷ μέρει [Tou kairou tēs phylakēs plēromenou, hē men boēsasa pros hypnon etrapeto. hē de tēn diadochēn hypodexamenē, hēs etychen asphaleias, antedōken en tō merei]. Ambrose expands this as follows: When the time of the watch has been completed, having discharged its duty it settles down to sleep after first giving a cry, so as to wake the sleeper to whom it is to hand over its turn of duty. And that one willingly takes up its lot, and does not, as is our habit, renounce sleep unwillingly and sluggishly. Rather, it briskly shakes itself from its bed, performs its turn, and repays the favour it received with equal care and service. And Damir: في طبعه الخوف والتحارس بالنوبة فاذا قضي نوبتة قام الذي كان نايما يحرس مكانه حتي تقضي كله ما يلزمها من الحراسة [fī ṭabʿihi al-khawf wa-l-taḥārus bi-l-nawba fa-idhā qaḍā nawbatahu qāma alladhī kāna nāyiman yaḥrus makānahu ḥattā taqḍī kulluhu mā yalzamuhā min al-ḥirāsa] It is in its nature to be fearful and to keep guard by turns. And when one has completed its turn, one of those that were sleeping rises to keep watch in its place, until all have kept the watches incumbent on them. The second is that the watchmen carry a stone in the foot, which by its fall wakes them when asleep or about to fall asleep. Pliny: They keep watches at night, holding a pebble in the foot, which, loosened by sleep and falling, betrays their negligence. Solinus: The watchmen grasp small weights in their toes, which, if they should happen to fall, betray sleep. Plutarch: Αἱ προφυλακὴν ἔχουσαι νυκτὸς, ἐπὶ θατέρου σκέλους ὀχοῦνται τὸ σῶμα, τῷ δ᾽ ἑτέρῳ ποδὶ λίθον περιλαβοῦσαι κρατοῦσι· συνέχει γὰρ ὁ τῆς ἀφῆς τόνος ἐν τῷ μὴ καθεύδειν πολὺν χρόνον. Ὅταν δὲ ἀνῶσιν, ἐκπεσὼν ὁ λίθος ταχὺ διήγειρε τὴν προεμένην [Hai prophylakēn echousai nyktos, epi thaterou skelous ochountai to sōma, tō d᾽ heterō podi lithon perilabousai kratousi; synechei gar ho tēs haphēs tonos en tō mē katheudein polyn chronon. Hotan de anōsin, ekpesōn ho lithos tachy diēgeire tēn proemenēn]. Those that keep watch at night support the body on one of their legs, and hold a pebble grasped in the other foot. The effort of gripping holds off sleep for some time. But as soon as they relax, the falling stone at once wakes the negligent one. Aelian [III, 13.]: Ἑστᾶσι μὲν ἀσκωλιάζουσαι, τῷ γε μὲν μετεώρῳ ποδὶ λίθον κατέχουσι τοῖς ὄνυξι μάλα ἐγκρατῶς τε καὶ εὐλαβῶς· ἵνα ἐάν ποτε λάθωσιν ἑαυτὰς εἰς ὕπνον ὑπολισθαίνουσαι, πεσὼν καὶ ὑποκτυπήσας ὁ λίθος, ἀποδαρθάνειν καταναγκάσῃ [Hestasi men askōliazousai, tō ge men meteōrō podi lithon katechousi tois onyxi mala enkratōs te kai eulabōs; hina ean pote lathōsin heautas eis hypnon hypolisthainousai, pesōn kai hypoktypēsas ho lithos, apodarthanein katanankasē]. They rest on one foot only, and in the other, which is raised, they hold a pebble II, 626 with their claws very firmly and with the greatest care, so that, if sleep should ever steal upon them, the falling pebble may wake them with its noise. Philes and Tzetzes say the same. Accordingly, when Aelian says that cranes swallow pebbles ὡς ἔχειν καὶ δεῖπνον, καὶ πρὸς τὰς ἐμβολὰς τῶν ἀνέμων ἕρμα [hōs echein kai deipnon, kai pros tas embolas tōn anemōn herma], so as to have them both for a meal and as ballast against the onslaught of the winds, one should consider whether instead of δεῖπνον [deipnon] we ought to read δι᾽ ὕπνον [di᾽ hypnon], on account of sleep. For that pebbles should serve cranes as a meal is quite absurd. But perhaps Aelian meant that stones are swallowed by cranes not only to serve as ballast when they are about to fly across the Pontus, but also so that, once the sea has been crossed, they may be vomited up and serve to ward off sleep in the manner we have described. ¶

Gruum cum Pygmaeis pugnas ad australem Oceanum et Nili fontes, de quibus post Homerum multi multa nugantur, libenter praeterimus. Quamvis in ea fabula hoc saltem verum esse asserat Philosophus, pusillos homines in iis locis degere, Hist. lib. 8. cap. 12. Μεταβάλλουσιν, inquit, αἱ γέρανοι ἐκ τῶν Σκυ-θικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ τόπος οὗτος, περὶ ὃν οἱ Πυγμαῖοι κατοικοῦσιν. Οὐ γάρ ἐστι τοῦτο μῦθος, ἀλλ᾽ ἔστι κα-τὰ τὴν ἀλήθειαν γένος μικρὸν μὲν, ὥσπερ λέγεται, καὶ αὐτοὶ, καὶ οἱ ἵπποι. Τρωγλοδύται δ᾽ εἰσὶ τὸν βίον. Ex Scythicis oris grues migrant ad paludes, quae supra Aegyptum sunt, unde profluit Nilus. Circa quem locum Pygmaei habitant. Neque enim id est fabula. Sed re vera, ut ajunt, genus ibi parvum est tam homi- num, quam equorum. Vitam autem Troglodyticam, hoc est, in cavernis sub terra degunt. Veros Nili fontes veteribus fuisse prorsus ignotos, nemo est, qui nesciat. Itaque hic Nili nomine intelligitur Astaboras, quem Nili ramum esse col. 77 veteres crediderunt, ut alibi probavimus. Oritur autem Astaboras ex locis, Ava- litui sinui proximis, et Mosylo emporio. Unde est, quod Aethicus Nili fontes ibi describit. Fluviumque Nilum, inquit, qui de littore incipientis maris Rubri, videtur emergere in loco, qui dicitur Mossylon emporium. Et re vera Nonnosus, circa haec loca navigans, ut ex Homeritis iret ad Auxumitas, Ἐνέτυχέ τισι μορ-φὴν μὲν καὶ ἰδέαν ἔχουσιν ἀνθρωπίνην, βραχυτάτοις δὲ τὸ μέγεθος, καὶ μέ-λασι τὴν χρόαν· ὑπὸ δὲ τριχῶν δεδασυμμένοις διὰ παντὸς τοῦ σώματος· εἵπον-το δὲ τοῖς ἀνδράσι καὶ γυναῖκες παραπλήσιαι, καὶ παιδάρια ἔτι βραχύτερα. Incidit in quosdam forma quidem et figura humana, sed statura brevissimos, et co- lore nigros, et toto corpore pilosos. Sequebantur autem viros prope aequales foe- minae, et pueri adhuc breviores. Ita Photius in Excerptis ex Nonnosi historia, qui sub Iustiniano vixit. Sed et in Hesychio Νῶβαι sunt Πυγμαῖοι. Nobae, id est, Nubae, qui in illis locis degunt. Et Strabo de Aethiopibus sub Aegypto lib. 17. τά τε βοσκήματα αὐτοῖς ἐστι μικρὰ πρόβατα, καὶ αἶγες, καὶ βόες, καὶ κύνες μικροί· τραχεῖς δὲ καὶ μάχιμοι οἰκοῦντες μικροὶ ὄντες. Τάχα δὲ τοὺς Πυγμαίους ἀπὸ τῆς τούτων μικροφυΐας ἐπενόησαν καὶ ἀνέπλασαν· ἑωρακὼς II, 627 μὲν γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἐξηγεῖται τῶν πίστεως ἀξίων ἀνδρῶν. Pecora ipsis sunt parvae oves, et caprae, boves etiam, et canes parvi. Sed et ipsi incolae pusilli sunt, asperi ta- men et pugnaces. Et fortasse ob naturalem horum parvitatem (veteres ibi locorum) Pygmaeos confinxerunt. Eos enim fide dignus nemo est qui viderit. Nempe quales esse finguntur, trium palmorum altitudine, quales ne Homerus quidem dixerat. Sed hoc commentum auctores habet fabulosissimos scriptores, Megasthenem et Daimachum, de quibus Strabo lib. 2. Ἐνεκαίνισαν δὲ καὶ τὴν Ὁμηρικὴν τῶν Πυγμαίων γερανομαχίαν τρισπιθάμους εἴποντες· Homericam Pygmaeorum in grues pugnam interpolarunt, trium palmorum eos esse dicentes. Sed et Arabes idem referunt ex cujusdam Graeculi fide, qui Iacobo, Isaaci filio, Siragensi, fertur ita narrasse. ركبت في بحر الزنج والقتني الريح الي بعض الجزاير فوصلت منها الي مدينة اهلها ناس قاماتهم قدم نراع واكثرهم عور فاجتمع علي منهم جمع وانتهوا بي الي ملكهم فامر بحبسي فحبست في شبه قفص ثم رايتهم في بعض الايام يستعدون القتال وقالوا لنا عدو ياتينا في هذا الاوان فلم يلبث الي ان طلعت عليهم عصابة من الغرانيق وكان عورهم من نقرها اعينهم فاخذت عصي وشددت عليها وهربت واكرموني لذلك Navigabam aliquando in mari Zingitano, et impulit me ventus in quan- dam insulam. In cujus oppidum cum devenissem, reperi incolas cubitalis esse sta- turae, et plerosque coclites. Quorum multitudo, in me congregata, me deduxit ad col. 78 regem suum. Iussit is, ut captivus detinerer. Et in quandam caveae speciem con- jectus sum. Eos autem aliquando ad bellum instrui cum viderem, dixerunt, hostem imminere, et fore, ut propediem ingrueret. Nec multo post gruum exercitus in eos insurrexit. Atque ideo erant coclites, quod illorum oculos hae confodissent. At ego, virga sumpta, in eas impetum feci, et illae avolarunt atque aufugerunt. Ob quod facinus in honore fui apud illos.

The battles of the cranes with the Pygmies near the southern Ocean and the sources of the Nile, about which many writers since Homer have spun many tales, we gladly pass over. Yet the Philosopher asserts that in that fable at least this much is true, that very small men live in those places, Hist. book 8, ch. 12: Μεταβάλλουσιν, he says, αἱ γέρανοι ἐκ τῶν Σκυθικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ τόπος οὗτος, περὶ ὃν οἱ Πυγμαῖοι κατοικοῦσιν. Οὐ γάρ ἐστι τοῦτο μῦθος, ἀλλ᾽ ἔστι κατὰ τὴν ἀλήθειαν γένος μικρὸν μὲν, ὥσπερ λέγεται, καὶ αὐτοὶ, καὶ οἱ ἵπποι. Τρωγλοδύται δ᾽ εἰσὶ τὸν βίον. [Metaballousin hai geranoi ek tōn Skythikōn eis ta helē ta anō tēs Aigyptou, hothen ho Neilos rhei. Esti de ho topos houtos, peri hon hoi Pygmaioi katoikousin. Ou gar esti touto mythos, all᾽ esti kata tēn alētheian genos mikron men, hōsper legetai, kai autoi, kai hoi hippoi. Trōglodytai d᾽ eisi ton bion.] From the Scythian regions the cranes migrate to the marshes that lie above Egypt, whence the Nile flows. Around this place the Pygmies live. For this is no fable; in truth, as they say, there is a small race there, both of men and of horses. They lead a Troglodyte life, that is, they live in caves under the earth. Everyone knows that the true sources of the Nile were entirely unknown to the ancients. Here, therefore, by the name Nile is meant the Astaboras, which col. 77 the ancients believed to be a branch of the Nile, as we have shown elsewhere. The Astaboras rises in places nearest to the Avalites gulf and the trading-post of Mosylon. That is why Aethicus places the sources of the Nile there: And the river Nile, he says, which seems to emerge from the shore of the beginning of the Red Sea, in the place called the Mossylon trading-post. And indeed Nonnosus, sailing about these places on his way from the Homerites to the Axumites, Ἐνέτυχέ τισι μορφὴν μὲν καὶ ἰδέαν ἔχουσιν ἀνθρωπίνην, βραχυτάτοις δὲ τὸ μέγεθος, καὶ μέλασι τὴν χρόαν· ὑπὸ δὲ τριχῶν δεδασυμμένοις διὰ παντὸς τοῦ σώματος· εἵποντο δὲ τοῖς ἀνδράσι καὶ γυναῖκες παραπλήσιαι, καὶ παιδάρια ἔτι βραχύτερα. [Enetyche tisi morphēn men kai idean echousin anthrōpinēn, brachytatois de to megethos, kai melasi tēn chroan; hypo de trichōn dedasymmenois dia pantos tou sōmatos; heiponto de tois andrasi kai gynaikes paraplēsiai, kai paidaria eti brachytera.] came upon certain people, human indeed in form and shape, but very short in stature, black in colour, and hairy over the whole body. The men were followed by women of nearly the same size, and by children still shorter. So Photius in the Excerpts from the history of Nonnosus, who lived under Justinian. Moreover, in Hesychius Νῶβαι [Nōbai] are Πυγμαῖοι [Pygmaioi]: the Nobae, that is, the Nubae, who live in those places. And Strabo, book 17, on the Ethiopians below Egypt: τά τε βοσκήματα αὐτοῖς ἐστι μικρὰ πρόβατα, καὶ αἶγες, καὶ βόες, καὶ κύνες μικροί· τραχεῖς δὲ καὶ μάχιμοι οἰκοῦντες μικροὶ ὄντες. Τάχα δὲ τοὺς Πυγμαίους ἀπὸ τῆς τούτων μικροφυΐας ἐπενόησαν καὶ ἀνέπλασαν· ἑωρακὼς II, 627 μὲν γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἐξηγεῖται τῶν πίστεως ἀξίων ἀνδρῶν. [ta te boskēmata autois esti mikra probata, kai aiges, kai boes, kai kynes mikroi; tracheis de kai machimoi oikountes mikroi ontes. Tacha de tous Pygmaious apo tēs toutōn mikrophyias epenoēsan kai aneplasan; heōrakōs men gar oudeis exēgeitai tōn pisteōs axiōn andrōn.] Their livestock are small sheep and goats, oxen too, and small dogs. The inhabitants themselves are also tiny, yet rough and warlike. And perhaps on account of their natural smallness (the ancients of those parts) invented the Pygmies. For no one worthy of credit has seen them. That is, Pygmies such as they are imagined to be, three spans tall, such as not even Homer had described. But this invention has as its authors the most fabulous of writers, Megasthenes and Daimachus, of whom Strabo says in book 2: Ἐνεκαίνισαν δὲ καὶ τὴν Ὁμηρικὴν τῶν Πυγμαίων γερανομαχίαν τρισπιθάμους εἴποντες· [Enekainisan de kai tēn Homērikēn tōn Pygmaiōn geranomachian trispithamous eipontes;] They embellished the Homeric battle of the Pygmies with the cranes, saying that they were three spans tall. The Arabs too report the same on the word of a certain little Greek, who is said to have told it thus to Jacob, son of Isaac, of Siraf: ركبت في بحر الزنج والقتني الريح الي بعض الجزاير فوصلت منها الي مدينة اهلها ناس قاماتهم قدم نراع واكثرهم عور فاجتمع علي منهم جمع وانتهوا بي الي ملكهم فامر بحبسي فحبست في شبه قفص ثم رايتهم في بعض الايام يستعدون القتال وقالوا لنا عدو ياتينا في هذا الاوان فلم يلبث الي ان طلعت عليهم عصابة من الغرانيق وكان عورهم من نقرها اعينهم فاخذت عصي وشددت عليها وهربت واكرموني لذلك [rakibtu fī baḥr al-Zanj wa-alqatnī al-rīḥ ilā baʿḍ al-jazāyir fa-waṣaltu minhā ilā madīna ahluhā nās qāmātuhum qadm nrāʿ wa-aktharuhum ʿūr fa-ijtamaʿa ʿalayya minhum jamʿ wa-intahaw bī ilā malikihim fa-amara bi-ḥabsī fa-ḥubistu fī shibh qafaṣ thumma raʾaytuhum fī baʿḍ al-ayyām yastaʿiddūn al-qitāl wa-qālū lanā ʿaduww yaʾtīnā fī hādhā al-awān fa-lam yalbath ilā an ṭalaʿat ʿalayhim ʿiṣāba min al-gharānīq wa-kāna ʿawaruhum min naqrihā aʿyunahum fa-akhadhtu ʿaṣā wa-shadadtu ʿalayhā wa-harabat wa-akramūnī li-dhālik] I was once sailing on the Zanj sea, and the wind drove me to a certain island. When I reached its town, I found the inhabitants to be a cubit tall, and most of them one-eyed. A crowd of them gathered around me and led me to col. 78 their king. He ordered that I be held captive, and I was thrown into a kind of cage. When one day I saw them preparing for war, they said that an enemy was threatening and would soon fall upon them. Not long after, an army of cranes rose up against them. And they were one-eyed because the cranes had pecked out their eyes. But I took a stick and charged at them, and they flew off and fled. For this deed I was held in honour among them. ¶

Philosophus (Hist. lib. 8. cap. 12.) grues e Thracia migrare scribit τοῦ Μαι-μακτηριῶνος, Maemacterione mense: et Aelianus lib. 3. cap. 13. Φθινοπώρου ἤδη μεσοῦντος, medio autumno. Unde maxime confirmatur, quod contra Gazam contendit Scaliger, et contra Petavium Samuel Petit in Eclogis chronologicis lib. 4. in mensibus Atticis Maemacterionem non esse primum, sed secundum Au- tumnalium. Reditus tempus Aelianus ita definit, Ὅταν ὑπεύδια ἄρξηται, καὶ εἰρηναῖα τοῦ ἀέρος ὑποστρέφουσιν ὀπίσω· Cum incipit aëris serenitas et tranquillitas II, 628 postliminio revertitur. Et Maximus Tyrius, Αἱ γέρανοι ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ὥρα-θέρους ἀφισάμεναι, οὐκ ἀνεχόμεναι τὸ θάλπος, τείνασαι πτέρυγας ὥσπερ ἱςία, φέρονται διὰ τοῦ ἀέρος εὐθὺ τῶν Σκυθῶν γῆς· Grues per aestatem ex Aegypto abscedentes, quia calorem pati non possunt, alis velorum instar expansis per aërem ad Scythicam plagam recta feruntur. The Philosopher (Hist. book 8, ch. 12) writes that cranes migrate from Thrace τοῦ Μαιμακτηριῶνος [tou Maimaktēriōnos], in the month Maemacterion; and Aelian, book 3, ch. 13, Φθινοπώρου ἤδη μεσοῦντος [Phthinopōrou ēdē mesountos], in mid-autumn. This strongly confirms what Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] maintained against Gaza, and Samuel Petit against Petavius in his Eclogae chronologicae, book 4: that among the Attic months Maemacterion is not the first but the second of the autumn months. Aelian defines the time of their return thus: Ὅταν ὑπεύδια ἄρξηται, καὶ εἰρηναῖα τοῦ ἀέρος ὑποστρέφουσιν ὀπίσω [Hotan hypeudia arxētai, kai eirēnaia tou aeros hypostrephousin opisō]; when the fair weather begins and the calm of the air II, 628 returns, they come back. And Maximus of Tyre: Αἱ γέρανοι ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ὥραθέρους ἀφισάμεναι, οὐκ ἀνεχόμεναι τὸ θάλπος, τείνασαι πτέρυγας ὥσπερ ἱςία, φέρονται διὰ τοῦ ἀέρος εὐθὺ τῶν Σκυθῶν γῆς [Hai geranoi ex Aigyptou hōratherous aphisamenai, ouk anechomenai to thalpos, teinasai pterygas hōsper histia, pherontai dia tou aeros euthy tōn Skythōn gēs]; The cranes, departing from Egypt in summer because they cannot endure the heat, spread their wings like sails and are carried straight through the air to the Scythian region. Ὁ πρὶν ἐγὼ καὶ ψῆρα καὶ ἁρπάκτειραν ἐρύκων Σπέρματος ὑψιπετῆ Βιστόνιαν γέρανον.

Ego qui prius a semine (id est a granis) prohibens sturnum et altivolam Bistoniam gruem. Mundae igitur grues fuerint ex Aristeae definitione, qui pro mundis habet τῶν πτηνῶν τὰ πυροῖς καὶ ὀσπρίοις χρώμενα πρὸς τὴν τροφὴν, eas aves, quae tritico et leguminibus nutriuntur. Quid quod apud Graecos veteres grues non solum in cibis, sed et in deliciis fuere, maxime circa Thessaliam, ubi in villis opulentiores suas habuere γερανοβοτίας, vel γερανοβοσίας, id est, gruum pascua, quorum meminit Pollux post Platonem in Politico. Et Balearibus insulis in honore mensarum erat vipio, quomodo vocabant minorem gruem. Ita Plinius lib. 10. cap. 49. Miratur idem, cur sub Augusto scripserit Cornelius Nepos, ciconias magis placere quam grues, cum haec nunc ales, inquit, inter primas expetatur: illam nemo velit attigisse. Hinc Horatius in Nasidieni coena, cui Mecoenas interfuit lib. 2. Satyra 8.

I, who formerly kept the starling and the high-flying Bistonian crane away from the seed (that is, from the grains). Cranes, then, will have been clean by the definition of Aristeas, who counts as clean τῶν πτηνῶν τὰ πυροῖς καὶ ὀσπρίοις χρώμενα πρὸς τὴν τροφὴν [tōn ptēnōn ta pyrois kai ospriois chrōmena pros tēn trophēn], those birds which are nourished on wheat and pulse. What is more, among the ancient Greeks cranes were not only eaten but even counted among the delicacies, especially around Thessaly, where the wealthier had on their estates their own γερανοβοτίας [geranobotias] or γερανοβοσίας [geranobosias], that is, crane pastures, which Pollux mentions, following Plato in the Statesman. And in the Balearic islands the vipio, as they called the smaller crane, was held in honour at table. So Pliny, book 10, ch. 49. The same author wonders why Cornelius Nepos, writing under Augustus, said that storks were preferred to cranes, since this bird is now, he says, sought after among the very first, while no one would want to touch the other. Hence Horace, in the dinner of Nasidienus, at which Maecenas was present, book 2, Satire 8: ¶

— — — — deinde sequuti Mazonomo pueri magno discerpta ferentes Membra gruis sparsi sale multo, non sine farre.

Haec de voce עגור agor hactenus, in qua haesisse video priscos interpretes. In Esaia pro verbis כסוס עגור, ut hirundo et agor, id est, grus, Graeci Seniores solum habent ὡς χελιδὼν, et Arabs, مثل سنونوة ut hirundo, sequenti voce prorsus omissa: et Hieronymus, Sicut pullus hirundinis: Aquila, ὡς ἵππος ἀγώρ· et Theodotion, ὡς χελιδὼν ἀγὼρ, quia vocem ignotam describere sat habuerunt. At Syrus, ܐܝܟ ܣܢܘܢܝܬܐ ܕܡܨܦܪܐ, sicut hirundo quae garrit: et Chaldaeus כסוסיא דאחיד, sicut hirundo, quae capta est: cui gemina Symmachi versio, ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλειθεῖσα, ut hirundo inclusa; quasi legerint סגור sagur, pro עגור agor. Sed legendum esse עגור agor, docent Aquila, Symmachus, Hebraei omnes, et hujus loci collatio cum Ierem. VIII, 7. Ubi rursus eaedem voces סיס sis et עגור agor junctae sunt, et quidem ita junctae, ut appareat, עגור agor esse avis nomen, ut תור thor et סיס sis, quae praecedunt. Hieronymus ciconiam reddit, et Graeci ἀγροῦ στρουθία. Sed ἀγροῦ factum ex ἀγὼρ, et στρου-ϑία est Glossema, quod ex margine in textum irrepsit. Chaldaeus et Syrus optime ܟܘܪܟܝܐ kurkeja, id est, gruem exponunt, quamvis ordine non suo, ut col. 80 supra notavimus (3).

So much, then, for the word עגור agor, over which I see that the ancient translators stumbled. In Isaiah, for the words כסוס עגור, like a swallow and agor, that is, a crane, the Greek Elders have only ὡς χελιδὼν [hōs chelidōn], and the Arabic translator مثل سنونوة [mithl sunūnwa] like a swallow, the following word being omitted altogether; and Jerome, Like a young swallow; Aquila, ὡς ἵππος ἀγώρ [hōs hippos agōr]; and Theodotion, ὡς χελιδὼν ἀγὼρ [hōs chelidōn agōr], because they were content to transcribe an unknown word. But the Syriac has ܐܝܟ ܣܢܘܢܝܬܐ ܕܡܨܦܪܐ [איך סנוניתא דמצפרא], like a swallow that chatters; and the Chaldee כסוסיא דאחיד, like a swallow that has been caught; with which Symmachus's version agrees, ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλειθεῖσα [hōs chelidōn enkleitheisa], like a swallow shut in; as if they had read סגור sagur instead of עגור agor. But that עגור agor is to be read is shown by Aquila, Symmachus, all the Hebrews, and the comparison of this passage with Ierem. VIII, 7. There again the same words סיס sis and עגור agor are joined, and indeed so joined that it is clear that עגור agor is the name of a bird, like תור thor and סיס sis, which precede it. Jerome renders it stork, and the Greeks ἀγροῦ στρουθία [agrou strouthia]. But ἀγροῦ [agrou] arose from ἀγὼρ [agōr], and στρουθία [strouthia] is a gloss that crept from the margin into the text. The Chaldee and the Syriac very well explain it as ܟܘܪܟܝܐ [כורכיא] kurkeja, that is, crane, although not in its proper order, as col. 80 we noted above R3. ¶

Quamvis עגור agor pro grue hodie non usurpetur apud Arabes, tamen potuit olim usurpari, atque inde fieri, quod verbo عجر agara significant aliquem ad lares suos postliminio reverti, vel aufugere, quod gruum est maxime proprium. Illas enim amore patriae in Scythiam et Thraciam, unde ortae sunt, quotannis redire constat, adeoque τὴν ἑαυτῶν ἕκαστον καλιὰν ἀναγνωρίζειν, ὡς τὴν οἰκίαν ἀνθρώπους, unamquamque nidum suum recognoscere, ut homines domum: quod pluribus docet Aelianus lib. 3. cap. 13. et 23. Atque indidem fortasse factum, quod a Syris et Persis Ἄγγαροι aggari, vel angari dicebantur cursores regii. Angari enim cum gruibus certare credebantur celeritate cursus. Φασί τινες (inquit Xenophon libro octavo Cyropaediae) θᾶττον τῶν γεράνων ταύτην τὴν πορείαν ἀνύττειν· Quidam hoc iter gruum volatu celerius confici referunt. II, 631 De angarorum cursu ibi agi, patebit legenti locum. At Cypriis agor erat avis, a grue diversissima, nempe aquila, si his Hesychii credimus, Ἀγώρ· ἀετὸς, Κύπριοι. Sed fortasse scriptum fuerat Ἀγώρ· γερανός· Et vocis γερα-νὸς priore parte omissa propter praecedentis τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον, ex ἀνὸς factum ἀετός (4).

Although עגור agor is not used today for the crane among the Arabs, it may nevertheless once have been so used, and may derive from the fact that by the verb عجر [ʿajara] agara they mean that someone returns home by right of return, or flees away, which is most characteristic of cranes. For it is well known that out of love of their homeland they return every year to Scythia and Thrace, where they were born, and indeed τὴν ἑαυτῶν ἕκαστον καλιὰν ἀναγνωρίζειν, ὡς τὴν οἰκίαν ἀνθρώπους [tēn heautōn hekaston kalian anagnōrizein, hōs tēn oikian anthrōpous], that each recognizes its own nest, as men do their house: which Aelian sets out at length in book 3, ch. 13 and 23. And perhaps from the same source it came about that the royal couriers were called by the Syrians and Persians Ἄγγαροι [Angaroi], aggari or angari. For the angari were believed to rival cranes in speed of running. Φασί τινες θᾶττον τῶν γεράνων ταύτην τὴν πορείαν ἀνύττειν [Phasi tines thatton tōn geranōn tautēn tēn poreian anyttein] (says Xenophon in book 8 of the Cyropaedia): Some report that this journey is accomplished faster than the flight of cranes. II, 631 That the running of the angari is treated there will be clear to anyone who reads the passage. Among the Cypriots, however, agor was a bird very different from the crane, namely the eagle, if we believe these words of Hesychius: Ἀγώρ· ἀετὸς, Κύπριοι [Agōr: aetos, Kyprioi]. But perhaps it had been written Ἀγώρ· γερανός [Agōr: geranos]; and, the first part of the word γερανὸς [geranos] having been omitted because of its similar ending to the preceding word, ἀετός [aetos] was made out of ἀνὸς [anos] R4. ¶

R4 Rosenmüller, II, 631 But I can scarcely believe that Bochart has established anything certain by this whole very learned discussion. Let us weigh the force of his arguments. One will look in vain in this entire chapter for any explicit statement of them. At the very outset he assumes that עגור is the crane, but he is completely silent about the reasons that led him to it. Yet, so far as one can gather from the course of the whole discussion, he seems to have reasoned thus: "From the two passages of Isaiah and Jeremiah it is clear that עגור is a migratory bird and one using a strained voice; and we find both of these in the crane; therefore עגור is the crane." Nothing would be more certain than this, if those two features were peculiar to cranes. As it is, since there are many migratory birds that use a strained voice, you see how unsafe this argument is unless weightier ones are added. But Bochart is wholly without these, so that there is not even one of the ancients on whose authority he can safely rely. For the Syriac and the Chaldee put crane where the Hebrew text has סוס, but swallow in place of עגור; unless you say that the words have been transposed in both translators, which Bochart assumes without any adequate reason, merely in favour of his preconceived opinion. Equally weak is the other argument for the crane, drawn from the fact that the Arabic عجر [ʿjr] means he returned; for on that basis all migratory birds whatsoever could have been so named. But what chiefly restrains me from taking עגור as the name of the crane is this: that in Isaiah chirping (צפצף) is attributed to the birds סוס and עגור. — VELTHUSEN (in Symbolae Kilonienses vol. 2, p. 273—390) labours hard to show that the Hebrew עגור is the same word as نغر [nughar], the Arabic name of the nightingale, with an interchange of ن and ע (whose related sound in pronunciation once approached ng, like the French n in mon, ton), and of ג and غ, examples of whose interchange Schultens [=Albert Schultens, Liber Jobi (1737)] gives in the Clavis Dialectorum p. 199. He further argues that the nightingale was in the minds both of the Alexandrians, when they put στρουθια [strouthia], sparrows, in Jeremiah, that bird being of their kind; and of the Chaldee and the Syriac, who in their Jeremiah by כורכיא (from the verb כָּרַךְ to cover) would indicate a bird that hides itself amid thick foliage in mourning, i.e. the nightingale, and by that word sought at the same time to express the same notion of hiding in the verb עגר that can be derived from the Arabic عجر [ʿjr], in Conjugation 5 he covered himself. How contrived and far-fetched all this is, anyone will easily notice for himself. — EVERARD SCHEIDIUS, in his Dissertatio ad Canticum Hiskiae p. 86 ff., taking our עגור in Isaiah as a passive participle of the verb עגר, which, on the testimony of Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and Firuzabadi, has the notion of twisting and bending, considers it an epithet of the swallow, which, struck with fear, wheels this way and that and repeatedly turns aside in its course. — Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791) and Clavis über den Jesaias (1793)] too, in the Clavis to Isaiah, took the word for a participle; but since he there regards סוס as the name of the horse (cf. note 4 to the preceding Chapter), he interprets עגור as disturbed, anxious, calling to his aid the Syriac ܥܓܘܪܐ [עגורא] and also the Arabic عجر [ʿjr], to turn aside in the way, to wheel, so that the poet, tossed by the heat of fever, compares himself with an exhausted horse, panting from the depths of its chest and uttering a shrill and sharp sound, which is expressed by the verb II, 632 צפצף. — To me עגור seems to be the name of a bird in Isaiah too, chiefly for this reason: that in Jer 8:7 the same words סיס and עגור are joined, and joined in such a way that it is evident that עגור is the name of a bird, like תור and סיס, which precede. But which species of bird is meant, since the other dialects are silent and the ancients disagree remarkably, no one today can say.
