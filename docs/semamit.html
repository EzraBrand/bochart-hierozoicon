Probatur, שממית semamith Prov. XXX, 28. non esse Simiam, neque Araneam, sed Stellionem.

It is proved that שממית semamith in Prov 30:28 is neither the ape nor the spider, but the stellio.

E quatuor animalibus, quorum solertiam depraedicat Solomon XXX, 28. ul-timum est שממית semamith, quod manibus apprehendere scribit, et esse in Regum palatiis. De hoc animali magna lis inter doctores Hebraeos. Quibusdam explicantibus תולעת של מטר pluvialem vermem, aliis שרץ של כנפים, re- ptile alatum, aut שרץ מה מהעופים, reptile aliquod ex eorum numero, quae vo- lant. R. Ionas semamith Arabica voce خطاف chottaph, id est, hirundinem, exponit, quasi sit idem, quod סנונית senunith, quomodo hirundinem Chaldaei vocant, et Talmudici doctores. Quod est prorsus absurdum, cum hirundo non col. 1084 habeat manus, quibus contrectet et apprehendat, neque sit ex terrestribus animalculis, de quibus hic agit Solomon. Quatuor sunt, inquit, pusilla terrae. Itaque haec explicatio nihil habet sanum. Aben Ezra, Emmanuel et Kabuenaki, ut haec vitarent incommoda, שממית semamith maluerunt esse simiam, animal solertissimum, et in regum deliciis, et manibus instructum hominis instar. Sed meo judicio majus, quam ut censeri possit בקטני ארץ in pusillis animalculis, qualia sunt formica, choerogryllius et locusta. His enim exemplis Solomon antea usus est. Allusionem vocis semamith ad simiam quidam incassum urgent. Neque enim aliunde simiae nomen, quam a simis naribus. Ideo iidem enares a) olim dicti. Et Πιθηκοῦσσαι Graecorum Latinis Enariae insulae, ut in Solinum observat Cl. Salmasius. Multo aliter sentiunt R. Levi, Kimchius et Elias, nempe שממית semamith esse araneam, tam in regum turribus, quam in pauperum tabernis diversari solitam, atque inibi telam texere tanto pedum artificio, ut manuum vicem supplere queant. Ita etiam Arabs interpres manu-scriptus, qui hic habet العنكبوت araneam. Cui interpretationi summo consensu subscribunt Santes, Arias, Mercerus, Munsterus, Castalio, Iunius, Tremellius, Angli, Itali, Genevenses, et si qui alii sunt recentiores interpretes. At observare debuerant viri, Hebraicae linguae longe peritissimi, nullibi, nec in Scriptura, nec in veterum Hebraeorum scriptis araneam dici שממית semamith; sed ubique עכביש accabis, unde Chaldaicum עכובית accobith; et Arabicum عنكبوت ancabuths hodieque in usu. Praeterea fateor, pedes aranearum quandoque vocari digitos. Ut in Ranis Aristophanis Actu quinto, II, 511 Εἰλίσσετε δακτύλοις φάλαγγες ἰσότονα πηνίσματα,

Of the four animals whose cleverness Solomon extols in XXX, 28., the last is שממית semamith, which he writes takes hold with its hands, and is in kings' palaces. Over this animal there is a great dispute among the Hebrew doctors. Some explain it as תולעת של מטר, a rain-worm; others as שרץ של כנפים, a winged creeping thing, or שרץ מה מהעופים, some creeping thing of the number of those that fly. R. Jonah [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim] explains semamith by the Arabic word خطاف [khaṭṭāf] chottaph, that is, the swallow, as though it were the same as סנונית senunith, as the Chaldeans and the Talmudic doctors call the swallow. This is utterly absurd, since the swallow does not col. 1084 have hands with which to handle and grasp, nor is it one of the small land creatures with which Solomon deals here: There are four, he says, little things of the earth. So this explanation has nothing sound in it. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra], Emmanuel and Kabuenaki, to avoid these difficulties, preferred that שממית semamith be the ape, a most clever animal, a delight of kings, and furnished with hands like a man. But in my judgement it is too large to be counted בקטני ארץ among the little creatures, such as are the ant, the choerogryllius and the locust; for these are the examples Solomon used before. Some press in vain the resemblance of the word semamith to simia [ape]. For the name simia comes from nowhere else than from the simae [snub] noses. That is why the same animals were once called enares [noseless] a), and the Πιθηκοῦσσαι [Pithēkoussai] of the Greeks are the Enariae islands of the Latins, as the distinguished Salmasius observes on Solinus. Quite otherwise think R. Levi, Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Elias: namely, that שממית semamith is the spider, which is accustomed to dwell in the towers of kings as much as in the huts of the poor, and there to weave its web with such skill of its feet that they can take the place of hands. So too the manuscript Arabic translator, who has here العنكبوت [al-ʿankabūt], the spider. To this interpretation Santes, Arias, Mercerus, Munster, Castalio, Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], the English, Italian and Genevan versions, and any other more recent translators subscribe with complete agreement. But these men, by far the most skilled in the Hebrew language, ought to have observed that nowhere, neither in Scripture nor in the writings of the ancient Hebrews, is the spider called שממית semamith, but everywhere עכביש accabis, whence the Chaldee עכובית accobith and the Arabic عنكبوت [ʿankabūt] ancabuths, still in use today. Moreover, I admit that the feet of spiders are sometimes called fingers, as in the Frogs of Aristophanes, Act 5, II, 511 Εἰλίσσετε δακτύλοις φάλαγγες ἰσότονα πηνίσματα [Eilissete daktylois phalanges isotona pēnismata],

Digitis torquete, o araneae, licia, quibus textorium jugum intenditur. Et Ovi- dii Metamorphoseως libri sexti Fabula secunda,

Twist with your fingers, o spiders, the threads by which the weaver's beam is stretched. And in Ovid's Metamorphoses, book 6, fable 2,

In latere exiles digiti pro cruribus haerent.

Thin fingers cling to her sides in place of legs.

Et in Pisidae Hexaëmero versu 1152.

And in Pisides' Hexaemeron, line 1152:

Τίς τοὺς ἀράχνας λεπτοδακτύλους πλάσας, Νήθειν διδάσκει, καὶ πρὸς ἔργα τοὺς μίτους Ἐκ τῶν παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς ἐντέρων ἀναπτύειν;

Τίς τοὺς ἀράχνας λεπτοδακτύλους πλάσας, Νήθειν διδάσκει, καὶ πρὸς ἔργα τοὺς μίτους Ἐκ τῶν παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς ἐντέρων ἀναπτύειν; [Tis tous arachnas leptodaktylous plasas, Nēthein didaskei, kai pros erga tous mitous Ek tōn par' autois enterōn anaptyein?]

Quis araneas tenuibus digitis factas nere docet, et ad opera filos ex propriis visce- ribus exspuere? Sed qui manus illis tribuat, neminem reperire est. Quamvis enim araneae pedes digitos utcumque imitentur, tamen in iis nihil est, quod ad manus formam accedat. Quin apud Symposium Aenigmate decimo septimo aranea diserte negat, ullas sibi esse manus,

Who teaches the spiders, made with slender fingers, to spin, and to spit out threads for their works from their own entrails? But one cannot find anyone who attributes hands to them. For although the spider's feet imitate fingers after a fashion, there is nothing in them that approaches the form of hands. Indeed, in Symphosius, Riddle 17, the spider expressly denies that it has any hands:

Nulla mihi manus est, pedibus tamen omnia fiunt.

I have no hand, yet everything is done by my feet.

Dato tamen, non concesso, araneae pedes pro manibus posse haberi, quia manuum vice funguntur: tamen, eam manibus apprehendere, non dixisset Divinus Scriptor, sed nere, vel texere, vel stamina ducere: quia, quod pedibus contrectat et apprehendit, leve est, et illi commune cum multis animalibus; quod texit, peculiare et θαυματῶδες. Sed neque eam dixisset esse in regum palatiis; quia ad regalis aulae cultum non satis conveniunt aranearum sordes. Et diversantur potius in pauperis Euclionis aedibus, in quibus

But granting, without conceding, that the spider's feet can be taken for hands because they perform the office of hands, the Divine Writer would still not have said that it takes hold with its hands, but that it spins, or weaves, or draws out threads: for that it handles and grasps with its feet is a slight matter, and shared with many animals; that it weaves is peculiar and θαυματῶδες [thaumatōdes]. Nor would he have said that it is in kings' palaces, since the filth of spiders does not well suit the elegance of a royal court. They rather dwell in the house of poor Euclio, in which

— — — nihil est quaesti furibus, Ita inaniis sunt oppletae, atque araneis.

— — — there is nothing for thieves to gain, so filled is it with emptiness and spiders.

Ut in Aulularia scribit Plautus. In omnium ore versatur Apologus de podagra et aranea, inter quas convenisse ajunt, ut illa in opulentiorum palatiis, haec in pauperum tuguriis diversaretur. Proinde cum aliquis araneas in aedibus, aut in arca, aut in vasis, aut in loculis habere dicitur, idem est, ac si dicatur, in te- nui re vivere. Hinc apud Festum Afranius,

As Plautus writes in the Aulularia. Everyone knows the fable of the gout and the spider, who, they say, agreed that the former should dwell in the palaces of the rich and the latter in the hovels of the poor. Accordingly, when someone is said to have spiders in his house, or in his chest, or in his vessels, or in his purse, it is the same as saying that he lives in slender circumstances. Hence Afranius in Festus:

Tamne arcula tua plena est aranearum?

Is your little chest so full of spiders?

Et Catullus, ut se excuset apud Fabullum, quod vocatum ad coenam minus laute excipiat:

And Catullus, excusing himself to Fabullus for entertaining him, invited to dinner, less sumptuously:

— — — —, tui Catulli Plenus sacculus est aranearum. col. 1085

— — — —, your Catullus's purse is full of cobwebs. col. 1085

Hinc Hesiodus Operum versu 473.

Hence Hesiod, Works, line 473.

Ἐκ δ᾽ ἀγγέων ἐλάσειας ἀράχνια, II, 512 E vasis autem ejicies araneas, id est, inanitatem et inopiam. Proinde sic explicat Scholiastes, Εἰ καλῶς ἀροτριάσεις, καὶ σπείρεις, ἁδροὺς ἕξεις στάχους (lege στά-χυας). Ἐκ δὲ τῶν σιτοφόρων σου κιβωτίων τῶν μέχρι τότε κενῶν ἑστηκότων, καὶ διὰ τοῦτο ἀραχνιέντων, ἐξελάσειας τὰ ἀράχνια, καὶ ἐμπλήσεις δηλονότι ταῦτα τοῦ σίτου· Si bene araveris et seminaveris, densas habebis spicas: et ex frumentariis vasis tuis, quae eo usque vacua fuerant, ac proinde araneis plena, araneas ejicies, ut scilicet illa frumento impleas. Ideo Solomonem araneis domum assignasse in regum palatiis, parum est verissimile.

Ἐκ δ᾽ ἀγγέων ἐλάσειας ἀράχνια [Ek d᾽ angeōn elaseias arachnia], II, 512 But you will drive the spiders out of the vessels, that is, emptiness and want. Accordingly the Scholiast explains it thus: Εἰ καλῶς ἀροτριάσεις, καὶ σπείρεις, ἁδροὺς ἕξεις στάχους (lege στάχυας). Ἐκ δὲ τῶν σιτοφόρων σου κιβωτίων τῶν μέχρι τότε κενῶν ἑστηκότων, καὶ διὰ τοῦτο ἀραχνιέντων, ἐξελάσειας τὰ ἀράχνια, καὶ ἐμπλήσεις δηλονότι ταῦτα τοῦ σίτου [Ei kalōs arotriaseis, kai speireis, hadrous hexeis stachous (lege stachyas). Ek de tōn sitophorōn sou kibōtiōn tōn mechri tote kenōn hestēkotōn, kai dia touto arachnientōn, exelaseias ta arachnia, kai emplēseis dēlonoti tauta tou sitou]; If you plough and sow well, you will have thick ears of grain; and from your grain vessels, which until then had been empty and therefore full of spiders, you will drive out the spiders, namely so that you may fill them with grain. Therefore it is hardly likely that Solomon assigned spiders a home in the palaces of kings.

Quid igitur erit שממית semamith? Plane aliud animal, nempe stellio: quod tam multa probant, ut in alia interpretatione quaerenda docti frustra se fatigent, cum haec vera et certa sit, et loco maxime congrua. Hujus auctores habeo primum LXX Interpretes, quibus שממית semamith est καλαβώτης, stellio. Fallitur enim Mercerus post Complutenses, qui καλαβώτην ibi piscis genus esse affirmant. In piscium quidem genere est καλαβώτης, sed et in lacertorum. Utrumque est in Hesychio: Καλαβώτης· ἰχθῦς ποῖος, καὶ σαῦρος. Nimirum καλαβώτης idem, qui ἀσκαλαβώτης. Hinc Levit. XI, 30. Complutensis et Parisiensis editio, pro Hebraeo הלטאה, καλαβώτην habent, ubi reliquae ἀσκα-λαβώτην. Neque vero quisquam credat, Graecos ita insanivisse, ut ἐν ἐλαχί-στοις ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς piscem computaverint. 2. Graecis addo Hieronymum, apud quem שממית semamith hic est stellio. 3. Syrum quoque interpretem, cui ܐܡܩܬܐ amaktha, vel amkatha, id ipsum est, ut supra b) abunde probavimus in capite de אנקה anaka. 4. Ut et Chaldaeum אקמתא akmetha, mutato literarum ordine, ut in forma ex μόρφη.

What, then, will שממית semamith be? Plainly another animal, namely the stellio. This is proved by so many arguments that learned men weary themselves in vain seeking another interpretation, since this one is true and certain, and fits the passage best. As its authorities I have, first, the LXX translators, for whom שממית semamith is καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs], the stellio. For Mercerus, following the Complutensians, is mistaken in asserting that καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs] there is a kind of fish. Καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs] is indeed among the fishes, but also among the lizards. Both are in Hesychius: Καλαβώτης· ἰχθῦς ποῖος, καὶ σαῦρος [Kalabōtēs; ichthys poios, kai sauros]. Clearly καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs] is the same as ἀσκαλαβώτης [askalabōtēs]. Hence at Lev 11:30 the Complutensian and Parisian editions have καλαβώτην [kalabōtēn] for the Hebrew הלטאה, where the others have ἀσκαλαβώτην [askalabōtēn]. Nor should anyone believe that the Greeks were so mad as to count a fish among ἐν ἐλαχίστοις ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς [en elachistois epi tēs gēs]. 2. To the Greeks I add Jerome, in whom שממית semamith here is the stellio. 3. Also the Syriac translator, for whom ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] amaktha, or amkatha, is the very same thing, as we proved abundantly above b) in the chapter on אנקה anaka. 4. As also the Chaldee אקמתא akmetha, with the order of the letters changed, as in forma [form] from μόρφη [morphē].

Praeterea illa ipsa vox שממית semamith, pro lacertae genere, adeoque pro stellione, nobis etiam aliunde nota est. Quippe Lev. XI, 30. legitur [?] vel [?] semamitha, in Samaritano interprete, et in Chaldaeis duobus, Ionathane, et altero, cui ab urbe nomen. In illo, pro אנקה anaka: in his pro לטאה letaa, ubi in Graeca versione καλαβώτης, et stellio in Romana. Hebraeos denique veteres sic usurpavisse, palam est ex eo, quod legitur in Talmudico tractatu De Sabbatho cap. 8. pag. 77. b. אימת שממית על העקרב, Terror semamith super scorpionem. Quibus verbis innuunt, hoc animal scorpioni esse terrori. Unde apparet, semamith non esse araneam. Eam enim cum scorpione inimicitias exercere, aut illi terrori esse, nemo scripsit. At de scorpione sic Plinius libri vigesimi noni capite quarto, Scorpionibus contrarius maxime stellio traditur, ut visu quoque pavorem iis afferat, et torporem frigidi sudoris. Et Aelianus II, 513 Historiae libri sexti capite vigesimo secundo, Ἔχθιστα δὲ τῷ μὲν λέοντι πῦρ, καὶ ἀλεκτρυών· ὕαινα δὲ τῇ παρδάλει· σκορπίος δὲ ἀσκαλαβώτῃ, νάρ-κη γοῦν τὸν σκορπίον καταλαμβάνει προαχθέντος οἱ τοῦ ζώου τοῦ προειρημένου· Inimicissima sunt leoni quidem ignis, et gallus; et hyaena pantherae; et stellioni scorpius, quo admoto, torpore afficitur. Et Galenus libri primi De theriaca ad Pisonem capite nono, Ὁ οὖν ἀσκαλαβώτης, ὁραθεὶς ὑπὸ τῶν σκορπίων, πή-γνυσιν αὐτοὺς, καὶ οὕτως ἀναιρεῖ· Stellio, visus a scorpionibus, obstupefacit eos, atque ita interimit. Et Isidorus libri duodecimi capite quarto, Scorpionibus stellio adeo contrarius traditur, ut, viso eo, pavorem iis afferat et terrorem. Et Phile capite vigesimo nono ex Aeliano, perperam lecto et intellecto:

Moreover, that very word שממית semamith, for a kind of lizard, and indeed for the stellio, is known to us from elsewhere as well. For at Lev 11:30 one reads [?] or [?] semamitha in the Samaritan translator and in the two Chaldee ones, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and the other, named after the city: in the former for אנקה anaka, in the latter for לטאה letaa, where the Greek version has καλαβώτης [kalabōtēs] and the Roman stellio. Finally, that the ancient Hebrews used it in this way is evident from what is read in the Talmudic tractate On the Sabbath, ch. 8, p. 77b: אימת שממית על העקרב, The terror of the semamith is upon the scorpion. By these words they indicate that this animal is a terror to the scorpion. Hence it appears that the semamith is not the spider. For no one has written that it carries on enmity with the scorpion or is a terror to it. But concerning the scorpion Pliny, book 29, chapter 4, says thus: The stellio is reported to be especially hostile to scorpions, so that even by its sight it brings them fear and a torpor of cold sweat. And Aelian, History II, 513 book 6, chapter 22: Ἔχθιστα δὲ τῷ μὲν λέοντι πῦρ, καὶ ἀλεκτρυών· ὕαινα δὲ τῇ παρδάλει· σκορπίος δὲ ἀσκαλαβώτῃ, νάρκη γοῦν τὸν σκορπίον καταλαμβάνει προαχθέντος οἱ τοῦ ζώου τοῦ προειρημένου [Echthista de tō men leonti pyr, kai alektryōn; hyaina de tē pardalei; skorpios de askalabōtē, narkē goun ton skorpion katalambanei proachthentos hoi tou zōou tou proeirēmenou]; Most hostile to the lion are fire and the cock; the hyena to the panther; and the scorpion to the stellio, at whose approach it is seized with torpor. And Galen, On Theriac, to Piso, book 1, chapter 9: Ὁ οὖν ἀσκαλαβώτης, ὁραθεὶς ὑπὸ τῶν σκορπίων, πήγνυσιν αὐτοὺς, καὶ οὕτως ἀναιρεῖ [Ho oun askalabōtēs, horatheis hypo tōn skorpiōn, pēgnysin autous, kai houtōs anairei]; The stellio, when seen by scorpions, stupefies them and so kills them. And Isidore, book 12, chapter 4: The stellio is reported to be so hostile to scorpions that, when it is seen, it brings them fear and terror. And Phile, chapter 29, from Aelian, wrongly read and understood:

— — — ἡ δ᾽ ὕαινα πάρδαλιν, Καὶ ἀσκαλαβώτην ὁ φθορεὺς κλὼψ σκορπίος, Νάρκη δὲ τοῦτον· — — — — —

— — — ἡ δ᾽ ὕαινα πάρδαλιν, Καὶ ἀσκαλαβώτην ὁ φθορεὺς κλὼψ σκορπίος, Νάρκη δὲ τοῦτον· — — — — — [hē d᾽ hyaina pardalin, Kai askalabōtēn ho phthoreus klōps skorpios, Narkē de touton]

Et hyaena pantheram (supple δυσμενῆ κρινεῖ, infensam putat, quod e superiori- col. 1086 bus repetendum) et stellionem perditor fur scorpius, torpedo autem illum. Cum in Aeliani verbis, νάρκη γοῦν τὸν σκορπίον καταλαμβάνει, vox νάρκη non sit torpedo piscis, sed torpor, qui scorpionem invadit, stellione viso. Si cum nonnullis corrigas ναρκοῖ δὲ τοῦτον, eum autem (stellionem) obstupefacit (scorpius), Phile rem falsissimam dixerit, nempe stellionem a scorpione obstupefieri, cum contra scorpionem obstupefaciat stellio. Quin Democritus, Plinius, Galenus, Aëtius, Diophanes, alii stellionem dissectum, et in oleo mersum, et putrefactum praescribunt, ut remedium efficax contra scorpionum ictus. Neque in eo leve momentum est, quod hodieque vulgari Graecorum lingua stellio appellatur σαμιαμίνθη, palam detorto nomine ex Hebraeo שממית semamith. Glossae, Ἀσκαλαβώτης· τὸ σαμιαμίνθη. A Gesnero scribitur θαμιάμιθος, et ψαμα-μύθη· a Bellonio SAMIAMITOS, qui pro Chalcidica lacerta perperam sumit. Vide Salmasium in Solinum pag. 1161.

And the hyena the panther (supply δυσμενῆ κρινεῖ [dysmenē krinei], considers hostile, which is to be repeated from the preceding col. 1086) and the thieving destroyer scorpion the stellio, but numbness seizes it. For in Aelian's words, νάρκη γοῦν τὸν σκορπίον καταλαμβάνει [narkē goun ton skorpion katalambanei], the word νάρκη [narkē] is not the torpedo [electric ray] fish, but the torpor [numbness] which seizes the scorpion when it sees the stellio. If, with some, you correct it to ναρκοῖ δὲ τοῦτον [narkoi de touton], but it (the scorpion) numbs it (the stellio), Phile would have said something utterly false, namely that the stellio is numbed by the scorpion, whereas on the contrary the stellio numbs the scorpion. Indeed Democritus, Pliny, Galen, Aëtius, Diophanes and others prescribe a stellio cut open, steeped in oil and left to putrefy, as an effective remedy against the stings of scorpions. Nor is it of slight weight that even today in the vulgar tongue of the Greeks the stellio is called σαμιαμίνθη [samiaminthē], a name plainly distorted from the Hebrew שממית semamith. The Glossae: Ἀσκαλαβώτης· τὸ σαμιαμίνθη [Askalabōtēs; to samiaminthē]. By Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] it is written θαμιάμιθος [thamiamithos] and ψαμαμύθη [psamamythē]; by Bellonius SAMIAMITOS, who wrongly takes it for the Chalcidian lizard. See Salmasius on Solinus, p. 1161.

Sunt, qui שׁממית schemamith per Schin scribunt, ac si fiat a שמם schamam, id est, obstupefacere. Quod ipsum recte convenit naturae stellionis, qui scorpionem solo visu obstupefacit, ut docuimus. Sed in Kimchio et Bibliorum purissimis exemplaribus שׂממית semamith scribitur per Sin Semol; ut סממית per Samech in Talmudicis, et in paraphrasi Samaritana. Ita, ut deducatur a סמם, non a שמם, id est, a veneno, non a stupore. Ut igitur vera ratio nominis intelligatur, sciendum est, duas tradi stellionum species; unam innocui morsus, alteram venenati. Quamvis, quibus nascantur locis, non conveniat inter scriptores. Venenatos Philosophus c) Italiae et Siciliae adscribit, innoxios Graeciae. II, 514 Contra Theophrastus apud Plinium libri octavi capite trigesimo primo auctor est, stelliones, mortiferi in Graecia morsus, innoxios esse in Sicilia. Etiam in Thracia stellio perniciosi morsus est, ut habetur in Nicandro. Et Compluti Bustamantinus stelliones tam perniciosos esse asserit, ut, casu cibis permisti, integros populos vastaverint. Hinc in stellionis morsum tot remedia praescribunt Aëtius, Paulus et Avicenna. Et Columella libri decimi capite septimo alvearium foramina vult esse angustissima, ne venenatus stellio per laxiora spatia januae favos populetur. Venenati epithetum addidit διακριτικῶς, quia alius est innoxius. Venenato stellionis nomen magis convenit, si vere asserit Verrius apud Varronem et Festum, stellionem ideo dici, quod virus stillet. Eidem nomen סממית semamith impositum; quia סם sam Hebraeis et Syris est φάρμακον, seu venenum; et Arabice سم samma verbum veneno inficere. Ergo proprie סממית semamith est σαῦρα φαρμακὶς, vel φαρμακῖτις. Quod ipsum nomen a Graecis usurpari video sensu parum diverso. Nicander in Alexipharmacis versu 537.

There are some who write שׁממית schemamith with Shin, as if it came from שמם schamam, that is, to stupefy. This itself fits well the nature of the stellio, which stupefies the scorpion by its mere sight, as we have shown. But in Kimchi and in the purest copies of the Bible it is written שׂממית semamith with Sin Semol, just as סממית with Samech in the Talmudic writings and in the Samaritan paraphrase. Thus it is to be derived from סמם, not from שמם, that is, from poison, not from stupor. So, to understand the true reason for the name, one must know that two species of stellio are reported: one with a harmless bite, the other with a venomous one. The writers, however, do not agree in which places they are born. The Philosopher c) assigns the venomous ones to Italy and Sicily, the harmless ones to Greece. II, 514 On the other hand Theophrastus, in Pliny, book 8, chapter 31, states that stellios, deadly in their bite in Greece, are harmless in Sicily. In Thrace too the stellio has a pernicious bite, as is found in Nicander. And Bustamantinus of Complutum asserts that stellios are so pernicious that, mixed by chance with food, they have laid waste entire populations. Hence Aëtius, Paulus and Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] prescribe so many remedies against the bite of the stellio. And Columella, book 10, chapter 7, wants the openings of beehives to be very narrow, lest the venomous stellio ravage the combs through the wider spaces of the entrance. He added the epithet venomous διακριτικῶς [diakritikōs], because another kind is harmless. The name stellio suits the venomous one better, if Verrius, in Varro and Festus, truly asserts that the stellio is so called because it drips venom. To the same was given the name סממית semamith, because סם sam among the Hebrews and Syrians is φάρμακον [pharmakon], or poison; and in Arabic سم [samm] samma is the verb to infect with poison. Therefore סממית semamith is properly σαῦρα φαρμακὶς [saura pharmakis], or φαρμακῖτις [pharmakitis]. I see that this very name is used by the Greeks in a sense little different. Nicander in the Alexipharmaca, line 537:

Φαρμακίδος σαύρης πολυμήδεος, ἣν σαλαμάνδρην Κλείουσιν. — — — — — — —

Vides, ibi σαῦραν φαρμακίδα salamandram esse, lacertae genus, stellioni tam simile, ut pro stellionis specie a nonnullis habeatur. Proinde ibi Scholiastes, Ἡ σαλαμάνδρα· εἶδος ἀσκαλαβώτου. Tamen in Aëtii Tetrab. 4. Serm. 4. cap. 52. salamandra, stellio et lacerta venenata Graecis σαῦρα φαρμακῖτις, diversa sunt animalcula, sed inter se simillima. Salamandra, inquit, animal est, stellioni simile, asperius, et scabrum magis lacerta venenata. Forte stellionis nomine solum innoxium, et nomine lacertae pharmacitidis, seu venenatae, a salamandra diversae, stellionem, morsu noxium, intelligit. Sic lacerta pharmacitis erit eadem, quae סממית semamith.

You see that there the σαῦρα φαρμακίς [saura pharmakis] is the salamander, a kind of lizard so similar to the stellio that some take it for a species of stellio. Accordingly the Scholiast there: Ἡ σαλαμάνδρα· εἶδος ἀσκαλαβώτου [Hē salamandra; eidos askalabōtou]. Yet in Aëtius, Tetrab. 4, Serm. 4, ch. 52, the salamander, the stellio and the venomous lizard, in Greek σαῦρα φαρμακῖτις [saura pharmakitis], are different little animals, but very similar to one another. The salamander, he says, is an animal similar to the stellio, rougher, and more scaly than the venomous lizard. Perhaps by the name stellio he means only the harmless one, and by the name pharmacitis lizard, or venomous lizard, distinct from the salamander, the stellio with a harmful bite. Thus the pharmacitis lizard will be the same as the סממית semamith.

The same point is supported by the fact that the Arabs record two species of venomous stellio, which differ only in size. In the name sammabras, compounded of سام [sām] and ابرص [abraṣ], the first part is shortened from semamith and means venomous; the second means leprous, or marked with spots of leprosy. So, literally, sammabras is leprous semamith, or a species of lizard notable for venom and for spots of leprosy. For two things are to be noted in the stellio: first, that it is venomous, which has already been treated; second, that it is leprous, that is, marked with spots as if of leprosy. The Arabic SAMMABRAS contains both of these, the Hebrew SEMAMITH only the first. — — — — aptumque colori Nomen habet variis stellatus corpora guttis.

Solertiae stellionis multa sunt argumenta. Hesychius in Κωλώτης, stellionem ait, εὐστόχως ἅλλεσθαι περὶ τὰς μυίας, industrie salire circa muscas; nempe, ut eas captet. Phavorinus inepte, περὶ τοὺς μῦς, circa mures: quibus cum stellione nihil est negotii. Sed de muscis etiam Augustinus libri decimi Confessionum capite trigesimo quinto: Quid, cum me domi sedentem stellio, muscas captans, vel aranea, retibus suis irruentes implicans, saepe intentum facit? Aliud affert Theophrastus in libro De anim. invid. sed minus certum: Ὅτι ὁ γαλεώτης, φθονῶν τῆς ὠφελείας τοῖς ἀνθρώποις, καταπίνει τὸ δέρμα, ὅταν ἐκδύηται· ἔστι γὰρ βοήθημα ἐπιλήπτῳ· Stellionem, hominum utilitati invidentem, proprium vorare corium, quamprimum eo exuitur. Iuvat enim illud comitiali morbo laborantes. Idem habent Antigonus Mirabilium capite vigesimo quarto, et Apulejus Apologia pro se prima. Et inde concludit Plinius libri trigesimi capite decimo, nullum animal fraudulentius invidere homini. Indidem stellionum et stellionatus nomen in maledictum translatum. Sunt et qui Galeotas, in Sicilia portentorum interpretes solertissimos, ab hujus animalis industria nomen traxisse putent. Quo haec Stephani referunt: Γαλεῶται μάντεων εἶδος Σικελῶν· Γα-λεὸς δὲ, ὁ ἀσκαλαβώτης (ubi pro γαλεὸς legendum γαλεώτης). Sed quia Hebraice גלה gala est revelare, quod vatum maxime proprium, Galeotarum nomen Hebraicum esse suspicor, ut et alia magno numero, quae, cum Phoenicum coloniis, in Siciliam translata fuisse, alibi probavimus.

There are many proofs of the stellio's skill. Hesychius, under Κωλώτης [Kōlōtēs], says that the stellio εὐστόχως ἅλλεσθαι περὶ τὰς μυίας [eustochōs hallesthai peri tas myias], leaps skilfully about flies; that is, in order to catch them. Phavorinus ineptly has περὶ τοὺς μῦς [peri tous mys], about mice: with which the stellio has nothing to do. But Augustine also speaks of flies in book 10 of the Confessions, chapter 35: What of it, when, as I sit at home, a stellio catching flies, or a spider entangling them as they rush into its webs, often holds my attention? Theophrastus, in the book De anim. invid., adduces another proof, but a less certain one: Ὅτι ὁ γαλεώτης, φθονῶν τῆς ὠφελείας τοῖς ἀνθρώποις, καταπίνει τὸ δέρμα, ὅταν ἐκδύηται· ἔστι γὰρ βοήθημα ἐπιλήπτῳ· [Hoti ho galeōtēs, phthonōn tēs ōpheleias tois anthrōpois, katapinei to derma, hotan ekdyētai; esti gar boēthēma epilēptō;] The stellio, begrudging men the benefit, devours its own skin as soon as it has shed it. For it helps those suffering from epilepsy. Antigonus has the same in the Mirabilia, chapter 24, and Apuleius in his first Apology for himself. And from this Pliny, book 30, chapter 10, concludes that no animal more deceitfully begrudges man. From the same source the names stelliones [swindlers] and stellionatus [fraud] were transferred into terms of abuse. There are also those who think that the Galeotae, the most skilful interpreters of portents in Sicily, took their name from the cleverness of this animal. To this Stephanus refers these words: Γαλεῶται μάντεων εἶδος Σικελῶν· Γαλεὸς δὲ, ὁ ἀσκαλαβώτης [Galeōtai manteōn eidos Sikelōn; Galeos de, ho askalabōtēs] (where for γαλεὸς [galeos] one should read γαλεώτης [galeōtēs]). But since in Hebrew גלה gala means to reveal, which is most characteristic of seers, I suspect that the name of the Galeotae is Hebrew, like a great many others which, as I have proved elsewhere, were carried over into Sicily with the colonies of the Phoenicians.

But Solomon himself amply proves the stellio's skill with these words: It takes hold with its hands, and is in kings' palaces. Why he attributes hands to it is obscure to no one. Just as lacertus [lizard] is named from lacerti, that is, arms, so the stellio is called in Greek κωλώτης [kōlōtēs] from τοῖς κώλοις [tois kōlois]. Κῶλα [Kōla] are hands and feet. I call the forefeet hands together with Avicenna e), who writes that غظاية [ghaẓāya] adhajam, that is, the salamander (in Avicenna's view), is preserved in honey only if it is gutted and يداها ورجلاها [yadāhā wa-rijlāhā] its hands and feet are cut off: where Dioscorides has φυλάσσεται ἐν μέλιτι ἐξεντεριθεῖσα, τῶν ποδῶν ἀφαιρεθέντων [phylassetai en meliti exenteritheisa, tōn podōn aphairethentōn]. If the salamander has hands, why not also the stellio, an animal very like the salamander? Thus Damir, in the chapter حردون [ḥardūn] Chardaun, or On the land crocodile: له كف ككف الانسان مقسومة الاصابع الا انامل [lahu kaff ka-kaff al-insān maqsūmat al-aṣābiʿ illā anāmil] It has a hand like the hand of a man, with fingers divided into joints. Indeed, you may rightly call the feet of any lizard, especially II, 518 the forefeet, hands, since they are like hands. Gesner, from his own observation: The lacertus is named from its likeness to the lacerti [arms] of a man, because their feet col. 1089 are likewise split into fingers, as if from hollow hands or palms. The same: Very slender fingers, five in front and five behind, with little claws. On the hind feet the longest is the one that occupies the place of the index finger; the thumb is in a lower position, as in the hands of man: but the front feet have the thumb in the same position as the other fingers. You see that the forefeet, or more truly the arms, are in the shape of a human arm and hand, with the same number of fingers, and in the same position. How much better does the name hands suit such arms than the legs of spiders?

Supinum autem incedere, disertis verbis asserit Philosophus Historiae libri noni capite nono, ubi de pico sic agit: Πορεύεται ὕπτιος καθάπερ οἱ ἀσκαλαβῶται· Ingreditur supinus ut stelliones. Et in Mirabilibus, Τὸν δρυο-κολάπτην τὸ ὄρνεον ἐπὶ τῶν δένδρων βαδίζειν φασὶν ὥσπερ τοὺς ἀσκαλαβώτας, καὶ ὕπτιον, καὶ ἐπὶ τὴν γαστέρα· Picum in arboribus incedere ajunt, ut stelliones, tam supinum, quam pronum. Mirum cur ἀσκαλαβώτας feles reddat Plinius, et ὕπτιον in subrectum pro supino, cum dicit libri decimi capite decimo octavo, arborum cavatores (id est, δρυοκολάπτας picos) scandere in subrectum felium modo. Maxime, cum idem libri vigesimi noni capite quarto agnoscat, stellionem Graece vocari coloten, ascalaboten et galeoten: ne quis Graeca stellionis vocabula Plinium ignorasse putet. Tale est, quod legitur in Plutarchi libro De facie in orbe Lunae, esse valde paradoxum, quod Geographi docent, ἀντίποδας οἰκεῖν, ὥσπερ θρίπας, ἢ γαλεώτας, τραπέντα ἄνω τὰ κάτω, τῇ γῇ προϊσχομένους, terram inhabitare antipodes, qui, instar teredinum, aut stellionum, imis corporum partibus sursum obversis, terrae adhaereant, Ubi itidem primi interpretes pro stellionibus feles scripserant.

Now, that the stellio is said to take hold with its hands, I refer either to its manner of moving or to its hunting. For when it walks upside down, hanging from a ceiling, it must either fix its little claws in small cracks or clasp with its hands whatever projects or is rough. That it walks upside down is stated in express words by the Philosopher, History book 9, chapter 9, where he speaks of the woodpecker thus: Πορεύεται ὕπτιος καθάπερ οἱ ἀσκαλαβῶται [poreuetai hyptios kathaper hoi askalabōtai]· It walks upside down like stellios. And in the Mirabilia: Τὸν δρυοκολάπτην τὸ ὄρνεον ἐπὶ τῶν δένδρων βαδίζειν φασὶν ὥσπερ τοὺς ἀσκαλαβώτας, καὶ ὕπτιον, καὶ ἐπὶ τὴν γαστέρα [ton dryokolaptēn to orneon epi tōn dendrōn badizein phasin hōsper tous askalabōtas, kai hyption, kai epi tēn gastera]· They say that the woodpecker walks on trees like stellios, both upside down and face down. It is surprising that Pliny renders ἀσκαλαβώτας [askalabōtas] as feles [cats], and ὕπτιον [hyption] as in subrectum [upwards] instead of supinus [upside down], when he says in book 10, chapter 18, that the tree-hollowers (that is, δρυοκολάπτας [dryokolaptas], woodpeckers) climb upwards in the manner of cats. All the more so since the same author, in book 29, chapter 4, acknowledges that the stellio is called in Greek colotes, ascalabotes and galeotes, so that no one should think Pliny was ignorant of the Greek names of the stellio. Similar is what we read in Plutarch's book On the Face in the Orb of the Moon: that what the geographers teach is highly paradoxical, ἀντίποδας οἰκεῖν, ὥσπερ θρίπας, ἢ γαλεώτας, τραπέντα ἄνω τὰ κάτω, τῇ γῇ προϊσχομένους [antipodas oikein, hōsper thripas, ē galeōtas, trapenta anō ta katō, tē gē proischomenous], that antipodes inhabit the earth, who, like woodworms or stellios, cling to the earth with the lower parts of their bodies turned upward. There too the first translators had written feles [cats] for stelliones [stellios].

Manibus etiam ad venationem stellionem uti diximus, cum muscas captat, aut araneas. Bustamantinus: Arripit manibus secura pollicentibus. Pedes imo fallaces, qui motui inservire tenebantur, vice manuum succedunt, rapinam exercent, et manus sunt illi pedes, quibus innititur in motu, et pedes manus, quibus arripit praedam. Merito igitur Solomon, Manibus apprehendit stellio, seu gradiatur supinus, aut in praedam se conjiciat. Utrumque mirabile et notatu dignissimum in tenui animalculo. Graeci hic habent, Καλαβώτης χερσὶν ἐρειδό-μενος· et Hieronymus, Stellio manibus nititur; Arabs etiam يتنسد بيديه nititur manibus suis: tanquam ad incessum haec referentes. Sed nemo facile dixerit, quid in Chaldaeo sit אקמתא דמסככה בידיה. Neque enim II, 519 capio, quid sibi velit Latina versio, Aranea manibus tegumenta facit. Nec multo melius Gabriel, qui Syrae paraphrasis eadem verba sic reddidit: Stellio, quae serpit manibus suis. Et legerim potius sepit; quia סכך sepire est. Sed quorsum stellio sepire dicatur, aut tegumenta facere, nullus capio. Hic quoque haesit Drusius Observationum libri decimi capite sexto, et locus illi est de mendo suspectus. Et, pro מסככה, sepit, aut tegit, modo legi vult מסבכה, per Beth, implicans, aut perplexum opus faciens; quod dicatur de araneae tela: modo מסמכה, nitens; quod de stellione dicatur: ut sic Chaldaeo conveniat cum Graecis et Romano interprete. Sed Chaldaeum interpretem praestat cum Hebraeo textu conciliare, in quo חפש est apprehendere. Proinde Theodotion reddiderat ἐλλαμβανόμενος. Malim igitur pro מסככה mesacceca legere מפככה mephacceca, Pe pro Samech, quae literae figura non sunt prorsus absimiles. Nempe פכך Chaldaeis idem, quod Hebraeis חפש, capere, apprehendere. Sic Prov. V, 22. in editione Veneta, Funibus peccatorum suorum אתפכך, captus, aut apprehensus est.

We have said that the stellio also uses its hands for hunting, when it catches flies or spiders. Bustamantinus: It seizes with its hands things that promise safety. Indeed its deceptive feet, which were bound to serve for movement, take the place of hands and carry out the plunder; and those are hands to it, the feet on which it rests in moving, and those are feet, the hands with which it seizes its prey. Rightly, then, does Solomon say, The stellio takes hold with its hands, whether it walks upside down or throws itself upon its prey. Both are marvellous and most worthy of note in a tiny creature. The Greeks have here Καλαβώτης χερσὶν ἐρειδόμενος [Kalabōtēs chersin ereidomenos]; and Jerome, The stellio leans on its hands; the Arabic translator also, يتنسد بيديه [yatanassad bi-yadayhi], it leans on its hands: as if referring these words to its manner of moving. But no one could easily say what אקמתא דמסככה בידיה in the Chaldee means. For I do not II, 519 understand what the Latin version means, The spider makes coverings with its hands. Nor much better is Gabriel, who rendered the same words of the Syriac paraphrase thus: The stellio, which creeps with its hands. I would rather read sepit [fences]; for סכך means to fence. But why the stellio should be said to fence or to make coverings, I do not understand at all. Drusius also stuck here, Observations book 10, chapter 6, and he suspects the passage of corruption. And instead of מסככה, fences or covers, he would read sometimes מסבכה, with Beth, entangling or making an intricate work, which would be said of the spider's web; sometimes מסמכה, leaning, which would be said of the stellio: so that the Chaldee would thus agree with the Greek and the Roman translators. But it is better to reconcile the Chaldee translator with the Hebrew text, in which חפש means to take hold. Accordingly Theodotion had rendered ἐλλαμβανόμενος [ellambanomenos]. I would therefore prefer to read, instead of מסככה mesacceca, מפככה mephacceca, Pe for Samech, letters not altogether unlike in shape. For פכך is in Chaldee the same as חפש in Hebrew, to catch, to take hold. Thus Prov 5:22 in the Venice edition: With the cords of his sins אתפכך, he is caught or taken hold of.

Semamith in palatiis regum versari addit Solomon: quod unum habuit col. 1090 Mercerus, quod objiceret. Semamith, inquit, noster interpres stellionem exponit, quod quadrare non videtur, quia in aulis regum non versatur. At in domibus versari stellionem, credo, non ignoravit vir doctissimus, cum in uno ore clament fere, quotquot sunt, qui de stellione scripserunt. Proinde apud Eusebium Porphyrius vocat κατοικιδίους σκαλαβώτας, domesticos stelliones. Suidas, Etymologus, Phavorinus et Graecorum alii definiunt, ζωΰφιον ἐοικὸς σαύρᾳ ἐν τοῖς τοίχοις τῶν οἰκημάτων ἀνέρπον, lacertae simile animalculum, per domorum parietes serpens. Ita etiam Elluchasem, Bagdadensis medicus, in libro De animalibus: Stellio est lacerta, inhabitans domos. Hinc τοιχοβάτην appellat Kiranides. Et in Hispania Catalauni dragon de las casas, id est, draconem domorum. In veteribus praesertim aedificiis habitare, tradunt Niphus in Aristotelem, et Ponzettus in libro De venenis. Sed et in novis conspici, Marcellinus apud Hermolaum asserit. Iuxta Philosophum f) degit in praesepibus: juxta Antonium Liberalem g), παρ᾽ ὀχετὸν, prope cloacas: juxta Plinium h), in locis hostiorum fenestrarumque, aut cavernis sepulcrisve: juxta Arnobium i), sub simulacrorum cavis nidamenta ponit. Eidem assignat Matthiolus k) cava murorum prope terram. Sed laquearia veteres, et convexa tectorum, ubi supinus ambulet. Proinde, praeter Aristophanem et Dioscoridem, quos supra l) jam citavi, etiam in Avicenna m) legas, quandoque stellionem superne in potum cadere, et in venenum II, 520 illud vertere, si in eo moriatur (4). Haec, nisi fallor, abunde probant, agreste animal non esse stellionem, sed domesticum. Porro nullus video, quo privilegio regales domus ab hac peste sint immunes. Imo, quo major in illis est lapidum strues et congeries, eo sunt profundiores in parietibus fissurae et rimulae. Unde non absurde colligas, talia animalcula, quae in murorum foraminibus fere versantur, in regum aulis et palatiis etiam tutius degere, quam in privata domo.

Solomon adds that the semamith is in kings' palaces. This was the one objection that Mercerus had to raise col. 1090. "Our translator," he says, "renders semamith as stellio, which does not seem to fit, because it does not live in the courts of kings." But that the stellio lives in houses was not, I believe, unknown to this most learned man, since nearly all who have written about the stellio proclaim it with one voice. Thus in Eusebius, Porphyry speaks of κατοικιδίους σκαλαβώτας [katoikidious skalabōtas], domestic stellios. Suidas, the Etymologicum, Phavorinus and others among the Greeks define it as ζωΰφιον ἐοικὸς σαύρᾳ ἐν τοῖς τοίχοις τῶν οἰκημάτων ἀνέρπον [zōyphion eoikos saura en tois toichois tōn oikēmatōn anerpon], a little animal like a lizard, creeping along the walls of houses. So also Elluchasem, a physician of Baghdad, in his book On Animals: The stellio is a lizard that inhabits houses. Hence Kiranides calls it τοιχοβάτην [toichobatēn]. And in Spain the Catalans call it dragon de las casas, that is, dragon of the houses. That it lives especially in old buildings is reported by Niphus on Aristotle and by Ponzettus in his book On Poisons. But that it is also seen in new ones is asserted by Marcellinus in Hermolaus. According to the Philosopher f) it lives in mangers; according to Antoninus Liberalis g), παρ᾽ ὀχετὸν [par᾽ ocheton], near drains; according to Pliny h), about doors and windows, or in caves or tombs; according to Arnobius i), it builds its nests under the hollows of statues. Matthiolus k) assigns to it the hollows of walls near the ground. But the ancients assign it ceiling panels and the vaults of roofs, where it may walk upside down. Accordingly, besides Aristophanes and Dioscorides, whom I have already cited above l), you may also read in Avicenna m) that the stellio sometimes falls from above into a drink and turns it II, 520 into poison if it dies in it R4. These things, if I am not mistaken, amply prove that the stellio is not a wild animal but a domestic one. Further, I see no privilege by which royal houses should be immune from this pest. Indeed, the greater the pile and mass of stones in them, the deeper are the fissures and cracks in the walls. From this you may not unreasonably conclude that such little animals, which live mostly in holes in walls, dwell even more safely in the courts and palaces of kings than in a private house.
