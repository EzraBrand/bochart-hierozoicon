Gallus dicitur Arabice ديك dic, et Syriace ܬܪܢܓܠ tarnegol; utroque col. 110 nomine, nisi fallor, valde vetusto. Nam prioris vestigia sunt in Hebraeo דוכיפת dukiphat, quo Moses upupam vocat; voce, ut videtur, composita ex דוך, et כיפה. Ita, ut gallum saxorum, seu saxatilem significet, ut alibi docebimus. Et a Syro ܬܪܢܓܠ tarnegol Phoenices, qui cum Cadmo in Boeotiam venerant, Tanagrae puto dedisse nomen, Boeotiae urbi antiquissimae, in qua Tanagraei galli: quos ut pugnacissimos commendant Varro, Columella, Plinius, Pausanias, Hesychius, Suidas, alii, quorum loca citavimus in nostro col. 111 Chanaan lib. 1. cap. 16. Nempe a gallis illis urbs ipsa Tarnegol, aut Tarnegal, a Phoenicibus appellata est. Et Graeci, l finali rejecto, ut in Ἀννίβα et Ἀσδρού-βα, ex Tarnegal primo Tarnegam, deinde Tanagram fecerunt, unius literae trajectione. Ita, ut urbs Tanagra sit urbs galli, sive gallorum. Horum scilicet tanta fuit nobilitas, ut apud veteres gallus et Ταναγραῖος fere sint ἀντίστροφα, quasi omnes gallos necesse sit Tanagra esse oriundos. Unde est, quod in Gallo Luciani Micyllus dicit Pythagorae, in gallum transmutato, Εἰπὲ δὲ ὅμως, ὦ Μνησάρχου παῖ, ὅπως ἡμῖν ἀντὶ μὲν ἀνθρώπου ὄρνις, ἀντὶ δὲ Σαμίου Τανα-γραῖος ἀναπέφηνας· Tamen expone, Mnesarchi fili, qui factum sit, ut ex homine avis, et ex Samio Tanagraeus nobis apparueris. Tanagraeus, id est, gallus תרנגול. Interim neutrum galli nomen in Propheticis libris apparet. Sed neque satis scitur, quomodo Hebraei veteres gallum appellaverint; quanquam ad gallinaceum genus interpretes multa Prophetarum loca referant, sed conjecturis non satis certis, ut ea percurrenti constabit, quod sequentia palam facient.

The cock is called ديك [dīk] dic in Arabic, and ܬܪܢܓܠ [תרנגל] tarnegol in Syriac; both names, if I am not mistaken, are very old. col. 110 For traces of the former appear in the Hebrew דוכיפת dukiphat, by which Moses names the hoopoe; a word, as it seems, compounded of דוך and כיפה, so that it means cock of the rocks, or rock-dwelling cock, as we shall show elsewhere. And from the Syriac ܬܪܢܓܠ [תרנגל] tarnegol, I think, the Phoenicians who had come with Cadmus into Boeotia gave its name to Tanagra, a very ancient city of Boeotia, home of the Tanagraean cocks. These are praised as most pugnacious by Varro, Columella, Pliny, Pausanias, Hesychius, Suidas and others, whose passages we have cited in our col. 111 Chanaan, book 1, ch. 16. That is, the city itself was called Tarnegol or Tarnegal by the Phoenicians after those cocks. And the Greeks, dropping the final l, as in Ἀννίβα [Anniba] and Ἀσδρούβα [Asdrouba], made from Tarnegal first Tarnegam, then Tanagram, by the transposition of a single letter. Thus the city of Tanagra is the city of the cock, or of cocks. Their renown was so great that among the ancients cock and Ταναγραῖος [Tanagraios] are almost interchangeable, as if all cocks must have originated from Tanagra. Hence in Lucian's Cock, Micyllus says to Pythagoras, who has been transformed into a cock: Εἰπὲ δὲ ὅμως, ὦ Μνησάρχου παῖ, ὅπως ἡμῖν ἀντὶ μὲν ἀνθρώπου ὄρνις, ἀντὶ δὲ Σαμίου Ταναγραῖος ἀναπέφηνας· [Eipe de homōs, ō Mnēsarchou pai, hopōs hēmin anti men anthrōpou ornis, anti de Samiou Tanagraios anapephēnas] Yet explain, son of Mnesarchus, how it has come about that you have appeared to us as a bird instead of a man, and as a Tanagraean instead of a Samian. Tanagraean, that is, cock, תרנגול. Meanwhile, neither name for the cock appears in the Prophetic books. Nor is it sufficiently known what the ancient Hebrews called the cock; although interpreters refer many passages of the Prophets to the gallinaceous kind, but on conjectures that are not certain enough, as will be clear to anyone who runs through them, as what follows will make plain. ¶

I. Primus, quod meminerim, locus est Levit. XIV, 4. ubi leprosus, qui mundatus est, jubetur offerre Deo δύο ὀρνίθια ζῶντα καθαρὰ, id est, ut nonnullis placet, duas gallinulas vivas puras. Origenes in Leviticum Homilia 8. Praecipit, ut accipiantur duae gallinae vivae ei, qui mundatur. Et paullo post, Videntur mihi etiam hic duae istae gallinae habere similitudinem quandam duorum II, 677 hircorum, ex quibus unus Domino offertur, alius in eremum emittitur: ita enim et hic ex duabus gallinis una immolatur, et alia in campum dimittitur. Et rursus, Qui purgatur a lepra, et offert gallinas. Item, Propter quod mandat Legisla- tor, ut eadem die, qua purgatur, accipiantur duae gallinae, intellectus latenter habeatur, per quam purificatio efficitur peccatoris, de qua scriptum est, „Quoties „volui congregare filios tuos sicut gallina,“ etc. Quae, quia sunt ἀσύνδετα, videntur ita esse legenda: Propterea quod mandat Legislator, ut eadem die, qua purgatur, accipiantur duae gallinae, gallinae intellectus latenter habeatur, per quam, etc. Nempe per gallinas illas vult Christum significari, qui in Evangelio cum gallina se confert. Unde est, quod additur, Occidetur gallina in vasculo fictili, in quo aqua viva sit missa, ut et aqua adsumatur ad purificationem, et com- pleatur plenitudo mysterii in aqua et sanguine, quod dicitur exiisse de latere Salva- toris. Et de eadem gallina multo plura legere est in sequentibus. Tomo nono Operum Hieronymi auctor Epistolae trigesimae nonae De diversis leprarum gene- ribus, idem sequutus, ita habet: Sed quid pro mundatione leprarum duae galli- nae jubentur offerre? Duae gallinae animam indicare videntur, et carnem: sed unam, inquit, gallinam occides, alteram ablues et dimittes, etc. Tamen in He- braeo textu de gallinis nihil habetur: tantum in purificatione leprae sumi jubet Deus שתי צפורים, duas aves (alias duas aviculas, aut duos passeres) quod Grae- ci non male reddiderunt δύο ὀρνίθια· sed ambigua voce, quae Patrum aevo nonnisi gallinas, aut plerumque gallinas sonabat. Athenaeus libri noni capite quarto, ubi de gallinaceo genere, Ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ ὄρνιθας, καὶ ὀρνίθια νῦν μόνον ἡ συνήθεια καλεῖ τὰς θηλείας· Nunc quidem mos invaluit, ut gallorum foemi- nae (id est gallinae) solum ὄρνιθες et ὀρνίθια dicantur. Quod ansam dedit errori. Tamen Athenaeo fere aequalis Plutarchus in Artuxerxe dicit, Rhyndacem esse μικρὸν ἐν Πέρσαις ὀρνίθιον, id est, parvam in Persis aviculam. Et in Lege, δύο ὀρνίθια καθαρὰ esse duas aviculas mundas, satis constare debuit vel Hebraeae linguae ignaris. Neque enim dixisset legislator, duas gallinas mundas. Nam frustra fuisset hoc epithetum, cum omnis gallina sit munda.

I. The first passage, as far as I recall, is Lev 14:4, where the leper who has been cleansed is commanded to offer to God δύο ὀρνίθια ζῶντα καθαρὰ [dyo ornithia zōnta kathara], that is, as some would have it, two living clean little hens. Origen, Homily 8 on Leviticus: He commands that two living hens be taken for him who is cleansed. And a little later: These two hens also seem to me here to bear a certain likeness to the two II, 677 goats, of which one is offered to the Lord and the other is sent out into the wilderness: for so here too, of the two hens one is sacrificed and the other is released into the field. And again: He who is purged of leprosy and offers hens. Likewise: Because the Lawgiver commands that on the same day on which he is purged two hens be taken, let there be a hidden understanding, through which the purification of the sinner is effected, of which it is written, “How often would I have gathered thy children together, as a hen,” etc. Since these words are ἀσύνδετα [asyndeta], they seem to need reading thus: Because the Lawgiver commands that on the same day on which he is purged two hens be taken, let there be a hidden understanding of the hen, through which, etc. That is, by those hens he wants Christ to be signified, who in the Gospel compares himself to a hen. Hence the addition: The hen shall be killed in an earthen vessel into which running water has been put, so that water too may be taken for the purification, and the fullness of the mystery may be fulfilled in the water and the blood which is said to have come forth from the side of the Saviour. And much more about this same hen can be read in what follows. In the ninth volume of the Works of Jerome, the author of the thirty-ninth Epistle, On the various kinds of leprosies, following the same view, says: But why are two hens commanded to be offered for the cleansing of leprosies? The two hens seem to indicate the soul and the flesh: but one hen, he says, you shall kill, the other you shall wash and release, etc. Yet in the Hebrew text nothing is said of hens: God merely commands that in the purification of leprosy שתי צפורים be taken, two birds (or two little birds, or two sparrows), which the Greeks rendered not badly as δύο ὀρνίθια [dyo ornithia], but with an ambiguous word, which in the age of the Fathers meant only hens, or for the most part hens. Athenaeus, book 9, chapter 4, where he treats of the gallinaceous kind: Ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ ὄρνιθας, καὶ ὀρνίθια νῦν μόνον ἡ συνήθεια καλεῖ τὰς θηλείας· [alla mēn kai ornithas, kai ornithia nyn monon hē synētheia kalei tas thēleias] Now indeed the custom has prevailed that only the females of cocks (that is, hens) are called ὄρνιθες [ornithes] and ὀρνίθια [ornithia]. This gave occasion to the error. Yet Plutarch, nearly contemporary with Athenaeus, says in the Artaxerxes that the rhyndax is μικρὸν ἐν Πέρσαις ὀρνίθιον [mikron en Persais ornithion], that is, a small little bird among the Persians. And that in the Law δύο ὀρνίθια καθαρὰ [dyo ornithia kathara] are two clean little birds ought to have been clear enough even to those ignorant of the Hebrew language. For the lawgiver would not have said two clean hens. That epithet would have been pointless, since every hen is clean. ¶

II. The second passage is 1 Reg. IV, 23. [var. V, 3.], where among Solomon's provisions col. 112 are reckoned ברבורים אבוסים, that is, תרנגולין פטומין, fattened cocks, as it is found in Selomo, whom Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae] follows. Likewise for Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] ברבור barbur is תרנגול סריס, a castrated cock, that is, a capon. Nor do the Greek translators depart far from this; here they have ἐκτὸς ὀρνίθων ἐκλεκτῶν νομάδων [ektos ornithōn eklektōn nomadōn], besides choice pasture-fed hens. I prefer this to rendering it, as others do, besides birds, etc. For although ὄρνιθες [ornithes] are often birds in general, yet for the Greek translators, as for the Evangelists, a bird is ὄρνεον [orneon], and a hen is ὄρνις [ornis]. Moreover, ὄρνις νομὰς [ornis nomas] is nothing other than a fattened or pasture-fed hen. In Nicander's Alexipharmaca, line II, 678 295, there is βοσκὰς ὀρταλὶς [boskas ortalis], where the Scholiast has νομὰς ὄρνις [nomas ornis], the very words of the Greek translators. And in Artemidorus's Ὀψαρτυτικά [Opsartytika], as cited by Athenaeus, book 14, chapter 23: Νομάδα παχεῖαν ἕψε, καὶ νεοσσοὺς τῶν ἤδη κοκκυζόντων [Nomada pacheian hepse, kai neossous tōn ēdē kokkyzontōn]; Boil a fat pasture-fed hen, and, of the chicks, those that already crow. So it must be rendered, not, as the translator most ineptly has it, a fat dove, etc., and of the chicks those that still peep. For the subject is a mattya to be made from a hen. Accordingly it is immediately added: Εἶτ᾽ ἐξελὼν τὰ λάχανα εἰς τρυβλίον, καὶ τῆς ὄρνιθος τῶν κρεῶν ἐπιθεὶς [Eit᾽ exelōn ta lachana eis trublion, kai tēs ornithos tōn kreōn epitheis]; Then taking out the vegetables, put them into a dish, and placing the hen's meat on them. And ἤδη [ēdē] does not mean still, but already. Nor is κοκκύζειν [kokkyzein] proper to doves, but to cocks, as is clear from Aristophanes, Aristotle, Theocritus, Aelian and Lucian. For it is an onomatopoeic word, like the vernacular coq for the cock, which we name thus from its crowing. Finally, νομὰς [nomas] is a hen, yet not a Numidian one, as the most learned commentator would have it, but a pasture-fed one. Others call them σιτιστὰς [sitistas] or σιτευτὰς [siteutas]. Athenaeus, book 14, chapter 21: Σιτιστῶν δὲ ὀρνίθων μὲν μνημονεύει Μάτρων ἐν ταῖς Παρῳδίαις οὕτως [Sitistōn de ornithōn men mnēmoneuei Matrōn en tais Parōdiais houtōs], ¶

Altilium gallinarum ita meminit Matron in Parodiis, Quo dicto illi riserunt, moxque attulerunt altiles gallinas in patinis argenteis. Altiles gallinas dico cum Metello, pontifice maximo, cujus verba sunt in descriptione coenae pontificialis apud Macrobium Saturnalium libri tertii capite decimo tertio, Ante coenam echinos, ostreas crudas, quantum vellent, peloridas, sphondylos, turdum, asparagos, subtus gallinam altilem. Neque aliud voluit Plinius altilium nomine, cum dixit de myrices semine libri vigesimi quarti capite nono, Cum altilium vero pingui furunculis imponitur. Cato pascales dixerat: ex quo Festus, Pascales et oves et gallinae appellantur, quod passim pascantur. Pascales, id est, νομάδες, ut altiles σιτισταὶ, vel σιτευταί. Itaque ex Graecorum sententia ברבורים אבוסים sunt gallinae pascuales, aut altiles. Atque idem sensisse quosdam ex Hebraeis vetustioribus, in Selomone habetur. Quod tamen haud quaquam potest pro certo et comperto haberi: cum vox nulla sit, de cujus significatione plus ambigatur, ut infra docebimus capite de ea voce peculiari.

Matron mentions fattened hens thus in his Parodies: When he had said this, they laughed, and then brought in fattened hens on silver platters. I say fattened hens with Metellus, the Pontifex Maximus, whose words are in the description of the pontifical dinner in Macrobius, Saturnalia, book 3, chapter 13: Before the dinner, sea urchins, raw oysters, as many as they wished, giant clams, spondyli, a thrush, asparagus, and beneath it a fattened hen. Nor did Pliny mean anything else by the term altilia when he said of the seed of the tamarisk, book 24, chapter 9: Applied with the fat of fattened fowls to boils. Cato had said pascales; whence Festus: Both sheep and hens are called pascales, because they graze about everywhere. Pascales, that is, νομάδες [nomades], as altiles are σιτισταὶ [sitistai] or σιτευταί [siteutai]. Thus in the view of the Greeks ברבורים אבוסים are pasture-fed or fattened hens. And that some of the older Hebrews held the same view is found in Selomo. This, however, can by no means be held as certain and established, since there is no word whose meaning is more disputed, as we shall show below in a chapter devoted to that word. ¶

Ad verbum, סכות בנות Succothbenoth, sunt filiarum, vel puellarum tabernacula. Itaque cum Babylonii leguntur fecisse Succoth-benoth, eos struxisse intelligo sacellum Dearum, quae בנות benoth, id est, puellae dicebantur. Ut Graeci Proserpinam κόρην, id est, puellam appellabant. Sic Amos V, 26. סכות מלככם, tabernaculum regis vestri, id est, idoli vestri sacellum (1). Et נרגל Nergal, quid sit nescio: nisi quod huic idolo fere cognominis est palmae species hodieque Persis, Arabibus et Indis نارجيل nergil dicta, de qua mira praedicant. Videndum igitur, an ab hac palma denominari potuerit Persarum idolum: ut in Syria ܪܡܘܢ Rimmon, a malis Punicis, 2 Reg. V, 18.

III. The third passage about the cock is 2 Reg. XVII, 30., where, of the peoples transferred by Shalmaneser from Assyria into Judaea, the Babylonians are said to have worshipped סכות־בנות Succoth-Benoth, and the Cuthaeans Nergal. Succoth-Benoth, that is, the hen with her chicks; and Nergal, that is, the cock, as the Hebrews would have it. For so the Talmudists in Sanhedrin, chapter 7, folio 63b: אנשי בבל עשו את סכות־בנות ומאי ניהו תרנגולת אנשי כותה עשו נרגל ומאי ניהו תרנגול, The men of Babel made II, 679 Succoth-Benoth. What (idol) was this? A hen. The men of Cutha made Nergal. What was that? A cock. Solomon Jarchi [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac), called Jarchi by the Christian Hebraists]: סכות־בנות דמות תרנגולת עם אפרוחיה נרגל דמות תרנגול, Succoth-Benoth was the image of a hen with her chicks, and Nergal the image of a cock. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]: ובדברי רז״ל סכות־בנות זה תרנגולת היו עובדים והיה שמה סכות בנות וכמו שקורין לתרנגול סכוי כן היו col. 113 קורין לתרנגולת סכות ובנות פירוש עם אפרוחיה נרגל זה תרנגולת הבר, In the words of our masters of blessed memory, Succoth-benoth is the hen, which they worshipped under this name. For just as they called the cock שכוי secui, so they called the hen סכות succoth. And the added benoth signifies that she was with her chicks. And nergal is the wild hen. The most trifling of trifles. Literally, סכות בנות Succoth-benoth are tents of daughters, or of girls. So when the Babylonians are said to have made Succoth-benoth, I understand that they built a shrine of goddesses who were called בנות benoth, that is, girls, just as the Greeks called Proserpina κόρην [korēn], that is, girl. So in Amos V, 26. סכות מלככם, the tabernacle of your king, that is, the shrine of your idol R1. And what נרגל Nergal is, I do not know, except that almost the same name as this idol belongs to a species of palm called even today by the Persians, Arabs and Indians نارجيل [nārjīl] nergil, of which they tell wonders. It must therefore be considered whether the idol of the Persians could have been named from this palm, as in Syria ܪܡܘܢ [רמון] Rimmon was from pomegranates, 2 Reg. V, 18. ¶

IV. The fourth passage is Job 3:7. There, for the words of Job in which he curses the night in which he was born, Let no רננה, song, come in it, the Chaldee has: לא תעול רננא דתרנגול ברא למקלסה בה, Let not the song of the wild cock come to give praise in it. V. Quintus locus est Iob. XXXVIII, 32. ubi Chaldaeus habet in Veneta et Basiliensi editione וזגתא על אפרחהא תדברנון, Et gallinam cum pullis suis deduces? Ad verbum, super pullos suos. Sed super pullos, id est, cum pullis. Ut Deuter. XXII, 6. Non capies matrem super pullos, i. e. cum pullis. Gallina est זגתא, ut in tractatu Talmudico Bechoroth cap. 1. fol. 8. 2. אייתו לה תרי ביעי אמר ליה הי דוגתא אוכמתי והי דוגתא חיוורתי, Allatis duobus ovis dixit ei, Il-lud ovum est gallinae nigrae, et istud gallinae albae. Nec זגתא sonat Zagtha, ut II, 680 putant, sed Zaggatha. Est enim e geminantibus secundam, ut Arabicum gallinae nomen دجاجة degiagia, quod deducunt a verbo دج daggia, id est, le-niter incedere. Ego a voce factum puto, quam varii varie exprimunt. Aut a Chaldaeo זגג, Zain et Daleth permutatis, quod in illis linguis tralatitium. De caetero in Iobi paraphrasi cave ne vulgarem gallinam intelligas. Agitur enim de coelesti, quae vulgari lingua pullicenaria dicitur, Graecis Πλειὰς, vel Πλειά-δες, et Vergiliae Latinis (2). Sed in Hebraeo est, ועיש על בניה תנחם, et עיש cum filiis suis deduces? Et eo loco עיש plane aliud quam Pleiades. Nempe עיש idem, quod עש Iob. IX, 9. Et עש as, per ἀφαίρεσιν factum ex Arabico نعش nas, prout initiale N saepe excidit. Sic גיא gia, splendor, factum a נגה naga, splendere; גר gar, fluvius, a נגר nagar, fluere; שיא si, vel שאת seeth, elatio, a נשה nasa, extollere; שיג sig, insectatio, a נשג nasag, insequi vel assequi. Porro Arabibus نعش nas primo est feretrum: ut Luc. VII, 14. et in Eutychio Alexandrino pag. 277. deinde sidus ursae. Sic Astrologis النعش الكبري feretrum majus, id est, ursa major: النعش الصغري feretrum minus, id est, ursa minor. Nempe quatuor stellae, in col. 114 quadrum digestae, illis pro feretro habentur, seu sarcophago, quo cadaver effertur. Et tres sequentes (nobis caudae ursae) illis sunt filiae, vel filii, qui comitantur funus. Alcamus, بنات نعش الكبري سبعة كواكب اربعة منها نعش وثلاث بنات Filiae feretri majoris, quarum quatuor sunt feretrum, et tres sunt filiae. Et alibi, الحية كواكب ما بين الغرقدين وبنات نعش Serpens (seu draco) stellae, quae sunt inter alpharkadin (stellas polares) et filias feretri. Rursus alibi, II, 681 الصبا ريح مهبها من مطلع الثريا الي بنات نعش Saba, ventus flans ab ortu Pleiadum ad filias feretri, id est, Coecias. Giauhari notat, apud poetas subinde legi بنى نعش filios feretri, et de singulis in Singulari numero ابن نعش filium feretri (3). Unde clara lux loco Iobi , in quo versamur, An עיש feretrum deduces cum filiis suis? (4) Et quamvis hujus locutionis ratio fugerit interpretes, tamen recte vidit Aben Ezra, agi de signo septem stellarum in aquilone. Quod signum alii ursam, alii plaustrum, alii helicen, alii vocant Septentriones. Nec moror Talmudicos, quorum aliis עש est זנב טלה, cauda arietis; aliis רישא דעגלא, caput tauri, in tractatu Berachoth fol. 58. b. Quas sententias Glossator conciliare nititur; quia ראש השור סמוך לזנב טלה שכן הסדר טלה שור תאומים, Caput tauri junctum est caudae arietis, cum hic sit signorum ordo: aries, taurus, gemini, etc. Nec me latet, quorsum Chaldaeus עיש voluerit esse Pleiades. ערש Hebraeis est coire et congregari, Ioel IV, 11. Illi igitur visum est hoc signum עיש dici, tanquam a coitione. Prout Graece dicitur Πλειὰς, ὡς ἐκ πλειόνων ἀςέρων οὖσα. Et Arabice ثري indidem. Nam ثرا est multiplicari. At Graeci interpretes quorsum reddunt, Καὶ ἕσπερον ἐπὶ κόμης αὐτοῦ ἄξεις αὐτὸν, Et vesperum, an coma sua ducis illum? Pro בניה filios ejus, legerunt בנית, comam. Et עיש, vel עש deduxerunt ex עשי, quomodo Arabes vesperam vocant. In Vulgata veteres ediditiones habent, Et vesperum super fines terrae consurgere facis? Et novae super II, 682 filios terrae. Sed utrumque puto mendose. Legendum enim, Et vesperum super filios tune consurgere facis? Illud tune, alioqui in illa versione rarum, reperitur tamen 2 Reg. II, 20. et 3 Reg. XIII, 13. 14. et XVIII, 17. Scribae locum corruperunt, quia non capiebant, quid essent filii vesperi. Sed vetustissima est corruptio, jam enim inoleverat Gregorii Magni aetate, quem apparet le-gisse filios terrae libri vigesimi noni in Iobum capite decimo septimo.

V. The fifth passage is Job 38:32, where the Chaldee in the Venetian and Basel editions has וזגתא על אפרחהא תדברנון, And will you lead the hen with her chicks? Literally, over her chicks. But over the chicks means with the chicks. So Deuter. XXII, 6. You shall not take the mother over the young, i.e. with the young. The hen is זגתא, as in the Talmudic tractate Bechoroth ch. 1, fol. 8. 2: אייתו לה תרי ביעי אמר ליה הי דוגתא אוכמתי והי דוגתא חיוורתי, When two eggs were brought, he said to him: That egg is of a black hen, and this one of a white hen. Nor is זגתא pronounced Zagtha, as II, 680 some think, but Zaggatha. For it belongs to the verbs that double the second radical, like the Arabic name of the hen, دجاجة [dajāja] degiagia, which they derive from the verb دج [dajja] daggia, that is, to walk gently. I think it is formed from the bird's call, which different people render differently. Or from the Chaldee זגג, with Zain and Daleth interchanged, which is customary in those languages. For the rest, beware of understanding an ordinary hen in the paraphrase of Job. For it is a matter of the celestial one, which in the vernacular is called pullicenaria [hen with chicks], by the Greeks Πλειὰς [Pleias] or Πλειάδες [Pleiades], and by the Latins Vergiliae R2. But the Hebrew has ועיש על בניה תנחם, and will you lead עיש with its sons? And in that passage עיש is plainly something other than the Pleiades. Namely, עיש is the same as עש in Job 9:9. And עש as is formed by ἀφαίρεσιν [aphairesin] from the Arabic نعش [naʿsh] nas, as an initial N often drops out. Thus גיא gia, splendour, from נגה naga, to shine; גר gar, river, from נגר nagar, to flow; שיא si, or שאת seeth, elevation, from נשה nasa, to lift up; שיג sig, pursuit, from נשג nasag, to pursue or overtake. Further, for the Arabs نعش [naʿsh] nas is first a bier: as in Luke 7:14 and in Eutychius of Alexandria p. 277; then the constellation of the bear. Thus for the astrologers النعش الكبري [al-naʿsh al-kubrī] is the greater bier, that is, the Great Bear; النعش الصغري [al-naʿsh al-ṣughrī] the lesser bier, that is, the Little Bear. Namely, four stars arranged col. 114 in a square are regarded by them as a bier, or coffin, on which a corpse is carried out. And the three following (for us the bear's tail) are for them the daughters, or sons, who accompany the funeral. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: بنات نعش الكبري سبعة كواكب اربعة منها نعش وثلاث بنات [banāt naʿsh al-kubrī sabʿat kawākib arbaʿa minhā naʿsh wa-thalāth banāt] The daughters of the greater bier: of which four are the bier, and three are the daughters. And elsewhere: الحية كواكب ما بين الغرقدين وبنات نعش [al-ḥayya kawākib mā bayn al-gharqadayn wa-banāt naʿsh] The Serpent (or Dragon): the stars that are between alpharkadin (the polar stars) and the daughters of the bier. Again elsewhere: II, 681 الصبا ريح مهبها من مطلع الثريا الي بنات نعش [al-ṣabā rīḥ mahabbuhā min maṭlaʿ al-thurayyā ilā banāt naʿsh] Saba, a wind blowing from the rising of the Pleiades to the daughters of the bier, that is, Caecias. Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] notes that in the poets one sometimes reads بنى نعش [banī naʿsh] sons of the bier, and of individual stars in the singular ابن نعش [ibn naʿsh] son of the bier R3. Hence clear light falls on the passage of Job we are dealing with: Will you lead עיש, the bier, with its sons? R4 And although the reason for this expression escaped the interpreters, Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] nevertheless rightly saw that it concerns the sign of seven stars in the north. This sign some call the Bear, others the Wagon, others Helice, others the Septentriones. Nor do I pay heed to the Talmudists, for some of whom עש is זנב טלה, the tail of the Ram; for others רישא דעגלא, the head of the Bull, in the tractate Berachoth fol. 58b The Glossator tries to reconcile these opinions, because ראש השור סמוך לזנב טלה שכן הסדר טלה שור תאומים, The head of the Bull is joined to the tail of the Ram, since this is the order of the signs: Ram, Bull, Twins, etc. Nor am I unaware why the Chaldee wanted עיש to be the Pleiades. ערש in Hebrew means to come together and to be gathered, Ioel IV, 11. So it seemed to him that this sign was called עיש as if from coming together. Just as in Greek it is called Πλειὰς [Pleias], ὡς ἐκ πλειόνων ἀςέρων οὖσα [hōs ek pleionōn asterōn ousa]. And in Arabic ثري [thurayy] from the same notion, for ثرا [tharā] means to be multiplied. But why do the Greek translators render Καὶ ἕσπερον ἐπὶ κόμης αὐτοῦ ἄξεις αὐτὸν [Kai hesperon epi komēs autou axeis auton], And the evening star, will you lead him by his hair? For בניה, its sons, they read בנית, hair. And they derived עיש, or עש, from עשי, the word by which the Arabs call evening. In the Vulgate the old editions have And do you make the evening star rise over the ends of the earth? And the new ones over II, 682 the sons of the earth. But I think both are corrupt. For one should read And do you make the evening star rise over its sons then? That tune [then], though otherwise rare in that version, is nevertheless found at 2 Reg. II, 20. and 3 Reg. XIII, 13. 14. and XVIII, 17. The scribes corrupted the passage because they did not understand what the sons of the evening star were. But the corruption is very ancient, for it had already taken root in the age of Gregory the Great, who evidently read sons of the earth, in book 29 on Job, chapter 17. ¶

VI. Sextus locus Iob. XXXVIII, 36. Hebraice sic habet: Quis posuit בטחות, in renibus sapientiam, aut dedit לשכוי, cordi intelligentiam? In Scriptura scilicet passim junguntur cor et renes, ut cogitationum propriae sedes ex Hebraeorum mente. Exempla vide Psalm. VII, 10. et XXVI, 2. et LXXIII, 21. Proverb. XXIII, 15. 26. Ierem. XI, 20. et XVII, 10. et XX, 12. Ita etiam hoc loco. Sed cum renes vulgo כליות, et cor לב, appellatur, utrisque hic inditur nomen aliud, idque magis Poeticum. Et renes quidem טוחות tuchoth, di- col. 115 cuntur hic, et Psal. LI, 8. a verbo טוח, quod illinire est et obducere; quia renes sunt adipe illiniti et obducti, ut jam alibi probatum fuit. Cor vero שכוי, a verbo שכה, quod cogitare et imaginari significat. Unde etiam שכיות sunt picturae et imagines, Es. II, 16. et אבן משכית, lapis pictus, Levit. XXVI, 1. Et משכיות לבב, imaginationes cordis, Psal. LXXIII, 7. et במשכיתו, in imaginatione ejus, Prov. XVIII, 11. Quae cum clara et aperta sint, et ab Hebraeorum doctissimis, Kimchio puta, et Aben Ezra, et R. Levi disertis verbis confirmata: libuit tamen aliis sic interpretari Iobi verba, quae diximus, Quis posuit in renibus sapientiam, aut dedit gallo intelligentiam? Quasi שכוי ibi loci gallum significaret. Commenti primus auctor fuit quidam R. Simeon, filius Lakis, cujus verba sic citantur in tractatu Talmudico De capite anni fol. 26. a. כשהלכתי לתחום קן נשריא הוו קורין לכלה נינפי ולתרנגול שכוי, Cum iter facerem per fines Kennasariae, ibi nympham sponsam vocabant, et gallum secui. Ibidem nugantur alii, sponsam vocari nympham, quia mons Sion dicitur pulcher נוף noph, seu climate Psal. XLVIII, 3. (Latuit enim recutitos doctores vox Graeca νύμφη). Gallum addunt vocari שכוי secui, a Iobi loco, quem diximus. Ex tam impuro fonte ortus error statim latius propagatus est, et multos habuit assertores. In his Chaldaeus interpres duplicem exhibet hujus loci interpretationem: unam sanam, et veram, מן יהב ללבא בינתא, Quis dedit cordi intelligentiam? Talmudicam alteram מן יהב לתרנגולא ברא בינתא למקלסא למרה, Quis gallo sylvestri dedit intelligentiam, ut laudaret Dominum suum? Et R. Selomo ita utramque asserit, למכוי זה תרנגול לשון חכמים וי״א הלב, Secui lingua sapientum gallum sonat, quamvis alii cor explicent. Et Aquinas postquam protulit Iobi locum, יש מפרשים תרנגול והוא שגור מאד במשנה, Sunt, qui gallum interpretantur, quod in Misna valde obvium est. Quod pluribus probat Kimchius, quamvis in II, 683 altera sit sententia. Proinde in libro Precum, toties cuso et recuso, inter matutinas preces habetur haec oratio, בא״י אמ״ה אשר נתן לשכוי בינה להבין בין יום ובין לילה, Benedictus tu, Domine, Deus noster, Rex saeculi, qui τῷ secui (id est, gallo) dedisti intelligentiam, ut diem a nocte dignosceret. In qua oratione palam est ad Iobi locum alludi. Et Hieronymus, haud dubie a Iudaeo illo persuasus, quem pro praeceptore habuit, eundem locum ita reddidit: Quis posuit in visceribus hominis sapientiam? vel quis dedit gallo intelligentiam? Duo ἀσύν-δετα in eodem commate sic jungens, nempe hominis sapientiam cum naturali galli instinctu, quo certis horis ad canendum provocatur. Idque in eo libro, in quo ejusdem versus duo hemistichia fere sunt ἀντίστροφα. Obiter monebo, unde nata sit Graecorum versio, quae videri possit prorsus aliena, Τίς δὲ ἔδωκεν γυναιξὶν ὑφάσματος σοφίαν, ἢ ποικιλτικὴν ἐπιστήμην; Quis autem dedit mulieribus texturae sapientiam, aut variegandi scientiam? Pro בטחות, in renibus, le-gerunt בטוות, in netricibus. Et לשכות, ad pingendum, aut variandum, pro לשכוי, cordi. Quomodo si scripseris, Hebraea ad verbum sic reddentur: Quis in netricibus posuit sapientiam? Aut quis dedit eis intelligentiam ad pingendum, seu variandum? Sed ex nendi scientia fecerunt Graeci interpretes ὑφά-σματος σοφίαν, quia νῆμα et ὕφασμα saepe promiscue sumuntur, ut ex Homero, Hesychio et Eustathio probare sit obvium.

VI. The sixth passage, Job 38:36, reads thus in Hebrew: Who has put בטחות wisdom in the reins, or given לשכוי understanding to the heart? In Scripture, namely, heart and reins are frequently joined, as being, in the mind of the Hebrews, the proper seat of thoughts. For examples see Ps 7:10 and XXVI, 2. and LXXIII, 21., Proverb. XXIII, 15. 26. Jer 11:20 and XVII, 10. and XX, 12. So also in this passage. But whereas the reins are commonly called כליות and the heart לב, here each is given another name, and a more poetic one. The reins are called טוחות tuchoth col. 115 here and in Ps 51:8, from the verb טוח, which means to smear and to coat, because the reins are smeared and coated with fat, as has already been proved elsewhere. The heart, on the other hand, is called שכוי, from the verb שכה, which means to think and to imagine. Hence also שכיות are pictures and images, Isa 2:16, and אבן משכית is a painted stone, Lev 26:1; and משכיות לבב, imaginations of the heart, Ps 73:7; and במשכיתו, in his imagination, Prov 18:11. Although these things are clear and plain, and confirmed in explicit words by the most learned of the Hebrews, namely Kimchi, Aben Ezra and R. Levi, others have nevertheless chosen to interpret the words of Job just cited thus: Who has put wisdom in the reins, or given understanding to the cock? As if שכוי in that place meant cock. The first author of this fiction was a certain R. Simeon, son of Lakis, whose words are cited as follows in the Talmudic tractate On the beginning of the year, fol. 26a: כשהלכתי לתחום קן נשריא הוו קורין לכלה נינפי ולתרנגול שכוי, When I was travelling through the territory of Kennasaria, there they called a bride nympha, and a cock secui. In the same place others trifle that a bride is called nympha because Mount Zion is called beautiful in נוף noph, that is, in situation, Ps 48:3 (For the Greek word νύμφη [nymphē] escaped these circumcised doctors.) They add that the cock is called שכוי secui from the passage of Job we have mentioned. From so impure a source the error at once spread more widely and found many defenders. Among them the Chaldee translator gives a twofold interpretation of this passage: one sound and true, מן יהב ללבא בינתא, Who gave understanding to the heart?; the other Talmudic, מן יהב לתרנגולא ברא בינתא למקלסא למרה, Who gave the wild cock understanding, to praise his Lord? And R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] asserts both thus: למכוי זה תרנגול לשון חכמים וי״א הלב, Secui in the language of the sages means cock, although others explain it as heart. And Aquinas, after quoting the passage of Job: יש מפרשים תרנגול והוא שגור מאד במשנה, There are some who interpret it as cock, which is very common in the Mishnah. Kimchi proves this at some length, although he II, 683 is of the other opinion. Accordingly, in the Prayer Book, printed and reprinted so many times, this prayer is found among the morning prayers: בא״י אמ״ה אשר נתן לשכוי בינה להבין בין יום ובין לילה, Blessed art thou, O Lord our God, King of the world, who hast given τῷ [tō] secui (that is, the cock) understanding to distinguish day from night. In this prayer the allusion to the passage of Job is evident. And Jerome, no doubt persuaded by that Jew whom he had as his teacher, rendered the same passage thus: Who has put wisdom in the inward parts of man? or who has given understanding to the cock? He thus joins in the same verse two ἀσύνδετα [asyndeta], namely the wisdom of man with the natural instinct of the cock, by which it is prompted to crow at fixed hours. And this in a book in which the two hemistichs of a verse are almost always ἀντίστροφα [antistropha]. I shall note in passing the origin of the version of the Greeks, which might seem entirely foreign: Τίς δὲ ἔδωκεν γυναιξὶν ὑφάσματος σοφίαν, ἢ ποικιλτικὴν ἐπιστήμην; [Tis de edōken gynaixin hyphasmatos sophian, ē poikiltikēn epistēmēn?] And who gave women wisdom in weaving, or skill in embroidery? For בטחות, in the reins, they read בטוות, in spinning women; and לשכות, for painting or variegating, for לשכוי, to the heart. Written that way, the Hebrew is rendered word for word: Who has put wisdom in spinning women? Or who has given them understanding for painting, or variegating? But the Greek translators turned the skill of spinning into ὑφάσματος σοφίαν [hyphasmatos sophian], because νῆμα [nēma] and ὕφασμα [hyphasma] are often used interchangeably, as it would be easy to prove from Homer, Hesychius and Eustathius. ¶

XXIV, 3. נאם הגבר, Dixit vir, Baal Hatturim sic accipiens, quasi legatur, Dixit gallus, inde sum- col. 119 pta occasione, ridiculam Balaami cum gallo collationem his verbis instituit: קרא גבר שדומה בלעם לתרנגול כמו שהתרנגול הוא נואף מכל כן בלעם רבע אתונו וכמו שהתרנגול יודע בעתים כן בלעם יודע לכוין השעה שהקב״ה כועס וכמו שהתרנגול דרכו לעמוד על רגל אחד כן בלעם חגר באחד רגליו, Vocat גבר, quia Balaam gallo fuit similis. Ut gallus est moechus inprimis; ita Balaam asinam suam iniit: ut gallus novit tempora; sic Balaam novit explorare tempus, quo irascitur Deus Sanctus Benedictus: ut gallus solet uno pede insistere, ita Ba-laam fuit uno pede claudus. Inprimis absurdum est, quod reliquis sacrificiis, quae Deus in Lege sua praescripserat, jam abolitis, tamen die, qui praecedit festum expiationis, nemo est Iudaeorum, qui non offerat pro se gallum, haec ingeminans, dum illum suo capiti ter allidit, זה חליפתי זה תחתי זה כפרתי זה התרנגול ילך למיתה ואני אלך לחיים טובים עם ירשאל אמן, Iste gallus sit permutatio pro me: Iste sit loco meo: Iste sit expiatio pro me: Iste gallus eat ad mortem: Ego autem cum toto Israele ad beatam vitam. Amen. Reliqua pete ex Cl. Buxtorfii Synagoga capite vigesimo. Hic enim id sufficit addere, errori ansam dedisse vocem aequivocam גבר, qua tam virum, quam gallum significari volunt. Aequum igitur est, inquiunt, ut, cum peccaverit גבר, peccati poenam II, 688 גבר sustineat. Quasi Gallus et homo pro uno haberi possint coram Dei tribunali, quia per vocis abusum eodem nomine appellantur. Consulto praetereo quae de gallo habentur in secundo Thargum Esther; quia non sunt paraphrases, sed auctoris mera commenta.

XII. The twelfth passage is Es. XXII, 17, where the Prophet says to Shebna, יהוה מטלטלך טלטלה גבר, The Lord will carry you away with a carrying away, O man. Others render with the carrying away of a man, that is, they say, a manly, strong, vehement one. But this version has two drawbacks. The first is that no example of this expression is adduced. For when we read elsewhere of the way of a man, the sorrow of a man, the redemption of a man, the teaching of a man, no one understands vehemence. We only infer from it that these things either are in a man or proceed from a man. Thus the carrying away of a man would be nothing but that by which a man carries away or is carried away, which would be very weak. Besides, the carrying away of a man would be expressed in Hebrew as טלטלת גבר, not טלטלה. For who would grant what some assert here, that the final He in טלטלה is epenthetic and not the mark of the feminine, since this too is without example? Finally, the accent tiphcha on טלטלה suspends the voice and prevents it from being joined with the following גבר. Clearly, then, גבר is in the vocative case, as if it read אתה הגבר, O you man. This Aben Ezra and Kimchi rightly observed. And long before them the Syriac translator, who has: ܡܪܝܐ ܡܛܠܛܠ ܠܟ ܛܘܠܛܠܐ ܓܒܪܐ [מריא מטלטל לך טולטלא גברא], The Lord will surely cast you out, O man. Yet there are some among the Hebrews who render, The Lord will carry you away with the carrying away II, 687 of a man, or even with the carrying away of a cock. For although the word גבר, very frequent in Scripture, everywhere else means man, here they chose to do violence to the sacred text and interpret it as cock, compelled by no necessity but only by an itch and bad habit of καινοτομία [kainotomia]. Hence on the words טלטלה גבר R. Selomo has כהדין תרנגולא דמטלטל מאתר לאתר, Like a cock that is carried from place to place. This is taken from the Midrash on the Psalms, as Kimchi observes. Accordingly Jerome here has: The Lord will make you be carried off, as a cock is carried off. This, of course, follows the view of that Jew from whom he had learned Hebrew letters, as he himself notes in his Notes, where he paraphrases (παραφράζει [paraphrazei]) this passage thus: And I say this, that you should not labour at hewing out a sepulchre, because the Lord will transfer your priesthood, as גבר is wont to be transferred, which all have interpreted as man. But the Hebrew who instructed us in the reading of the Old Testament translated it cock. “As (he says) a cock is transferred from one place to another on the shoulder of a carrier, so the Lord will lightly carry you away from your place”. Accordingly in the Talmud גבר is sometimes used for cock, as when they say קריאת הגבר for cockcrow. And in the tractate On the Sabbath, p. 67b, גברית is the same as תרנגלת, hen. Indeed, among the paraphrasts Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] calls Ezion-geber כרך תרנגולא, the fortress of the cock, Num 33:35–36. and Deut 2:8, as if Ezion-geber were named from a cock, whereas עציון גבר is the spine of a man. That Ezion-geber was called by this name we have shown in our Phaleg, p. 764, because it is situated in a place inaccessible because of reefs and rocks (ῥαχίαις καὶ σπίλοις ἀπροσίτῳ [rhachiais kai spilois aprositō]), like Dyrrachium in Macedonia. And what is read of Balaam in Num 24:3, נאם הגבר, The man said, Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] takes as if it read The cock said, and taking occasion col. 119 from this he draws a ridiculous comparison of Balaam with a cock in these words: קרא גבר שדומה בלעם לתרנגול כמו שהתרנגול הוא נואף מכל כן בלעם רבע אתונו וכמו שהתרנגול יודע בעתים כן בלעם יודע לכוין השעה שהקב״ה כועס וכמו שהתרנגול דרכו לעמוד על רגל אחד כן בלעם חגר באחד רגליו, He calls him גבר, because Balaam was like a cock. As the cock is lecherous above all, so Balaam lay with his she-ass; as the cock knows the times, so Balaam knew how to find out the hour at which God, the Holy One, blessed be He, is angry; as the cock is wont to stand on one foot, so Balaam was lame in one foot. Most absurd of all is this: although the other sacrifices which God had prescribed in his Law have now been abolished, yet on the day preceding the feast of Atonement there is no one among the Jews who does not offer a cock for himself, repeating these words while he strikes it against his head three times: זה חליפתי זה תחתי זה כפרתי זה התרנגול ילך למיתה ואני אלך לחיים טובים עם ירשאל אמן, Let this cock be an exchange for me; let this be in my place; let this be an atonement for me; let this cock go to death, but I, with all Israel, to a happy life. Amen. For the rest, consult the distinguished Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum]'s Synagoga, chapter 20. Here it suffices to add that the occasion of the error was the ambiguous word גבר, by which they hold that both man and cock are signified. It is fitting, therefore, they say, that since גבר has sinned, גבר II, 688 should bear the penalty of the sin. As if a cock and a man could be held as one before God's tribunal because, through an abuse of the word, they are called by the same name. I deliberately pass over what is found about the cock in the second Targum of Esther, because these are not paraphrases but the author's mere fabrications. ¶
