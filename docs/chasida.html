III, 85 col. 326 Chapter 29. ¶

Probatur, חסידה hasida esse Ciconiam.

It is proved that חסידה hasida is the stork. ¶

Non diu deliberandum, ex tot interpretamentis vocis חסידה hasida, quod po- col. 327 tissimum sit seligendum. Omnino enim חסידה est ciconia. Quod probatur primo ex nomine. Nam חסידה piam et benignam sonat. Talis autem est ciconia. Romanorum usu [inquit Ambrosius a)] avis pia vocatur. Et in Publio pietati-cultrix, in versibus notissimis, qui citantur a Petronio:

There is no need for long deliberation over which of so many interpretations of the word חסידה hasida should above all col. 327 be chosen. For חסידה is certainly the stork. This is proved, first, from the name. For חסידה means pious and kind. And such is the stork. In Roman usage [says Ambrose a)] it is called the pious bird. And in Publius it is pietati-cultrix [cultivator of piety], in the very well-known verses quoted by Petronius: ¶

Ciconia etiam grata, peregrina, hospita,
Pietati-cultrix, gracili-pes, crotalistria.

The stork too, welcome, a wanderer, a guest, cultivator of piety, slender-footed, castanet-player. ¶

Ratio est in promtu. Pietatis est ἀμοιβὰς ἀποδιδόναι τοῖς προγόνοις, mutuas parentibus vices rependere: quod jubet Paulus 1 Tim. V, 4. Id vero praestare ciconiam, uno ore testantur omnes scriptores. E vetustioribus ea de re Platonem consule in fine primi Alcibiadis, et Aristotelem libri noni Historiae animalium capite decimo tertio, et Aristophanem in Avibus, ubi graculus ita loquitur,

The reason is obvious. It belongs to piety ἀμοιβὰς ἀποδιδόναι τοῖς προγόνοις [amoibas apodidonai tois progonois], to repay one's parents in turn, as Paul commands, 1 Tim. V, 4. And that the stork does this all writers testify with one voice. Among the older writers, consult on this matter Plato at the end of the first Alcibiades, and Aristotle, History of Animals, book 9, chapter 13, and Aristophanes in the Birds, where the jackdaw speaks thus, ¶

Ἀλλ’ ἔστιν ἡμῖν τοῖσιν ὄρνισιν νόμος
Παλαιὸς ἐν τοῖς τῶν πελαργῶν κύρβεσιν,
Ἐπὴν ὁ πατὴρ ὁ πελαργὸς ἐκπετησίμους
Πάντας ποιήσῃ τοὺς πελαργιδεῖς τρέφων,
Δεῖ τοὺς νεοττοὺς τὸν πατέρα πάλιν τρέφειν.

Ἀλλ’ ἔστιν ἡμῖν τοῖσιν ὄρνισιν νόμος Παλαιὸς ἐν τοῖς τῶν πελαργῶν κύρβεσιν, Ἐπὴν ὁ πατὴρ ὁ πελαργὸς ἐκπετησίμους Πάντας ποιήσῃ τοὺς πελαργιδεῖς τρέφων, Δεῖ τοὺς νεοττοὺς τὸν πατέρα πάλιν τρέφειν. [All’ estin hēmin toisin ornisin nomos / Palaios en tois tōn pelargōn kyrbesin, / Epēn ho patēr ho pelargos ekpetēsimous / Pantas poiēsē tous pelargideis trephōn, / Dei tous neottous ton patera palin trephein.] ¶

Sed nobis avibus lex antiqua est in ciconiarum tabulis, ut, postquam pater ciconia evolabiles fecerit omnes pullos nutriens, ipsos patrem vicissim nutrire oporteat. Hinc Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo tertio, Genitricum senectam invicem educant. Et Solinus, Eximia illis inest pietas. Etenim quantum temporis impenderint foetibus educandis, tantum et ipsae a pullis suis invicem aluntur. Et Aelianus libri tertii capite vigesimo tertio, Τρέφειν μὲν τοὺς πατέρας πελαγοὶ γε-γηρακότας καὶ ἐθέλουσι, καὶ ἐμελέτησαν. κελεύει δ’ αὐτοὺς νόμος ἀνθρωπικὸς οὐδὲ εἰς τοῦτο, ἀλλ’ αἰτία τούτων φύσις ἀγαθή. Patres jam senes ciconiae alere volunt et student. Non quod eas ad id adigat ulla humana lex, sed in causa est naturae bonitas. Quocirca septem Sapientes cum interrogasset Croesus, Τί τῶν ζώων εὐδαιμονέστατον; Quod esset animal felicissimum? Πελαργοὺς, ciconias nonnulli responderunt, quia δίχα νόμου τῇ φύσει τὸ δίκαιον ἔχουσι, natura et sine lege justitiam observant. Ita Apostolius Proverbio 1141. Et apud Origenem contra Celsum libro quarto Celsus asserit, ob id ipsum εὐσεβεστέρους εἶναι τοὺς πελαργοὺς III, 86 τῶν ἀνθρώπων, ciconias esse hominibus magis pias. Et Aeliani libri decimi capite decimo sexto, Αἰγύπτιοι τοὺς πελαργοὺς καὶ προσκυνοῦσιν, ἐπεὶ τοὺς πα-τέρας γηροκομοῦσιν, Aegyptii ciconias adorant, quia parentes senes alunt. Et in Hori Hieroglyphicis, libri secundi capite quinquagesimo quinto, Φιλοπάτορα βουλόμενοι σημῆναι ἄνθρωπον, πελαργὸν ζωγραφοῦσιν· ὑπὸ γὰρ τῶν γεννησάν-των ἐκτραφεὶς οὐ χωρίζεται τῶν ἰδίων πατέρων, ἀλλὰ παραμένει αὐτοῖς ἄχρις ἐσχάτου γήρως, θεράπειαν αὐτοῖς ἀπονέμων. Cum filium, patris amantem, significare volunt, ciconiam pingunt: quae, a parentibus enutrita, ab iis non divellitur, sed ad extremum usque senium una permanet, cultum illis rependens. Quale est, quod, ut innuerent, vim justitiae subesse, ἐν τοῖς σκήπτροις ἀνωτέρω μὲν πελαργὸν τυποῦσι, κατωτέρω δὲ ποτάμιον ἵππον, in summo sceptro ciconiam, in infimo hippopotamum effingunt. Ita Scholiastes Aristophanis in Avibus, et Suidas in voce Ἀντιπελαργεῖν, quae more ciconiarum par pari rependere significat; quo sensu a veteribus passim usurpari videas. Aristaenetus libri primi Epistola vigesima quinta, Οὕτω με νῦν ἀντιπελαργοῦσα δικαί-αν ἀποδίδωσι χάριν. Basilius in Hexaëmeron Homilia octava, Τινὲς τῶν εὐερ-γετημάτων ἀντίδοσιν ἀντιπελάργωσιν ὀνομάζουσι. Ambrosius in Hexaëmeron col. 328 libri quinti capite decimo sexto, Retributio beneficiorum ἀντιπελάργωσις nominatur. Eodem refer, quae habentur in Porta coeli fol. 30. d. ואומרים מין עוף אחד שנקרא ללא״ק שמפרנסין אביו ואמו אחר שיגדל כמו שפרנסו אותו בקטנותו, Ajunt, speciem avium esse lalak (lege לקלאק laklak) nomine, quae parentes suos vicissim alunt, cum senuerunt, quomodo ab iis parvuli nutriti fuerant. Sed de hoc argumento plura qui volet, legat in iis locis Basilium et Ambrosium, Philonem in Decalogum, Epiphanium in Physiologo capite vigesimo quinto, Eustathium in Hexaëmeron, Philen capite sexto De animalibus, et Paroemiographos Zenobium, Apostolium, Erasmum, ad voces Ἀντιπελαργεῖν, et Πελαρ-γικοὶ νόμοι. Neque enim nobis vacat in re omnium tritissima diutius immorari. Tantum addo, ciconias non solum erga parentes, sed et erga quosvis alios gratitudinem suam testari, si vera est Aeliani b) de Tarentina muliere, Heracleïde, narratio, quae, cum ciconiae crus fractum obligasset, et remediis suis ad incolumitatem perduxisset, anno sequente accepti beneficii memor ciconia in ejus sinum lapidem conjecit, cujus fulgore noctu splendebat domus, ὡς εἰσκομισθεί-σης δᾳδὸς, tanquam illata face. Lychnin, vel lychnidem, vel lychnitem intelligo, per quam noctu collucent aedes, ut lucernis accensis. Vide Lucianum in Syria Dea, et Plinium libri trigesimi septimi capite septimo, et, quod maxime ad rem facit, Philostratum, apud quem πελαργοὶ καλιὰς οὐκ ἂν πήξαιντο, μὴ πρότερον αὐταῖς ἐναρμόσαντες τὸν λυχνίτην λίθον, ciconiae nunquam nidos exstruunt, III, 87 quin lychnitem lapidem iis imponant. Simile quid Vesaliae accidisse, refert Goblerus apud Gesnerum. Ibi scilicet ciconiam, quotannis in eadem domo nidificare solitam, postquam hospitis clementiam per multos annos esset experta, e consueta migratione sub veris tempus redeuntem, viridem Zingiberis radicem, tanquam pro xenio et hospitali munere, ad ejus pedes gratulabundam projecisse. Sed et in pullos ciconia est maxime φιλόστοργος, et, si cibus, quo illos alat, non suppetat aliunde, pelicani instar, ut ajunt, τὴν ἑαυτοῦ χτιζὴν (τροφὴν) ἀνεμέσας ἐκείνους τρέφει, hesterno cibo evomito eos alit. Ita Aelianus ubi supra. Ex quibus lectori fit planum, nomen חסידה hasida, quasi benignam et piam dicas, ciconiae convenire multis de causis (1).

But for us birds there is an ancient law in the tables of the storks: that after the father stork, by feeding them, has made all the chicks able to fly, they in turn must feed their father. Hence Pliny, book 10, chapter 23: They in turn rear their mothers in old age. And Solinus: They have remarkable piety. For as much time as they have spent rearing their young, for so long are they in turn fed by their own chicks. And Aelian, book 3, chapter 23: Τρέφειν μὲν τοὺς πατέρας πελαγοὶ γεγηρακότας καὶ ἐθέλουσι, καὶ ἐμελέτησαν. κελεύει δ’ αὐτοὺς νόμος ἀνθρωπικὸς οὐδὲ εἰς τοῦτο, ἀλλ’ αἰτία τούτων φύσις ἀγαθή [Trephein men tous pateras pelagoi gegērakotas kai ethelousi, kai emeletēsan. keleuei d’ autous nomos anthrōpikos oude eis touto, all’ aitia toutōn physis agathē]. Storks are willing and eager to feed their fathers when they are old. Not that any human law compels them to it; rather, the goodness of nature is the cause. Therefore, when Croesus asked the Seven Sages, Τί τῶν ζώων εὐδαιμονέστατον; [Ti tōn zōōn eudaimonestaton?] Which animal was the happiest?, some answered Πελαργούς [Pelargous], storks, because δίχα νόμου τῇ φύσει τὸ δίκαιον ἔχουσι [dicha nomou tē physei to dikaion echousi], they observe justice by nature and without law. So Apostolius, Proverb 1141. And in Origen, Against Celsus, book 4, Celsus asserts that for this very reason εὐσεβεστέρους εἶναι τοὺς πελαργοὺς III, 86 τῶν ἀνθρώπων [eusebesterous einai tous pelargous tōn anthrōpōn], storks are more pious than men. And Aelian, book 10, chapter 16: Αἰγύπτιοι τοὺς πελαργοὺς καὶ προσκυνοῦσιν, ἐπεὶ τοὺς πατέρας γηροκομοῦσιν [Aigyptioi tous pelargous kai proskynousin, epei tous pateras gērokomousin], the Egyptians worship storks, because they feed their parents in old age. And in the Hieroglyphics of Horus, book 2, chapter 55: Φιλοπάτορα βουλόμενοι σημῆναι ἄνθρωπον, πελαργὸν ζωγραφοῦσιν· ὑπὸ γὰρ τῶν γεννησάντων ἐκτραφεὶς οὐ χωρίζεται τῶν ἰδίων πατέρων, ἀλλὰ παραμένει αὐτοῖς ἄχρις ἐσχάτου γήρως, θεράπειαν αὐτοῖς ἀπονέμων [Philopatora boulomenoi sēmēnai anthrōpon, pelargon zōgraphousin; hypo gar tōn gennēsantōn ektrapheis ou chōrizetai tōn idiōn paterōn, alla paramenei autois achris eschatou gērōs, therapeian autois aponemōn]. When they wish to signify a son who loves his father, they paint a stork: for, having been reared by its parents, it is not separated from them, but stays with them right up to extreme old age, repaying them with care. Of the same kind is the fact that, to indicate that the force of justice lies beneath, ἐν τοῖς σκήπτροις ἀνωτέρω μὲν πελαργὸν τυποῦσι, κατωτέρω δὲ ποτάμιον ἵππον [en tois skēptrois anōterō men pelargon typousi, katōterō de potamion hippon], they carve a stork at the top of the sceptre and a hippopotamus at the bottom. So the Scholiast on Aristophanes on the Birds, and Suidas under the word Ἀντιπελαργεῖν [Antipelargein], which means to repay like with like, in the manner of storks; you may see it used in this sense everywhere by the ancients. Aristaenetus, book 1, Epistle 25: Οὕτω με νῦν ἀντιπελαργοῦσα δικαίαν ἀποδίδωσι χάριν [Houtō me nyn antipelargousa dikaian apodidōsi charin]. Basil, Hexaemeron, Homily 8: Τινὲς τῶν εὐεργετημάτων ἀντίδοσιν ἀντιπελάργωσιν ὀνομάζουσι [Tines tōn euergetēmatōn antidosin antipelargōsin onomazousi]. Ambrose, Hexaemeron col. 328 book 5, chapter 16: The repayment of benefits is called ἀντιπελάργωσις [antipelargōsis]. Refer to the same point what is found in the Porta coeli, fol. 30d: ואומרים מין עוף אחד שנקרא ללא״ק שמפרנסין אביו ואמו אחר שיגדל כמו שפרנסו אותו בקטנותו, They say that there is a species of bird named lalak (read לקלאק laklak) which in turn feeds its parents when they have grown old, just as it was fed by them when little. But whoever wants more on this subject should read Basil and Ambrose in those passages, Philo on the Decalogue, Epiphanius in the Physiologus, chapter 25, Eustathius on the Hexaemeron, Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)], On Animals, chapter 6, and the paroemiographers Zenobius, Apostolius and Erasmus [=Desiderius Erasmus, Adagia], at the words Ἀντιπελαργεῖν [Antipelargein] and Πελαργικοὶ νόμοι [Pelargikoi nomoi]. For we have no leisure to dwell longer on a matter so utterly hackneyed. I add only that storks show their gratitude not only towards their parents but towards anyone else, if Aelian's b) story of the woman of Tarentum, Heracleis, is true. When she had bound up a stork's broken leg and brought it back to health with her remedies, the following year the stork, mindful of the kindness received, dropped into her lap a stone by whose brilliance the house shone at night, ὡς εἰσκομισθείσης δᾳδὸς [hōs eiskomistheisēs dados], as if a torch had been brought in. I understand this as the lychnis, or lychnites, by which houses are lit up at night as though by lighted lamps. See Lucian, On the Syrian Goddess, and Pliny, book 37, chapter 7, and, what is most to the point, Philostratus, in whom πελαργοὶ καλιὰς οὐκ ἂν πήξαιντο, μὴ πρότερον αὐταῖς ἐναρμόσαντες τὸν λυχνίτην λίθον [pelargoi kalias ouk an pēxainto, mē proteron autais enarmosantes ton lychnitēn lithon], storks never build nests III, 87 without placing the lychnites stone in them. Goblerus, in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], reports that something similar happened at Wesel. There, namely, a stork that used to nest every year on the same house, after experiencing the kindness of its host for many years, on returning from its usual migration around the beginning of spring, threw at his feet, as if in thanks, a green ginger root, as a guest-gift and token of hospitality. Moreover the stork is also extremely φιλόστοργος [philostorgos] towards its chicks, and, if food to nourish them is not available from elsewhere, then, like the pelican, as they say, τὴν ἑαυτοῦ χτιζὴν (τροφὴν) ἀνεμέσας ἐκείνους τρέφει [tēn heautou chtizēn (trophēn) anemesas ekeinous trephei], it feeds them by vomiting up yesterday's food. So Aelian in the passage cited above. From all this it becomes plain to the reader that the name חסידה hasida, as if you were to say kind and pious, suits the stork for many reasons R1. ¶

Altera probatio ducitur ex iis Scripturae locis, in quibus de hasida agitur. In his enim quidquid hasidae tribuitur, ciconiae maxime convenit. Quod ut le-ctori planum fiat, juvat illa omnia percurrere atque excutere.

The second proof is drawn from those passages of Scripture in which the hasida is treated. For in them whatever is attributed to the hasida fits the stork best. To make this plain to the reader, it is worth running through and examining all of them. ¶

I. Moses hasidam censet inter immundas aves, et ejus esum Iudaeis prohibet. Id in ciconiam apprime quadrat, quae serpentibus vescitur et venenosis animalibus, neque in nido pullos suos aliis alit cibis. Iuvenalis Satyra decima quarta,

I. Moses counts the hasida among the unclean birds and forbids the Jews to eat it. This suits the stork exceedingly well, for it feeds on serpents and venomous animals, and in the nest it feeds its young on no other food. Juvenal, Satire 14: ¶

— — serpente ciconia pullos
Nutrit, et inventa per devia rura lacerta.

— — the stork feeds its young
on the serpent, and on the lizard found in the trackless fields. ¶

Unde est, quod in Thessalia magni fiebat. Aristoteles in Mirabilibus, Περὶ Θετταλίαν μνημονεύουσιν ὄφεις ζωογονηθῆναι τοσούτους, ὥστε. εἰ μὴ ὑπὸ τῶν πε-λαργῶν ἀνῃροῦντο, ἐκχωρῆσαι ἂν αὐτούς· Circa Thessaliam ajunt tot generari serpentes, ut a ciconiis nisi interimerentur, regio esset deserenda. Plutarchus in Iside, Θεσσαλοὶ δὲ πελαργοὺς (ἐτίμησαν), ὅτι, πολλοὺς ὄφεις τῆς γῆς ἀναδιδού-σης, ἐπιφανέντες ἐξώλεσαν ἅπαντας· Thessali ciconias in honore habuerunt, III, 88 quod, cum multos serpentes terra eorum edidisset, adventu suo omnes perdiderint. Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo tertio, Honos iis serpentum exitio tantus, ut in Thessalia capitale fuerit occidisse. Solinus de Thessalia, Ubi serpentum imma- nis copia est, quos dum escandi gratia (ciconiae) insectantur, regionibus Thessalicis plurimum mali detrahunt. Ut de Aegypto Bellonius, Ciconias Aegyptii merito col. 329 amant, cum propter ranas, quas illic nimis abunde nascentes consumunt, tum quia serpentes etiam integros devorant. Proinde hodieque Mahumetani disputant, utrum licitus sit esus اللغلق alleklek, id est, ciconiae. Et nonnulli quidem concedunt, كالكركي ut gruis: sed plerique prohibent, بانه ياكل الحيات quia comedit serpentes. Itaque ex regula, qua conceduntur omnes aquaticae, excipitur haec sola avis, altera insuper addita ratione, quod دف في طيرانه كما تفعل الجوارح alas inter vo- landum agitat, ac si illis verberaret, quomodo rapaces aves, quae omnes sunt prohibitae. Quod apud Damirem pluribus explicant impurae sectae theologi. Leklek esse ciconiam, non gruem, ut in Avicenna multi explicant, cum ipsa descriptio nos docet, tum haec Abenbitaris, فالرغوس هو اللغلق Πελαργὸς est alleklek. Scribitur etiam per Gain اللغلغ allegleg, quamvis in Alcamus nonnulli لقلق leklek, et لغلغ legleg, velint esse aves diversas; sed perperam: cum avis لغلغ legleg, a ciconia diversae, descriptio nusquam extet. Et Damir tractatum De ciconia sic incipiat, اللغلق طاير طويل العنق وربما قالوا اللغلغ Alleklek, avis longo collo, quam quandoque vocant allegleg. Ex utroque conflatum Turcicum ciconiae nomen leglek apud Gesnerum, pro quo legendum leklek, aut legleg. Et male Turcicum vocatur, cum sit mere Arabicum. Turcicum autem est tuigon, ut Busbequius asserit in Epistolis De legatione Turcica (2).

Hence it was highly prized in Thessaly. Aristotle in the Marvels: Περὶ Θετταλίαν μνημονεύουσιν ὄφεις ζωογονηθῆναι τοσούτους, ὥστε. εἰ μὴ ὑπὸ τῶν πελαργῶν ἀνῃροῦντο, ἐκχωρῆσαι ἂν αὐτούς· [Peri Thettalian mnēmoneuousin opheis zōogonēthēnai tosoutous, hōste ei mē hypo tōn pelargōn anērounto, ekchōrēsai an autous] They say that around Thessaly so many serpents are bred that, were they not destroyed by the storks, the region would have to be abandoned. Plutarch in Isis: Θεσσαλοὶ δὲ πελαργοὺς (ἐτίμησαν), ὅτι, πολλοὺς ὄφεις τῆς γῆς ἀναδιδούσης, ἐπιφανέντες ἐξώλεσαν ἅπαντας· [Thessaloi de pelargous (etimēsan), hoti, pollous opheis tēs gēs anadidousēs, epiphanentes exōlesan hapantas] The Thessalians held storks in honour, because, when their land had produced many serpents, III, 88 they destroyed them all by their arrival. Pliny, book 10, chapter 23: So great is the honour paid them for destroying serpents that in Thessaly it was a capital crime to have killed one. Solinus on Thessaly: Where there is an enormous quantity of serpents; by hunting them for food (the storks) remove a great deal of harm from the Thessalian districts. So too Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] on Egypt: The Egyptians rightly col. 329 love storks, both because of the frogs, which are born there in excessive abundance and which they consume, and because they even swallow serpents whole. Accordingly the Muslims still dispute today whether it is lawful to eat اللغلق [al-laghlaq] alleklek, that is, the stork. Some indeed permit it, كالكركي [ka-l-kurkī] like the crane: but most forbid it, بانه ياكل الحيات [bi-annahu yaʾkul al-ḥayyāt] because it eats serpents. Thus from the rule by which all water birds are permitted this bird alone is excepted, with a further reason added, that دف في طيرانه كما تفعل الجوارح [daffa fī ṭayarānihi kamā tafʿal al-jawāriḥ] it moves its wings in flight as if beating with them, as birds of prey do, all of which are forbidden. The theologians of that impure sect explain this at greater length in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]. That the leklek is the stork, not the crane, as many explain it in Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], is shown both by the description itself and by this statement of Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār]: فالرغوس هو اللغلق [fālarghūs huwa al-laghlaq] Πελαργὸς [Pelargos] is alleklek. Having thus examined the passages of Scripture, the third argument by which we have proved that the hasida is the stork rests on the authority of many interpreters. For although we have shown by a long enumeration that most have gone astray, there still remain many for whom the hasida is the stork. Among these are, 1., those of the Greeks whom Olympiodorus refutes with worthless reasons: The stork, he says, does not lay its eggs on the ground. But neither does the ἀσίδα [asida], as he would have it, deceived by the version of the Greeks, who seem to attribute to the asida what the Hebrew text attributes to the ostrich. Nor is what he adds of greater weight, that τοὺς ὀρνιθιακὰ γράψαντας [tous ornithiaka grapsantas] distinguished the asida from the stork. For the Greek and Roman writers who treated of birds have nothing about the name asida, because it is Hebrew, not Greek or Latin. The others before Olympiodorus I do not regard, most of whom were entirely ignorant both of Hebrew and of the nature of birds. 2. To the Greeks we add the Syrians, for whom the hasida is ܚܘܪܒܐ [חורבא] hurba. So they render it everywhere, except in the passage of Job. Moreover, for the most learned of the Maronites ܚܘܪܒܐ [חורבא] is the stork R5. 3. To these are added the Arabic translators of the Prophets, who in Ierem. VIII, 7. and Zachar. V, 9. have for hasida القعقع [al-qaʿqaʿ] alkoko, that is, the stork, not the crane, as Gabriel would have it, much less the magpie. Iam, si hanc versionem cum reliquis conferas, eam esse deprehendes longe praestantissimam. Nam in aliis omnibus vitium est manifestum. Sic in ar- dea, fulica, cygno, milvo, struthione nihil est, cur a pietate denominentur. Et, si milvum excipias, harum nulla est migratoria. Idem de upupa judicium. Nec in arboribus nidulantur fulica, cygnus, struthio. Atque, utut caetera quadrarent, cum ad Iobi locum deventum est, ubi cum struthionis pennis confertur penna hasidae, excepta ciconia, non aliam avem video, cui possit hoc competere III, 96 (7). Quae exempli causa afferimus. Nam caetera per se praestabit, quisquis locorum de hasida collationem instituet.

Now, if you compare this version with the rest, you will find it by far the best. For in all the others the fault is manifest. Thus in the heron, the coot, the swan, the kite and the ostrich there is nothing for which they should be named from piety. And, if you except the kite, none of them is migratory. The same judgement applies to the hoopoe. Nor do the coot, the swan and the ostrich nest in trees. And, however well the rest might fit, when one comes to the passage of Job, where the plumage of the hasida is compared with the feathers of the ostrich, I see no bird other than the stork to which this could apply III, 96 R7. These things we offer by way of example. For anyone who undertakes a comparison of the passages on the hasida will supply the rest for himself. ¶

It remains to add a few remarks on the various names of storks. The Hebrew חסידה hasida and the Greek πελαργὸς [pelargos] have already been fully explained. The Syriac ܚܘܪܒܐ [חורבא] hurba seems to have been formed by metathesis from ܚܘܒܪܐ [חוברא] hubra, which would mean the sociable bird. Of the Israelites it is said in Iud. XX, 11.: All the Israelites were gathered together, as one man, חברים, companions. See the same thing in Pliny about storks! When about to depart they gather in a fixed place, and they keep together in such a way that none of their kind is left behind, unless it is captive and enslaved. The Arabic لقلق [laqlaq] leklek and لغلغ [laghlagh] legleg were formed from the stork's cry, as the Arabs expressly teach. Damir: وصوته اللقلقه وكذلك كل صوت فيه حركه واضطراب [wa-ṣawtuhu al-laqlaqa wa-kadhālika kull ṣawt fīhi ḥaraka wa-iḍṭirāb] And laklaka is the cry of the stork, and every sound that is produced with vibration and agitation. Accordingly laklaka is also the name for the hiss of a serpent, produced with the tongue thrust out. Moreover, this cry of the stork does not come from deep in the throat, but is made by the two parts of the bill striking against each other, like a castanet or a rattle. Hence Publilius Syrus calls it crotalistria [castanet-player], and Ovid, in book 6 of the Metamorphoses, says ¶

— — crepitante ciconia rostro.

— — the stork with rattling bill. ¶

Et Solinus sonum, quo crepitant, oris potius, quam vocis, esse scripsit. Sed melius rostri dixisset. Philostratus quoque in Epistola ad Epictetum, Καὶ τοὺς πελαργοὺς, ἐπειδὰν παριόντας ἡμᾶς κροτῶσιν, id est, Ciconias, cum ad nos praetereuntes crepitant. Id enim ibi κροτῶσιν, unde crotalistria. Sed et eodem refertur CICONIAE Latinum nomen. Isidorus; CICONIAE, quasi cicaniae, a sono, quo crepitant, dictae sunt; quem rostro quatiente faciunt. And Solinus wrote that the sound with which they rattle is of the mouth rather than of the voice. But he would have done better to say of the bill. Philostratus too, in his Letter to Epictetus: Καὶ τοὺς πελαργοὺς, ἐπειδὰν παριόντας ἡμᾶς κροτῶσιν [Kai tous pelargous, epeidan pariontas hēmas krotōsin], that is, The storks, when they rattle at us as we pass by. For the word there is κροτῶσιν [krotōsin], whence crotalistria. The Latin name CICONIA also goes back to the same thing. Nor is there any doubt that the Arabic العقعق [al-ʿaqʿaq] alkoko, which we explained above i), alludes to this very thing. ¶
