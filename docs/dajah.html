II, 782 col. 195 CHAPTER IX.

Probatur, דיה daja esse Vulturem nigrum.

It is proved that דיה daja is the black vulture.

Post איה aja sequitur דיה daja, Deut. XIV, 13. Quod omissum in Levitico, vel ab ipso Propheta, qua de causa nescimus, vel a scribis post Prophetam propter τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον duarum vocum האיה haaja et הדיה hadaja, quod ultimum puto esse vero propius. Quid sit דיה daja, non satis notum est. Hieronymus, et Arabes (1), et multi recentiorum milvum interpretantur, quasi sit II, 783 idem, quod דאה daa, Levit. XI, 14. (2). Quod vix videtur posse ferri: quia, ut probavimus, דאה daa et ראה raa idem sunt. Porro, ראה raa et דיה daja non possunt idem esse, cum in eodem versu Iudaeis prohibeantur tanquam diversa animalia. Ita enim habet sacer textus: Haec sunt, ex quibus non comedetis, aquila, etc. והראה ואת האיה והדיה, et raa, et aja, et daja. Ubi cum ראה raa sit idem, quod in Levitico דאה daa, milvus scilicet, omnino דיה daja aliud est: nempe vultur, ut quidem puto. Quomodo reddunt R. Selomo, Castalio, col. 196 Schindlerus, Anglica et Italica versio. Fullerus quoque Miscellaneorum lib. 6. cap. 4. Cui versioni firmandae multum faciunt haec Es. XXXIV, 15. de Idumaeae desolatione, Ibi דיות dajoth inter se convenerunt. Nam דיות dajoth, Plurale τοῦ דיה daja, non possunt esse milvi, qui non sunt gregales aves, neque unquam inter se conveniunt, aut congregantur (3). Sed possunt esse vultures, de quibus Gesnerus pag. 785. Vultures soli uncunguium gregatim degunt, ita, ut aliquando quinquaginta in uno grege appareant, ut in Aegypto se observasse scribit II, 784 Bellonius. Hinc Philosophus Hist. libro sexto capite quinto, Πολλοὶ ἐξαί-φνης φαίνονται ἀκολουθοῦντες τοῖς στρατεύμασι· Multi derepente apparent, qui sequuntur exercitus. Et libri noni capite undecimo idem scribit, φαίνεσθαι τα-χὺ πολλοὺς, subito apparere multos. Basilius quoque in Hexaëmeron Homilia octava, Ἴδοις ἂν μυρίας ἀγέλας γυπῶν τοῖς στρατοπέδοις παρεπομένας· Vulturum videas innumeros greges exercitus sequentes. Et Ambrosius, hunc imitatus, in Hexaëmeron libri quinti capite vigesimo tertio, Cum bellum lacrymabile inter se adversae acies instaurant, multo praedictae volucres sequuntur agmine. Petrus Bembus Historiae Venetae libro quinto, Constat illis diebus, quibus bellum contra Bajazetem parabatur in Apulia, corvos vulturesque tanta ex aëre vi, tantisque agminibus inter se conflixisse, ut carri duodecim eorum cadaveribus explerentur. Non possum hic omittere duodecim vultures, a Romulo visos, tum, cum Romam urbem condidit: unde collegit Vectius, Romano imperio post exacta totidem saecula finem fore. Quod post Varronem scripsit Censorinus capite decimo quarto, anno ab urbe condita circiter millesimo. Quot, inquit, saecula urbi Romae debeantur, dicere meum non est. Sed, quid apud Varronem legerim, non tacebo, qui libro Antiquitatum duodevigesimo ait fuisse Vectium Romae in augurio non ignobilem ingenio, cuivis doctori in disceptando parem. Eum se audivisse dicentem, Si ita essent, ut tradebant Historici de Romuli urbis condendae auguriis, ac duodecim vulturibus, quoniam CXX annos incolumis praeteriisset, populum Romanum ad mille et ducentos perventurum. Neque defuit eventus vaticinio. Nam paullo post exacta duodecim illa saecula, reipublicae viribus ex Occidente in Orientem translatis, Romana dignitas consumpta est, et occidentale imperium defecit in Augustulo. Is enim purpuram deposuit Herulorum metu, qui Adovacre duce in Italiam irruperant, ut scribit Paulus Diaconus libro decimo sexto. Ita Caesari, cum in campum Martium exercitum deduceret, sex vultures apparuerunt. Conscendenti deinde rostra creato consuli iterum sex vultures conspecti, veluti Romuli auspiciis novam urbem condituro, signum dederunt. Ita tradit Iulius Obsequens. Et apud Suetonium Octavio Augusto, primo consulatu augurium capienti, duodecim se vultures, ut Romulo, ostenderunt. Quod paullo aliter refert Dio, Πρώτῃ τῶν ἀρχαιρεσιῶν ἐς τὸ πεδίον τὸ Ἄρειον εἰσελθὼν, γύπας ἓξ· καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα δημηγορῶν τι πρὸς τοὺς στρατειώ-τας, ἄλλους δώδεκα εἶδε. Comitiorum prima die, in campum Martium ingressus, sex vultures: nec paullo post, cum militibus orationem haberet, duodecim alios vidit. Ex quibus hoc saltem colligere est, vultures saepe congregari, quod de avibus aliis rapacibus non scribitur. Frustra objeceris, quod legitur Matth. XXIV, 28. et Luc. XVII, 37. etiam aquilas ad cadaver congregari. Neque enim ibi agitur de genuinis aquilis, sed de percnopteris, seu gypaeetis, quae sunt aquilae degeneres, et vulturina specie, atque ex aquilae cum vulture concubitu, II, 785 ut videtur, ortae; quod supraa) docuimus. Utut sit, quisquis in Esaiae verbis, quibus דיות dajoth convenire, aut congregari scribit, milvos intelligi volet, is necesse habebit docere, vel experientia teste, vel ex idoneis scriptoribus, milvos quandoque congregari. Quod a milvorum genio tam abhorret, quam vulturum naturae convenit. Omnino igitur דיות dajoth sunt vultures, non mil- col. 197 vi. Non tamen putaverim, omne vulturum genus hoc nomine contineri, sed solum nigros. 1. Quia Deut. XIV, 13. 17. a Mose distinguuntur tanquam diversa animalia דיה daja et רחם racham; quorum hoc significare vulturis genus, magna ex parte album, infra dicetur. 2. Quia דיה daja et דיו dejo, atramen- tum, sunt Conjugata. Ne dicam, a Syris et Persis daemonem dici ܕܝܘܐ dajava, vel deva, ab atri coloris specie, qua potissimum hominibus se conspiciendum exhibet, ut graviorem intuentibus terrorem incutiat. Unde est, quod quaedam sabinae species Persice ديودار diudar dicitur, id est, daemonis ar- bor. Itaque etiam a colore דיה daja vocatur id vulturis genus, quod nigrum est. Et recte Elias, דיה Daja dicitur avis, quae nigra est כדיו, ut atramentum. Nec immerito Kimchius דיה et דיו revocat ad eandem Radicem. Porro de nigro vulture multa legere est apud veteres. Ita Plinius libri decimi capite sexto dicit, vulturum praevalere nigros. Et apud poëtas inter inferorum poenas una est e gravissimis, ab atro vulture rodi. Hinc Iuvenalis Satyra decima tertia,

After איה aja comes דיה daja, Deut 14:13. It is omitted in Leviticus, either by the Prophet himself, for a reason we do not know, or by scribes after the Prophet because of the τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον [to homoioptōton] of the two words האיה haaja and הדיה hadaja; I think the latter is nearer the truth. What דיה daja is, is not sufficiently known. Jerome, the Arabs R1, and many of the more recent writers interpret it as the kite, as though it were II, 783 the same as דאה daa, Lev 11:14 R2. This seems scarcely tenable, because, as we have proved, דאה daa and ראה raa are the same. Moreover, ראה raa and דיה daja cannot be the same, since in the same verse they are forbidden to the Jews as different animals. For the sacred text reads: These are the ones of which you shall not eat: the eagle, etc. והראה ואת האיה והדיה, and the raa, and the aja, and the daja. Here, since ראה raa is the same as דאה daa in Leviticus, namely the kite, דיה daja is certainly something else: namely the vulture, as I at least think. So render R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], col. 196 Schindler, and the English and Italian versions; also Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], Miscellanea book 6, ch. 4. This rendering is strongly confirmed by Es. XXXIV, 15., on the desolation of Idumaea: There the דיות dajoth have gathered together. For דיות dajoth, the plural of דיה daja, cannot be kites, which are not gregarious birds and never gather or flock together R3. But they can be vultures, of which Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] says, p. 785: Vultures alone among the birds with hooked talons live in flocks, so that sometimes fifty appear in a single flock, as Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] writes that he observed II, 784 in Egypt. Hence the Philosopher, Hist. book 6, chapter 5: Πολλοὶ ἐξαίφνης φαίνονται ἀκολουθοῦντες τοῖς στρατεύμασι [polloi exaiphnēs phainontai akolouthountes tois strateumasi]; Many suddenly appear, following armies. And in book 9, chapter 11, he writes likewise, φαίνεσθαι ταχὺ πολλοὺς [phainesthai tachy pollous], that many suddenly appear. Basil too, in the Hexaemeron, Homily 8: Ἴδοις ἂν μυρίας ἀγέλας γυπῶν τοῖς στρατοπέδοις παρεπομένας [Idois an myrias agelas gypōn tois stratopedois parepomenas]; You may see countless flocks of vultures following armies. And Ambrose, imitating him, in the Hexaemeron, book 5, chapter 23: When opposing lines renew a lamentable war among themselves, the aforesaid birds follow in a great column. Pietro Bembo [=Pietro Bembo, Historia Veneta (1551)], History of Venice, book 5: It is established that in those days when war against Bajazet was being prepared in Apulia, ravens and vultures fought one another in the air with such force and in such hosts that twelve carts were filled with their carcasses. I cannot here omit the twelve vultures seen by Romulus when he founded the city of Rome, from which Vettius inferred that the Roman empire would end after as many centuries had passed. Censorinus, following Varro, wrote of this in chapter 14, about the thousandth year from the founding of the city. How many centuries, he says, are owed to the city of Rome it is not for me to say. But I will not keep silent about what I have read in Varro, who in book 18 of the Antiquities says that there was at Rome one Vettius, not undistinguished in augury for his talent, equal to any learned man in debate; and that he had heard him say that if things were as the historians reported about the auguries of Romulus at the founding of the city and the twelve vultures, then, since the Roman people had passed 120 years unharmed, it would reach a thousand two hundred. Nor did the prophecy lack fulfilment. For shortly after those twelve centuries had passed, when the strength of the state had been transferred from the West to the East, the Roman dignity was exhausted and the Western empire came to an end in Augustulus. For he laid down the purple out of fear of the Heruli, who under their leader Odoacer had burst into Italy, as Paul the Deacon writes in book 16. Likewise, when Caesar was leading his army down into the Campus Martius, six vultures appeared to him. Then, when he mounted the rostra after being made consul, six vultures were again seen and gave a sign, as though he were to found a new city under the auspices of Romulus. So Julius Obsequens reports. And in Suetonius, twelve vultures showed themselves to Octavius Augustus, as to Romulus, when he was taking the auspices in his first consulship. Dio reports this somewhat differently: Πρώτῃ τῶν ἀρχαιρεσιῶν ἐς τὸ πεδίον τὸ Ἄρειον εἰσελθὼν, γύπας ἓξ· καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα δημηγορῶν τι πρὸς τοὺς στρατειώτας, ἄλλους δώδεκα εἶδε [Prōtē tōn archairesiōn es to pedion to Areion eiselthōn, gypas hex; kai meta tauta dēmēgorōn ti pros tous strateiōtas, allous dōdeka eide]. On the first day of the elections, having entered the Campus Martius, he saw six vultures; and shortly afterwards, while addressing the soldiers, twelve others. From these it can at least be inferred that vultures often flock together, which is not written of other birds of prey. In vain would you object what we read in Matt 24:28 and Luke 17:37, that eagles too gather at a carcass. For there it is not a question of true eagles but of percnopteri, or gypaeeti, which are degenerate eagles of vulturine appearance, born, II, 785 as it seems, from the mating of an eagle with a vulture, as we showed abovea). However that may be, whoever would have kites understood in the words of Isaiah where he writes that the דיות dajoth gather or flock together, will have to show, either from experience or from reliable writers, that kites sometimes flock together. This is as foreign to the disposition of kites as it is suited to the nature of vultures. Therefore the דיות dajoth are certainly vultures, not kites. col. 197 Yet I would not think that every kind of vulture is contained under this name, but only the black ones. 1. Because in Deut 14:13 17. Moses distinguishes דיה daja and רחם racham as different animals; and that the latter signifies a kind of vulture largely white will be shown below. 2. Because דיה daja and דיו dejo, ink, are cognates. Not to mention that the Syrians and Persians call a demon ܕܝܘܐ [דיוא] dajava, or deva, from the appearance of a black colour, in which it chiefly shows itself to men, to strike greater terror into those who see it. Hence a certain species of savin is called in Persian ديودار [dīvdār] diudar, that is, demon's tree. So also that kind of vulture which is black is called דיה daja from its colour. And Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] rightly says: The bird that is black כדיו, like ink, is called דיה Daja. Nor without reason does Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] refer דיה and דיו to the same root. Moreover, much can be read about the black vulture in the ancients. Thus Pliny, book 10, chapter 6, says that among vultures the black ones prevail. And among the poets, one of the gravest punishments of the underworld is to be gnawed by a black vulture. Hence Juvenal, Satire 13,

— — — nec saxum, aut vulturis atri Poena. — — — — — —

— — — nor the rock, or the punishment of the black vulture. — — — — — —

Et Seneca in Thyeste de Tityo,

And Seneca in the Thyestes, of Tityus:

Visceribus atras pascit effossis aves.

With his dug-out entrails he feeds the black birds.

Atras aves, id est, vultures nigros. Medici quoque in pharmacis suis li-bentius utuntur atro vulture. Sic Samonicus capit decimo quarto ad oculorum dolorem mitigandum adhiberi vult

Black birds, that is, black vultures. Physicians, too, prefer to use the black vulture in their remedies. Thus Samonicus, in chapter 14, would have applied, to soothe pain of the eyes,

— — — — vulturis atri Fella, chelidoniae fuerint quis gramina mista.

— — — — the gall of the black vulture, with which blades of celandine have been mixed.

Et capite trigesimo quarto πρὸς εὐτοκίαν

And in chapter 34, πρὸς εὐτοκίαν [pros eutokian] [for an easy delivery]:

— — stercus supponunt vulturis atri, Sentiat ut minime partus quam proximus urget. II, 786 Et capite quinquagesimo octavo ad comitialem morbum,

— — they place beneath the dung of the black vulture, so that she may scarcely feel the birth that presses close. II, 786 And in chapter 58, for the falling sickness:

Prodest cum veteri Baccho fel vulturis atri.

The gall of the black vulture is of help with old Bacchus.

Quamvis alii ampli legant: et excipiat Plinius remedia contra serpentes, in quibus nigros vultures minus valere scribit libri vigesimi noni capite quarto. Ex volucribus (inquit) in auxilio contra serpentes primum vultur est. Annotatum quoque, minus virium esse nigris. Nigros illos vultures αἰγυπιοὺς proprie dici, ac utriusque sexus, contra quam alios vultures, et inter aquilinum et vulturinum genus, Aelianus his verbis tradit libri secundi capite quadragesimo sexto: Τοὺς δὲ αἰγυπιοὺς (λέγουσιν) ἐν μεθορίῳ γυπῶν ὄντας καὶ αἰετῶν, εἶναι καὶ ἄῤῥενας, καὶ τὴν χρόαν πεφυκέναι μέλανας. Et Phile aegypios pro Aegyptiis dici insinuat, cum capite secundo scribit, εἶναι δὲ καὶ τοὺς μέλανας ὡς Αἰγυπτίους, eos etiam esse nigros, ut Aegyptios(4). Sed aliud αἰγυπιὸς, aliud Αἰγύπτιος. Illud ἀπὸ τῆς γυπός· hoc vero factum ex Cophti.

Others, however, read ampli [large]; and Pliny makes an exception for remedies against serpents, in which, he writes in book 29, chapter 4, black vultures are less effective. Among birds (he says) the vulture is foremost as a help against serpents. It has also been noted that the black ones have less power. That those black vultures are properly called αἰγυπιοὺς [aigypious], that they are of both sexes, unlike other vultures, and that they stand between the eagle kind and the vulture kind, Aelian reports in book 2, chapter 46, in these words: Τοὺς δὲ αἰγυπιοὺς (λέγουσιν) ἐν μεθορίῳ γυπῶν ὄντας καὶ αἰετῶν, εἶναι καὶ ἄῤῥενας, καὶ τὴν χρόαν πεφυκέναι μέλανας [tous de aigypious (legousin) en methoriō gypōn ontas kai aietōn, einai kai arrhenas, kai tēn chroan pephykenai melanas]. And Philes [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)] hints that aegypii are so called for Aegyptii [Egyptians], when he writes in chapter 2: εἶναι δὲ καὶ τοὺς μέλανας ὡς Αἰγυπτίους [einai de kai tous melanas hōs Aigyptious], that they too are black, like EgyptiansR4. But αἰγυπιὸς [aigypios] is one thing, Αἰγύπτιος [Aigyptios] another. The former comes from γυπός [gypos]; the latter is formed from Cophti.

Here are Belon's own words (de avibus book 2, ch. 28): Once, when we were on the shore of the Euxine Sea, in the part where Thrace lies, at about the end of April, on a very lofty hill next to the column that stands at the mouth of the Bosporus, where a fowler was spreading a net to catch sparrowhawks flying in from the right-hand stretch of the sea, we observed kites arriving there in flocks on a straight course, in such numbers and so dense a column that it seemed a marvel to us. — But when summer comes on, they return to Europe to escape the excessive heat of those regions. But there is yet another thing that prevents us from taking דיות as kites. It is clear from Isa 34:15 that the דיות inhabit desolate places and wildernesses, whereas kites shun the wilderness and seek out cities and places frequented by men, as Aldrovandi [=Ulisse Aldrovandi, Ornithologia (1599–1603)] reports from his own observation and the testimony of many others, Ornithol. vol. 1, book 5, ch. 15, p. 401. Belon too, loc. cit., writes that in Egypt kites are so tame that they fly right up to the windows of houses.
