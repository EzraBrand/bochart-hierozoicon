III, 97 CHAPTER 30. col. 335 ¶

Hebraea voce אנפה anapha significari avem aliquam valde iracundam; fortasse Arabum زمج zummag, aquilae genus, cui ab ira nomen est.

That the Hebrew word אנפה anapha signifies some very irascible bird; perhaps the زمج [zmj] zummag of the Arabs, a kind of eagle that has its name from anger. ¶

Inter aquilarum species una est, nobis aliunde ignota, quam Arabes زمج zummag, barbari scriptores zumach et zimiech vocant. Damir in capite De العقاب Alokab, seu Aquila, وهو نوعان عقاب وزمج Ejus duae sunt species, okab et zummag. Tardivus apud Gesnerum pag. 170. Aqui- lae duo genera sunt: aquila absolute; et altera dicta zimiech, vel zumach. Et rursus, Albedo in capite aut dorso aquilae signum est aquilae melioris, quae Arabice vocatur ZUMMACH, Syriace NAPAM, Graece Philadelphe, Latine MILION. Ad quae verba Gesnerus, Ex his Graecum et Latinum aquilae nomen apud nul- lum idoneum auctorem reperias. Tamen philadelphe palam detortum est ex Grae- co φιλάδελφος· quo nomine hanc avem a Graecis recentioribus fuisse nuncupatam, etsi alibi non legerim, ideo mihi videtur vero consentaneum, quia non potuit illi indi nomen aptius, neque quod naturam ejus magis ad vivum exprimat. Aquilarum enim generi proprium illud est, ut ament solitudinem, et omnis consortii sint prorsus impatientes. Unde est, quod, quamprimum pulli aquilae sunt ad volatum habiles, matres ἐκ τῆς νεοττίας αὐτοὺς ἐκβάλλουσι, καὶ ἐκ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ περὶ αὐτὴν παντὸς ἀπελαύνουσιν· ἐπέχει γὰρ ἓν ζεῦγος αἰετῶν πολὺν τόπον. διόπερ οὐκ ἐᾷ πλησίον αὑτῶν ἄλλους αὐλισθῆναι. Ita Philosophus libri noni capite trigesimo secundo: et eum sequutus Plinius libri decimi capite tertio; Adultos quoque persequitur parens, et longe fugat, aemulos scilicet rapi- nae. Et alioquin unum par aquilarum magno ad populandum tractu, ut satietur, indiget. Determinant ergo spatia, nec in proximo praedantur. Aelianus libri secundi capite trigesimo nono, Κοινωνίαν τε τὴν πρὸς ἄλλον ἥκιστα ἐνδέχεται· Consortium cum aliis nullum admittit. Item, Ἀλλήλων ἀπῳκισμένας οἰκοῦσι κα- col. 336 λιάς, ὑπὲρ τοῦ μὴ διαφέρεσθαι ὑπὲρ θήρας· Aliae ab aliis longissime nidificant, ne pro praeda sit iis contendendum. Tamen ex illo genere زمج zummag vere φιλάδελφος, consorte delectatur, neque solet solus venari, sed comite sibi ad-juncto. Alcamus, الزمج طاير فارسية دو برادران لانه اذا اعجز عن صيده اعانه اخوه Zummag, avis, quae Persice voca- tur DU-BRADRAN (id est, duo fratres), quia, cum praeda ei excidit, illi adest frater ejus. Alleiths, الجمز طاير دون العقاب حمرته III, 98 غالبة تسميه العجم دبران ومن طبعه انه اذا عجز عن صيده اعانه اخوه علي اخذه Giummaz (aliud exemplar melius الزمج zummag) avis infra aquilam (id est, aquilae similis, sed minor), in qua rubedo praepollet. Vocatur in Agem (id est, in Perside) DABRAN imo, ut antea, دو برادران DU-BRADRAN, id est, duo fratres): e natura cujus est, ut, cum non potest praedam consequi, frater ei opem ferat ad eam capiendam. Quod de falconibus scribunt recentiores. Vincentius in Doctrinali libri decimi sexti capite centesimo quinquagesimo sexto, In ardeae captura duo falcones pariter relaxantur: quorum unus summa petit; alius juxta terram in imo volat. Ut videlicet ille volantem ardeam impulsu praecipitet, iste vero praecipitatam accipiat: De eadem ave zummag Sariph in Aben-bitare, هو طاير معروف يصيد به الملوك للطير Avis nota, qua utuntur reges in aucupiis. Et Giulbaki, الزمج جنس من الطير يصاد به Zummag, genus avis aucupatricis. Dixit et Abuchatim Andalusius, seu Hispanus, انه ذكر العقاب والجمع الزماميج marem esse aquilae, cujus Plurale zemamigio. Et Alkazuinius, زمج طاير يقال له بالفارسية رهل Zummag, avis, Persice RAHAL dicta. Quod alterum est hujus avis in Perside nomen, diversum a priore du-bradran. Et Damir, اهل البزدرة يعدونه من خغاف الجوارح وذلك معروف في عينه وحركته وشدة وثبه والمحمود منه ما كان لونه احمر وهو احد نوعي العقاب ويوصف بالغدر وقلة الالف لكثافة طبعه وقد يقبل التعليم ولكن بعد بطو ومن عادته ان يصيد علي وجه col. 337 الارض Populus Albizdarae (quae gens est Arabica) ponit eum in illarum avium censu, quae ad rapiendum sunt celerrimae. Quod docent oculus et motus ejus, et impetus, quo in praedam ruit. Praefertur in iis, qui maxime rubet. Una est e duabus aquilae speciebus. Dolosus describitur, sed ad discendum tardus, quia natura sua stolidus est. Tamen post moras aliquas tandem fit disciplinae capax. Aves ut plurimum aucupatur circa terrae superficiem. Huic avi cognominis est, sed diversi generis, زمج الما zummag aquae, quam avem suo loco a) catarractem esse docuimus. Quia Zummag aquilae genus, et zummag aquae, seu III, 99 catarrhactes, pari impetu in praedam ruunt. Itaque tam aquilae dicuntur κα-ταῤῥάσσειν, id est, cum impetu in praedam se projicere, quam aves catarrhactae, quibus inde nomen obtigit. Hinc Eustathius in Iliad. τ΄. Ἅρπη δὲ, ἢ ὁ ἰκτῖνος, ἢ ζῶον ἄλλο ἀετῶδες, ὃ εἰς ὕψος ἀρθὲν εὐχερῶς ὅπη θέλει καταῤῥάσσει· Harpe aut est milvus, aut avis alia aquilini generis, quae, in altum sublata, facile, quocunque vult, cum impetu ruit. Et Plinius libri decimi capite tertio, Haliaeetus, librans ex alto sese, visoque in mari pisce, praeceps in eum ruens, id est, καταῤ-ῥάσσων. In medicorum libris multa habentur de remediis ex hac ave. Collyrium e felle sanat caligines. Stercus illitum abstergit pannum et lentigines. Stercus hominis, qui ejus carne vescitur, ad tremores cordis est utile. Haec in Damire, Abenbitare et Alkazuinio reperire est. Graecos recentis aevi hanc Φιλ-άδελφον ex argumento appellasse, observavimus. Arabicum nomen زمج zummag iracundam esse insinuat, cum sit ab ira factum. Nam Arabice زمج zamigia est irasci, زمج zamag ira, مزمج musinig iratus. Unde valde probabili conjectura colligas, Hebraeis אנפה anapha (avis speciem, quam post חסידה hasidam recenset Moses) id ipsum esse, quod Arabicum زمج zummag. Quippe etiam אנפה anapha avis iracunda esse creditur; quia Verbum Hebraeum אנף anaph irasci sonat, et contractum Nomen אף aph iram, vel iracundiam. Itaque in Aben Ezra sic appellari dicitur, שיתאנף מהרה, quia facile irascitur. Et in Talmudico tractatu Cholin. cap. 3. pag. 63. a. Hebraei definiunt דיה רגזנית, vulturem iracundum. Nempe vultures cum aquilis saepe confundi, probatum supra b). At, inquies, additur ratio, cur vocetur anapha, quae cum natura τοῦ zummag parum convenit, למה נקרא שמה אנפה שמתאנפת עם חברותיה, Cur vocatur anapha, nomine, ab ira sumto? Quia in socios irascitur. Contra zummag est Φιλάδελφος, et fratrem, seu comparem, in praedae societatem admittit. Respondeo, fieri posse, ut, qui in aucupio concordes fuerunt et unanimes, iidem in praedae divisione inter se dissideant, et ad iram mutuo concitentur.

Among the species of eagles there is one, unknown to us from other sources, which the Arabs call زمج [zmj] zummag, and barbarous writers zumach and zimiech. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter On العقاب [al-ʿuqāb] Alokab, or the Eagle: وهو نوعان عقاب وزمج [wa-huwa nawʿān ʿuqāb wa-zummaj] Of it there are two species, okab and zummag. Tardivus in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], p. 170: There are two kinds of eagle: the eagle simply so called; and another, called zimiech or zumach. And again: Whiteness on the head or back of an eagle is a sign of the better eagle, which is called ZUMMACH in Arabic, NAPAM in Syriac, Philadelphe in Greek, MILION in Latin. On these words Gesner remarks: Of these, you will find the Greek and Latin name of the eagle in no reliable author. Yet philadelphe is plainly distorted from the Greek φιλάδελφος [philadelphos]. That this bird was called by this name by the later Greeks, although I have not read it elsewhere, seems to me probable for this reason: no more fitting name could have been given to it, nor one that expresses its nature more vividly. For it is characteristic of the eagle kind to love solitude and to be entirely intolerant of all company. Hence, as soon as the eaglets are able to fly, the mothers ἐκ τῆς νεοττίας αὐτοὺς ἐκβάλλουσι, καὶ ἐκ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ περὶ αὐτὴν παντὸς ἀπελαύνουσιν· ἐπέχει γὰρ ἓν ζεῦγος αἰετῶν πολὺν τόπον. διόπερ οὐκ ἐᾷ πλησίον αὑτῶν ἄλλους αὐλισθῆναι [ek tēs neottias autous ekballousi, kai ek tou topou tou peri autēn pantos apelaunousin; epechei gar hen zeugos aietōn polyn topon. dioper ouk ea plēsion hautōn allous aulisthēnai]. So the Philosopher, book 9, chapter 32; and following him Pliny, book 10, chapter 3: The parent also pursues the grown young and drives them far away, as rivals in plunder, of course. And besides, a single pair of eagles needs a large tract to plunder in order to be satisfied. They therefore mark out territories and do not prey close by one another. Aelian, book 2, chapter 39: Κοινωνίαν τε τὴν πρὸς ἄλλον ἥκιστα ἐνδέχεται [Koinōnian te tēn pros allon hēkista endechetai], It admits no fellowship with others. Likewise: Ἀλλήλων ἀπῳκισμένας οἰκοῦσι κα- col. 336 λιάς, ὑπὲρ τοῦ μὴ διαφέρεσθαι ὑπὲρ θήρας [Allēlōn apōkismenas oikousi kalias, hyper tou mē diapheresthai hyper thēras]; They nest very far from one another, so that they need not fight over prey. Yet the زمج [zmj] zummag of that kind is truly φιλάδελφος [philadelphos]: it delights in a companion, and does not usually hunt alone, but with a comrade joined to it. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الزمج طاير فارسية دو برادران لانه اذا اعجز عن صيده اعانه اخوه [al-zummaj ṭāʾir fārisiyya dū barādarān li-annahu idhā aʿjaza ʿan ṣaydihi aʿānahu akhūhu] Zummag, a bird which in Persian is called DU-BRADRAN (that is, two brothers), because, when its prey escapes it, its brother comes to its aid. Alleiths: الجمز طاير دون العقاب حمرته III, 98 غالبة تسميه العجم دبران ومن طبعه انه اذا عجز عن صيده اعانه اخوه علي اخذه [al-jmz ṭāʾir dūn al-ʿuqāb ḥumratuhu ghāliba tusammīhi al-ʿajam dbrān wa-min ṭabʿihi annahu idhā ʿajaza ʿan ṣaydihi aʿānahu akhūhu ʿalā akhdhihi] Giummaz (another copy reads better الزمج [al-zummaj] zummag) is a bird below the eagle (that is, like the eagle but smaller), in which redness predominates. It is called in Agem (that is, in Persia) DABRAN (or rather, as before, دو برادران [dū barādarān] DU-BRADRAN, that is, two brothers): it is of its nature that, when it cannot overtake its prey, its brother helps it to catch it. More recent writers say the same of falcons. Vincent, in the Doctrinale, book 16, chapter 156: In catching a heron two falcons are released together: one of them seeks the heights; the other flies low near the ground; so that the one may bring down the flying heron by its onset, and the other may take it when brought down. Of the same bird zummag, Sariph in Abenbitar [=Ibn al-Bayṭār]: هو طاير معروف يصيد به الملوك للطير [huwa ṭāʾir maʿrūf yaṣīdu bihi al-mulūk lil-ṭayr] A well-known bird which kings use in fowling. And Giulbaki: الزمج جنس من الطير يصاد به [al-zummaj jins min al-ṭayr yuṣād bihi] Zummag, a kind of fowling bird. Abuchatim the Andalusian, or Spaniard, also said: انه ذكر العقاب والجمع الزماميج [annahu dhakar al-ʿuqāb wa-al-jamʿ al-zamāmīj] that it is the male of the eagle, whose plural is zemamigio. And Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: زمج طاير يقال له بالفارسية رهل [zummaj ṭāʾir yuqālu lahu bil-fārisiyya rhl] Zummag, a bird called in Persian RAHAL. This is another name of this bird in Persia, different from the former du-bradran. And Damir: اهل البزدرة يعدونه من خغاف الجوارح وذلك معروف في عينه وحركته وشدة وثبه والمحمود منه ما كان لونه احمر وهو احد نوعي العقاب ويوصف بالغدر وقلة الالف لكثافة طبعه وقد يقبل التعليم ولكن بعد بطو ومن عادته ان يصيد علي وجه col. 337 الارض [ahl al-bzdra yaʿuddūnahu min khghāf al-jawāriḥ wa-dhālika maʿrūf fī ʿaynihi wa-ḥarakatihi wa-shiddat wathbihi wa-al-maḥmūd minhu mā kāna lawnuhu aḥmar wa-huwa aḥad nawʿay al-ʿuqāb wa-yūṣafu bil-ghadr wa-qillat al-ilf li-kathāfat ṭabʿihi wa-qad yaqbalu al-taʿlīm wa-lākin baʿda buṭūʾ wa-min ʿādatihi an yaṣīda ʿalā wajh al-arḍ] The people of Albizdara (which is an Arab tribe) count it among those birds which are swiftest in seizing prey. This is shown by its eye and its movement, and the onset with which it rushes on its prey. Among them the one that is reddest is preferred. It is one of the two species of eagle. It is described as treacherous, but slow to learn, because it is stupid by nature. Yet after some delay it at last becomes capable of training. It mostly hunts birds near the surface of the ground. Of the same name, but of a different kind, is the bird زمج الما [zummaj al-mā] zummag of the water, which we have shown in its place a) to be the catarractes. For the Zummag, a kind of eagle, and the zummag of the water, or III, 99 catarrhactes, rush upon their prey with equal impetus. Thus eagles are said καταῤῥάσσειν [katarrhassein], that is, to hurl themselves with force upon their prey, as well as the catarrhactes birds, which got their name from this. Hence Eustathius on Iliad 19: Ἅρπη δὲ, ἢ ὁ ἰκτῖνος, ἢ ζῶον ἄλλο ἀετῶδες, ὃ εἰς ὕψος ἀρθὲν εὐχερῶς ὅπη θέλει καταῤῥάσσει [Harpē de, ē ho iktinos, ē zōon allo aetōdes, ho eis hypsos arthen eucherōs hopē thelei katarrhassei]; Harpe is either the kite, or another bird of the eagle kind, which, having risen on high, easily swoops with force wherever it wishes. And Pliny, book 10, chapter 3: The haliaeetus, poising itself on high, and on seeing a fish in the sea, rushing headlong upon it, that is, καταῤῥάσσων [katarrhassōn]. In the books of the physicians there is much about remedies from this bird. An eye-salve from its gall cures dimness of sight. Its dung, smeared on, removes blotches and freckles. The dung of a man who eats its flesh is useful for palpitations of the heart. These things are to be found in Damir, Abenbitar and Alkazuinius. We have observed that the Greeks of a later age called it Φιλάδελφον [Philadelphon] from its character. The Arabic name زمج [zmj] zummag suggests that it is irascible, since it is formed from anger. For in Arabic زمج zamigia is to be angry, زمج zamag anger, مزمج musinig angry. From this you may infer by a very probable conjecture that the Hebrew אנפה anapha (the species of bird which Moses lists after חסידה hasidah) is the very same as the Arabic زمج [zmj] zummag. For the אנפה anapha too is believed to be an irascible bird, because the Hebrew verb אנף anaph means to be angry, and the contracted noun אף aph means anger or wrath. Thus in Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] it is said to be so called שיתאנף מהרה, because it is easily angered. And in the Talmudic tractate Cholin, ch. 3, p. 63a, the Hebrews define it as דיה רגזנית, the irascible vulture. For it has been proved above b) that vultures are often confused with eagles. But, you will say, a reason is added why it is called anapha which agrees little with the nature of the zummag: למה נקרא שמה אנפה שמתאנפת עם חברותיה, Why is it called anapha, a name taken from anger? Because it is angry with its companions. The zummag, on the contrary, is Φιλάδελφος [Philadelphos], and admits its brother, or mate, into partnership in the prey. I answer that it may happen that those who were harmonious and of one mind in the hunt fall out with one another in dividing the prey, and are provoked to anger against each other. ¶

Etiam ab אנפה anapha deduci potuit nomen ἀνόπαια, quo Homerus utitur pro aquilae specie, cum de Minerva dicit Odyss. ά. versu 320.

The name ἀνόπαια [anopaia] could also have been derived from אנפה anapha; Homer uses it for a species of eagle when he says of Minerva, Odyss. ά. line 320: ¶

Ὄρνις δ᾽ ὡς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο,

Ὄρνις δ᾽ ὡς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο, [Ornis d' hōs anopaia dieptato,] ¶

Ut avis anopaea avolavit. In quem locum Eustathius, Ἀνόπαια, εἶδός τινες ὀρνέου λέγουσιν ἀετώδους Φήνῃ ἐοικότος· Anopaea, ut quidam tradunt, species est avis, ex aquilarum genere, ossifragae similis. Alii cum hac ave Minervam conferri col. 338 III, 100 volunt, διὰ τὸ ὡς ὄρνις ταχέως ὁρμῆσαι, quia ut avis cito ruit, ut habet vetus Scholiastes. Quod maxime conveniret, si avis ἀνόπαια eadem esset, quae אנפה anapha, seu زمج zummag. Hanc enim Arabes esse ajunt من خفاف الجوارح e rapacium velocissimis. Graecis tamen avis زمج zummag nota fuisse non videtur, cum ejus descriptio neque in Aristotele, neque in aliis uspiam occurrat. Sed Phoenices, qui in Graeciam cum Cadmo trajecerunt, putaverim nomine אנפה anapha, aliam avem appellasse, illi similem, quae Graece dicta sit ἀνόπαια.

Like an anopaea bird she flew away. On this passage Eustathius says: Ἀνόπαια, εἶδός τινες ὀρνέου λέγουσιν ἀετώδους Φήνῃ ἐοικότος· [Anopaia, eidos tines orneou legousin aetōdous Phēnē eoikotos;] Anopaea, as some report, is a species of bird of the eagle kind, similar to the ossifrage. Others hold that Minerva is compared col. 338 III, 100 with this bird διὰ τὸ ὡς ὄρνις ταχέως ὁρμῆσαι [dia to hōs ornis tacheōs hormēsai], because like a bird she rushes swiftly, as the old Scholiast has it. This would fit best if the bird ἀνόπαια [anopaia] were the same as אנפה anapha, or زمج [zummaj] zummag. For the Arabs say this is من خفاف الجوارح [min khifāf al-jawāriḥ] among the swiftest of birds of prey. Yet the bird زمج [zummaj] zummag does not seem to have been known to the Greeks, since its description occurs neither in Aristotle nor anywhere in other authors. But I would think that the Phoenicians who crossed into Greece with Cadmus called by the name אנפה anapha another bird similar to it, which was called in Greek ἀνόπαια [anopaia]. ¶

Est in falconum genere, qui montanus dicitur, barbaris montanarius, de quo Albertus: Corpus ei breve et valde spissum, praecipue vero cauda hujusmodi. Pectus plane rotundum, et magnum; crura fortia, et brevia proportione corporis; pedes nodosi; ungues fortes. Solet autem pedes frequenter respicere. Dorso alisque exterius color cinereus, qui paullatim magis magisque, quo saepius scilicet pennas mutaverit, clarior efficitur et pallidior, modica et fusca varietate interposita. Huic falconi maxime competeret nomen anapha; quia nulla est avis in iram pronior, si Alberto credimus, cujus verba sunt: Est hoc falconum genus ferum, et malis praeditum moribus, iracundum, inconstantis irae. Itaque rarus est falconarius, qui mores ejus satis perspectos habeat. Addit, raro manu tenendum, praeterquam in aurora et aucupii tempore, et cum pascendus est. Sic enim mitescere ad manum falconarii tanquam beneficam, et iracundiam deponere. Item, Irascenti falconarius ne multum resistat, sic enim citius remittitur ejus ira. Et rursus, Si praeda evaserit, prae caeteris indignatur, ita, ut per iracundiam aliquando revocantem se invadat, et caput, aut faciem falconarii, vel, cui is insidet, equi dilaniet. Addit, Est quando falco unus alterum invadit: qua in re falconarius patiens requiritur, qui non adversetur, sed patienter dissimulet revocationem, donec, ira deposita, mitescat animus falconis. There is a kind of falcon which is called montanus [mountain falcon], by the barbarians montanarius, about which Albertus says: Its body is short and very thick, and the tail especially so. The breast is quite round and large; the legs are strong and short in proportion to the body; the feet knotty; the talons strong. It is accustomed to look at its feet frequently. On the back and the outside of the wings the colour is ash-grey, which gradually becomes ever clearer and paler the more often it has moulted, with a moderate dusky variegation interspersed. Thus a falconer who has sufficiently understood its habits is rare. He adds that it should seldom be held on the hand, except at dawn, at the time of hunting, and when it is to be fed. For thus it grows gentle toward the falconer's hand as toward a benefactor, and lays aside its anger. Likewise: When it is angry, let the falconer not resist it much, for thus its anger subsides more quickly. He adds: There are times when one falcon attacks another; in this case a patient falconer is required, who does not oppose it, but patiently refrains from calling it back until, its anger laid aside, the falcon's spirit grows gentle. But, to return to the parrot: like the peacock, discussed above e), I could hardly believe that the parrot was known to the Jews in the age of Moses. For it is an Indian bird. Writers testify to this with great agreement. Thus Ctesias writes in the Indica, as quoted by Photius, περὶ τοῦ ὀρνέου τοῦ βιττάκου, ὅτι γλῶσσαν ἀνθρωπίνην ἔχει, καὶ φωνὴν [peri tou orneou tou bittakou, hoti glōssan anthrōpinēn echei, kai phōnēn], concerning the bird bittacus, III, 106 (as they then called it) that it has a human tongue and voice. Yet, to say nothing of the New World, modern voyages teach us that they are also brought from Africa, though from its interior. Nor had this escaped Pliny, who in book 6, chapter 29, in the itinerary from Syene to the island of Meroë published by Nero's scouts, describing the island of Gagaudes, says: there parrots were first seen. This is confirmed by Paulus Venetus [=Marco Polo], writing about Abasia in book 3, chapter 45: It has parrots and very beautiful epimachi. But I cannot at all make sense of these words of Diodorus, book 2, page 95: Αἱ δὲ τῆς Συρίας ἐσχατιαὶ ψιττακοὺς, καὶ πορφυρίωνας, καὶ μελεαγρίδας (ἐκτρέφουσι) [Hai de tēs Syrias eschatiai psittakous, kai porphyriōnas, kai meleagridas (ektrephousi)]. For it is a paradoxical claim, and indeed plainly false, that parrots are born in Syria. I therefore suspect that the passage is corrupt, although what should be restored does not occur to me. ¶
