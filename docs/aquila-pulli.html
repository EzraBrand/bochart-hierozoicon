De Aquilae affectu erga pullos suos ex Deut. XXXII, 11.

On the eagle's love for its young, from Deut 32:11. ¶

Nunc dicendum de aquilae affectu ergo pullos suos, quem esse tenerrimum, haec verba Mosis indicant Deuter. XXXII, 11. Ut aquila excitat nidum suum, et super pullos suos volitat, extensis alis, et accipiens eos gestat super pennas suas. Nidum suum, id est, pullitiem suam; Continens pro contento. Quod loquendi genus Romanis frequens. Virgilius libro 4. Georgicorum de merope et hirundine,

Now we must speak of the eagle's love for its young. That it is most tender is shown by these words of Moses, Deuter. XXXII, 11: As the eagle stirs up its nest, and hovers over its young, with wings spread out, and taking them carries them upon its feathers. Its nest, that is, its brood: the container for the contained. This manner of speaking is frequent among the Romans. Virgil, in book 4 of the Georgics, says of the bee-eater and the swallow, ¶

Ore ferunt dulcem nidis immitibus escam:

They carry in their beaks sweet food to the pitiless nests: ¶

id est, crudelibus pullis, ut ibi Servius. Hinc Nonius, Nidos non solum domicilia avium, sed etiam foetus earum, Virgilio auctore, dicere possumus. Et Colu- col. 177 mella in Horto id sequutus.

that is, to the cruel chicks, as Servius explains there. Hence Nonius: On the authority of Virgil we may call nidi not only the dwellings of birds but also their offspring. And Columella col. 177 followed this in his Garden: ¶

Veris et adventum nidis cantarit hirundo.

And the swallow will have sung the coming of spring to its nests. ¶

Ut Solinus de ibi, Ea serpentum pabulatur ova, gratissimamque ex his escam nidis suis defert. Et de eadem Ammianus libro vigesimo secundo, Nidulis suis ad cibum suggerens ova serpentum. Unde Glossae, Νεοσσὸς, Nidus, pullus. Et Iuvenalis Satyra quinta de liberis quoque usurpat, II, 760 Sed tua nunc Mygale pariat licet, et pueros tres In gremium patris fundat semel, ipse loquaci Gaudebit nido. — — — —

So too Solinus on the ibis: It feeds on the eggs of serpents, and brings from them a most welcome food to its nests. And Ammianus, book 22, of the same bird: supplying the eggs of serpents as food to its little nests. Hence the Glosses: Νεοσσὸς [neossos], nidus, chick. And Juvenal, Satire 5, uses it also of children: II, 760 But now, though your Mygale should give birth, and pour three boys at once into their father's lap, he himself will delight in the chattering nest. — — — — ¶

Excitandi verbum ita explicat R. Selomo: הזה רחמני על בניו ואינו נכנס לקנו פתאם עד שהוא מקשקש ומטרף על בניו בכנפיו בין אילן לאילן בין שוכה לחברתה כדי שיעורו בניו ויהא בהן כח לקבלו, Aquila, cum sit misericors erga pullos suos, non ingreditur in nidum tumultuarie: sed prius super eos obstrepit et plaudit, alis ad duas arbores, aut ad duos ramis allisis, ut pulli, sic excitati, satis habeant virium (id est, sint habiliores) ad eam excipiendam. Quo pertinet, quod sequitur, על גוזליו ירחף אינו מכביד עצמו עליהם אלא מחופף נוגע ואינו נוגע, Super pullos suos incubat (ita enim accipit Hebraicum ירחף), id est, in eos non gravitat, sed ita se sublevat, ut tangat, quasi non tangeret. Haec ille. Sed Hieronymi versio, quamvis liberior, tamen Prophetae mentem multo fidelius exhibet: Sicut aquila, provocans ad volandum pullos suos, et super eos volitans, etc. Omnino enim excitare nidum, vel pullitiem suam aquila dicitur, cum eam ad volatum provocat. Et Hebraeum רחף est movere se, ut Ierem. XXIII, 9. רחפו, Se commoverunt omnia ossa mea. Ita in creatione Spiritus Dei מרחפת, motita- bat se super faciem aquarum. Inde translatum ad aves רחף, est motitare alas, ut volitantes faciunt. Ex quo deduxere Talmudici ejusdem notionis verbum רפרף, elisa Gutturali, et reliquis geminatis. Unde in tractatu Cholin fol. 38. b. והעוף אפילו לא רפרף אלא גפו ולא כשכש אלא זנבו הרי זה פירכוס, Et avis, si solum alam motitet, aut si solum caudam agitet, haec est trepidatio. Itaque in Beresith Rabba Spiritus מרחפת Gen. I, 3. legitur esse כעוף הזה שהוא מרפרף בכנפיו, ut avis illa, quae alas suas motitat. Arabes quoque idem verbum ita usurpant. Alcamus reddit بسط جناحها Extendit alas suas. Et verbale nomen الرفرفة eidem est الصوت وتحريك الظليم جناحيها حول الشي الذي يريد ان يقع عليه Strepitus et motus alarum struthionis circa rem, in quam vult incidere. Quod quia struthioni familiare est, inde ipsa avis vocatur رفراف riphraph. Ut et alia avis, a struthione diversissima, cujus alterum nomen est خاطف ظله id est, σκιάρπαξ, umbrae suae raptrix: quia, cum imaginem suam in aqua videt, alas motitans in eam involat tanquam direptura. Sed et verbum رف in quarta Coujugatione, id est, ارف arappha, avibus tribuitur, quae alas extendunt ad incubandum. Idem Alcamus, ارفت الدجاجة علي col. 178 بياضها بسطت الجناح Arapphath gallina super ova sua, id est, alas extendit, ut iis scilicet incubaret. Tamen verum est, quod diximus, II, 761 hoc verbo proprie significari alarum extensionem, quae fit cum motitatione. Quod aquilae maxime convenit, aut alii avi cuilibet, quae pullos suos, adhuc teneros, docet primos πτερυγισμοὺς et ὀρταλισμούς. Itaque Propheta post ירחף addit ἐπεξηγητικῶς יפרש כנפיו, alas suas extendit; quia non motitat alas aquila, nisi extensas. Quid quod aquilae tam naturalis est motus alarum, ut pulli etiam inter edendum perpetuo alas agitent. Ita tradunt Arabes in Damire, ومن عادة هذا الطاير ان يزق كل فرخ ضايع Etiam e natura hujus avis est, quod pullus omnis alas agitet, dum cibatur: nempe a matre, rostro in rostrum inserto. Id enim proprie زق.

R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] explains the verb to stir up thus: הזה רחמני על בניו ואינו נכנס לקנו פתאם עד שהוא מקשקש ומטרף על בניו בכנפיו בין אילן לאילן בין שוכה לחברתה כדי שיעורו בניו ויהא בהן כח לקבלו, The eagle, being compassionate towards its young, does not enter its nest abruptly; rather it first makes a noise over them and claps, striking its wings against two trees or against two branches, so that the chicks, thus roused, may have enough strength (that is, may be better prepared) to receive it. To this belongs what follows: על גוזליו ירחף אינו מכביד עצמו עליהם אלא מחופף נוגע ואינו נוגע, It broods over its young (for so he takes the Hebrew ירחף), that is, it does not press its weight on them, but holds itself up so that it touches them as if not touching. Thus he. But Jerome's version, though freer, nevertheless renders the Prophet's meaning much more faithfully: As the eagle, provoking its young to fly, and hovering over them, etc. For the eagle is certainly said to stir up its nest, or its brood, when it provokes it to fly. And the Hebrew רחף means to move oneself, as in Jer 23:9. רחפו, All my bones have trembled. So at the creation the Spirit of God מרחפת, was moving to and fro over the face of the waters. From there, transferred to birds, רחף means to move the wings to and fro, as birds do when hovering. From this the Talmudists derived a verb of the same meaning, רפרף, with the guttural elided and the other letters doubled. Hence in the tractate Cholin, fol. 38b: והעוף אפילו לא רפרף אלא גפו ולא כשכש אלא זנבו הרי זה פירכוס, And a bird, if it only moves its wing, or only wags its tail, this is a convulsive movement. Accordingly in Beresith Rabba the Spirit מרחפת of Gen 1:3 is said to be כעוף הזה שהוא מרפרף בכנפיו, like that bird which moves its wings to and fro. The Arabs too use the same verb in this way. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] renders it بسط جناحها [basaṭa janāḥahā] It spread out its wings. And the verbal noun الرفرفة [al-rafrafa] is for him الصوت وتحريك الظليم جناحيها حول الشي الذي يريد ان يقع عليه [al-ṣawt wa-taḥrīk al-ẓalīm janāḥayhā ḥawla al-shay al-ladhī yurīdu an yaqaʿa ʿalayhi] The noise and movement of the wings of the ostrich around the thing on which it wishes to alight. Since this is habitual to the ostrich, the bird itself is called رفراف [rafrāf] riphraph. So too another bird, very different from the ostrich, whose other name is خاطف ظله [khāṭif ẓillihi], that is, σκιάρπαξ [skiarpax], snatcher of its own shadow: because, when it sees its image in the water, it flies at it, moving its wings, as if to seize it. Moreover the verb رف [raffa] in the fourth conjugation, that is, ارف [arafa] arappha, is attributed to birds that spread their wings to brood. The same Alcamus: ارفت الدجاجة علي col. 178 بياضها بسطت الجناح [arafat al-dajāja ʿalā bayāḍihā basaṭat al-janāḥ] Arapphath the hen over her eggs, that is, she spread out her wings, namely in order to brood on them. Yet what we have said is true, II, 761 that this verb properly signifies a spreading of the wings accompanied by moving them to and fro. This is especially fitting for the eagle, or for any other bird that teaches its still tender chicks their first πτερυγισμοὺς [pterygismous] and ὀρταλισμούς [ortalismous]. Accordingly the Prophet, after ירחף, adds by way of explanation (ἐπεξηγητικῶς [epexēgētikōs]) יפרש כנפיו, it spreads its wings; because the eagle does not move its wings to and fro unless they are spread. Indeed, the movement of the wings is so natural to the eagle that even the chicks constantly flap their wings while eating. So the Arabs report in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: ومن عادة هذا الطاير ان يزق كل فرخ ضايع [wa-min ʿādat hādhā al-ṭāyir an yuzaqqa kull farkh ḍāyiʿ] It is also of the nature of this bird that every chick flaps its wings while it is being fed: namely by its mother, beak inserted into beak. For that is properly زق [zaqq]. ¶

Porro aquilae pulli Prov. XXX, 17. vocantur בני נשר, filii aquilae, et Iob. XXXIX, 33. (al. 30.) אפרוחיו, pulli ipsius, voce generali ad omnes avium pullos. At hic גוזליו, καταχρηστικῶς. Nam גוזל gozel est columbae pullus, aut avis congeneris, ut suo loco a) docuimus. Ita Lampridio pipiones sunt pulli columbarum. Tamen aliis pipiunculus est accipiter. Isidori Glossae, Pipiunculus, accipiter, acceptor. Aliud hic observandum, nempe tam Iobum, quam Mosem, in loco, in quo versamur, uni aquilae plures pullos tribuere, cum aquilae plerumque non alant nisi singulos. Quod alibi per sex, alibi per octo continuos annos in Germania ab aucupibus in nidis aquilinis observatum refert Albertus: causa etiam hac allata, quod, quamvis duo, aut tria mater excludat, tamen pluribus pullis educandis non sufficit, quia multo alimento egent ob calorem temperamenti. Nec multo aliter veteres. Quo pertinet Musaei vetus de aquila carmen apud Philosophum Hist. lib. 6. cap. 6.

Further, the eagle's chicks are called in Prov 30:17. בני נשר, sons of the eagle, and in Job 39:33 (var. 30.) אפרוחיו, its chicks, a general word applied to the chicks of all birds. But here they are called גוזליו, καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs]. For גוזל gozel is the chick of a dove, or of a related bird, as we have shown in its place a). Likewise in Lampridius pipiones [squabs] are the chicks of doves. Yet for others pipiunculus is a hawk. The Glosses of Isidore: Pipiunculus, accipiter, acceptor [pipiunculus, hawk]. Something else is to be noted here, namely that both Job and Moses, in the passage we are dealing with, attribute several chicks to a single eagle, although eagles for the most part rear only one each. Albertus reports that this was observed by fowlers in eagles' nests in Germany, in one place for six, in another for eight successive years. He also gives this reason: although the mother hatches two or three, she is nevertheless not equal to rearing more chicks, because they need much food on account of the heat of their temperament. The ancients say much the same. To this belongs the old verse of Musaeus about the eagle in the Philosopher, Hist. book 6, ch. 6. ¶

Ὃς τρία μὲν τίκτει, δύο δ᾽ ἐκλέπει, ἓν δ᾽ ἀλεγίζει,

Ὃς τρία μὲν τίκτει, δύο δ᾽ ἐκλέπει, ἓν δ᾽ ἀλεγίζει, [Hos tria men tiktei, dyo d᾽ eklepei, hen d᾽ alegizei,] ¶

Quae tria quidem parit, duo excludit, et unum curat: aut ἓν δ᾽ ἀλυβάζει, unum alit; ut Plutarchus in Mario citat. Et fatetur Philosophus, plerumque ita accidere. Tamen, ut idem addit, ἤδη καὶ τρεῖς νεοττοὶ ὠμμένοι εἰσὶν, quandoque etiam tres aquilae pulli visi sunt. Plinius quoque, Pariunt ova terna, excludunt pullos binos, visi sunt et tres aliquando. Quin apud Plutarchum narrat Marius, se olim valde juvenem ὑποδέξασθαι τῷ ἱματίῳ καταφερομένην ἀετοῦ νεοττίαν ἑπτὰ νεοττοὺς ἔχουσαν, delapsum aquilae nidum cum septem pullis toga excepisse. Quale est, quod de Clodio Albino refert Iulius Capitolinus, Septem aquilae parvae de nido allatae sunt, et quasi ad jocum circa cunas pueri constitutae. Arabes scribunt, melanaeetum e tribus, quos excludit, plerumque duos alere. Ita Damir ex Ibnozohare, العقاب تبيض ثلاث بيضات في II, 762 الغالب وتحضنها ثلاثين يوما وما عداها من الجوارح تبيض بيضتين وتحضن عشرين يوما col. 179 فاذا خرجت فراخ العقاب القت واحدا منها لانه يثقل عليها طعم الثلاث Alokab (id est, melanaeetus) ponit plerumque ova tria, quae fovet triginta diebus. Cum aliae rapaces aves solum ova duo ponant, quae per viginti dies fovent. Et cum exclusi sunt pulli alokab, ex iis unum ejicit, quia grave ipsi est, tres alere. Itaque duo supersunt, quos alendos sibi proponit.

Which lays three, hatches two, and cares for one: or ἓν δ᾽ ἀλυβάζει [hen d᾽ alybazei], rears one, as Plutarch quotes it in the Marius. And the Philosopher admits that this is mostly what happens. Yet, as he also adds, ἤδη καὶ τρεῖς νεοττοὶ ὠμμένοι εἰσὶν [ēdē kai treis neottoi ōmmenoi eisin], sometimes even three eagle chicks have been seen. Pliny also: They lay three eggs and hatch two chicks; three have sometimes been seen as well. Indeed, in Plutarch, Marius relates that once, when very young, he ὑποδέξασθαι τῷ ἱματίῳ καταφερομένην ἀετοῦ νεοττίαν ἑπτὰ νεοττοὺς ἔχουσαν [hypodexasthai tō himatiō katapheromenēn aetou neottian hepta neottous echousan], caught in his toga an eagle's nest that had fallen down, with seven chicks. Of the same kind is what Julius Capitolinus reports of Clodius Albinus: Seven little eagles were brought from a nest and set, as if in play, around the boy's cradle. The Arabs write that the melanaeetus, of the three it hatches, mostly rears two. So Damir from Ibnozohar: العقاب تبيض ثلاث بيضات في [al-ʿuqāb tabīḍu thalāth bayḍāt fī] II, 762 الغالب وتحضنها ثلاثين يوما وما عداها من الجوارح تبيض بيضتين وتحضن عشرين يوما [al-ghālib wa-taḥḍunuhā thalāthīn yawman wa-mā ʿadāhā min al-jawāriḥ tabīḍu bayḍatayn wa-taḥḍunu ʿishrīn yawman] col. 179 فاذا خرجت فراخ العقاب القت واحدا منها لانه يثقل عليها طعم الثلاث [fa-idhā kharajat firākh al-ʿuqāb alqat wāḥidan minhā li-annahu yathqulu ʿalayhā ṭuʿm al-thalāth] Alokab (that is, the melanaeetus) mostly lays three eggs, which it incubates for thirty days, whereas other birds of prey lay only two eggs, which they incubate for twenty days. And when the chicks of alokab are hatched, it throws out one of them, because it is burdensome for it to feed three. So two remain, which it undertakes to rear. ¶

Sequitur apud Prophetam, Accipiens eos gestat super pennas. Ad verbum, accipit eum, gestat eum. Sed eum, id est, quemlibet ex illis. Vel eum, id est, illos, per numeri enallagen. Vel refertur ad remotius, id est, ad nidum, qua voce pullitiem intelligi dictum. Super pennas suas, aut super alas suas. Hebraice, על אברתו. Graeci, ἐπὶ τῶν μεταφρένων αὐτοῦ. Unde Hieronymus, in humeris suis. Neque enim solum rostro, aut unguibus, sed et humeris et alis gestare putatur aquila. Ita apud Plutarchum, De civili institutione, Αἰσώ-που βασίλισκος ἐπὶ τῶν ὤμων τοῦ ἀετοῦ κομισθεὶς, Aesopi regulus, aquilae humeris vectus, etc. In Aeliano Hist. lib. 12. cap. 21. cum Tilgamus, puer Babylonius, ex arcis fastigio praeceps delaberetur, ὁ αἰετὸς, πρὶν ἢ τῇ γῇ προσαῤῥαχθῆναι τὸ βρέφος, ὑπῆλθεν αὐτὸ, καὶ τὰ νῶτα ὑπέβαλεν (vel κατὰ νῶτα ὑπέλα-βεν, ut est in Apostolio Proverbio 1793.), priusquam terrae illideretur, aquila subiens eum dorso excepit. Aristomenes quoque in Pausaniae Messeniacis, cum a Lacedaemoniis in Caeadam conjectus fuisset, ὄρνιθα τὸν ἀετὸν ὑποπέτεσθαί (φα-σι) καὶ ἀνέχειν ταῖς πτέρυξιν, ἐς ὃ κατήνεγκεν αὐτὸν ἐς τὸ πέρας, οὔτε πηρω-θέντα οὐδὲν τοῦ σώματος, οὔτε τραῦμα ἐπιλαβόντα, aquilam subvolasse dicunt, et alis eum sustinuisse, donec neque mutilatum, neque ulla ex parte laesum in fun- dum detulisset. Quae non profero tanquam vera, sed ut fabulas, ab iis confictas, quibus persuasum id fuit, ab aquilae natura non esse alienum, ut quid humeris atque alis in ipso volatu ferat. Quamvis pullos ab aquilis continua vectione gestari haudquaquam putem: tantum volaturientes, et in ipso conatu deficientes, parentum alis excipi et sustineri, verisimile est, donec, resumtis animis, sese rursus audeant aëri committere. Aliud affert R. Selomo subtilius, quam solidius. כשבא ליטלן ממקום למקום אינו נוטלן ברגליו כשאר עופות לפי ששאר עופות יראים מן הנשר שהוא מגביה לעוף ופורח עליהם לפיכך נושאין ברגליו מפני הנשר אבל הנשר אינו ירא אלא מן החץ לפיכך נושאן על כנפיו אומר מוטב שיכנס החץ בי ולא יכנס בבני, Cum aquila pullos suos e loco ad locum transfert, non transfert eos pedibus suis, quomodo caeterae volucres, quia aqui- lam timent, quae volatu sursum se effert, et iis volat altius; ac proinde pedibus II, 763 pullos ferunt, ne aquilae sint obvii. Sed aquila nihil timet praeter sagittam; unde est, quod pullos fert super alas, tanquam apud se dicens, Malo, ut me feriat sagitta, quam pullos meos. Sed hoc commentum mihi non placet; quia neque aquila dorso, neque aliae aves pedibus pullos suos solent e loco in locum certo consilio transferre. Et, si facerent, solo naturae ductu id facerent, non ob rationes allatas, de quibus aves cogitare, nemo mihi persuaserit. Apud Isidorum Orig. lib. 12. cap. 7. Tuci (quomodo Hispanica voce veteri cuculos vocat) veniendi tempus habent, milvorum scapulis suscepti, propter breves et parvos volatus, ne per longa aëris spatia fatigati deficiant. Quod si ab idoneo scriptore afferretur, non parum ea illustraret, quae de pullorum aquilae gestatione dicta sunt. col. 180 Sed praeter Isidorum, historiae naturalis parum peritum scriptorem, nullus vetustiorum occurrit, qui idem asserat. Tamen, si Aeliano credimus Hist. lib. 7. cap. 17. Cerylos maritos suos, prae senio fatiscentes, αἱ ἀλκυόνες περιάγουσιν ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν τῶν καλουμένων μετοπτερυγίων, halcyones circumferunt super ipsa quae vocantur interscapilia. Hinc Plutarchus de halcyone in libro Utra anim. Ὅταν δὲ διὰ γῆρας ἀσθενὴς ὁ ἄῤῥην γένηται, συνέπεσθαι καὶ βαρὺς, ὑπολαβοῦσα γη-ροφορεῖ, καὶ γηροτροφεῖ· Marem, jam annis gravem et aegre sequentem, suscipiens fert et alit. Et multo ante idem docuerat Antigonus Hist. 27. Τῶν δὲ ἀλκυόνων οἱ ἄρσενες κήρυλοι καλοῦνται. ὅτ᾽ ἂν οὖν ὑπὸ τοῦ γήρως ἀσθενήσωσι, καὶ μηκέτι δύνωνται πέτεσθαι, φέρουσιν αὐτοὺς αἱ θήλειαι, ἐπὶ τῶν πτερῶν λα-βοῦσαι· Mares halcyonum ceryli vocantur. Hi, cum prae senio infirmi sunt, neque amplius volare possunt, eos, alis susceptos, ferunt foeminae. Unde vulgatum illud Alcmanis; quod ibidem citatur, Οὔ μ᾽ ἔτι παρθενικαὶ, etc. Sed et de ciconiis idem traditur. Or it refers to the more remote noun, that is, to nest, a word which we have said means the brood. Upon its feathers, or upon its wings. In Hebrew, על אברתו. The Greeks: ἐπὶ τῶν μεταφρένων αὐτοῦ [epi tōn metaphrenōn autou]. Hence Jerome: on its shoulders. For the eagle is thought to carry not only with its beak or talons, but also with its shoulders and wings. Thus in Plutarch, On Civil Instruction: Αἰσώπου βασίλισκος ἐπὶ τῶν ὤμων τοῦ ἀετοῦ κομισθεὶς [Aisōpou basiliskos epi tōn ōmōn tou aetou komistheis], Aesop's wren, carried on the shoulders of the eagle, etc. In Aelian, Hist. book 12, ch. 21, when Tilgamus, a Babylonian boy, was falling headlong from the top of the citadel, ὁ αἰετὸς, πρὶν ἢ τῇ γῇ προσαῤῥαχθῆναι τὸ βρέφος, ὑπῆλθεν αὐτὸ, καὶ τὰ νῶτα ὑπέβαλεν [ho aietos, prin ē tē gē prosarrhachthēnai to brephos, hypēlthen auto, kai ta nōta hypebalen] (or κατὰ νῶτα ὑπέλαβεν [kata nōta hypelaben], as it reads in Apostolius, Proverb 1793), before he was dashed against the ground, the eagle came beneath him and caught him on its back. Aristomenes too, in Pausanias' Messeniaca, when he had been thrown by the Lacedaemonians into the Caeadas: ὄρνιθα τὸν ἀετὸν ὑποπέτεσθαί (φασι) καὶ ἀνέχειν ταῖς πτέρυξιν, ἐς ὃ κατήνεγκεν αὐτὸν ἐς τὸ πέρας, οὔτε πηρωθέντα οὐδὲν τοῦ σώματος, οὔτε τραῦμα ἐπιλαβόντα [ornitha ton aeton hypopetesthai (phasi) kai anechein tais pteryxin, es ho katēnenken auton es to peras, oute pērōthenta ouden tou sōmatos, oute trauma epilabonta], they say that an eagle flew beneath him and held him up with its wings until it had brought him down to the bottom neither maimed nor injured in any part. I do not offer these as true, but as fables invented by people who were convinced that it is not foreign to the eagle's nature to carry something on its shoulders and wings in flight itself. Yet I by no means think that the chicks are carried by eagles in continuous transport. It is only likely that those eager to fly, and failing in the very attempt, are caught and held up by their parents' wings until, having regained courage, they dare to commit themselves to the air again. R. Selomo offers something else, more subtle than solid: כשבא ליטלן ממקום למקום אינו נוטלן ברגליו כשאר עופות לפי ששאר עופות יראים מן הנשר שהוא מגביה לעוף ופורח עליהם לפיכך נושאין ברגליו מפני הנשר אבל הנשר אינו ירא אלא מן החץ לפיכך נושאן על כנפיו אומר מוטב שיכנס החץ בי ולא יכנס בבני, When the eagle moves its chicks from place to place, it does not carry them in its feet as other birds do, because these fear the eagle, which soars aloft in flight and flies higher than they; and therefore they carry II, 763 their chicks in their feet, so as not to expose them to the eagle. But the eagle fears nothing except the arrow; that is why it carries its chicks on its wings, as if saying to itself: Better that the arrow strike me than my chicks. But this invention does not please me, because neither does the eagle on its back, nor do other birds in their feet, usually move their chicks from place to place by deliberate design. And if they did, they would do it by the sole guidance of nature, not for the reasons alleged, about which no one will persuade me that birds reflect. In Isidore, Orig. book 12, ch. 7: The tuci (as he calls cuckoos with an old Spanish word) have a set time of coming, carried on the shoulders of kites because of their short and small flights, lest, exhausted over long stretches of air, they should fail. If this were reported by a reliable writer, it would shed no little light on what has been said about the eagle carrying its chicks. col. 180 But apart from Isidore, a writer hardly skilled in natural history, none of the older authors is found asserting the same. Yet, if we believe Aelian, Hist. book 7, ch. 17, their mates the ceryli, worn out by old age, αἱ ἀλκυόνες περιάγουσιν ἐπ᾽ αὐτῶν τῶν καλουμένων μετοπτερυγίων [hai alkyones periagousin ep᾽ autōn tōn kaloumenōn metopterygiōn], the halcyons carry about upon what are called the interscapulars. Hence Plutarch on the halcyon in the book Whether Land or Sea Animals: Ὅταν δὲ διὰ γῆρας ἀσθενὴς ὁ ἄῤῥην γένηται, συνέπεσθαι καὶ βαρὺς, ὑπολαβοῦσα γηροφορεῖ, καὶ γηροτροφεῖ [Hotan de dia gēras asthenēs ho arrhēn genētai, synepesthai kai barys, hypolabousa gērophorei, kai gērotrophei]; taking up the male, now heavy with years and following with difficulty, she carries and feeds him. When they are weak from old age and can no longer fly, the females take them up on their wings and carry them. Hence that well-known saying of Alcman, quoted in the same place: Οὔ μ᾽ ἔτι παρθενικαὶ [Ou m᾽ eti parthenikai], etc. The same is also told of storks. The Scholiast on Aristophanes, on the Birds: Οἱ γὰρ πελαργοὶ, δικαιοπραγεῖς ὄντες, ἐπὶ πτερῶν βαδίζουσι τοὺς πατέρας γεγηρακότας βαστάζοντες [Hoi gar pelargoi, dikaioprageis ontes, epi pterōn badizousi tous pateras gegērakotas bastazontes]; Storks, since they observe justice, migrate carrying on their wings their fathers, worn out with age. So also Suidas under Ἀντιπελαργεῖν [Antipelargein]. And Basil, Hexaemeron, Homily 8: Τὴν δυνατὴν καὶ ἐν τῇ πτήσει παρέχονται βοήθειαν, ἠρέμα τῷ πτερῷ κουφίζοντες ἑκατέρωθεν [Tēn dynatēn kai en tē ptēsei parechontai boētheian, ērema tō pterō kouphizontes hekaterōthen]. And Eustathius of Antioch: Εἰ δὲ τῇ πτήσει ὑποκάτωθεν αὐτὸν ὑποβαστάζουσιν, καὶ τοῖς ἰδίοις πτεροῖς ἠρέμα αὐτὸν ὑποκουφίζουσιν ἑκατέρωθεν [Ei de tē ptēsei hypokatōthen auton hypobastazousin, kai tois idiois pterois ērema auton hypokouphizousin hekaterōthen]. From the same source Ambrose, Hexaemeron book 5, ch. 16: so that, lifting up the old one from this side and that with the prop of their wings, they exercise him in flying. Tacitus also tells the fable that the phoenix takes up its father's body. That is why Artemidorus declares that whoever sees a phoenix in his dreams will be reduced to such poverty that he will be forced, after the manner of the phoenix, to carry out his parent's corpse on his own shoulders. See the distinguished Vossius on Mela, p. 297. ¶

Ut post tot spinas avulsas, lectorem, his tricis atque apinis diu detentum, aliquo modo recreem, libet Mosis locum, quem explicavimus, nobilissimi poetae II, 764 verbis efferre, qui superiore saeculo scripsit in Canticum Mosis eximam paraphrasim,

Ac velut alituum princeps, fulvusque Tonantis Armiger, implumes et adhuc sine robore natos Sollicita refovet cura, pinguisque ferinae Indulget pastus: mox ut cum viribus alae Vesticipes crevere, vocat si blandior aura, Expansa invitat pluma, dorsoque morantes Excipit, attollitque humeris, plausuque secundò Fertur in arva, timens oneri, et tamen impete presso Remigium lentans alarum, incurvaque pinnis Vela legens, humiles tranat sub nubibus oras. Hinc sensim supera alta petit: jamjamque sub astra Erigitur, cursusque leves citus urget in auras, Omnia pervolitans late loca, et agmine foetus Fertque refertque suos vario, moremque volandi Addocet. Illi autem, longa assuetudine docti, Paulatim incipiunt pennis se credere coelo Impavidi. Tantum a teneris valet addere curam.

And as the prince of birds, the tawny armour-bearer of the Thunderer, cherishes with anxious care his featherless and still feeble young, and indulges them with feasts of rich game; then, when the clothed-headed chicks have grown in strength of wing, if a gentler breeze calls, he invites them with spread plumage, takes the lingering ones upon his back and raises them on his shoulders, and with favourable beat is borne over the fields, fearing for his burden, and yet with checked impetus slowing the oarage of his wings, and gathering the curved sails of his pinions, he swims through the low regions beneath the clouds. Thence he gradually seeks the heights above: and now beneath the stars he rises, and swiftly presses his light course into the air, flying far and wide over all places, and in varied formation carries his offspring to and fro, and teaches them the manner of flying. And they, taught by long habit, little by little begin to entrust themselves on their wings to the sky, fearless. So much does care from tender years avail. ¶

Dum aquilarum φιλοστοργίαν Moses tantopere commendat, fortasse videbitur parum illi convenire cum naturae scriptoribus, qui statuunt plane secus. Aquilam enim describunt ut avem prorsus ἄστοργον et immisericordem. Aristoteles libri noni capite trigesimo quarto, Ἐκβάλλει (τὰ τέκνα) ὁ ἀετὸς πρὸ ὥρας, ἔτι βίου δεόμενα, καὶ οὔπω δυνάμενα πέτεσθαι· ἐκβάλλειν δὲ δοκεῖ τοὺς νεοττοὺς ὁ ἀετὸς διὰ φθόνον· φύσει γάρ ἐστι φθονερὸς, καὶ ὀξύπεινος. Ita etiam Eustathius Hexaëm. p. 26. Sed his quod regeram non deest. In Aeliano Hist. lib. 2. cap. 40. caput integrum legas Περὶ τῆς τῶν αἰετῶν στοργῆς, De aquilarum amore erga pullos, in quo inter caetera haec ha-bentur: Ζηλοτυπώτατον δέ ἐστι ζῶον ἀετὸς περὶ τὰ νεόττια. ἐὰν γοῦν θεάση-ταί τινα προσιόντα, ἀπελθεῖν ἀτιμώρητον οὐκ ἐπιτρέπει· παίει γὰρ τοῖς πτεροῖς II, 765 αὐτὸν, καὶ τοῖς ὄνυξι λυμαίνεται. Ex omnibus animalibus maximo studio in foetus suos existit. Quare acriter eum persequitur, quem in nidum invasisse conspexerit, nec inultum dimittit: alis enim eum verberat et dilacerat unguibus. Quae fere eadem tradit Apostolius Cent. 2. Prov. 6. Oppianus quoque De Vena- tione libri tertii versu 115. in censu avium, quae parvulos amore perpetuo fovent, aquilas his verbis ponit:

While Moses so highly commends the φιλοστοργίαν [philostorgian] of eagles, he will perhaps seem to agree little with the writers on nature, who hold quite the opposite. For they describe the eagle as a bird wholly ἄστοργον [astorgon] and pitiless. Aristotle, book 9, chapter 34: Ἐκβάλλει (τὰ τέκνα) ὁ ἀετὸς πρὸ ὥρας, ἔτι βίου δεόμενα, καὶ οὔπω δυνάμενα πέτεσθαι· ἐκβάλλειν δὲ δοκεῖ τοὺς νεοττοὺς ὁ ἀετὸς διὰ φθόνον· φύσει γάρ ἐστι φθονερὸς, καὶ ὀξύπεινος [Ekballei (ta tekna) ho aetos pro hōras, eti biou deomena, kai oupō dynamena petesthai; ekballein de dokei tous neottous ho aetos dia phthonon; physei gar esti phthoneros, kai oxypeinos]. The eagle casts out its chicks before it is time, while they still need feeding and have not yet acquired the power of flight; and it seems to cast them out from envy. For by nature it is envious and ravenous. And a little later: Φθονεῖ οὖν τοῖς νεοττοῖς ἁδρυνομένοις, ὅτι φαγεῖν ἀγαθοὶ γίνονται, καὶ σπᾷ τοῖς ὄνυξι [Phthonei oun tois neottois hadrynomenois, hoti phagein agathoi ginontai, kai spa tois onyxi]; It envies its chicks, then, as they grow up, because they are very voracious, and it plucks at them with its talons. Likewise: Ὁ δὲ ἐκβάλλει, καὶ κόπτει αὐτοὺς· οἱ δὲ ἐκβαλλόμενοι βοῶσι, καὶ οὕτως ὑπολαμβάνει αὐτοὺς ἡ φήνη [Ho de ekballei, kai koptei autous; hoi de ekballomenoi boōsi, kai houtōs hypolambanei autous hē phēnē]. col. 181 It casts them out and beats them; the ejected ones cry out, and so the ossifrage takes them up. Accordingly Basil, in the eighth Homily on the Hexaemeron: Ἀδικώτατος περὶ τὴν τῶν ἐκγόνων ἐκτροφὴν ὁ ἀετός [Adikōtatos peri tēn tōn ekgonōn ektrophēn ho aetos]. So too Eustathius, Hexaem. p. 26. But I am not without a reply to these. In Aelian, Hist. book 2, ch. 40, you may read a whole chapter Περὶ τῆς τῶν αἰετῶν στοργῆς [Peri tēs tōn aietōn storgēs], On the love of eagles for their chicks, in which among other things this is found: Ζηλοτυπώτατον δέ ἐστι ζῶον ἀετὸς περὶ τὰ νεόττια. ἐὰν γοῦν θεάσηταί τινα προσιόντα, ἀπελθεῖν ἀτιμώρητον οὐκ ἐπιτρέπει· παίει γὰρ τοῖς πτεροῖς II, 765 αὐτὸν, καὶ τοῖς ὄνυξι λυμαίνεται [Zēlotypōtaton de esti zōon aetos peri ta neottia. ean goun theasētai tina prosionta, apelthein atimōrēton ouk epitrepei; paiei gar tois pterois auton, kai tois onyxi lymainetai]. Of all animals it shows the greatest devotion to its young. Therefore it fiercely pursues anyone it sees to have invaded its nest, and does not let him go unpunished: for it beats him with its wings and tears him with its talons. Almost the same is related by Apostolius, Cent. 2, Prov. 6. Oppian too, On Hunting, book 3, line 115, in his list of birds that cherish their little ones with constant love, places eagles in these words: ¶

Πῶς δ᾽ ἄρ ἐν οἰωνοῖσι ποθὴν ἀλίαστον ἔχουσιν ῟Ων τεκέων φῆναί τε, βαρύφθογγοί τε πέλειαι, Αἰετόεντά τε φῦλα· — — — —

Πῶς δ᾽ ἄρ ἐν οἰωνοῖσι ποθὴν ἀλίαστον ἔχουσιν ῟Ων τεκέων φῆναί τε, βαρύφθογγοί τε πέλειαι, Αἰετόεντά τε φῦλα· — — — — [Pōs d᾽ ar en oiōnoisi pothēn aliaston echousin / Hōn tekeōn phēnai te, baryphthongoi te peleiai, / Aietoenta te phyla] ¶

Quomodo etiam inter aves suorum foetuum amorem perpetuum habent et ossifraga, et grave-sonantes columbae, et aquilina saecula. Vere igitur Hieronymus in Esai- am capite 66. Ajunt, qui de animantium scripsere naturis, omnium quidem be- stiarum, et jumentorum, et pecudum, aviumque ingenitum esse in filios pullosque suos affectum: sed maximum esse amorem aquilarum. Sed, quod maxime ad rem facit, Suidas voce Εὔξενος haec habet de aquilae pullis, auctoris nomine suppresso: Οἱ νέοι τῶν ἀετῶν ἐν ἁπαλῷ μὲν τῷ πτερῷ παραπέτονται τοῖς γεινα-μένοις, μελετώμενοι ὑπ᾽ αὐτῶν τὴν πτῆσιν· Aquilarum pulli pennis adhuc infir- mis circa parentes volitant, volatum ab illis addiscentes. Quae Mosis verbis tam similia sunt, ut vel ab ipso Mose, vel ab aliquo Mosis interprete descripta videri queant. Quin sumpsit ex Philostrato, Apollonii sui libri primi capite quinto, ubi leguntur αὐτολεξεί. Nec tamen is Mosem legerat; sed uterque naturam aquilarum hac in parte descripsit. Nec dissimile est, quod asturem, id est, majorem accipitrem, Albertus se vidisse refert, qui vivas aves, a se captas, pullis suis objiciebat, ut eas insequerentur, caperent, et occiderent, et sic ad venationem paullatim se assuefacerent. His frustra objicitur, quod dicit Aristoteles, aquilam pullos e nido ejicere, cum adolescunt. Neque id absolute verum est. Sed e duobus, aut tribus unum ejicit; quia non sufficit educandis pluribus: ut Romani veteres e liberis eos exponebant, quibus educandis erant impares. Quod docet ipse Philosophus Hist. lib. 6. cap. 6. Ἐκβάλλει δ᾽ αὐξανομένων τὸν ἕτερον τῶν νεοττῶν, ἀχθόμενος τῇ ἐδωδῇ· Cum pulli adolescunt, unum ejicit, taedio nutriendi. Nempe tempore nutricationis gravissimis incommodis mater saepe premitur, ac prope opprimitur, ut sequitur in Philosopho: Λέγεται ἐν τῷ χρόνῳ τούτῳ ἄπαστος γίνεσθαι, ὅπως μὴ ἁρπάζῃ τοὺς τῶν θηρίων σκύμνους. οἵ τε οὖν ὄνυχες αὐτοῦ διαστρέφονται ὀλίγας ἡμέρας, καὶ τὰ πτερὰ λευκαίνεται. Quod Plinius ita imitatur: Quippe eo tempore ipsis cibum negavit natura, pro- spiciens, ne omnium ferarum foetus raperentur. Ungues quoque earum invertun- tur diebus iis, et albescunt inedia pennae. In his igitur angustiis si unum ex pullis ejicit mater, ad id cogitur mera necessitate; ut cum naufragii metu vasa in II, 766 mare conjiciuntur: ut Ion. I, 5. et Act. XXVII, 19. Aliud respondet Ambrosius ubi supra, nempe aquilam eos solum abjicere, qui radiis solis objecti lumina tanquam degeneres inflectunt: et sic inclementem fieri, non avaritia nutriendi, sed examine judicandi: nec quasi suum abdicare pullum, quem ex nido expellit, sed quasi alienum recusare.

Just as among birds, too, both the ossifrage and the deep-voiced doves and the races of eagles have a constant love for their offspring. Truly, then, does Jerome say on Isaiah, chapter 66: Those who have written on the nature of animals say that an affection for their offspring and young is inborn in all beasts, beasts of burden, cattle and birds, but that the greatest love is that of eagles. But, what is most to the point, Suidas, under the word Εὔξενος [Euxenos], has this about the eagle's chicks, without naming his author: Οἱ νέοι τῶν ἀετῶν ἐν ἁπαλῷ μὲν τῷ πτερῷ παραπέτονται τοῖς γειναμένοις, μελετώμενοι ὑπ᾽ αὐτῶν τὴν πτῆσιν· [Hoi neoi tōn aetōn en hapalō men tō pterō parapetontai tois geinamenois, meletōmenoi hyp᾽ autōn tēn ptēsin] The chicks of eagles, while their feathers are still weak, fly about their parents, learning flight from them. These words are so similar to those of Moses that they could seem to have been copied either from Moses himself or from some translator of Moses. In fact he took them from Philostratus, Apollonius, book 1, chapter 5, where they are read word for word. Yet Philostratus had not read Moses; rather, both described the nature of eagles in this respect. Not unlike this is what Albertus reports he saw: a goshawk, that is, a larger hawk, which threw live birds it had caught to its chicks so that they might pursue, catch and kill them, and thus gradually accustom themselves to hunting. Against this it is objected in vain that Aristotle says the eagle throws its chicks out of the nest when they grow up. Nor is that true without qualification. Rather, out of two or three it throws out one, because it is not able to rear more, just as the ancient Romans exposed those of their children whom they were unable to bring up. The Philosopher himself teaches this, Hist. book 6, ch. 6: Ἐκβάλλει δ᾽ αὐξανομένων τὸν ἕτερον τῶν νεοττῶν, ἀχθόμενος τῇ ἐδωδῇ· [Ekballei d᾽ auxanomenōn ton heteron tōn neottōn, achthomenos tē edōdē] When the chicks grow up, it throws one out, weary of feeding it. For during the time of rearing the mother is often burdened, and almost overwhelmed, by very severe hardships, as follows in the Philosopher: Λέγεται ἐν τῷ χρόνῳ τούτῳ ἄπαστος γίνεσθαι, ὅπως μὴ ἁρπάζῃ τοὺς τῶν θηρίων σκύμνους. οἵ τε οὖν ὄνυχες αὐτοῦ διαστρέφονται ὀλίγας ἡμέρας, καὶ τὰ πτερὰ λευκαίνεται. [Legetai en tō chronō toutō apastos ginesthai, hopōs mē harpazē tous tōn thēriōn skymnous. hoi te oun onyches autou diastrephontai oligas hēmeras, kai ta ptera leukainetai.] Pliny imitates this as follows: For at that time nature has denied them food, taking care that the young of all wild beasts should not be carried off. Their talons too are turned back during those days, and their feathers turn white from want of food. If, then, in these straits the mother throws out one of the chicks, she is forced to it by sheer necessity, as when for fear of shipwreck the cargo is thrown into II, 766 the sea, as in Jonah 1:5 and Acts 27:19 Ambrose, in the place cited above, gives another answer: namely, that the eagle casts out only those which, when exposed to the rays of the sun, turn their eyes away as degenerate; and so it becomes harsh not from stinginess in feeding but from the test of judgment; nor does it, as it were, disown its own chick when it expels it from the nest, but rejects it as a stranger's. ¶
