II, 774 col. 188 CAP. VI.

II, 774 col. 188 Chapter VI. ¶

Probatur, עזניה oznija non esse Haliaeetum, ut plerique volunt, sed Melanaeetum, seu Valeriam aquilam.

It is proved that עזניה oznija is not the haliaeetus, as most hold, but the melanaeetus, or Valerian eagle. ¶

In immundis avibus tertia est עזניא oznija. Ἁλιαίετον Graeci reddunt, unde Hieronymus, Munsterus, Castalio, Pagninus, Arias, Iunius, Deodatius, haliaeetum, vel aquilam marinam: et Angli ospray, quod idem est: Genevenses falconem, quorsum, non satis scio, nisi quod aquilam marinam pro falcone nonnulli habent, quos tamen refellit Gesnerus. In voce עזניה oznija Hebraei monent, Nun esse נוסף, seu epentheticum: et עזניה oznija dici pro עזיה ozja. Proinde Onkelos hic habet עזיה ozja. Et hanc aquilae speciem ab עוז oz, seu robore sic appellari, in confesso est. Unde valde verissimili ratione colligo, potius esse μελαναίετον, id est, nigram aquilam; quae, utut aquilarum minima, tamen est robustissima. Proinde Valeriam Romani vocant, ut quae viribus maxime valeat. Plinius ubi supra, Melanaeetos a Graecis dicta, eademque Valeria, minima magnitudine, viribus praecipua, colore nigricans, etc. Et ante Plinium Philosophus lib. 9. cap. 32. Ἕτερος δὲ μέλας τὴν χρόαν, καὶ μέγεθος ἐλάχιστος, καὶ κράτιστος τούτων. οὗτος οἰκεῖ ὄρη, καὶ ὕλας· καλεῖται δὲ μελαναίετος, καὶ λαγωφόνος. Alia autem est aquila, colore nigra, magni- col. 189 tudine minima, sed viribus praestantissima. Ea montes et sylvas colit: vocaturque melanaeetus (id est, nigra aquila) et leporicida. Et ante Philosophum Poeta summus de Achille Iliad. φ΄. versu 252.

Third among the unclean birds is עזניא oznija. The Greeks render it Ἁλιαίετον [Haliaieton], whence Jerome, Munster, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Diodati have haliaeetus or sea eagle; the English have ospray, which is the same; the Genevans have falcon, for what reason I do not well know, unless because some take the sea eagle for a falcon, whom, however, Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] refutes. In the word עזניה oznija the Hebrews note that the Nun is נוסף, that is, epenthetic, and that עזניה oznija is said for עזיה ozja. Accordingly Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] here has עזיה ozja. And it is agreed that this species of eagle is so named from עוז oz, that is, strength. Hence I infer on very probable grounds that it is rather the μελαναίετον [melanaieton], that is, the black eagle, which, though the smallest of eagles, is nevertheless the strongest. For this reason the Romans call it Valeria, as being most powerful (valeat) in strength. Pliny, in the place cited above: "Called melanaeetos by the Greeks, and also Valeria, smallest in size, outstanding in strength, blackish in colour", etc. And before Pliny, the Philosopher, book 9, ch. 32: Ἕτερος δὲ μέλας τὴν χρόαν, καὶ μέγεθος ἐλάχιστος, καὶ κράτιστος τούτων. οὗτος οἰκεῖ ὄρη, καὶ ὕλας· καλεῖται δὲ μελαναίετος, καὶ λαγωφόνος. [Heteros de melas tēn chroan, kai megethos elachistos, kai kratistos toutōn. houtos oikei orē, kai hylas; kaleitai de melanaietos, kai lagōphonos.] "But there is another eagle, black in colour, smallest in col. 189 size, but most excellent in strength. It inhabits mountains and forests, and is called melanaeetus" (that is, black eagle) "and hare-killer." And before the Philosopher, the greatest Poet, of Achilles, Iliad 21, line 252: ¶

Αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων μέλανος, τοῦ θηρητῆρος· Ὅς θ᾽ ἅμα κάρτιστός τε, καὶ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν·

Αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων μέλανος, τοῦ θηρητῆρος· Ὅς θ᾽ ἅμα κάρτιστός τε, καὶ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν· [Aietou oimat' echōn melanos, tou thērētēros; Hos th' hama kartistos te, kai ōkistos peteēnōn;] ¶

Aquilae impetum habens nigrae, venatricis, quae alitum simul est fortissima et celerrima. Ad quem locum Eustathius, Αἰετοῦ μέλανος τῆς θηρητῆρος, ὃν καὶ μελαναίετόν τινες συνθέτως φασὶ, τὸν καὶ φασσοφόνον (forte legendum λα-γωφόνον) μικρὸν μέν φασιν ὄντα, τῶν ἄλλων δὲ ἰσχυρότερον· Aquilae nigrae venatricis, quam composita voce quidam melanaeetum et palumbariam, parvam quidem, ut ajunt, sed aliis robustiorem. Homeri vetus Scholiastes scribit, Aristotelem pro μέλανος τοῦ, conjunctis vocibus legisse μελανόστου, ut significetur, aquilam habere nigra ossa, et alios μελανόσσου, quasi sit nigris oculis. Sed in Aristotelis libris, qui extant, nihil occurrit simile. Et frustra sollicitatur Poetae le-ctio, quae commoda est et erudita, eum conciliat in descriptione nigrae aquilae cum Aristotele et Plinio. Horum igitur consensu cum nigra aquila sit omnium validissima, quidni a robore עזניה osnija ab Hebraeis dicta fuerit. ut a Romanis valeria? Praeterea inter aquilarum species hanc a Mose fuisse omissam, verissimile II, 775 non est, cum post majorem illam et genuinam aquilam, quae proprie נשר vocatur, haec sit aquilarum omnium ἀξιομνημονεύτατος. Nam praeter eximium robur plurimas habet dotes, quae illam maxime commendant. Has ita describit Philosophus ubi supra, Ἐκτρέφει δὲ μόνος οὗτος τὰ τέκνα, καὶ ἐξ-άγει· ἔστι δὲ ὠκυβόλος, καὶ εὐθήμων, καὶ ἄφθονος, καὶ ἄφοβος, καὶ μάχι-μος, καὶ εὔφημος· οὐ γὰρ μινυρίζει, οὐδὲ λέληκεν. Una haec foetus suos alit at-que educit. Est et pernix, elegans, sine invidia, sine metu, strenua, boni ominis; neque clangit, neque murmurat. Proinde illam cum chrysaëto ita confert amplissimus Thuanus lib. I. De re accipitraria,

"Having the onset of the black eagle, the hunter, which is at once the strongest and swiftest of birds." On this passage Eustathius: Αἰετοῦ μέλανος τῆς θηρητῆρος, ὃν καὶ μελαναίετόν τινες συνθέτως φασὶ, τὸν καὶ φασσοφόνον [Aietou melanos tēs thērētēros, hon kai melanaieton tines synthetōs phasi, ton kai phassophonon] (perhaps one should read λαγωφόνον [lagōphonon]) μικρὸν μέν φασιν ὄντα, τῶν ἄλλων δὲ ἰσχυρότερον· [mikron men phasin onta, tōn allōn de ischyroteron;] "Of the black hunting eagle, which some call by a compound word melanaeetus, and dove-killer; small indeed, as they say, but stronger than the others." The old Scholiast of Homer writes that Aristotle, instead of μέλανος τοῦ [melanos tou], read with the words joined μελανόστου [melanostou], meaning that the eagle has black bones, and others μελανόσσου [melanossou], as if with black eyes. But in the extant books of Aristotle nothing similar occurs. And the Poet's reading is tampered with in vain, for it is apt and learned, and brings him into agreement with Aristotle and Pliny in describing the black eagle. Since, then, by their consensus the black eagle is the strongest of all, why should it not have been called עזניה osnija by the Hebrews from its strength, as valeria by the Romans? Moreover, it is not at all likely II, 775 that this species was omitted by Moses among the eagles, since after that greater and genuine eagle, properly called נשר, this is the most noteworthy (ἀξιομνημονεύτατος [axiomnēmoneutatos]) of all eagles. For besides its exceptional strength it has very many qualities that especially commend it. The Philosopher describes them thus in the place cited: Ἐκτρέφει δὲ μόνος οὗτος τὰ τέκνα, καὶ ἐξάγει· ἔστι δὲ ὠκυβόλος, καὶ εὐθήμων, καὶ ἄφθονος, καὶ ἄφοβος, καὶ μάχιμος, καὶ εὔφημος· οὐ γὰρ μινυρίζει, οὐδὲ λέληκεν. [Ektrephei de monos houtos ta tekna, kai exagei; esti de ōkybolos, kai euthēmōn, kai aphthonos, kai aphobos, kai machimos, kai euphēmos; ou gar minyrizei, oude lelēken.] "This one alone rears and brings out its young. It is also swift, elegant, without envy, without fear, vigorous, of good omen; it neither screams nor murmurs." Accordingly the most distinguished Thuanus, book 1 De re accipitraria, compares it with the chrysaetus thus: ¶

Optima et ipsa aequans animis ingentibus illam, Corporis inferior quamvis sit mole, nec ungue, Aut rostro tantum valeat, digitisve: sed ortus Non mentita suos, animive oblita virilis, Accipitres collata nothos facit. Hanc quoque nostri Valeriam dicunt, Graeci μελαναίετον, atrum Quod referat rostro, pennis atque ungue colorem (1).

"Excellent, she too equals that one in mighty spirit, though she is inferior in bulk of body, and not so strong in talon, beak or toes; but, not belying her birth nor forgetful of a manly spirit, she makes hawks look bastard by comparison. Her too our people call Valeria, the Greeks μελαναίετον [melanaieton], because in beak, feathers and talon she shows a dark colour" R1. ¶

Notandum, tam עזניה oznija, quam פרס peres, a nonnullis pro gryphe haberi. Ac proinde utrumque עוז uz, vel עוזא uza reddi, quae vox paraphrastis et Talmudicis gryphem sonat. Ita in Ionathanis paraphrasi עוזא uza habetur pro פרס peres. At in Samaritana ࠏࠅࠆࠀ uza est pro ࠏࠆࠍࠉࠄ oznija. Et in Talmudico tractatu Kelim cap. 17. §. 14. ubi agitur de vasis, quae fiunt מכנף העוז, ex ala τοῦ uz, id est, gryphis, Glossator haec habet: עוף הוא ששמו עזניה ורגילים היו לעשות כלים מכנף אותו העוז, Avis est, quae (in textu sacro) vocatur oznija, ex cujus alis solebant fieri vasa. Ubi fortasse pro מכנף miccanaph (ex ala) legendum מכף miccaph (ex planta). Et paullo post, כף העוז מכל הכפים, Planta (avis) uz omni alia praestat, pro כנף העוז מכל הכנפים, Ala avis uz omni ala praestat. Planta, id est, pes et unguis. Id enim כף caph in avibus. Sic Gen. VIII, 9. Columba non invenit requiem לכף רגלה: et Arabs Erpenianus لكف رجلها Plantae pedis sui, id est, τοῖς ποσὶν αὐτῆς, pedibus suis, ut ibi Graeci habent. Porro ex gryphis pedibus, vel unguibus vasa fieri dictitant, non item ex ejus alis. Damir capite De col. 190 عنقا anka, seu Gryphe, فيصنع من مخاليبها اقداح II, 776 عظام للشرب Et ex unguibus ejus fiunt vasa potoria magna. Gesnerus de gryphe, Iohannes de Montevilla scribit, ungues ejus tanquam cornua bovis esse, e quibus magni pretii pocula fiunt. Et paullo post, Memini ego me videre apud aurifabrum in civitate nostra cornu nigrum laeve, et in summo aduncum, fere ut in rupicapris, triplo forte crassius, cujus labris argentum inducturus erat auri- faber, ut pro poculo mensis adhiberetur. Id forte bovis alicujus peregrini, ut quem Indicum vocant, aut bubali cornu erat. Ille vero pro gryphis ungue accepe- rat. Aliud afferam, unde constet, עזניה oznija gryphem esse ex Hebraeorum sententia. In libro בעל החיים, seu De naturis animalium, quem ex Arabico Abulsaphae in Hebraeum transtulit Calonymus, עזניה oznija, in aves rapaces late imperans, eas undequaque convocat, et in illarum concilio ita loquitur: הנה אני עם גודל גופי ואומץ כוחי ומהירות עופפותי עזבתי בתיהם וברחתי מהם אל האיים הרחוקים ואל ההרים הרמים והנשואים, Ego quidem, utut corpore vasta, et viribus praestans, et alis pernix, domos eorum (hominum) reliqui, et ab iis in remotas insulas fugi; et in arduos celsosque montes. Nec multo aliter Maimonides de peres et oznija, in tractatu De cibis vetitis cap. 1. §. 17. פרס ועזניה אינן מצויין בישוב אלא במדברות איי הים הרחוקות עד מאד שהן סוף הישוב, Perez et oznija in habitato orbe (ἐν τῇ οἰκουμένῃ) non reperiuntur, sed in maris insulis remotissimis in extrema parte orbis. Haec ad Arabum ancam, seu gryphem pertinere, palam est, quem fabulantur olim inter homines in Arabia vixisse, ferasque et aves habuisse pro pabulo: sed, cum aliquando pueros et sponsam etiam rapuisset, Handalae Prophetae precibus relegatum fuisse in insulam, ab hominum commercio remotissimam, unde egredi, ut ad loca cultiora se conferat, sit illi nefas. Proinde pro Hebraeo עזניה oznija in Arabicis paraphrasibus, tam Saadiae, quam aliorum, legere est العنقا alanka. Atque ideo Saadiae succenset Aben Ezra, quod Deum prohibuisse velit הדבר שלא היה, rem, quae non extat. Quod ipsi fatentur Arabes. In his Alcamus, a quo العنقا alanka definitur eleganti paronomasia طاير معروف الاسم ومجهول الجسم avis noti alismi, id est, nominis, sed ignoti algismi, id est, corporis. Et poeta, qui dicit, الجود والغول والعنقا ثلاثة اسما اشيا لم توجد ولم تكن Probitas, et algol (seu empusa) et alanca (seu gryphus) tria sunt nomina rerum, quae non reperiuntur, nec usquam extant. Sed alibi Saadiam atque alios paraphrastas, qui hac voce utuntur, excusari posse doceo. Quia, utut anca plerunque sit avis fictitiae nomen, ut nobis gryphes, tamen etiam pro vera ave quandoque occurrit. Sed, quidquid illi in mente ha-buerint, hoc certum est, עזניה oznija in Mose significare veram avem, et valde verissimile, aquilam esse nigram, quam Graeci composita voce μελαναίετον appellant.

It should be noted that both עזניה oznija and פרס peres are taken by some for the griffin, and that accordingly both are rendered עוז uz or עוזא uza, a word that among the paraphrasts and the Talmudists means griffin. Thus in Jonathan's [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrase עוזא uza stands for פרס peres, while in the Samaritan ࠏࠅࠆࠀ [עוזא] uza stands for ࠏࠆࠍࠉࠄ [עזניה] oznija. And in the Talmudic tractate Kelim, ch. 17, § 14, where vessels made מכנף העוז, from the wing of the uz, that is, of the griffin, are discussed, the Glossator has this: עוף הוא ששמו עזניה ורגילים היו לעשות כלים מכנף אותו העוז, It is a bird which (in the sacred text) is called oznija, from whose wings vessels used to be made. Here perhaps instead of מכנף miccanaph (from the wing) we should read מכף miccaph (from the sole). And shortly after, כף העוז מכל הכפים, The sole (of the bird) uz surpasses every other, instead of כנף העוז מכל הכנפים, The wing of the bird uz surpasses every wing. Sole, that is, foot and talon; for that is what כף caph means in birds. So Gen 8:9. The dove found no rest לכף רגלה: and Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] has لكف رجلها [li-kaff rijlihā] for the sole of her foot, that is, τοῖς ποσὶν αὐτῆς [tois posin autēs], for her feet, as the Greeks have there. Moreover, they commonly say that vessels are made from the griffin's feet or talons, not from its wings. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter On col. 190 عنقا [ʿanqā] anka, or the Griffin: فيصنع من مخاليبها اقداح II, 776 عظام للشرب [fa-yuṣnaʿ min makhālībihā aqdāḥ ʿiẓām li-l-shurb] And from its talons large drinking vessels are made. Gesner, on the griffin: John Mandeville writes that its talons are like the horns of an ox, from which cups of great value are made. And shortly after: I remember seeing at a goldsmith's in our city a smooth black horn, hooked at the top, almost as in chamois, perhaps three times as thick, whose rim the goldsmith was about to mount in silver, so that it might be used as a cup at table. It was perhaps the horn of some foreign ox, such as the one they call Indian, or of a buffalo. But he had received it as a griffin's talon. I shall adduce another testimony showing that עזניה oznija is the griffin in the opinion of the Hebrews. In the book בעל החיים, or On the Natures of Animals, which Calonymus translated from the Arabic of Abulsapha into Hebrew, the עזניה oznija, ruling far and wide over the birds of prey, summons them from every side and speaks thus in their council: הנה אני עם גודל גופי ואומץ כוחי ומהירות עופפותי עזבתי בתיהם וברחתי מהם אל האיים הרחוקים ואל ההרים הרמים והנשואים, I indeed, though vast in body, outstanding in strength and swift of wing, have left their (men's) dwellings and fled from them to remote islands and to steep and lofty mountains. Not very differently Maimonides [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] on the peres and the oznija, in the tractate On Forbidden Foods, ch. 1, § 17: פרס ועזניה אינן מצויין בישוב אלא במדברות איי הים הרחוקות עד מאד שהן סוף הישוב, The Perez and the oznija are not found in the inhabited world (ἐν τῇ οἰκουμένῃ [en tē oikoumenē]), but in the most remote islands of the sea at the farthest edge of the world. It is obvious that this refers to the anca of the Arabs, or griffin, which they fable once lived among men in Arabia and fed on beasts and birds; but when it had once carried off children and even a bride, it was banished at the prayers of the Prophet Handala to an island most remote from human intercourse, from which it is forbidden to come out to betake itself to more cultivated places. Accordingly, for the Hebrew עזניה oznija one reads in the Arabic paraphrases, of Saadia as of others, العنقا [al-ʿanqā] alanka. And for this reason Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] is angry with Saadia for holding that God forbade הדבר שלא היה, a thing that does not exist. The Arabs themselves admit this. Among them is Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author], by whom العنقا [al-ʿanqā] alanka is defined with an elegant paronomasia as طاير معروف الاسم ومجهول الجسم [ṭāʾir maʿrūf al-ism wa-majhūl al-jism], a bird of known alism, that is, name, but unknown algism, that is, body. And the poet who says الجود والغول والعنقا ثلاثة اسما اشيا لم توجد ولم تكن [al-jūd wa-l-ghūl wa-l-ʿanqā thalātha asmā ashyā lam tūjad wa-lam takun] Liberality, and algol (or empusa) and alanca (or griffin) are three names of things that are not found and exist nowhere. But elsewhere I show that Saadia and the other paraphrasts who use this word can be excused, because, although anca is mostly the name of a fictitious bird, as griffins are for us, yet it sometimes also occurs for a real bird. But whatever they had in mind, this is certain: עזניה oznija in Moses signifies a real bird, and very probably it is the black eagle, which the Greeks call by the compound word μελαναίετον [melanaieton]. ¶
