De Accipitre, qui נץ nets Hebraeis.

On the hawk, which is נץ nets to the Hebrews. ¶

Sequitur נץ nets, id est, accipiter. Ita enim reddunt omnes interpretes sum- col. 266 mo consensu. Et Syris id ipsum est ܢܨܐ natsa, vel nitsa; Chaldaeis quoque, ut נצצא netsatsa; et Samaritanis ࠍࠑࠑࠄ netsatsah. Nomen a volatu factum. Nam Hebraeis נצה natsa volare est. Et Arabibus نض alas motitare inter volandum. Porro hanc avem volatu maxime praestare, non uno in loco testatur vatum nobilissimus. Ut Iliad. ό, versu 237. ubi Apollo ab Ida descendit,

Next comes נץ nets, that is, the hawk. For so all the translators render it, in complete col. 266 agreement. And among the Syrians the very same bird is ܢܨܐ [נצא] natsa or nitsa; among the Chaldeans too, as נצצא netsatsa; and among the Samaritans ࠍࠑࠑࠄ [נצצה] netsatsah. The name is formed from flight. For in Hebrew נצה natsa means to fly. And in Arabic نض [naḍ] means to keep moving the wings while flying. Moreover, that this bird excels above all in flight is attested in more than one place by the most renowned of poets. Thus Iliad 15, line 237, where Apollo descends from Ida, ¶

— — — ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς
Ὠκέϊ, φασσοφόνῳ, ὅς τ᾽ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν,

— — — ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς [irēki eoikōs] Ὠκέϊ, φασσοφόνῳ, ὅς τ᾽ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν [ōkei, phassophonō, hos t᾽ ōkistos peteēnōn], ¶

Accipitri similis veloci, palumborum occisori, qui avium est velocissimus. Et Odyss. ν´, versu 86. ubi Phaeacum navem, quae Ulyssem in Ithacam vexit,

Like a swift hawk, the killer of wood pigeons, which is the swiftest of birds. And Odyssey 13, line 86, where the ship of the Phaeacians that carried Ulysses to Ithaca is described, ¶

— — — οὐδὲ μὲν ἴρηξ
Κίρκος ὁμαρτήσειεν ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν,

— — — οὐδὲ μὲν ἴρηξ [oude men irēx] Κίρκος ὁμαρτήσειεν ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν [kirkos homartēseien elaphrotatos peteēnōn], ¶

Ne accipiter quidem circus sequatur, utut avium pernicissimus. Et Odyss. ό. versu 526.

Not even the circus hawk could keep pace with it, though the fleetest of birds. And Odyssey 15, line 526: ¶

Κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος, —

Κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος [kirkos, Apollōnos tachys angelos], — ¶

Circus Apollinis velox nuntius. Proinde Iliad. ν´. versu 819. Hectori praedicitur, fore, ut optet aliquando,

The circus, Apollo's swift messenger. Accordingly, in Iliad 13, line 819, it is foretold to Hector that he will one day wish ¶

Θάσσονας ἰρήκων ἔμεναι καλλίτριχας ἵππους,

Θάσσονας ἰρήκων ἔμεναι καλλίτριχας ἵππους [thassonas irēkōn emenai kallitrichas hippous], ¶

Ut pulchre-comati equi sui sint accipitribus velociores. Neque solus Apollo, sed Thetis a) quoque et Neptunus feruntur ἴρηξ ὡς, instar accipitris; vel ὥς τ᾽ ἴρηξ ὠκύπτερος, ut accipiter citis alis. Et in Hesiodi Apologo,

That his fair-maned horses were swifter than hawks. And not Apollo alone, but Thetis a) and Neptune too are said to move ἴρηξ ὡς [irēx hōs], like a hawk; or ὥς τ᾽ ἴρηξ ὠκύπτερος [hōs t᾽ irēx ōkypteros], like a hawk with swift wings. And in Hesiod's fable, ¶

Ὡς ἔφατ᾽ ὠκυπετὴς ἴρηξ, τανυσίπτερος ὄρνις,

Ὡς ἔφατ᾽ ὠκυπετὴς ἴρηξ, τανυσίπτερος ὄρνις [hōs ephat᾽ ōkypetēs irēx, tanysipteros ornis], ¶

Ut dixit pernix accipiter, extensis alis avis. Et apud Aristophanem in Avibus Sycophanta,

So spoke the swift hawk, the bird with outstretched wings. And in Aristophanes, in the Birds, the Sycophant says, ¶

Ἀλλὰ πτέρου με ταχέσι καὶ κούφοις πτεροῖς
Ἱέρακος, ἢ κερχνῇδος. — — —

Ἀλλὰ πτέρου με ταχέσι καὶ κούφοις πτεροῖς Ἱέρακος, ἢ κερχνῇδος [alla pterou me tachesi kai kouphois pterois hierakos, ē kerchnēdos]. — — — ¶

Sed instrue me citis et levibus alis accipitris, aut cerchneidis: quae et ipsa est accipitris species; Romani tinnunculum vocant. Sed volatum accipitris nemo est, III, 6 qui vel uberius, vel elegantius describat amplissimo Thuano, qui inter accipitrum species triorchen, seu buteonem, volantem ita expressit libro primo De re accipitraria,

But fit me with the swift and light wings of a hawk, or of a cerchneis: this too is a species of hawk; the Romans call it tinnunculus [kestrel]. But no one describes the flight of the hawk III, 6 either more fully or more elegantly than the illustrious Thuanus, who, among the species of hawks, depicted the triorches, or buteo [buzzard], in flight thus in book 1 of De re accipitraria: ¶

Aspice non, quo more alii, stridentibus alis
Subvehitur, crebrosque alternat in aëre gyros:
Sed recto incedens graditur per inane volatu
Arduus, et magni conscendit sidera coeli:
Ut vero nubes inter caput extulit altas,
Praecipiti raptus lapsu ruit, et furit ardens,
Subjectamque oculis praedam deturbat ab alto.

See how, not in the manner of others, with whirring wings he is borne aloft and wheels in frequent circles in the air; but advancing in straight flight he strides through the void, soaring, and climbs to the stars of the great heaven. Then, when he has raised his head among the lofty clouds, he is swept down in a headlong plunge, rushes and rages in his fury, and strikes from on high the prey lying beneath his eyes. ¶

Et libro secundo idem de accipitre in genere haec habet,

And in book 2 the same author says this of the hawk in general: ¶

Vix credas parvo quam tempore longa locorum
Intervalla volans superet, quam multa peragret
Aequora. — —

You would scarcely believe in how short a time, flying, he overcomes long distances between places, how many seas he traverses. — — ¶

Et infra de Francisci, regis, accipitre, qui una brevi nocte e Villeriae castro col. 267 Londinum se contulit, et notis suis agnitus ad dominum remissus est;

And further on, of the hawk of King Francis, which in one short night flew from the castle of Villers col. 267 to London, and, recognized by its marks, was sent back to its master: ¶

— — — fugit ille per auras Perfidus emansor, rapidisque per aëra pennis Tormento properat, volucrique citatior Euro, Et Tartessiaco dum sol lavit aequore crines, Rursus et Eoâ mortales lampade tingit, Oceanum supra fertur, Tamesimque Britannum, Londini et fessus tandem super astitit arce.

— — — he flees through the breezes, the faithless truant, and with rapid wings through the air hastens faster than a missile, swifter than the winged Eurus; and while the sun bathes his locks in the Tartessian sea, and again tinges mortals with his Eastern lamp, he is borne over the Ocean and the British Thames, and at last, weary, alights upon the citadel of London. ¶

Merito igitur apud Aegyptios accipiter ventorum fuit symbolum. Horus libri secundi capite decimo quarto, Τὴν ἀνατολὴν ἱέραξ ἐςὶ μετεώρου θέων ἀνέμους σημαίνει· Accipiter, ortum versus in sublime tendens, ventos significat. Quod ipsum designabat ἱέραξ διατεταμένος τὰς πτέρυγας, accipiter alis extensis, ut ab eodem additur. Quamvis aliter Tentyritae, qui, accipitres cum igne conferentes, μαρτύριον ἐπάγουσιν ἀπότομον αὐτῶν, pro testimonio afferunt praecipitem eorum velocitatem. Nec desunt, qui ob id ipsum velint Soli fuisse consecratos. Eustathius in Odyss. ό. Ἱέραξ ἱεροῦται Ἡλίῳ Ἀπόλλωνι διά τε τὸ ὀξὺ τῆς κινήσεως· ὠκυπετὴς γὰρ ὁ ἱέραξ, καὶ ὁ Ἥλιος ὀξυκίνητος, καὶ διὰ τὴν ἐξ ἐτυμολογίας συγγένειαν. καὶ γὰρ ἵεται ῥᾷον ὁ Ἥλιος. ἐντεῦθεν δὲ καὶ ὁ ἱέραξ ὠνόμασαι. Accipiter Soli Apollini sacer est propter motus celeritatem: pernix enim est accipiter, ut Sol est velocis motus, et propter etymi affinitatem. Sol enim it facile. Vnde accipitri nomen est. Cui tamen etymo Virgilianum praefero li-bro undecimo Aeneidos, III, 7 Quam facile accipiter saxo sacer ales ab alto.

Rightly, then, among the Egyptians the hawk was the symbol of the winds. Horus, book 2, chapter 14: Τὴν ἀνατολὴν ἱέραξ ἐςὶ μετεώρου θέων ἀνέμους σημαίνει [tēn anatolēn hierax es[t]i meteōrou theōn anemous sēmainei]; A hawk, rising aloft toward the east, signifies the winds. The same was signified by ἱέραξ διατεταμένος τὰς πτέρυγας [hierax diatetamenos tas pterygas], a hawk with outstretched wings, as the same author adds. The Tentyrites, however, see it otherwise: comparing hawks with fire, μαρτύριον ἐπάγουσιν ἀπότομον αὐτῶν [martyrion epagousin apotomon autōn], they adduce as evidence their headlong speed. Nor are there lacking those who hold that for this very reason hawks were consecrated to the Sun. Eustathius on Odyssey 15: Ἱέραξ ἱεροῦται Ἡλίῳ Ἀπόλλωνι διά τε τὸ ὀξὺ τῆς κινήσεως· ὠκυπετὴς γὰρ ὁ ἱέραξ, καὶ ὁ Ἥλιος ὀξυκίνητος, καὶ διὰ τὴν ἐξ ἐτυμολογίας συγγένειαν. καὶ γὰρ ἵεται ῥᾷον ὁ Ἥλιος. ἐντεῦθεν δὲ καὶ ὁ ἱέραξ ὠνόμασαι [hierax hieroutai Hēliō Apollōni dia te to oxy tēs kinēseōs; ōkypetēs gar ho hierax, kai ho Hēlios oxykinētos, kai dia tēn ex etymologias syngeneian. kai gar hietai rhaon ho Hēlios. enteuthen de kai ho hierax ōnomasai]. The hawk is sacred to Apollo the Sun because of the swiftness of its motion: for the hawk is fleet, as the Sun is of swift motion; and because of the kinship of etymology. For the Sun goes easily. Hence the hawk has its name. To this etymology, however, I prefer Virgil's, in book 11 of the Aeneid: III, 7 As easily as the hawk, the sacred bird, from a high rock. ¶

Ubi sacer ales est ἱέραξ· ut apud Demetrium in Praefatione ad Hieracosophium, Τῷ τῶν ἱερῶν ὀρνίθων γένει πρὸς σωτηρίαν τινὰ πορίσασθαι μηχανὴν, Sacrarum avium generi ad salutem aliquam artem invenire. Et rursus, Ἐσπούδασά τινα χρήσιμον θεραπείαν πρὸς σωτηρίαν τῷ ἱερῷ τῶν ὀρνίθων γένει καταλι-πεῖν, Utilem aliquem ad salutem curationem sacro avium generi relinquere conatus sum. In quibus verbis per sacras aves et sacrarum avium genus, τοὺς ἱέρακας, id est, accipitres, designari palam est. Et cur accipiter Graece ita dictus sit (inquit Servius) ratione non caret, quae nota est sacrorum peritis. Ego hoc natum putaverim ex Aegyptiorum superstitione, quibus ex sacris animalibus nihil fuit tam sacrum, quam ibis et accipiter. Itaque, si quis aliud animal invitus necavisset, ad sacerdotum arbitrium mulctabatur. Ὃς δ' ἂν ἶβιν, ἢ ἴρηκα ἀπο-κτένῃ, ἤν τε ἑκὼν, ἤν τε ἀέκων, τεθνάναι ἀνάγκη· At quisquis ibin, aut accipitrem necaverat, sive volens, aut invitus necasset, eum mori necesse erat. Ita habetur in Euterpe Herodoti [c. 65].

Where the sacer ales [sacred bird] is ἱέραξ [hierax]; as in Demetrius, in the Preface to the Hieracosophium: Τῷ τῶν ἱερῶν ὀρνίθων γένει πρὸς σωτηρίαν τινὰ πορίσασθαι μηχανὴν [Tō tōn hierōn ornithōn genei pros sōtērian tina porisasthai mēchanēn], "to find some art for the preservation of the race of sacred birds." And again: Ἐσπούδασά τινα χρήσιμον θεραπείαν πρὸς σωτηρίαν τῷ ἱερῷ τῶν ὀρνίθων γένει καταλιπεῖν [Espoudasa tina chrēsimon therapeian pros sōtērian tō hierō tōn ornithōn genei katalipein], "I have endeavoured to leave to the sacred race of birds some useful treatment for its preservation." In these words it is plain that by sacred birds and the race of sacred birds τοὺς ἱέρακας [tous hierakas], that is, hawks, are meant. "And why the hawk is so called in Greek," says Servius, "is not without a reason, which is known to those versed in sacred rites." I would think this arose from the superstition of the Egyptians, for whom among the sacred animals none was so sacred as the ibis and the hawk. Thus, if anyone had killed some other animal unwillingly, he was fined at the discretion of the priests. Ὃς δ' ἂν ἶβιν, ἢ ἴρηκα ἀποκτένῃ, ἤν τε ἑκὼν, ἤν τε ἀέκων, τεθνάναι ἀνάγκη [Hos d' an ibin, ē irēka apoktenē, ēn te hekōn, ēn te aekōn, tethnanai anankē]; "But whoever had killed an ibis or a hawk, whether willingly or unwillingly, had to die." So it stands in the Euterpe of Herodotus [ch. 65]. ¶

Quin hodieque sacrum censent inter accipitrum aut falconum species. Verum, ut dicam, quod res est, vox Latina non est, sed Arabica صقر vel سقر aut زقر, ut scribunt nonnulli per Sin et per Ze, quamvis frequentius scribatur per Sad, a verbo صقر sakara, quod acute videre sonat, plane ut Chaldaeum סכר sekar. Alkamus, الصاقر حديد البصر Sakir, qui est acuti visus. Itaque الصقر, nomen inde deductum, (Gall. sacre) primo significat كل طاير يصيد, omnem avem aucupatoriam. Secundo unam e quatuor rapacium avium speciebus. Hae scilicet sunt صقر sacer, et شاهين sahin, et عقاب okab, et بازي bazi. Et صقر sacer in tres alias species subdividitur: nempe in صقر sacrum proprie dictum, كونج chauneg, et يويو col. 268 juju. Quae singula hic describere, longum esset et taediosum. Proinde solum agemus de sacro proprie dicto. Is aliter vocatur اجدل agdal, robustus; اكدر achdar, fuscus; et قماطي kumati, carnis avidus; et a Damire, et aliis sic describitur: هو من الجوارح بمنزلة البغال من الدواب لانه اصبر علي الشدة واحمل الغليظ الاذي واحسن القاء واشد اقدام علي جملة من الكركي وغيره ومزاجه ابرد من ساير ما تقدم ذكره من الحوارح وارطب وبهذا السبب يضرا علي الغزال والارنب وهو اهدي من البازي نفسا واسرع بالناس انسا واكثر منة III, 8 منه قنعا ولبرد مزاجه لا يشرب ماء ولو اقام دهرا وكذلك يوصف بالبخر ونتي الفم وله كفان في يده كالسبع لانه يكف بهما علي ما اخذ اي يجمع, Se habet ad alias aves rapaces, ut mulus ad reliqua jumenta. Est enim viribus fortior, et aptior ad ferendas graviores laesiones, et occursu melior, et pedibus firmior contra omne gruum genus, et avium hujusmodi. Et frigidioris est temperamenti, atque etiam humidioris rapacium avium aliis, de quibus supra est actum. Ac proinde instituitur ad caprearum et leporum venationem. Et τῷ bazi, seu accipitre docilior est, et ab hominibus citius cicuratur, et facilius illi sa- tisfit. Propter temperamenti sui frigiditatem aquam non bibit unquam, quantum- cunque temporis maneat (sine potu). Unde est, quod describitur oris foetidi et male-olentis. In manu sua volas habet ferarum instar, quibus in id involet, quod capit, et illud teneat. Scribit Alkazuinius, nullius rapacium aucupium tam esse mirabile, cum sacri duo in capreae oculos involantes, aut eam alis diverberantes detineant, donec in venatorum manus incidat. Miratur idem, quod avis tantula لشجاعة خلقها الله تعالي فيه, propter auda- ciam, quam illi indidit summus Deus, in gruem insilire audeat, quae molis est tanto majoris. Hujus aucupii apud Arabes primus auctor fuit Alharits, Mua- viae filius. Haec obiter de sacro (1).

Indeed, even today they count the sacer among the species of hawks or falcons. But, to say what the matter really is, the word is not Latin but Arabic: صقر [ṣaqr] or سقر [saqr] or زقر [zaqr], as some write it with Sin and with Ze, although it is more often written with Sad, from the verb صقر [ṣaqara] sakara, which means to see sharply, just like the Chaldee סכר sekar. The Kamus: الصاقر حديد البصر [al-ṣāqir ḥadīd al-baṣar] Sakir, "one who is sharp-sighted." Thus الصقر [al-ṣaqr], the noun derived from it (French sacre), first signifies كل طاير يصيد [kull ṭāyir yaṣīd], every bird used in fowling. Secondly, one of the four species of birds of prey. These are namely صقر [ṣaqr] sacer, شاهين [shāhīn] sahin, عقاب [ʿuqāb] okab, and بازي [bāzī] bazi. And the صقر [ṣaqr] sacer is subdivided into three further species: namely the صقر [ṣaqr] sacer properly so called, the كونج [kawnaj] chauneg, and the يويو [yūyū] col. 268 juju. To describe each of these here would be long and tedious. Therefore we shall treat only of the sacer properly so called. It is otherwise called اجدل [ajdal] agdal, robust; اكدر [akdar] achdar, dusky; and قماطي [qumāṭī] kumati, greedy for flesh; and it is described by Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and others thus: هو من الجوارح بمنزلة البغال من الدواب لانه اصبر علي الشدة واحمل الغليظ الاذي واحسن القاء واشد اقدام علي جملة من الكركي وغيره ومزاجه ابرد من ساير ما تقدم ذكره من الحوارح وارطب وبهذا السبب يضرا علي الغزال والارنب وهو اهدي من البازي نفسا واسرع بالناس انسا واكثر منة III, 8 منه قنعا ولبرد مزاجه لا يشرب ماء ولو اقام دهرا وكذلك يوصف بالبخر ونتي الفم وله كفان في يده كالسبع لانه يكف بهما علي ما اخذ اي يجمع [huwa min al-jawāriḥ bi-manzilat al-bighāl min al-dawābb li-annahu aṣbar ʿalā al-shidda wa-aḥmal al-ghalīẓ al-adhā wa-aḥsan ilqāʾ wa-ashadd iqdām ʿalā jumla min al-kurkī wa-ghayrihi wa-mizājuhu abrad min sāyir mā taqaddama dhikruhu min al-ḥawāriḥ wa-arṭab wa-bi-hādhā al-sabab yuḍrā ʿalā al-ghazāl wa-al-arnab wa-huwa ahdā min al-bāzī nafsan wa-asraʿ bi-al-nās unsan wa-akthar mna minhu qanaʿan wa-li-bard mizājihi lā yashrab māʾ wa-law aqāma dahran wa-kadhālika yūṣaf bi-al-bakhar wa-ntī al-fam wa-lahu kaffān fī yadihi ka-al-sabuʿ li-annahu yakuff bihimā ʿalā mā akhadha ay yajmaʿ], "It stands to the other birds of prey as the mule to the other beasts of burden. For it is stronger, more able to bear heavier injuries, better in attack, and firmer of foot against every kind of crane and birds of that sort. And it is of a colder and also moister temperament than the other birds of prey treated above. Therefore it is trained for hunting gazelles and hares." And than the bazi, or hawk, it is more docile, is tamed by men more quickly, and is more easily satisfied. Because of the coldness of its temperament it never drinks water, however long it remains (without drink). Hence it is described as having a fetid and foul-smelling mouth. In its foot it has palms like wild beasts, with which it pounces on what it catches and holds it." Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] writes that no hunting by birds of prey is so marvellous as when two sacres, flying at a gazelle's eyes or beating it with their wings, detain it until it falls into the hunters' hands. He also marvels that so small a bird لشجاعة خلقها الله تعالي فيه [li-shajāʿa khalaqahā Allāh taʿālā fīhi], "because of the boldness which God Most High has put in it," dares to leap upon a crane, which is of so much greater bulk. The first originator of this kind of fowling among the Arabs was Alharits, son of Muawiya. So much in passing about the sacer R1. ¶

Accipitrum genera sex Callimachus, decem Aristoteles, sedecim censet Pli- nius. Sed ratione molis ad duo revocantur, de quibus Philosophus Historiae li-bri octavi capite tertio, Ἱέρακες ἄμφω, ὅτε φαβοτύπος, καὶ ὁ σπιζίας, δια-φέρουσι τὸ μέγεθος πολὺ ἀλλήλων· Accipitres ambo, palumbarius scilicet et frin- gillarius, magnitudine ab invicem multum differunt. Quo eodem refertur Nisi metamorphosis, regis Megarensium; quamvis eum pro accipitre in haliaeetum fuisse mutatum, poëtae fingunt. Ovidius libro octavo Metamorphoseωn,

Callimachus reckons six kinds of hawks, Aristotle ten, Pliny sixteen. But by size they are reduced to two, of which the Philosopher speaks in book 8, chapter 3 of the History: Ἱέρακες ἄμφω, ὅτε φαβοτύπος, καὶ ὁ σπιζίας, διαφέρουσι τὸ μέγεθος πολὺ ἀλλήλων [Hierakes amphō, hote phabotypos, kai ho spizias, diapherousi to megethos poly allēlōn]; "Both hawks, namely the dove-hawk and the finch-hawk, differ much from each other in size." The former more recent writers call astur (French autour); the latter nisus and sparverius. Iulius Firmicus indeed mentions asturs col. 269 in book 5, chapter 7. But the name nisus seems taken from the Hebrew נץ nets, or from the Chaldee נצה netsa. To the same is referred the metamorphosis of Nisus, king of the Megarians, although the poets feign that he was changed not into a hawk but into a sea eagle. Ovid, in book 8 of the Metamorphoses: ¶

Et modo factus avis fulvis haliaeetus alis.

And now, made a bird, a haliaeetus with tawny wings. ¶

Virgilius, aut quisquis alius, in Ciri: III, 9 Fecit et in terris haliaeetus ales ut esset.

Virgil, or whoever else it was, in the Ciris: III, 9 And made her a winged haliaeetus, to dwell upon the earth. ¶

Sed inter accipitrum, seu falconum, species lanius, seu lanarius, haliaeeto est tam similis, ut non aliter distinguantur, quam ex diverso pastu. Thuanus in haliaeeti descriptione libro primo De re accipitraria,

But among the species of hawks, or falcons, the lanius or lanarius [lanner] is so similar to the haliaeetus that they are distinguished only by their different diet. Thuanus, in his description of the haliaeetus in book 1 of De re accipitraria: ¶

Forma eadem huic, lanioque eadem est, atque inter utrumque
Obscurum discrimen habet, quo quisque marinus,
Vel lanius dici possit. Quin saepe periti
Ambiguo peccant decepti lumina vultu;
Et, nisi diverso pastu gauderet uterque,
Saepe repertores artis turpissimus error
Argueret. — — — —

This one has the same form as the lanner, and between the two
There lies an obscure difference, by which each may be called
Marine or lanner. Indeed, even the skilled often err,
Their eyes deceived by the ambiguous look;
And, did each not delight in a different diet,
A most shameful error would often convict
The masters of the art. — — — — ¶

Confundi igitur potuere a veteribus, atque iisdem nominibus indigetari propter formae similitudinem.

So they could have been confused by the ancients and called by the same names because of the similarity of their form. ¶

De accipitre sic Deus Iob. XXXIX, 26. [al. 29.] An per prudentiam tuam plumescit accipiter, expansis alis suis versus Austrum? Plumescit Hebraice יאבר. Ita reddit Hieronymus; ita etiam Genevenses: quia יאבר, Futurum Hiphil, factum videtur a Nomine אבר, vel אברה, quod pennam sonat. Sic Ierem. LI, 11. הברו חחצים, aliis tergite sagittas, tanquam a ברר: ut Es. XLIX, 2. לחץ ברור, in sagittam tersam. Sed Iosepho Kimchio πτεροῦτε τὰ βέλη, alas addite sagittis (Gall. empennès les flesches), ac si sit Imperativum τοῦ האביר: sensu non incommodo; cum in Homero quoque legas πτερόεντες ὀϊστοὶ, et in Silio,

Of the hawk God speaks thus in Iob. XXXIX, 26. [var. 29.]: Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom, spreading its wings toward the south? Grows feathers is in Hebrew יאבר. So Jerome renders it, and so also the Genevan translators, because יאבר, the future Hiphil, seems to be formed from the noun אבר or אברה, which means feather. Thus in Ierem. LI, 11, הברו חחצים is rendered by some polish the arrows, as if from ברר, as in Es. XLIX, 2, לחץ ברור, into a polished arrow. But for Joseph Kimchi it is πτεροῦτε τὰ βέλη [pteroute ta belē], fit wings to the arrows (French empennès les flesches), as if it were the imperative of האביר: not an unsuitable sense, since in Homer too you read πτερόεντες ὀϊστοὶ [pteroentes oistoi], and in Silius, ¶

— pennigeris pharetram implevisse sagittis.

— to have filled the quiver with feathered arrows. ¶

Porro in accipitre plumescere est abjectis veteribus plumis renovari; quomodo de aquila dictum. De eo vide Demetrium in Hieracosophio, capitibus, quibus titulus, Πρὸς τὸ συνεργῆσαι τὴν ἀποβολὴν τῶν πτερῶν, Quo remedio pennae celeriter abjiciantur: et Πρὸς τὸ πτεροφυῆσαι τὸν ἱέρακα τάχιστα, Ut pennae accipitris cito renoventur: et Πρὸς λειποπτεροῦντα ἱέρακα, Ad accipitrem, cui pennae desunt, et Πρὸς τροφὴν πτερῶν, Ad plumarum alimentum: et Εἰς τύφλω-σιν πτερῶν, Ad pennarum excaecationem. Ex quibus utut colligatur, humanam industriam in eo aliquid posse, ut accipitri novae plumae cito succrescant in deperditarum locum, tamen Iobo dixit Deus non immerito; An per prudentiam (sive industriam) tuam plumescit accipiter? cum media et remedia nihil possint sine Deo, et medicinae tota vis ex illo pendeat. Ne dicam, aetate Iobi accipitrariam hanc artem nondum fuisse in usu. Haec quoque optime cohaerent, An per prudentiam tuam plumescit accipiter, alis suis ad Austrum expansis? Vere enim Gregorius ad hunc locum, Domesticis accipitribus, quo melius plumescere debeant, humida ac tepentia loca requiruntur. Agrestibus vero moris est, ut III, 10 flante Austro alas expandant, quatenus eorum membra ad laxandam pennam veterem venti tepore concalescant. Cum vero ventus deest, alis contra radios solis extensis, atque percussis, tepentem sibi auram faciunt: sicque capta (lege captato) tepore, apertis poris vel veteres exsiliunt, vel novae succrescunt. Nec multo aliter Petrus Crescentiensis in libro De animalibus; Accipitres mutantur singulis annis. Debent autem poni mense Martio, vel Aprili, in gabia (cavea) magna, de industria ad hoc facta, exposita ad solem in loco calido, aut juxta muros, plagae meridionali oppositos, etc.

Further, in the hawk to grow feathers means to be renewed by casting off the old feathers, as is said of the eagle. On this see Demetrius in the Hieracosophion, in the chapters entitled Πρὸς τὸ συνεργῆσαι τὴν ἀποβολὴν τῶν πτερῶν [Pros to synergēsai tēn apobolēn tōn pterōn], By what remedy the feathers may be quickly cast; and Πρὸς τὸ πτεροφυῆσαι τὸν ἱέρακα τάχιστα [Pros to pterophyēsai ton hieraka tachista], That the hawk's feathers may be quickly renewed; and Πρὸς λειποπτεροῦντα ἱέρακα [Pros leipopterounta hieraka], For a hawk that lacks feathers; and Πρὸς τροφὴν πτερῶν [Pros trophēn pterōn], For the nourishment of the plumage; and Εἰς τύφλωσιν πτερῶν [Eis typhlōsin pterōn], For the blinding of the feathers. Although one may gather from these that human skill can do something here, so that new feathers quickly grow in a hawk in place of those lost, yet God said to Job not without reason, Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom (or skill)? For means and remedies can do nothing without God, and the whole power of medicine depends on him. Not to mention that in Job's time this art of hawking was not yet in use. These words also fit together very well: Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom, its wings spread toward the south? For Gregory rightly says on this passage: Domestic hawks, that they may grow feathers better, require moist and warm places. But wild hawks are accustomed, III, 10 when the south wind blows, to spread their wings, so that their limbs may be warmed by the mildness of the wind to loosen the old feathers. When there is no wind, they spread their wings against the rays of the sun and beat them, and so make themselves a warm air; and thus, having caught (read having sought) the warmth, with their pores opened either the old feathers drop out or new ones grow. Not very differently Petrus de Crescentiis in the book On Animals: Hawks moult every year. They should be placed in March or April in a large gabia (cage), made expressly for this purpose, exposed to the sun in a warm place, or next to walls facing the southern quarter, etc. ¶

Tamen Iobi verba nonnulli reddunt, Nunquid per prudentiam tuam volabit accipiter alis ad Austrum expansis? quomodo Aben Ezra, Kimchius et R. Levi. col. 270 Ex quorum sententia ad volatum pertinent, non ad pennarum defluvium et renovationem, ut sensus sit, „An providentiae tuae debet id imputari, quod, in-„gruente hyeme, ad australes mundi plagas avolat accipiter“, כי יבקש מקום חם, ut locum caloris quaerat, ut hic habet Aben Ezra? Nam inter aves migratorias censentur accipitrum pleraeque species: et solum buteonem b) Philosophus, solum aesalonem excipit c) Plinius, quos semper apparere tradunt. Quin ex accipitrum genere sunt (inquit Budaeus in Philologia) quos sublimivagos dicunt, qui, neglecta interdum praeda, cum disserenavit, ad meridiem plerumque apricatione delectati, in nubes licentius subvehi solent, et diu ex hominum prospectu auferri, quasi quidam emansores. In quibus notanda haec verba, ad meridiem plerumque apricatione delectati, ut quae sint Iobi verbis consona ex secunda interpretatione, Volat accipiter alis ad Austrum expansis. Itaque haec interpretatio tam potest admitti, quam prior.

Yet some render Job's words, Will the hawk fly by your wisdom, with wings spread toward the south? So Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra], Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and R. Levi. col. 270 In their view the words concern flight, not the shedding and renewal of feathers, so that the sense is: "Is it to be credited to your providence that, when winter comes on, the hawk flies away to the southern regions of the world", כי יבקש מקום חם, to seek a warm place, as Aben Ezra has it here? For most species of hawks are counted among migratory birds: the Philosopher excepts only the buzzard b), Pliny only the aesalon c), which they report are always to be seen. Indeed, of the hawk kind (says Budaeus in his Philologia) are those they call high-rangers, which, sometimes neglecting their prey, when the sky has cleared, delighting mostly in basking toward the south, are wont to soar quite freely into the clouds and to be carried for a long time out of men's sight, like truants. In which these words are to be noted, delighting mostly in basking toward the south, as agreeing with Job's words under the second interpretation, The hawk flies with wings spread toward the south. So this interpretation can be admitted as well as the former. ¶

Sed Graeci quid sibi volunt hac sua versione, Ἐκ δὲ τῆς σῆς ἐπιστήμης ἕστη-κεν ἱέραξ, ἀναπετάσας τὰς πτέρυγας ἀκίνητος, καθορῶν τὰ πρὸς νότον; An ex tua scientia stat accipiter expandens alas immotus, aspiciens versus Austrum? An pro יאבר legerunt יעמד, stat, et de suo addiderunt ἀκίνητος καθορῶν, ut sensus esset clarior? Utut sit, puto, illos id respexisse, quod, ut aquila, ita etiam accipiter solem inconniventibus oculis intueri traditur, neque inde quidquam offendi. Aelianus libri decimi capite decimo quarto, Ὁρῶσι γὰρ ἱέρακες ὀρνί-θων μόνοι ἀεὶ ἐν ταῖς ἀκτῖσι τοῦ ἡλίου ῥαδίως, καὶ ἀβασανίστως βλέποντες, καὶ δυσωπούμενοι ἥκιστα, πορείαν τε τὴν ἀνωτάτω ἴασι, καὶ αὐτοὺς ἡ θεία φλὸξ λυπεῖ οὐδέν· Soli avium accipitres solares radios facile et sine labore constanter intuentes, neque visu affecti, ad summa ita contendunt, ut eos divina flamma nullo III, 11 modo offendat. Atque ob id ipsum Aegyptii accipitrem Horo, id est, Soli, vel Apollini sacrum esse voluerunt.

But what do the Greeks mean by their version, Ἐκ δὲ τῆς σῆς ἐπιστήμης ἕστηκεν ἱέραξ, ἀναπετάσας τὰς πτέρυγας ἀκίνητος, καθορῶν τὰ πρὸς νότον; [Ek de tēs sēs epistēmēs hestēken hierax, anapetasas tas pterygas akinētos, kathorōn ta pros noton?] Does the hawk stand by your knowledge, spreading its wings motionless, looking toward the south? Did they read יעמד, stands, in place of יאבר, and add ἀκίνητος καθορῶν [akinētos kathorōn] on their own, to make the sense clearer? However that may be, I think they had in view the tradition that the hawk, like the eagle, gazes at the sun with unblinking eyes and is in no way harmed by it. Aelian, book 10, chapter 14: Ὁρῶσι γὰρ ἱέρακες ὀρνίθων μόνοι ἀεὶ ἐν ταῖς ἀκτῖσι τοῦ ἡλίου ῥαδίως, καὶ ἀβασανίστως βλέποντες, καὶ δυσωπούμενοι ἥκιστα, πορείαν τε τὴν ἀνωτάτω ἴασι, καὶ αὐτοὺς ἡ θεία φλὸξ λυπεῖ οὐδέν· [Horōsi gar hierakes orni­thōn monoi aei en tais aktisi tou hēliou rhadiōs, kai abasanistōs blepontes, kai dysōpoumenoi hēkista, poreian te tēn anōtatō iasi, kai autous hē theia phlox lypei ouden] Hawks alone among birds, gazing steadily at the rays of the sun easily and without effort, and unaffected in their sight, strive upward to the heights in such a way that the divine flame in no III, 11 way harms them. And for this very reason the Egyptians held the hawk to be sacred to Horus, that is, to the Sun or Apollo. ¶

Ioculare est, quod a R. Selomone accipitris nomine hic intelligitur מלאך דומה לרמות נץ יעמיד קושי הריח דרומית בפרישית כנפיו שלא תחריב את העולם, Angelus accipitri similis, qui alarum suarum extensione australis venti impetum sistit, ne universum orbem desolet. Id, inquam, joculare est; quia in his Iobi capitibus omnia sumi ad literam patet. Neque sub accipitrum, sed sub aquilarum typo Scriptura proponit angelos. Fatendum tamen, ad angelorum officium hoc pertinere, ut ventorum impetum sistant. Quo Iohannes alludens Apoc. VII, 1. narrat, se vidisse quatuor angelos ad quatuor terrae angulos, κρα-τοῦντας τοὺς τέσσαρας ἀνέμους τῆς γῆς, tenentes quatuor ventos terrae, ne flaret ventus super terram, etc. Quin Satanam vocat Apostolus τὸν ἄρχοντα τῆς ἐξ-ουσίας τοῦ ἀέρος, Eph. II, 2. Cujus potestatis specimen edidit Iob. I, 19.

It is laughable that R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] understands here by the name of hawk מלאך דומה לרמות נץ יעמיד קושי הריח דרומית בפרישית כנפיו שלא תחריב את העולם, an angel resembling a hawk, who by spreading his wings checks the force of the south wind, lest it lay waste the whole world. This, I say, is laughable, since it is plain that in these chapters of Job everything is to be taken literally. Nor does Scripture present angels under the figure of hawks, but under that of eagles. It must be admitted, however, that it belongs to the office of angels to check the force of the winds. Alluding to this, John in Rev 7:1 relates that he saw four angels at the four corners of the earth, κρατοῦντας τοὺς τέσσαρας ἀνέμους τῆς γῆς [kratountas tous tessaras anemous tēs gēs], holding the four winds of the earth, that the wind should not blow upon the earth, etc. Indeed, the Apostle calls Satan τὸν ἄρχοντα τῆς ἐξουσίας τοῦ ἀέρος [ton archonta tēs exousias tou aeros], Eph. II, 2. A specimen of this power he displayed in Job 1:19. ¶

Ad hunc locum quaerit Chrysostomus, Cur Deus boum, et ovium, atque aliorum hujusmodi non meminerit, ἀλλὰ τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι, sed rerum inutilium, quaeque frustra videntur esse: quasi accipiter sit avis nullius usus, et a summo opifice frustra condita; maxime, si cum domestico pecore conferatur. At plane contra Demetrius in praefatione ad Hieracosophion, tam multos de equis, bobus, canibus, mulis et canoris avibus scripsisse demiratus, μόνον, inquit, τῆς τῶν ἱεράκων ἀνατροφῆς, καὶ θεραπείας, οὐκ οἶδ᾽ ὅ, τι παθόντες, κατεμέλησαν, καὶ ταῦτα οὐκ ἔλαττον εἰς τέρψιν καὶ χρείαν τῷ ἀνθρώπων γένει, τοῖς προῤῥηθεῖσι ζώοις συνηνεγκαμένοις, τούτοις, solum accipitrum educationem, et medicinam, nescio quo pacto, neglexerunt, maxime cum ad humani generis delectationem et usum non minus conferant, quam quae diximus animalia. Si aevo Chrysostomi artem aucupandi per accipitres ignotam fuisse dixeris, et inde esse, quod accipitres et reliquas rapaces aves inutiles esse censuit, multa videbuntur posse objici. Apud Ctesiam, scriptorem vetustissimum, in col. 271 Photii Excerptis, Indi λαγώους, καὶ ἀλώπεκας θηρεύουσιν, οὐ τοῖς κυσὶν, ἀλλὰ κόραξι, καὶ ἰκτῖσι, καὶ κορώναις, καὶ ἀετοῖς, lepores et vulpes venantur, non canibus, sed corvis, milvis, cornicibus et aquilis. Quod ex eodem Ctesia pluribus explicat Aelianus Historiae libri quarti capite vigesimo sexto. Et in Mirabilibus docet Aristoteles, qua arte, circa Thraciae Amphipolim, ἐξιόντες οἱ παῖδες ἐκ τῶν κωμῶν, καὶ τῶν ἐγγὺς χωρίων, ἐπὶ θήραν τῶν ὀρνιθαρίων, συνθηρεύειν παραλαμβάνουσι τοὺς ἱέρακας, ex pagis et agris finitimis ad avicularum aucupium egredientes pueri accipitres in aucupii societatem assumant. Quod idem narrat Antigonus Historia trigesima quarta. Plinius quoque libri decimi capite octavo, et quadragesimo tertio, cujusdam Crateri meminit Monocerotis cognomine, III, 12 in Erizena (vel Troezena potius) regione Asiae, corvorum opera venantis; eo quod devehebat in sylvis eos, confidentes corniculis, humerisque: illi autem vestigabant agebantque, eo perducta consuetudine, ut exeuntem sic comitarentur et feri. Et Martialis in Apophoretis de accipitre haec habet libri decimi quarti Epigrammate 216.

On this passage Chrysostom asks why God made no mention of oxen, sheep and other such animals, ἀλλὰ τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι [alla tōn achrēstōn, kai eikē dokountōn einai], but of useless things, which seem to exist to no purpose: as if the hawk were a bird of no use, created by the supreme Maker to no purpose, especially when compared with domestic livestock. But quite to the contrary, Demetrius, in the preface to the Hieracosophion, marvelling that so many had written about horses, oxen, dogs, mules and songbirds, says: μόνον τῆς τῶν ἱεράκων ἀνατροφῆς, καὶ θεραπείας, οὐκ οἶδ᾽ ὅ, τι παθόντες, κατεμέλησαν, καὶ ταῦτα οὐκ ἔλαττον εἰς τέρψιν καὶ χρείαν τῷ ἀνθρώπων γένει, τοῖς προῤῥηθεῖσι ζώοις συνηνεγκαμένοις, τούτοις [monon tēs tōn hierakōn anatrophēs, kai therapeias, ouk oid' ho, ti pathontes, katemelēsan, kai tauta ouk elatton eis terpsin kai chreian tō anthrōpōn genei, tois prorrhētheisi zōois synēnenkamenois, toutois], only the rearing and medical care of hawks they have neglected, I know not how, especially since these contribute no less to the delight and use of the human race than the animals we have mentioned. If you say that in Chrysostom's age the art of fowling with hawks was unknown, and that this is why he judged hawks and the other birds of prey useless, much will seem open to objection. In Ctesias, a very ancient writer, in col. 271 Photius's Excerpts, the Indians λαγώους, καὶ ἀλώπεκας θηρεύουσιν, οὐ τοῖς κυσὶν, ἀλλὰ κόραξι, καὶ ἰκτῖσι, καὶ κορώναις, καὶ ἀετοῖς [lagōous, kai alōpekas thēreuousin, ou tois kysin, alla koraxi, kai iktisi, kai korōnais, kai aetois], hunt hares and foxes, not with dogs, but with ravens, kites, crows and eagles. Aelian explains this more fully from the same Ctesias in the History, book 4, chapter 26. And in the Mirabilia Aristotle teaches by what art, around Amphipolis in Thrace, ἐξιόντες οἱ παῖδες ἐκ τῶν κωμῶν, καὶ τῶν ἐγγὺς χωρίων, ἐπὶ θήραν τῶν ὀρνιθαρίων, συνθηρεύειν παραλαμβάνουσι τοὺς ἱέρακας [exiontes hoi paides ek tōn kōmōn, kai tōn engys chōriōn, epi thēran tōn ornitharion, synthēreuein paralambanousi tous hierakas], boys going out from the villages and neighbouring fields to catch small birds take hawks as partners in the fowling. Antigonus relates the same in History 34. Pliny too, book 10, chapters 8 and 43, mentions a certain Craterus, surnamed Monoceros, III, 12 in the region of Erizena (or rather Troezena) in Asia, who hunted with the help of ravens; for he carried them into the woods perched on his crests and shoulders, and they tracked and drove the game, the habit having been brought so far that even wild ones accompanied him as he went out. And Martial in the Apophoreta, book 14, Epigram 216, has this about the hawk: ¶

Praedo fuit volucrum, famulus nunc aucupis: idem
Decipit, et captas non sibi moeret aves.

He was a plunderer of birds; now he is the fowler's servant: he still deceives them, and grieves that the birds he catches are not for himself. ¶

Cum quibus conferat lector, quae in Graeca paraphrasi Oppiani Ixeuticorum se legisse tradit Gesnerus in Avibus pag. 24. accipitris ab aucupe admoti metu aviculas sub arborum frondibus se occultare, et, cum avolare non audeant, facile capi. Et haec Apuleii in prima Apologia, Memento tamen, tam ridiculum argumentum fore, desiderata ad res Venereas marina obscoena, quam si dicas marinum pectinem comando capillo quaesitum, vel aucupandis volantibus piscem accipitrem. Denique nihil est expressius his Iulii Firmici, qui sub Constantino vixit, Matheseωs libri quinti capite septimo, de iis, quorum Horoscopus Venerem habuerit in Aquario: Accipitres, falcones, astures, aquilas et aves hujuscemodi, equosque ad venandum alere studebunt. Et capite sequenti, In Virgine Mercurius si fuerit inventus, quicunque sic eum habuerint, fortes erunt, industrii, sagaces, equorum nutritores, accipitrum, falconum, caeterarumque avium, quae ad aucupia pertinent. Proinde, cum aevo Chrysostomi, qui sub quarti saeculi finem demum floruit, accipitrarium aucupium dudum videatur fuisse in multo usu, est, quod miremur, cur accipitres posuerit in censu τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι. Et responderi quidem posset, magnum virum, ut erat severus admodum virtutis custos, rigidusque satelles, potuisse hujusmodi aucupia habere pro superfluis, et iis accensere, in quibus tempus inutiliter teritur. Sed, quod puto verius, pleraque haec parum sunt ad rem, neque accipitrarii aucupii vetustatem satis adstruunt. Nam quae ex Ctesia de Indis, ex Philosopho de Thracibus, ex Plinio de Cratero afferuntur, rudimenta sunt artis, eaque solum apud barbaros, nondum apud Graecos recepta. Et apud Martialem et Oppianum accipiter aves decipit, aut percellit, nondum capit. Et in Apuleio aucupandis avibus quaeritur: non ut ipse capiat, sed ut captantibus adsit. Solus igitur superest Iulius Firmicus, qui cum eo saeculo vixerit et scripserit, ex quo supersunt tot monumenta, non possum id mirari satis, quod illo aevo nullus alius meminerit aucupii tam nobilis, atque ipsa novitate notabilis ac memorabilis. Praeterea nomen asturum, quod ibi legas, alibi nusquam extat apud veteres: ac proinde haec loca mihi valde suspecta sunt; et in iis scribae videntur aliquid de suo addidisse. Certum est tamen, hoc aucupium floruisse non diu post Chrysostomum. Nam, qui statim sequitur, Prosper, ita habet De vita contemplativa III, 13 libri tertii capite decimo septimo: Utuntur accipitribus ac saginatis canibus ad venatum. Et Sidonius in Panegyrico ad Avitum socerum, imperatorem col. 272 et consulem anno 456.

With these the reader may compare what Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], in his Birds, p. 24, reports that he read in the Greek paraphrase of Oppian's Ixeutica: that small birds, in fear of a hawk brought near by the fowler, hide themselves under the foliage of trees and, since they do not dare to fly away, are easily caught. And this passage of Apuleius in the first Apology: "Remember, however, that it would be as ridiculous an argument to say that obscene sea creatures were sought for love affairs as to say that a sea comb was sought for combing hair, or a hawk-fish for catching flying creatures." Finally, nothing is more explicit than these words of Julius Firmicus, who lived under Constantine, in book 5, chapter 7 of the Mathesis, concerning those whose horoscope has Venus in Aquarius: "They will be eager to keep hawks, falcons, goshawks, eagles and birds of this kind, and horses for hunting." And in the following chapter: "If Mercury is found in Virgo, whoever have him so will be strong, industrious, shrewd, breeders of horses, of hawks, of falcons, and of the other birds that belong to fowling." Accordingly, since in the age of Chrysostom, who flourished only at the end of the fourth century, fowling with hawks seems long to have been in wide use, we may well wonder why he placed hawks in the class τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι [tōn achrēstōn, kai eikē dokountōn einai]. It could indeed be answered that the great man, being a very strict guardian and rigid attendant of virtue, could have regarded such fowling as superfluous and counted it among the things on which time is uselessly spent. But, as I think more true, most of these passages are little to the point and do not sufficiently establish the antiquity of fowling with hawks. For what is adduced from Ctesias about the Indians, from the Philosopher about the Thracians, and from Pliny about Craterus are rudiments of the art, and these received only among barbarians, not yet among the Greeks. And in Martial and Oppian the hawk deceives or frightens birds, but does not yet catch them. And in Apuleius it is sought for catching birds: not that it may catch them itself, but that it may assist those who catch them. Only Julius Firmicus therefore remains; and since he lived and wrote in an age from which so many records survive, I cannot wonder enough that in that age no one else mentioned so noble a kind of fowling, one notable and memorable for its very novelty. Moreover, the name astures [goshawks], which you read there, occurs nowhere else among the ancients; and therefore these passages are highly suspect to me, and in them the scribes seem to have added something of their own. It is certain, however, that this fowling flourished not long after Chrysostom. For Prosper, who follows immediately after him, says this in On the Contemplative III, 13 Life, book 3, chapter 17: "They use hawks and fattened dogs for hunting." And Sidonius in the Panegyric to Avitus, his father-in-law, emperor col. 272 and consul in the year 456: ¶

Quid volucrum studium, dat quas natura rapaces
In vulgus prope cognatum? Quis doctior isto
Instituit varias per nubila jungere lites!
Alite vincit aves, celerique per aethera plausu
Hoc nulli melius pugnator militat unguis.

What of his zeal for birds, which nature makes rapacious against a crowd almost of their own kin? Who more skilfully than he has taught them to join varied combats through the clouds! With a bird he conquers birds, and with swift beating of wings through the air no taloned fighter wages war better for anyone than for him. ¶

Et ad Herodicum, hujus Aviti filium, Epistola tertia libri tertii, Tibi pila, pyrgus, accipiter, canis, equus, arcus ludo fuere. Et ad Industrium Epistola nona libri quarti, in Vectii illustris viri commendationem; Illa leviora, quod ipse, quem loquimur in equis, canibus, accipitribus instituendis, spectandis, circumferendis nulli secundus. Exinde, crescente luxu, cum in Gallia Clerici quoque canes et accipitres haberent, id vetarunt multa concilia toto sexto saeculo: ut anno 506. Agathense, canone quinquagesimo quinto; Episcopis, presbyteris, diaconibus, canes ad venandum, aut accipitres habere non liceat. Quod ipsum repetitur anno 517. in Epaonensis concilii canone quarto: et anno 585. in Matisconensis secundi Canone decimo tertio, Ubi Dei est assiduitas cantilenae, monstrum est, et dedecoris nota, canes ibi, vel accipitres habitare. Itaque graviter falluntur Leander et Pancirolla, qui Friderico Barbarossae; Et Pandulphus Collenutius, qui Henrico Sexto, imperatori, tribuunt hujus aucupii inventum.

And to Herodicus, the son of this Avitus, in Letter 3 of book 3: "Ball, dice-box, hawk, dog, horse, bow were your play." And to Industrius, Letter 9 of book 4, in commendation of the illustrious Vectius: "Those are lighter matters, that he of whom we speak is second to none in training, watching and carrying about horses, dogs and hawks." Thereafter, as luxury grew, since in Gaul the clergy too kept dogs and hawks, many councils throughout the sixth century forbade it: as in the year 506 the Council of Agde, canon 55: "Let it not be lawful for bishops, presbyters and deacons to keep dogs for hunting, or hawks." This same is repeated in the year 517 in canon 4 of the Council of Epaone, and in the year 585 in canon 13 of the second Council of Mâcon: "Where there is the constant singing of God, it is a monstrous thing and a mark of disgrace for dogs or hawks to dwell there." Therefore Leander and Pancirolla, who attribute the invention of this fowling to the emperor Frederick Barbarossa, and Pandolfo Collenuccio, who attributes it to the emperor Henry VI, are gravely mistaken. ¶
