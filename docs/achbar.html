De vulgari Mure.

On the Common Mouse.

Hebraei murem עכבר achbar appellant, voce fortasse Chaldaea magis, quaeque proprie murem agrestem sonat. Videtur enim עכבר achbar dici pro עכלבר achalbar, eliso L: ut in vocibus Babel pro Balbel, et Samuel pro שאול-מאל Saul-meel, quasi petitum a Deo dicas. Nempe Chaldaeis עכל achal est consumere; et בר bar est ager: itaque עכלבר achalbar, et per contractionem עכבר achbar est agri consumtor, seu vastator (1). Nomen agresti muri valde congruum, qui agrorum est certa pestis. Aristoteles Hist. lib. 6. cap. 37. Ἀπορεῖται δὲ καὶ ἡ τῶν ἐπιπολαζόντων μυῶν γένεσις ἐν ταῖς χώραις, καὶ ἡ φορά. πολλα-χοῦ γὰρ εἴωθε γίνεσθαι πλῆθος ἀμύθητον τῶν ἀρουραίων, ὥστε ὀλίγον λείπε-θαι τοῦ σίτου παντός. γίνεται δὲ οὕτω ταχεῖα ἡ φορὰ, ὥστε ἔνιοι τῶν μὴ μεγά-λας γεωργίας ἐργαζομένων, τῇ προτέρᾳ ἰδόντες ὅτι θερίζειν ὥρα, τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ἕωθεν ἄγοντες τοὺς θεριστὰς, καταβεβρωμένα ἅπαντα καταλαμβάνουσιν. In quaestionem etiam vocatur murium in agris emergentium generatio et proventus. II, 430 Saepe enim exoritur agrestium murium tanta vis, ut relinquatur parum frumenti. Et illorum proventus tam est subitus, ut nonnulli eorum, qui non multum agrum araverunt, cum pridie metere statuerint, postridie mane messores adducentes, se- getes omnes absumtas deprehendant. Hinc Plinius de prodigiosa murium foecunditate loquutus lib. 10. cap. 65. Itaque, inquit, desinit mirum esse, unde vis tanta messes populetur murium agrestium. Et apud Horum lib. I. cap. 47. Aegyptii, ἀφανισμὸν δηλοῦντες, μῦν ζωγραφοῦσιν, ἐπειδὴ πάντα ἐσθίων μιαίνει καὶ ἀχρη-στοῖ, destructionem significantes, murem pingunt, quia omnia consumens inquinat et deperdit.

The Hebrews call the mouse עכבר achbar, a word that is perhaps rather Chaldee, and which properly means field mouse. For עכבר achbar seems to be said for עכלבר achalbar, with the L elided, as in the words Babel for Balbel, and Samuel for שאול-מאל Saul-meel, as if you were to say asked of God. For in Chaldee עכל achal is to consume; and בר bar is field: therefore עכלבר achalbar, and by contraction עכבר achbar, is consumer or devastator of the field R1. The name fits the field mouse very well, which is a sure plague of the fields. Aristotle, Hist. book 6, ch. 37: Ἀπορεῖται δὲ καὶ ἡ τῶν ἐπιπολαζόντων μυῶν γένεσις ἐν ταῖς χώραις, καὶ ἡ φορά. πολλαχοῦ γὰρ εἴωθε γίνεσθαι πλῆθος ἀμύθητον τῶν ἀρουραίων, ὥστε ὀλίγον λείπεθαι τοῦ σίτου παντός. γίνεται δὲ οὕτω ταχεῖα ἡ φορὰ, ὥστε ἔνιοι τῶν μὴ μεγάλας γεωργίας ἐργαζομένων, τῇ προτέρᾳ ἰδόντες ὅτι θερίζειν ὥρα, τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ἕωθεν ἄγοντες τοὺς θεριστὰς, καταβεβρωμένα ἅπαντα καταλαμβάνουσιν. [Aporeitai de kai hē tōn epipolazontōn myōn genesis en tais chōrais, kai hē phora. pollachou gar eiōthe ginesthai plēthos amythēton tōn arouraiōn, hōste oligon leipethai tou sitou pantos. ginetai de houtō tacheia hē phora, hōste enioi tōn mē megalas geōrgias ergazomenōn, tē protera idontes hoti therizein hōra, tē hysteraia heōthen agontes tous theristas, katabebrōmena hapanta katalambanousin.] The generation and increase of the mice that appear in the fields is also called into question. II, 430 For often so great a multitude of field mice arises that little grain is left. And their increase is so sudden that some of those who have not ploughed much land, having decided the day before to reap, on bringing the reapers early the next morning find all the crops consumed. Hence Pliny, having spoken of the prodigious fertility of mice, book 10, ch. 65, says: And so it ceases to be a wonder whence so great a multitude of field mice ravages the harvests. And in Horus, book 1, ch. 47: the Egyptians, ἀφανισμὸν δηλοῦντες, μῦν ζωγραφοῦσιν, ἐπειδὴ πάντα ἐσθίων μιαίνει καὶ ἀχρηστοῖ [aphanismon dēlountes, myn zōgraphousin, epeidē panta esthiōn miainei kai achrēstoi], signifying destruction, paint a mouse, because in consuming everything it defiles and ruins it.

Exempla sunt passim obvia. Sic 1 Sam. V, 6. in Graeca versione scribitur de iis Philistaeis, qui captivam arcam tenebant: Μέσον τῆς χώρας αὐτῆς ἀνε-φύησαν μύες. Et in Vulgata, Et ebullierunt villae et agri in medio regionis illius, et nati sunt mures. Et cap. VI, 1. rursus in Graeca, Καὶ ἐξέζεσεν ἡ γῆ αὐτῶν col. 1018 μύας. Et terra eorum mures ebulliit. Quae quidem sunt Glossemata: cum neque Hebraea origo, neque reliquae versiones, Chaldaea, inquam, Syra et Ara- bica, tale quidquam agnoscant. Tamen res in se vera est. Nam ejusdem sexti capitis versu quinto etiam Hebraea habent, Facietis, etc. צלמי עכבריכם המשחיתים את הארץ, imagines murium vestrorum, qui vastant terram مثال الجرذان التي تسلطت علي الارض لتفسدها Imagines murium (2), qui terrae incumbunt, ut eam vastent, ut habet Arabs interpres. Proinde merito Iosephus Antiquitatum lib. 6. capite 1. Τὰ δὲ ἐπὶ τῆς χώρας μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀνελθὸν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς, κατέβλαψε, μήτε φυτῶν, μήτε καρπῶν ἀποσχόμενον· Quae autem in regione erant murium copia, e terra emergens, de- vastabat, neque stirpibus, neque fructibus parcens. Et Philastrius in Musoritis, Cum fuisset capta arca Testamenti ab Allophylis, atque ex ira Dei produxisset ter- ra sorices, ut eorum fruges deperirent. Et Maginus in Palaestina sua, Nonnul- lis in locis Palaestinae agri sunt pene deserti ob murium et soricum abundantiam, quos nisi quaedam aves devorarent, nullam sementem facere possent incolae (3).

Examples are met with everywhere. Thus at 1 Sam 5:6 in the Greek version it is written of those Philistines who held the captured ark: Μέσον τῆς χώρας αὐτῆς ἀνεφύησαν μύες [Meson tēs chōras autēs anephyēsan myes]. And in the Vulgate: And the villages and fields in the midst of that region boiled up, and mice were born. And again at ch. VI, 1. in the Greek: Καὶ ἐξέζεσεν ἡ γῆ αὐτῶν col. 1018 μύας [Kai exezesen hē gē autōn myas]. And their land boiled up with mice. These are indeed glosses, since neither the Hebrew original nor the other versions, the Chaldee, I mean, the Syriac and the Arabic, recognize anything of the kind. Yet the thing is true in itself. For in verse five of the same sixth chapter the Hebrew too has: You shall make, etc. צלמי עכבריכם המשחיתים את הארץ, images of your mice that ravage the land; مثال الجرذان التي تسلطت علي الارض لتفسدها [mithāl al-jirdhān allatī tasallaṭat ʿalī al-arḍ li-tufsidahā], images of the mice R2 that fall upon the land to ravage it, as the Arabic translator has it. Accordingly Josephus, Antiquities book 6, chapter 1, rightly says: Τὰ δὲ ἐπὶ τῆς χώρας μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀνελθὸν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς, κατέβλαψε, μήτε φυτῶν, μήτε καρπῶν ἀποσχόμενον· [Ta de epi tēs chōras myōn plēthos, anelthon apo tēs gēs, keteblapse, mēte phytōn, mēte karpōn aposchomenon] And a multitude of mice, emerging from the earth, laid waste what was in the region, sparing neither plants nor fruits. And Philastrius, on the Musorites: When the ark of the Covenant had been captured by the Allophyli, and the earth, through the wrath of God, had produced shrews, so that their crops perished. And Maginus in his Palestine: In some places of Palestine the fields are almost deserted because of the abundance of mice and shrews, so that, if certain birds did not devour them, the inhabitants could make no sowing R3.

II, 431 Quod malum ne quis putet esse Palaestinae peculiare, Strabo lib. 3. etiam in Hispania nonnunquam increbuisse memorat μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀφ᾽ οὗ καὶ λοιμι-καὶ νόσοι πολλάκις ἠκολούθησαν, murium copiam, quam saepe pestifera lues subsequuta sit. Et in Cantabria Romani, pretio dato, μυοθηροῦντες πρὸς μέτρον ἀποδειχθὲν διεσώζοντο μόλις, mures ad certam mensuram venantes vix servabantur. Nec desunt, qui de Italia idem referant. Diodorus lib. 3. Περὶ τὴν Ἰτα-λίαν μυῶν πλῆθος ἀρουραίων. ἐγγενηθὲν τοῖς πεδίοις, ἐξέβαλέ τινας τῆς πα-τρίου χώρας· Circa Italiam murium agrestium vis, ex agris emersa, quosdam e patrio solo pepulit. Quod ipsum in Aeliano est Historiae lib. 17. cap. 41. Et uterque sequitur Agatharchidem lib. 5. cap. 28. Porro Cosanos intelligi verissimile est; quia de Cosa canit Rutilius Rufus,

II, 431 Lest anyone think this evil peculiar to Eretz Yisrael, Strabo, book 3, records that in Spain too there has sometimes arisen μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀφ᾽ οὗ καὶ λοιμικαὶ νόσοι πολλάκις ἠκολούθησαν [myōn plēthos, aph᾽ hou kai loimikai nosoi pollakis ēkolouthēsan], a multitude of mice, which has often been followed by a pestilential plague. And in Cantabria the Romans, a reward being offered, μυοθηροῦντες πρὸς μέτρον ἀποδειχθὲν διεσώζοντο μόλις [myothērountes pros metron apodeichthen diesōzonto molis], hunting mice to a fixed measure, were scarcely saved. Nor are there lacking those who report the same of Italy. Diodorus, book 3: Περὶ τὴν Ἰταλίαν μυῶν πλῆθος ἀρουραίων. ἐγγενηθὲν τοῖς πεδίοις, ἐξέβαλέ τινας τῆς πατρίου χώρας· [Peri tēn Italian myōn plēthos arouraiōn. engenēthen tois pediois, exebale tinas tēs patriou chōras] Around Italy a multitude of field mice, having emerged from the fields, drove some people from their native soil. This very thing is in Aelian, History book 17, ch. 41. And both follow Agatharchides, book 5, ch. 28. Further, it is most likely that the people of Cosa are meant, because Rutilius Rufus sings of Cosa:

Dicuntur cives, quondam migrare coacti, Muribus infestos deseruisse lares.

The citizens, it is said, once forced to emigrate, abandoned their homes, infested by mice.

Thraciae quoque Abderitis, qui, propter ranarum muriumque multitudinem relicto patriae solo, sedes quaerebant, Cassander agros in ultimis Macedoniae finibus assignavit, ut testatur Trogus, lib. 15. Chalcidenses itidem narrat Heraclides in Politiis Cleonas incoluisse, ἐν τῷ Ἄθῳ ἐξαναστάντας ἐξ Ἐλυμνίου ὑπὸ μυῶν, οἳ τὰ τ᾽ ἄλλα κατήθιον αὐτῶν, καὶ τὸν σίδηρον, Elymnio in Atho monte pulsos a muribus, qui cum alia devorabant, tum ipsum ferrum. Quod de Gyaro alii referunt. Plinius libri octavi capite vigesimo nono, M. Varro auctor est, etc. ex Gyaro, Cycladum insula, incolas a muribus fugatos. Et capite quinquagesimo septimo, Theophrastus auctor est, in Cyaro insula, cum incolas fugassent, ferrum quoque rosisse eos. Hinc Antigonus Mirabilium capite vigesimo primo, Τῆς δὲ Καρυστίας καὶ τῆς Ἀνδρίας χώρας ἐστὶν πλησίον νῆσος ἡ καλουμέ-νη Γύαρος· ἐνταῦθα οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι τὸν σίδηρον. Prope Carystiam et An-driam insula est nomine Gyarus, in qua mures ferrum exedunt. Et de eadem insula Stephanus Byzantius, Ἀντίγονος δ᾽ ὁ Καρύστιός φησι, ὅτι ἐν ταύτῃ οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι σίδηρον· Antigonus Carystius autem inquit, quod in hac insula mures II, 432 ferrum comedunt (4). Nec multo aliter Suidas. Mendose igitur Aristoteles in Mirabilibus [c. 24.], Ἐν Κύπρῳ τῇ νήσῳ λέγεται τοὺς μέτας τὸν σίδηρον ἐσθίειν· In Cypro insula metas ajunt ferrum devorare. Mendose etiam in Aeliano Historiae libri quinti capite decimo quarto, Ἐν τῇ Πάρῳ τῇ νήσῳ Ἀριστοτέλης λέγει μῦς εἶναι, καὶ μέν τοι καὶ γῆν σιτεῖσθαι τὴν σιδηρῶτιν· In Paro insula dicit Aristoteles mures esse, qui ferrariam terram exedant. Utrobique enim pro Κύπρῳ, vel Πάρῳ legendum Γυάρῳ· et in priore loco μύας pro μέτας, in posteriore τὴν σιδηρῖτιν. Quid quod Troadis incolae, quos Graeci decennali bello vix subegerunt, huic malo cedere coacti, patriam deseruerunt? Plinius libri decimi col. 1019 capite sexagesimo quinto, Plurimi mures ita ad Troadem proveniunt, et jam inde fugaverunt accolas. Et in Chrysa, Mysiae urbe, Crini sacerdoti infensus Apollo ἔπεμψεν αὐτοῦ τοῖς ἄγροις μύας, οἵτινες τοὺς καρποὺς ἐλυμαίνοντο, agris ipsius mures immisit, qui fructus deperdebant. Ita Homeri vetus Scholiastes ad vocem Σμινθεῦ libro primo Iliados. Quale est, quod tradit Aelianus Historiae libri duodecimi capite quinto, Τῶν Αἰολέων καὶ τῶν Τρώων τὰ λήϊα πολλὰς μυῶν μυριάδας ἐπελθούσας ἄωρα ὑποκείρειν, καὶ ἀτελῆ τὰ θέρη τοῖς σπείρα-σιν ὑποφαίνειν· Permultas olim murium myriades Aeolorum et Trojanorum segetes invasisse, atque immaturas succidisse, iisque, qui sementem fecissent, messem infructuosam reddidisse (5).

Likewise, to the Abderites of Thrace, who had left their native soil because of the multitude of frogs and mice and were seeking a place to settle, Cassander assigned lands in the farthest borders of Macedonia, as Trogus attests, book 15. Heraclides in the Politiae likewise relates that the Chalcidians settled in Cleonae, ἐν τῷ Ἄθῳ ἐξαναστάντας ἐξ Ἐλυμνίου ὑπὸ μυῶν, οἳ τὰ τ᾽ ἄλλα κατήθιον αὐτῶν, καὶ τὸν σίδηρον [en tō Athō exanastantas ex Elymniou hypo myōn, hoi ta t' alla katēthion autōn, kai ton sidēron], having been driven out of Elymnium on Mount Athos by mice, which devoured not only other things but even iron itself. Others report the same of Gyarus. Pliny, book 8, chapter 29: M. Varro is the authority, etc., that the inhabitants of Gyarus, an island of the Cyclades, were put to flight by mice. And in chapter 57: Theophrastus is the authority that on the island of Cyarus, after they had driven out the inhabitants, they gnawed even iron. Hence Antigonus, Mirabilia, chapter 21: Τῆς δὲ Καρυστίας καὶ τῆς Ἀνδρίας χώρας ἐστὶν πλησίον νῆσος ἡ καλουμένη Γύαρος· ἐνταῦθα οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι τὸν σίδηρον [Tēs de Karystias kai tēs Andrias chōras estin plēsion nēsos hē kaloumenē Gyaros; entautha hoi myes diatrōgousi ton sidēron]. Near Carystia and Andria there is an island named Gyarus, in which mice eat iron. And of the same island Stephanus of Byzantium: Ἀντίγονος δ᾽ ὁ Καρύστιός φησι, ὅτι ἐν ταύτῃ οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι σίδηρον [Antigonos d' ho Karystios phēsi, hoti en tautē hoi myes diatrōgousi sidēron]; and Antigonus of Carystus says that on this island mice II, 432 eat iron R4. Suidas says much the same. Thus the text of Aristotle in the Mirabilia [ch. 24] is corrupt: Ἐν Κύπρῳ τῇ νήσῳ λέγεται τοὺς μέτας τὸν σίδηρον ἐσθίειν [En Kyprō tē nēsō legetai tous metas ton sidēron esthiein]; On the island of Cyprus they say that the metae devour iron. Corrupt also is Aelian, History, book 5, chapter 14: Ἐν τῇ Πάρῳ τῇ νήσῳ Ἀριστοτέλης λέγει μῦς εἶναι, καὶ μέν τοι καὶ γῆν σιτεῖσθαι τὴν σιδηρῶτιν [En tē Parō tē nēsō Aristotelēs legei mys einai, kai men toi kai gēn siteisthai tēn sidērōtin]; Aristotle says that on the island of Paros there are mice which eat iron-bearing earth. For in both places, instead of Κύπρῳ [Kyprō] or Πάρῳ [Parō], one should read Γυάρῳ [Gyarō]; and in the former passage μύας [myas] instead of μέτας [metas], in the latter τὴν σιδηρῖτιν [tēn sidēritin]. What of the fact that the inhabitants of the Troad, whom the Greeks barely subdued in a ten-year war, were forced to yield to this plague and abandoned their homeland? Pliny, book 10, col. 1019 chapter 65: Very many mice thus spring up in the Troad, and they have already driven out the inhabitants from there. And at Chryse, a city of Mysia, Apollo, angered at the priest Crinis, ἔπεμψεν αὐτοῦ τοῖς ἄγροις μύας, οἵτινες τοὺς καρποὺς ἐλυμαίνοντο [epempsen autou tois agrois myas, hoitines tous karpous elymainonto], sent mice into his fields, which destroyed the crops. So the old Scholiast on Homer, on the word Σμινθεῦ [Smintheu] in book 1 of the Iliad. Of the same kind is what Aelian relates, History, book 12, chapter 5: Τῶν Αἰολέων καὶ τῶν Τρώων τὰ λήϊα πολλὰς μυῶν μυριάδας ἐπελθούσας ἄωρα ὑποκείρειν, καὶ ἀτελῆ τὰ θέρη τοῖς σπείρασιν ὑποφαίνειν [Tōn Aioleōn kai tōn Trōōn ta lēia pollas myōn myriadas epelthousas aōra hypokeirein, kai atelē ta therē tois speirasin hypophainein]; that once very many myriads of mice invaded the crops of the Aeolians and Trojans, cut them down unripe, and made the harvest fruitless for those who had sown R5.

Ex his abunde patet, muribus, iisque agrestibus, vix indi potuisse nomen aptius, quam ab agrorum vastatione. Itaque id proprie עכבר achbar, quod confirmat usus Arabum, quibus عكبر achbar non est mus quivis, sed muris agrestis species, nempe aljarbuo mas, ut supra a) docuimus. Proinde Esa. LXVI, 17. pro Hebraeo העכבר haachbar Arabs habet اليربوع aljarbuo, quasi haec duo fere sint synonyma. Atque hoc ipsum, quod Esaias ibi Iudaeis exprobrat, nonnullos eorum porcinam edere, et העכבר haachbar, suadet, עכבר esse murem agrestem, eumque اليربوع aljarbuo, quem solum comedi tradunt Arabes. Nam μύωξος, id est, glis, quem Hieronymus hic intelligit, non II, 433 censetur ab Arabibus in genere murium (6). Fateor quidem, in Historia Hebraica Templi secundi pag. 241. cum Hierosolymitani, a Romanis obsessi, comedisse leguntur נבלות הכלבים ועכברים, cadavera canum, et mures, domesticos necessario intelligi; quia in urbe, tam arcte obsessa, agrestes non erant obvii. Et in extrema fame in cibum admittere necesse erat, quidquid offerebatur. At Esaias agit de cibis, quos Iudaei sui temporis, in Dei legem rebelles, ultro sibi deligebant, neque ulla necessitate coacti. Murem porro domesticum nemo est qui in cibum expetat, cum aliae escae suppetant. Itaque in Esaia mures illi, qui in cibum erant, agrestes fuere, non domestici. Eodem facit, quod in Samuelis loco, quem citavimus, עכברים achbarim Philistaeorum agros devastant. Illud enim est murium agrestium proprium. Certum tamen est, posteriores Hebraeos עכבר achbar accipere pro mure quovis. Et potuit ipse Moses sic usurpare, latius accepta voce. Quod ut doceret Ionathan, Hebraeum העכבר haachbar sic παραφράζει Lev. XI, 29. ועכברא אוכמא וסמוקא וחיורא, id est, Achbar niger, rufus et albus: qui tres sunt murium colores. In his albus est rarissimus, non tamen ignotus Graecis. Aelianus Historiae lib. 12. cap. 5. ubi de templo Sminthii Apollinis in Troadis urbe Hamaxito: Ὑπὸ δὲ τῷ βωμῷ φω-λεύουσι λευκοὶ μῦες, Sub altari nidificant albi mures. Hi putantur omnium salacissimi: unde in proverbium abiit, Μῦς λευκὸς, ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκρατῶν περὶ τὰ Ἀφροδίσια· Mus albus, de iis, qui ad res venereas sunt maxime incontinentes. Vide Suidam, et quid ex Philemone habeat Aelianus Historiae libri duodecimi capite decimo.

From this it is abundantly clear that no name could more aptly have been given to mice, and specifically to field mice, than one taken from the laying waste of fields. Hence עכבר achbar properly means this. This is confirmed by the usage of the Arabs, for whom عكبر [ʿkbr] achbar is not just any mouse but a species of field mouse, namely the male aljarbuo, as we showed above a). Accordingly, at Esa. LXVI, 17, for the Hebrew העכבר haachbar the Arabic has اليربوع [al-yarbūʿ] aljarbuo, as though these two were nearly synonyms. And the very thing with which Isaiah there reproaches the Jews, that some of them ate swine's flesh and העכבר haachbar, suggests that עכבר is a field mouse, and indeed that اليربوع [al-yarbūʿ] aljarbuo, which alone, the Arabs report, is eaten. For the μύωξος [myōxos], that is, the glis [dormouse], which Jerome understands here, is not II, 433 reckoned by the Arabs among the mice R6. I admit, indeed, that in the Hebrew History of the Second Temple, p. 241, where the inhabitants of Jerusalem, besieged by the Romans, are said to have eaten נבלות הכלבים ועכברים, the carcasses of dogs, and mice, house mice must necessarily be understood, since in a city so closely besieged field mice were not to be had. And in extreme famine whatever was available had to be accepted as food. But Isaiah is speaking of foods which the Jews of his time, rebels against God's law, chose for themselves of their own accord, compelled by no necessity. Moreover, there is no one who seeks out the house mouse as food when other foods are available. Therefore in Isaiah the mice that served as food were field mice, not house mice. The same point is supported by the fact that in the passage of Samuel we have cited, עכברים achbarim lay waste the fields of the Philistines. For that is characteristic of field mice. It is certain, however, that the later Hebrews take עכבר achbar for any mouse. And Moses himself may have used it so, taking the word in a broader sense. To show this, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases the Hebrew העכבר haachbar at Lev 11:29 thus: ועכברא אוכמא וסמוקא וחיורא, that is, the black, red and white achbar: which are the three colours of mice. Of these the white is very rare, yet not unknown to the Greeks. Aelian, History book 12, ch. 5, on the temple of Apollo Smintheus in Hamaxitus, a city of the Troad: Ὑπὸ δὲ τῷ βωμῷ φωλεύουσι λευκοὶ μῦες [Hypo de tō bōmō phōleuousi leukoi myes], White mice nest beneath the altar. These are thought the most lustful of all; hence the proverb, Μῦς λευκὸς, ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκρατῶν περὶ τὰ Ἀφροδίσια· [Mys leukos, epi tōn akratōn peri ta Aphrodisia] A white mouse, said of those who are most incontinent in matters of sex. See Suidas, and what Aelian cites from Philemon in History, book 12, chapter 10.

Murem cur Lex pronunciet immundum esse animal, praeter causas, quae cuivis se offerunt, hae quoque afferuntur, quod inutile et noxium est, et typus eorum, qui dicunt,

As to why the Law declares the mouse an unclean animal, besides the reasons that occur to anyone, these too are adduced: that it is useless and harmful, and a type of those who say,

Nos numerus sumus, et fruges consumere nati.

We are mere numbers, born to consume the fruits of the earth.

In Cyrillo contra Iulianum libro nono γαλῆ καὶ μῦς γράφουσί πως ἐφ᾽ ἑαυ- col. 1020 τοῖς τὰ δειλὰ, καὶ ἄνανδρα, καὶ ψοφώδη τῶν κλεπτῶν γένη, mustela et mus furum II, 434 furum referant timida, et imbellia genera, atque obstrepera. In quibus cum illud obstreperum sit plane extra rem, pro ψοφώδη legendum contendo ψοφοδεῆ, id est, ad omnem strepitum metuentia. Quo verbo utuntur Lucianus in Scytha, et Plutarchus in lib. De sanitate tuenda: sed et idem in lib. Utra anim. de chamaeleonte dicit, Ὑπὸ δέους ἄλλως τρέπεται, φύσει ψοφοδεὴς ὢν, καὶ δειλός· Prae timore in aliud mutatur, cum natura meticulosus sit et timidus. Hinc in Hesychio ψοφοδεὴς est δειλὸς, κενοφόβος, ὁ καὶ τοὺς ψόφους καὶ τὰ ἐλάχιστα φοβούμε-νος. Quod muri quam conveniat, Poeta eximius sic docet in Apologo de mure rustico et urbano Satyra 6. lib. 2.

In Cyril, Against Julian, book 9: γαλῆ καὶ μῦς γράφουσί πως ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς τὰ δειλὰ, καὶ ἄνανδρα, καὶ ψοφώδη τῶν κλεπτῶν γένη [galē kai mys graphousi pōs eph᾽ heautois ta deila, kai anandra, kai psophōdē tōn kleptōn genē], col. 1020 the weasel and the mouse represent the timid, unwarlike and noisy kinds of II, 434 thieves. Since noisy is here plainly beside the point, I maintain that for ψοφώδη [psophōdē] we should read ψοφοδεῆ [psophodeē], that is, frightened at every noise. This word is used by Lucian in the Scythian and by Plutarch in the book On Preserving Health; and the same author, in the book Utra anim., says of the chameleon: Ὑπὸ δέους ἄλλως τρέπεται, φύσει ψοφοδεὴς ὢν, καὶ δειλός· [Hypo deous allōs trepetai, physei psophodeēs ōn, kai deilos] From fear it changes into something else, being by nature fearful and timid. Hence in Hesychius ψοφοδεὴς [psophodeēs] is δειλὸς, κενοφόβος, ὁ καὶ τοὺς ψόφους καὶ τὰ ἐλάχιστα φοβούμενος [deilos, kenophobos, ho kai tous psophous kai ta elachista phoboumenos]. How well this suits the mouse the excellent Poet shows in the fable of the country mouse and the town mouse, Satire 6, book 2.

Valvarum strepitus lectis excussit utrumque: Currere per totum pavidi conclave, magisque Exanimes trepidare, simul domus alta molossis Personuit canibus. — — — —

The crash of the doors shook both from their couches: in panic they ran through the whole room, and trembled the more, half-dead, as soon as the lofty house rang with Molossian hounds. — — — —

Aliud Hebraei afferunt, nempe murinae carnis esum oblivionem inducere. Quod doctissimus Buxtorfius probat ex Avoda Zera cap. 3. (ubi tamen non reperio) et ex Beth Iacob fol. 181. ubi ad quaestionem, Cur canis dominum suum agnoscat, felis autem non agnoscat? respondetur, feles ideo esse obliviosos, quia comedunt mures, quorum caro, imo quorum arrosa, si comedantur, turbant cerebrum et memoriam. Unde elicitur haec conclusio, האוכל ממה שהעכבר אוכל משכח תלמודו האוכל עכבר עצמו לא כ״ש Comedens de eo, quod comedit mus, obliviscitur doctrinae suae: comedens ergo murem ipsum quanto magis?

The Hebrews adduce another reason, namely that eating mouse flesh induces forgetfulness. The most learned Buxtorf proves this from Avoda Zera ch. 3 (where, however, I do not find it) and from Beth Iacob fol. 181, where to the question why the dog knows its master but the cat does not, the answer is given that cats are forgetful because they eat mice, whose flesh, indeed whose leavings, if eaten, disturb the brain and memory. From this the following conclusion is drawn: האוכל ממה שהעכבר אוכל משכח תלמודו האוכל עכבר עצמו לא כ״ש One who eats of what a mouse has eaten forgets his learning: how much more, then, one who eats the mouse itself?

Sennacheribi cladem, quam angelo tribuunt Sacri Scriptores b), et Iosephus c) ex Beroso pesti, immissae divinitus, mirum, cur ad agrestes mures referant Aegyptii in Herodoto d), quos narrant τοῖσι ἐναντίοισι αὐτοῖσι ἐπιχυθέντας νυκτὸς, κατὰ μὲν φαγέειν τοὺς φαρετρεῶνας αὐτέων, κατὰ δὲ τὰ τόξα, πρὸς δὲ τῶν ἀσπίδων τὰ ὄχανα· ὥς τε τῇ ὑστεραίῃ φευγόντων σφέων, γυμνῶν ὅπλων, πεσέειν πολλούς· hostibus ipsis noctu circumfusos, illorum tum pharetras, tum arcus, tum scutorum ansas exedisse, ita, ut postridie, armis exuti, multis amissis, fugam fecerint. An huc facit, quod λοιμὸς lues, et λαμὰς mus in Hesychio, sunt voces ita vicinae, ut una potuerit pro altera usurpari, atque sic muribus tribui clades, a lue accepta? Utut sit, Cretensibus illis, qui Troadis urbem Hamaxitum condiderunt, tale quidpiam accidisse memoratur. Cum enim consultus II, 435 Apollo de loco condendae urbis, illam ibi condendam esse respondisset, ἔνθα ἂν αὐτοῖς οἱ γηγενεῖς πολεμήσωσι, ubi terrigenae iis bellum inferrent, intellexerunt, Haxamitum designari; quia, castris ibi positis, μυῶν ἄφατόν τι πλῆθος ἐφερπύσαν τά τε ὄχανα αὐτοῖς τῶν ἀσπίδων διέτραγε, καὶ τὰς τῶν τόξων νευρὰς διέφαγεν, murium immensa vis erumpens clypeorum rosit ansas, et arcuum nervos exedit. Ita Aelianus Historiae libri duodecimi capite quinto. Nec multo aliter Homeri vetus Scholiastes ad librum primum Iliados, Νυκτὸς ἐπιγενομένης, μῦες ἔκοψαν αὐτῶν τοὺς τελαμῶνας τῶν ὅπλων, Cum nox venisset, mures armorum lora ipsis absciderunt. Proinde apud Aristotelem, Rhetorices libri secundi capite vigesimo quarto, mures ideo laudat Polycrates, ὅτι ἐβοήθησαν διατραγόντες τὰς νευρὰς, quia (popularibus suis) opem tulerunt, cum hostilium arcuum nervos corroserunt. Et, apud Clementem in Protreptico, Polemo refert populos, Troadi vicinos, τοὺς ἐπιχωρίους μῦς, οὓς σμίνθους καλοῦ-σι θρησβεύειν, ὅτι τὰς νευρὰς τῶν πολεμίων διέτρωγον τῶν τόξων· colere mures illius loci, quos sminthos vocant, quoniam hostilium arcuum nervos arroserant. Quae cum iis simillima sint, quae de Assyriis referunt Aegyptii, illos Creticam hanc, aut Troicam, si mavis, historiam interpolasse putaverim.

The defeat of Sennacherib, which the sacred writers ascribe to an angel b), and Josephus c), following Berosus, to a plague sent by God, the Egyptians in Herodotus d) strangely refer to field mice. They relate that these, τοῖσι ἐναντίοισι αὐτοῖσι ἐπιχυθέντας νυκτὸς, κατὰ μὲν φαγέειν τοὺς φαρετρεῶνας αὐτέων, κατὰ δὲ τὰ τόξα, πρὸς δὲ τῶν ἀσπίδων τὰ ὄχανα· ὥς τε τῇ ὑστεραίῃ φευγόντων σφέων, γυμνῶν ὅπλων, πεσέειν πολλούς· [toisi enantioisi autoisi epichythentas nyktos, kata men phagein tous pharetreōnas autеōn, kata de ta toxa, pros de tōn aspidōn ta ochana; hōs te tē hysteraiē pheugontōn sphеōn, gymnōn hoplōn, pesеein pollous] poured over the enemy themselves by night, gnawed through their quivers, their bows, and the handles of their shields, so that the next day, stripped of their arms and with many lost, they took to flight. Is it relevant here that λοιμὸς [loimos] plague and λαμὰς [lamas] mouse in Hesychius are words so close that one could have been used for the other, and thus a defeat brought about by plague was ascribed to mice? However that may be, something of the kind is recorded to have happened to those Cretans who founded Hamaxitus, a city of the Troad. For when Apollo II, 435, consulted about where to found their city, had answered that it must be founded ἔνθα ἂν αὐτοῖς οἱ γηγενεῖς πολεμήσωσι [entha an autois hoi gēgeneis polemēsōsi], where the earth-born should make war on them, they understood that Hamaxitus was meant; because, when they had pitched camp there, μυῶν ἄφατόν τι πλῆθος ἐφερπύσαν τά τε ὄχανα αὐτοῖς τῶν ἀσπίδων διέτραγε, καὶ τὰς τῶν τόξων νευρὰς διέφαγεν [myōn aphaton ti plēthos epherpysan ta te ochana autois tōn aspidōn dietrage, kai tas tōn toxōn neuras diephagen], an immense host of mice, bursting forth, gnawed the handles of their shields and ate through the strings of their bows. So Aelian, History, book 12, chapter 5. Not very differently the old Scholiast of Homer on book 1 of the Iliad: Νυκτὸς ἐπιγενομένης, μῦες ἔκοψαν αὐτῶν τοὺς τελαμῶνας τῶν ὅπλων [Nyktos epigenomenēs, myes ekopsan autōn tous telamōnas tōn hoplōn], When night had come, mice cut through the straps of their arms. Accordingly, in Aristotle, Rhetoric, book 2, chapter 24, Polycrates praises mice ὅτι ἐβοήθησαν διατραγόντες τὰς νευρὰς [hoti eboēthēsan diatragontes tas neuras], because they brought help (to his countrymen) when they gnawed through the strings of the enemy's bows. And in Clement's Protrepticus, Polemo reports that the peoples neighbouring the Troad τοὺς ἐπιχωρίους μῦς, οὓς σμίνθους καλοῦσι θρησβεύειν, ὅτι τὰς νευρὰς τῶν πολεμίων διέτρωγον τῶν τόξων· [tous epichōrious mys, hous sminthous kalousi thrēsbeuein, hoti tas neuras tōn polemiōn dietrōgon tōn toxōn] worship the mice of that place, which they call sminthoi, because they had gnawed the strings of the enemy's bows. Since these accounts are very similar to what the Egyptians relate of the Assyrians, I would think that they worked this Cretan, or if you prefer Trojan, story into their own.
