II, 560 CHAPTER 6. ¶

De Columba Noachi, et de Columbae specie, quae in Baptismo Christi apparuit.

On Noah's Dove, and on the Form of a Dove which appeared at the Baptism of Christ. ¶

Noachus, in arca inclusus, cum ex corvo non didicisset, quod scire avebat, quousque decrevissent aquae diluvii, columbam quoque emisit, avem docilem et homini amicam, quaeque diu et procul volat, Ps. V, 7. et libenter humi pascitur, quia est ἐκ τῶν σπερμολόγων, hocque habet a natura sua insi- col. 28 tum, ut ad nidum suum redeat undequaque, etiam e locis remotissimis. His igitur de causis columba a Noacho emissa est; sed rediit non multo post, quia quietis locum non reperit: quamvis enim montium cacumina jamdudum apparuissent, tamen in terra adhuc λιμναζομένῃ, ut ait Iosephus, id est, vel adhuc stagnante, vel lacunosa saltem et uliginosa, nihil tum erat firmum. At eadem, septiduo post rursus emissa, rediit sub vesperam, ramum olivae in ore gestans. Sunt ex Hebraeis, qui ramum illum velint in monte Oliveti prope Ierusalem fuisse decerptum, aut alibi in Iudaea, quae a diluvii aquis immunis fuerit. Be-resith Rabba cap. 33. מהיכן הביאה אותו רבי אבא בר כהנא אמר משבשושין שבארץ ישראל הביאה אותו רבי לוי אמר מהר המשחה הביאה אותו דלא טפת ארעה דישראל במבולא והוא שהקבה אומר ליחזקיאל ארץ לא מטוהרה היא ולא גושמה ביום זעם, Unde attulit (columba ramum) illum? Rabbi Abba, filius Cahana (sacerdotis) dixit: Ex arbustis terrae Israelis illum attulit. R. Levi dixit, Ex monte Oliveto attulit illum, quia terra Israelis diluvio non est submersa. Prout Deus Sanctus Benedictus Ezechieli dixit capitis vigesimi secundi versu vigesimo quarto: Terra illa non mundata est, neque pluviis aspersa die indignationis. Quae sunt absurdissima, cum aquis diluvii operti fuisse legantur etiam altissimi montes, qui sub universo coelo sunt, Gen. VII, 19. Praeterea quorsum terram Chanaan, nondum sacram, Deus ab inundatione praestitisset immunem? Quorsum Noachum jussisset arcam tanto molimine fabricare? Quidni potius eo secedere jussus est cum bestiis, feris et avibus? Et, si terrae Chanaan pepercerunt aquae diluvii, quid factum est de illius terrae incolis? An hi quoque communem cladem evaserunt, et horum posteri scilicet alium parentem agnoscunt praeter Noachum, e quo totum genus humanum deduxit Moses? Et e tanta clade superstites quomodo pelli permisit Deus a maledicto Chanaane, qui non diu post diluvii tempus illorum sedem invaserit? Denique cum aquae vicinos montes tot cubitis exsuperabant, quid obstabat, quo minus terram Chanaan obruerent? Dicent Dei virtute id factum, et fateor potuisse fieri. Sed, si miracula credimus cum scripta sunt, non tamen cuivis licet ea de suo sibi fingere, quod hi homines palam faciunt. Nam Ezechielem frustra citant, qui de diluvio II, 561 ne γρῦ quidem habet. Tantum, dum Israelitis varia crimina objicit, et varias poenas commemorat, illis inflictas, vel infligendas, inter caetera terram illorum pluviis aut aspersam fuisse, aut aspersum iri negat in die indignationis. Nempe juxta Legis minas Deuter. XI, 17. Ne in vos ira excandescens Dominus claudat coelos, ut non sit pluvia, et terra non edat proventum suum. Et prophetae Amosi IV, 7. ubi Deus ita loquitur: Ego quoque prohibui a vobis imbrem, etc. Et plui super unam civitatem, et super alteram non plui: pars una compluta est, et pars, super quam non pluit, exaruit. Quod illustri exemplo patuit, cum ad Eliae verbum coelum clausum est, neque in Iudaeam descendit ros et pluvia per triennium et amplius, 1 Reg. XVII. et XVIII. At frustra disputamus, cum ipsa Mosis verba abunde doceant, olivae ramum ex iis locis fuisse decerptum, quae diluvium inundaverat. Ita enim habet Moses de Noa Genes. VIII, 8. Emisit columbam a se, ut videret, an esset levata aquis terrae superficies. Et versu 11. Et venit ad eum columba vespertino tempore. Ecce autem folium olivae decerptum in ore ejus. Et sic cognovit Noe, levatam esse aquis terrae superficiem. Id falso diceretur, si folium illud fuisset allatum ex ea terra, quam diluvium non attigisset. Nam inde potuisset afferri, etiam tum, cum aquae alibi erant maxime elatae. Unde etiam id sequitur, folium hoc non fuisse decerptum in Paradiso terrestri, ut alii Iudaeorum asserunt. Beresith Rabba ubi supra, רב ביבי אמר col. 29 נפתחו לה שערי גן עדן והביאה אותו משם, R. Bibi dixit, Portae horti Edenis illi apertae sunt, unde hoc folium attulit. Quae eadem sententia cuidam Barachiae tribuitur in Vajekra Rabba in fine cap. 31. Nempe, si Hebraeis credimus, hortus Eden evectus est supra montium summos vertices, ita, ut aquae diluvii eo usque non pertigerint. Quo eodem inclinant multi veterum. In his Lactantius, vel Venantius potius, qui de Paradiso terrestri sic habet in carmine De Phoenice,

Noah, shut up in the ark, since he had not learned from the raven what he longed to know, namely how far the waters of the flood had decreased, also sent out the dove, a docile bird and friendly to man, which flies long and far, Ps. V, 7. and gladly feeds on the ground, because it is one of the ἐκ τῶν σπερμολόγων [ek tōn spermologōn], and has this implanted in it by its nature, that it returns to its nest from anywhere, even from the most remote places. col. 28 For these reasons, then, the dove was sent out by Noah; but it returned not long after, because it found no place of rest: for although the tops of the mountains had long since appeared, yet on an earth still λιμναζομένῃ [limnazomenē], as Josephus says, that is, either still standing under water, or at least full of pools and marshy, nothing was then firm. But the same dove, sent out again seven days later, returned toward evening, carrying an olive branch in its mouth. There are some among the Hebrews who will have it that this branch was plucked on the Mount of Olives near Jerusalem, or elsewhere in Judaea, which was untouched by the waters of the flood. Bereshit Rabbah ch. 33: מהיכן הביאה אותו רבי אבא בר כהנא אמר משבשושין שבארץ ישראל הביאה אותו רבי לוי אמר מהר המשחה הביאה אותו דלא טפת ארעה דישראל במבולא והוא שהקבה אומר ליחזקיאל ארץ לא מטוהרה היא ולא גושמה ביום זעם, Whence did it (the dove) bring it (the branch)? Rabbi Abba, son of Cahana (the priest), said: It brought it from the shrubs of the land of Israel. R. Levi said: It brought it from the Mount of Olives, because the land of Israel was not submerged by the flood. As God, the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Ezekiel in chapter 22, verse 24: That land is not cleansed, nor rained upon in the day of indignation. These things are utterly absurd, since we read that even the highest mountains under the whole heaven were covered by the waters of the flood, Gen 7:19. Moreover, to what end would God have kept the land of Canaan, not yet holy, free from the inundation? To what end would he have ordered Noah to build the ark with such great labour? Why was he not rather ordered to withdraw there with the beasts, wild animals and birds? And if the waters of the flood spared the land of Canaan, what became of the inhabitants of that land? Did they too escape the common destruction, and do their descendants, forsooth, acknowledge another ancestor besides Noah, from whom Moses derived the whole human race? And how did God allow the survivors of so great a destruction to be driven out by the accursed Canaan, who not long after the time of the flood seized their dwelling place? Finally, since the waters rose so many cubits above the neighbouring mountains, what prevented them from overwhelming the land of Canaan? They will say this was done by the power of God, and I grant that it could have been done. But although we believe miracles when they are written, still no one is permitted to invent them for himself on his own authority, which these men openly do. For they cite Ezekiel in vain, who has not one γρῦ [gry] about the flood. II, 561 He only, while reproaching the Israelites with various crimes and recounting the various punishments inflicted, or to be inflicted, on them, denies among other things that their land was, or would be, rained upon in the day of indignation. That is, in keeping with the threats of the Law, Deuter. XI, 17. Lest the Lord, his wrath kindled against you, shut up the heavens, so that there be no rain, and the land yield not its produce. And of the prophet Amos 4:7, where God speaks thus: I also withheld the rain from you, etc. And I caused it to rain upon one city, and upon another I caused it not to rain: one part was rained upon, and the part on which it did not rain withered. This was shown by a famous example when, at Elijah's word, the heaven was shut, and neither dew nor rain came down upon Judaea for three years and more, 1 Reg. XVII. and XVIII. But we argue in vain, since the very words of Moses amply show that the olive branch was plucked from those places that the flood had inundated. For thus Moses says of Noah, Genes. VIII, 8. He sent forth the dove from him, to see whether the surface of the earth was relieved of the waters. And in verse 11: And the dove came to him in the evening; and behold, a plucked olive leaf in her mouth. And so Noah knew that the surface of the earth was relieved of the waters. This would be falsely said if that leaf had been brought from a land that the flood had not touched. For it could have been brought from there even at the time when the waters elsewhere were at their very highest. From this it also follows that this leaf was not plucked in the earthly Paradise, as others of the Jews assert. Bereshit Rabbah, in the place cited above: רב ביבי אמר col. 29 נפתחו לה שערי גן עדן והביאה אותו משם, R. Bibi said: The gates of the garden of Eden were opened to it, and from there it brought this leaf. The same opinion is attributed to a certain Berechiah in Vayikra Rabbah at the end of ch. 31. That is, if we believe the Hebrews, the garden of Eden was raised above the highest summits of the mountains, so that the waters of the flood did not reach that far. Many of the ancients incline to the same view. Among them is Lactantius, or rather Venantius, who speaks thus of the earthly Paradise in the poem On the Phoenix, ¶

Et cum diluvium mersisset fluctibus orbem, Deucalionaeas exsuperavit aquas.

And when the deluge had drowned the world in its waves, it rose above the Deucalionian waters. ¶

Ita etiam statuunt plerique Scholasticorum in libro 2. Sententiarum Dist. 17. Tostatus quoque et Cajetanus in Genesim, et Bellarminus libri De gratia primi hominis cap. 14. et Malvenda cap. 84. De Paradiso. Sed huic sententiae multa opponimus in peculiari opere De Paradiso terrestri. Hoc igitur olivae folium unde dicetur allatum? Ex Assyria, ut videtur, quae 2 Reg. XVII, 32. legitur esse ארץ זית יצהר, vel, ut Chaldaeus habet, ארע דזיתהא עבדין משחא, terra, cujus oleae faciunt oleum, id est, terra, oleis foecunda, iisque fertilissimis. Noae scilicet arca substiterat in montibus Ararat, seu Gordyaeorum, quibus Assyria versus Austrum vicina est. In quam coeli partem columbam, ex arca exeuntem, devolasse putaverim, invitante austri calore, et vento etiam eo impellente. Vero II, 562 enim consentaneum est, ventum illum, quem excitaverat Deus, ut aquas exsiccaret, Gen. VIII, 1. ex aquilone flavisse in austrum. Ut apud Ovidium in Deucalionis diluvio pacatus Iupiter,

This is also the view of most of the Scholastics on book 2 of the Sentences, Dist. 17; of Tostatus and Cajetan on Genesis; of Bellarmine in the book De gratia primi hominis, ch. 14; and of Malvenda, De Paradiso, ch. 84. But we raise many objections to this view in a separate work, De Paradiso terrestri. From where, then, shall we say this olive leaf was brought? From Assyria, it seems, which in 2 Reg. XVII, 32. is said to be ארץ זית יצהר, or, as the Chaldee has it, ארע דזיתהא עבדין משחא, a land whose olive trees make oil, that is, a land abounding in olive trees, and very fruitful ones. For Noah's ark had come to rest on the mountains of Ararat, or of the Gordyaeans, to which Assyria lies adjacent toward the south. I would suppose that the dove, on leaving the ark, flew off toward that quarter of the sky, drawn by the warmth of the south and also driven by the wind. For II, 562 it is probable that the wind which God had raised to dry up the waters, Gen. VIII, 1., blew from the north toward the south. So in Ovid, in the flood of Deucalion, Jupiter, appeased, ¶

Nubila disjecit, nimbisque Aquilone remotis, Et coelo terras ostendit, et aethera terris.

Scattered the clouds, and, the rain-clouds removed by the North Wind, Showed the lands to the sky, and the heavens to the lands. ¶

Haec igitur columba, versus austrum in Assyriam volans, potuit olivae folium ex monte aliquo decerpere, cujus partem aquae decedentes aperuerint.

This dove, then, flying south into Assyria, could have plucked an olive leaf from some mountain, part of which the receding waters had laid bare. ¶

Si quaeratur, quomodo sub aquis ramus olivae virentibus foliis tamdiu duraverit, duo habebo, quae respondeam. Primo, quamvis in Hieronymi versione columba Noae legatur in ore suo portasse ramum virentibus foliis, tamen in Hebraeo solum legi, eam in ore habuisse עלה זית טרף, folium oleae decerptum. Secundo, a veteribus tradi, sub aquis vivere et virere non solum oleae, sed et aliarum arborum quasdam species. Theophrastus Hist. Plant. libri quarti capite octavo, Ἐν δὲ τῇ θαλάττῃ ἐρυθρᾷ δενδρά τινα φύεται· καὶ καλοῦσιν αὐτὰ δάφνην, καὶ ἐλαίαν. Ἔστι δὲ ἡ μὲν δάφνη ὅμοια τῇ ἀρίᾳ, ἡ δὲ ἐλαία τῷ φύλ-λῳ· καρπὸν δὲ ἔχει ἡ ἐλαία παραπλήσιον δὲ ταῖς ἐλαίαις. Quae ita contrahit Plinius lib. decimi tertii cap. vigesimo quinto, In mari vero rubro (mirum est) sylvas vivere, laurum maxime, et olivam, ferentem baccas. Et Theophrastus rursus, Ἐν δὲ ταῖς νήσοις ταῖς ὑπὸ τῆς πλυμμυρίδος καταλαμβανομέναις, δένδρα μεγάλα πεφυκέναι ἡλίκα πλάτανοι· συμβαίνειν δὲ ὅθ᾽ ἡ πλημμυρὶς ἐπέλθοι, τὰ μὲν ἄλλα κατακρύπτεσθαι ὅλα, τῶν δὲ μεγίστων ὑπερέχειν τοὺς κλάδους, etc. ἔχειν δὲ τὰ δένδρα φύλλον μὲν ὅμοιον τῇ δάφνῃ, ἄνθος δὲ τοῖς οἴσοις (cum Plinio lege τοῖς ἴοις) καὶ τῷ χρώματι, καὶ τῇ ὀσμῇ, καρπὸν δ᾽ ἡλίκον ἐλαίᾳ, καὶ τοῦτον εὐώδη σφόδρα, καὶ τὰ μὲν φύλλα οὐκ ἀποβάλλειν, τὸ δὲ ἄνθος, καὶ τὸν καρπὸν ἅμα τῷ φθινοπώρῳ γίνεσθαι· τοῦ δὲ ἔαρος ἀποῤῥεῖν. Quae Plinius ita reddidit, ordine non nihil mutato, Eodem tractu insularum sylvas operit aestus, quanquam altiores platanis populisque altissimis. Folia iis lauri, flos violae odore et colore, baccae, ut oleis, et ipsae odoris jucundi, autumno nascentes, foliis nunquam deciduis. Harum minores totas integit mare; maximarum cacumina extant. Addit Theophrastus, Ἀλλὰ δὲ ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ θαλάττῃ πεφυκέναι, ἀεί-φυλλα μὲν, τὸν δὲ καρπὸν ὅμοιον ἔχειν τοῖς θέρμοις. Unde Plinius, Alias quoque arbores in alto, etc. eodem in mari visas, semper folia retinentes, fructu earum lupino simili. If it is asked how an olive branch with green leaves could have lasted so long under the waters, I shall have two things to answer. First, although in Jerome's version Noah's dove is read to have carried in her mouth a branch with green leaves, yet in the Hebrew we read only that she had in her mouth עלה זית טרף, an olive leaf plucked off. Second, the ancients report that certain species not only of the olive but of other trees too live and stay green under water. Theophrastus, Hist. Plant. book 4, chapter 8: Ἐν δὲ τῇ θαλάττῃ ἐρυθρᾷ δενδρά τινα φύεται· καὶ καλοῦσιν αὐτὰ δάφνην, καὶ ἐλαίαν. Ἔστι δὲ ἡ μὲν δάφνη ὅμοια τῇ ἀρίᾳ, ἡ δὲ ἐλαία τῷ φύλλῳ· καρπὸν δὲ ἔχει ἡ ἐλαία παραπλήσιον δὲ ταῖς ἐλαίαις [En de tē thalattē erythra dendra tina phyetai; kai kalousin auta daphnēn, kai elaian. Esti de hē men daphnē homoia tē aria, hē de elaia tō phyllō; karpon de echei hē elaia paraplēsion de tais elaiais]. Pliny condenses this in book 13, ch. 25: But in the Red Sea (it is a marvel) woods live, chiefly the laurel and the olive, bearing berries. And Theophrastus again: Ἐν δὲ ταῖς νήσοις ταῖς ὑπὸ τῆς πλυμμυρίδος καταλαμβανομέναις, δένδρα μεγάλα πεφυκέναι ἡλίκα πλάτανοι· συμβαίνειν δὲ ὅθ᾽ ἡ πλημμυρὶς ἐπέλθοι, τὰ μὲν ἄλλα κατακρύπτεσθαι ὅλα, τῶν δὲ μεγίστων ὑπερέχειν τοὺς κλάδους [En de tais nēsois tais hypo tēs plymmyridos katalambanomenais, dendra megala pephykenai hēlika platanoi; symbainein de hoth᾽ hē plēmmyris epelthoi, ta men alla katakryptesthai hola, tōn de megistōn hyperechein tous kladous], etc. ἔχειν δὲ τὰ δένδρα φύλλον μὲν ὅμοιον τῇ δάφνῃ, ἄνθος δὲ τοῖς οἴσοις [echein de ta dendra phyllon men homoion tē daphnē, anthos de tois oisois] (read with Pliny τοῖς ἴοις [tois iois]) καὶ τῷ χρώματι, καὶ τῇ ὀσμῇ, καρπὸν δ᾽ ἡλίκον ἐλαίᾳ, καὶ τοῦτον εὐώδη σφόδρα, καὶ τὰ μὲν φύλλα οὐκ ἀποβάλλειν, τὸ δὲ ἄνθος, καὶ τὸν καρπὸν ἅμα τῷ φθινοπώρῳ γίνεσθαι· τοῦ δὲ ἔαρος ἀποῤῥεῖν [kai tō chrōmati, kai tē osmē, karpon d᾽ hēlikon elaia, kai touton euōdē sphodra, kai ta men phylla ouk apoballein, to de anthos, kai ton karpon hama tō phthinopōrō ginesthai; tou de earos aporrhein]. Pliny rendered this as follows, with the order somewhat changed: In the same region the tide covers woods of islands, although they are taller than plane trees and the tallest poplars. Their leaves are those of the laurel, the flower is that of the violet in scent and colour, the berries are like olives, and they too have a pleasant scent; they grow in autumn, and the leaves never fall. The sea covers the smaller of these entirely; the tops of the largest stand out. Theophrastus adds: Ἀλλὰ δὲ ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ θαλάττῃ πεφυκέναι, ἀείφυλλα μὲν, τὸν δὲ καρπὸν ὅμοιον ἔχειν τοῖς θέρμοις [Alla de en autē tē thalattē pephykenai, aeiphylla men, ton de karpon homoion echein tois thermois]. Hence Pliny: Other trees too have been seen in the deep, etc. in the same sea, always retaining their leaves, their fruit like the lupine. Plutarch too, in the book On the Face in the Orb of the Moon: Τῆς δὲ Γεδρωσίας καὶ Τρωγλοδύτιδος ἡ καθήκει πρὸς τὸν ὠκεανὸν, ἀφόρου διὰ ξηρότητα, καὶ ἀδένδρου παντάπασιν οὔσης, ἐν τῇ παρακειμένῃ καὶ παρακεχυμένῃ col. 30 θαλάττῃ θαυμαστὰ μεγέθη φυτῶν τρέφεται, καὶ κατὰ βυθοῦ τέθηλεν· ὧν τὰ μὲν ἐλαίας, τὰ δὲ δάφνας, τὰ Ἴσιδος τρίχας καλοῦσιν [Tēs de Gedrōsias kai Trōglodytidos hē kathēkei pros ton ōkeanon, aphorou dia xērotēta, kai adendrou pantapasin ousēs, en tē parakeimenē kai parakechymenē thalattē thaumasta megethē phytōn trephetai, kai kata bythou tethēlen; hōn ta men elaias, ta de daphnas, ta Isidos trichas kalousin]. But that part of the Gedrosian and Troglodytic region which reaches to the ocean, although it is wholly II, 563 barren through dryness and lacks trees, yet in the neighbouring sea that washes it plants of unusual size grow and sprout from the depths; some of them they call olives, others laurels, others hairs of Isis. At the baptism of Christ there are some of the Fathers who hold that a real dove descended. col. 35 Thus Tertullian, in the book On the Flesh of Christ, chapter 3, writes of the Spirit descending upon Christ: It was as truly a dove as it was also spirit; nor had it destroyed a) its own substance by assuming a foreign substance. Dia touto kai ho Euangelistēs ouk eipen, hoti en physei peristeras, all' en eidei peristeras; oukoun oude meta tauta ōphthē en toutō tō schēmati, alla tote monon.] The Son of God took on the nature of a man, but the Spirit did not take on the nature of a dove. Therefore the Evangelist did not say that he (descended) in the nature of a dove, but in the form of a dove. col. 37 Nor did he appear in that form afterwards, but only then. Augustine, however, who was wholly free of this error, believed only that a real dove, created by God for this purpose, was the symbol of the presence of the Holy Spirit. This is a much lighter error. For although this opinion cannot be reconciled with the words of the Evangelists, it differs from the former in that it contains nothing unworthy of the majesty of the Holy Spirit; for, without detriment to his dignity, he could be signified as well by a real dove as by the image of a dove. Fourth, it should be noted that Augustine's argument, by which he tries to prove that this was a real dove, has little force. We believe, he says, that both those bodies, namely those of Christ and of the Holy Spirit, were real bodies. For just as it was not fitting that the Son of God should deceive men, so it was not proper that the Holy Spirit should deceive men. I grant this. But I deny that anyone saw this form of a dove except Christ himself, Matt 3:16 and Mark 1:10, and John, John 1:32 Christ was indeed baptized in the midst of the crowds, Luke 3:21, but no one has written that the dove appeared to them. And that it was seen by no one apart from Christ except John is plainly taught by John 1:32: John bore witness to him, saying, I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and he remained upon him. For what need would there have been of the testimony of so great a II, 571 man to prove what others too had seen, indeed all the crowds that then stood around Christ and John? Moreover, this form of a dove did not deceive Christ, who was free from error; nor John, who had been forewarned by God that his Spirit would descend in visible form and remain upon his Son, and so, when he saw this form of a dove descend and settle upon Christ, he knew for certain that this was taking place in a vision, as God had foretold. Accordingly he does not say that he saw a dove, but as it were a dove. Yet even supposing, without conceding it, that this φάσμα [phasma] of a dove was seen by all and was believed by most to have been a real dove, not even so would the Holy Spirit have deceived them. For he does not truly deceive who places an image before the eyes with the intention not of deceiving but of teaching. Such was this vision, in which the form of a dove was set before the eyes, not so that it should be believed to be a real dove, but so that through it the presence of the Holy Spirit, and what his powers are, might be signified. If this is held to be falsehood, the use of all visions will be abolished, in which one thing is shown to the eyes and another to the mind. And the Holy Spirit would then have deceived not only in the vision of the dove but also in that of the fiery tongues. For those tongues were not real tongues, nor was it real fire. Accordingly, just as here he is said to have descended ὡσεὶ περιστερὰ [hōsei peristera], so there the tongues are said to have been seen ὡσεὶ πυρὸς [hōsei pyros], Acts 2:3. And a little before, a sound is said to have been heard, not of wind, but as of wind, ὥσπερ φερομένης πνοῆς βιαίας [hōsper pheromenēs pnoēs biaias], so that we may know that all these things happened in a vision. ¶

Non possum non addere, Hebraeos quoque Cant. II, 12. turturis nomine, quae avis est columbini generis, Spiritum Sanctum intelligere. Unde est, quod verba Sacri textus, Vox turturis audita est in terra nostra, Chaldaeus sic παρα-φράζει· וקול רוחא דקודשא דפורקנא דאמרית לאברהם אביכון כבר שמעתון Et vocem Spiritus Sancti, id est, liberationem, quam Abrahamo, patri vestro, praedixi, Gen. XV, 14. 16. jam audivistis. Sed et haec ipsa verba Origenes refert ad Spiritum Sanctum, quem per turturem significari vult, cum occultiora revelat, et per columbam, cum ea docet, quae ad vulgi captum sunt magis accommodata. Spiritus Sanctus, inquit, quando de magnis et occultioribus sacramentis, et quae multi capere non possunt, loquitur, in turturis appellatione signatur, id est, eius avis, quae semper in montium jugis et in arborum verticibus commoratur. In vallibus autem, et his, quae ad homines usque perveniunt, columba assumitur. Haec ille in Cantici verba: Vox turturis, etc. col. 38

I cannot refrain from adding that the Hebrews too, in Song 2:12, understand the Holy Spirit by the name of the turtledove, a bird of the dove kind. Hence the words of the sacred text, The voice of the turtledove is heard in our land, are paraphrased by the Chaldee thus: וקול רוחא דקודשא דפורקנא דאמרית לאברהם אביכון כבר שמעתון And the voice of the Holy Spirit, that is, the deliverance which I foretold to Abraham, your father, Gen 15:14 16., you have already heard. Origen too refers these very words to the Holy Spirit, who, he holds, is signified by the turtledove when he reveals more hidden things, and by the dove when he teaches things better suited to the understanding of the common people. The Holy Spirit, he says, when he speaks of great and more hidden mysteries, which many cannot grasp, is designated by the name of the turtledove, that is, of that bird which always dwells on mountain ridges and in the tops of trees. But in the valleys, and in those things which reach down to men, the dove is taken. So he writes on the words of the Canticle: The voice of the turtledove, etc. col. 38 ¶
