Solvuntur quinque quaestiones de Mosis Coturnicibus. I. Quoties coturnices Israelitis Deus dederit? II. An salubris sit cibus? III. Hae coturnices unde venerint? IV. Quanta copia venerint? V. Qualis fuerit poena coturnices comedentium?

Five questions about the quails of Moses are resolved. I. How many times did God give quails to the Israelites? II. Are they wholesome food? III. From where did these quails come? IV. In what quantity did they come? V. What was the punishment of those who ate the quails? ¶

I. To the first question many, without having examined the matter sufficiently, answer that they were sent only once. Yet many things not only suggest but plainly prove that this miracle was repeated. For in Exod 16:13 quails are given together with the manna in the desert of Sin, shortly after the exodus from Egypt, on the fifteenth day of the second month, before they had come to Mount Sinai, as is clear from the beginning of the chapter. But in Num 11:6, when the people had murmured because they saw nothing but manna, God, in verse 31 of the same chapter, grants them quails at Kibroth-thaava, where they finally arrived after leaving the desert of Sinai, Num 33:16, and that on the third day, Num 10:33. They had departed from Sinai, moreover, in the second year, on the twentieth day of the second month, Num 10:11. So this miracle must have happened a year later than the former. Furthermore, the first quails were granted by God for only one day, the later ones for a whole month. Accordingly the former covered only the camp; the latter were also outside the camp, scattered on this side and that for a day's journey. And it was at the eating of these, not of the former, that God, gravely angered, struck the people with a great plague. Therefore Josephus relates the two stories in different places: the former in chapter 1, the latter in chapter 13 of book 3 of the Antiquities. So also Jerome, Epistle 127 to Fabiola, at stations 10 and 13. Their objection, that there is no reason why God should have been angry at these rather than at the former, is refuted at the end of the chapter. Now, if you ask how Moses in Num 11:21 could doubt whether flesh could be found sufficient for so many thousands, if he had already seen a similar miracle, Exod 16:13, I answer that the earlier miracle was not in every respect like the later. For in the earlier one, as we have already observed, God had fed this people with flesh for only one day. But here he promises to do the same for a whole month, which seemed to Moses far greater. Therefore he raises this very point against God: The people, he says, among whom I am, are six hundred thousand footmen; and you say that you will give them flesh for a whole month. II, 657 Besides, who does not know that Moses, although at Raphidim he had drawn water from the struck rock, Exod 17:1 etc., nevertheless doubted when he was ordered to repeat the same miracle in the desert of Sin and at Kades, Num 10:1 etc.? So let no one be surprised that the same happened to him when God promised the people flesh which he had already given before elsewhere. ¶

II. On the second question, writers observe that quails feed on hellebore, or, what is the same, veratrum. Aristotle, On Plants, book 1, chapter 5: Ὁ ἐλλέβορος ἀνθρώποις μὲν δηλητήριον, τροφὴ δὲ τοῖς ὄρτυξι· [Ho elleboros anthrōpois men dēlētērion, trophē de tois ortyxi]; Hellebore is poison to men, but food to quails. Galen, to Piso: Ὁ ἐλλέβορος τῶν ὀρτυγίων ἐστὶ τροφή· [Ho elleboros tōn ortygiōn esti trophē]; Hellebore is the food of quails. And On Foods, book 2, ch. 6: Καὶ μὲν τὸν ἐλλέβορον οἱ ὄρτυγες, καὶ οἱ ψᾶρες τὸ κώνειον ἐσθίοντες οὐδὲν βλάπτονται· [Kai men ton elleboron hoi ortyges, kai hoi psares to kōneion esthiontes ouden blaptontai]; Quails are not harmed at all by eating hellebore, nor starlings by eating hemlock. And On Temperaments, book 3, ch. 4: Τοῖς μὲν ὄρτυξιν ἐλλέβορος τροφὴ, τοῖς δὲ ἀνθρώποις φάρμακον· [Tois men ortyxin elleboros trophē, tois de anthrōpois pharmakon]; To quails hellebore is food, but to men poison. So also Alexander of Aphrodisias in the Preface to book 1 of the Problems, and Basil, Ambrose and Eustathius on the Hexaemeron. To the same point belong these lines of Lucretius, book 4, verse 641: ¶

Praeterea nobis veratrum est acre venenum,
At capreis adipes et coturnicibus auget.

Moreover, veratrum is a sharp poison to us, but it fattens goats and quails. ¶

For it is the Hiphil of the verb נסע, which means to set out, whether from a place or to a place. So in the Hiphil הסיע is to cause someone to set out either from a place or to a place. Accordingly יסע there is not to take away but to bring in and to send in. For as a thing is taken away from a place, so it is brought into a place. Fourth, in this whole Psalm, as often elsewhere in the poetic books of the Hebrews, the two hemistichs are generally parallel and explain each other. From this it follows that יסע col. 102 II, 663 here is roughly the same as וינהג, and he brought in, in the second hemistich, in which we read that God by his power brought in the south wind. Fifth, if the matter is decided by authority, against the Greeks and Jerome we set the Chaldee and the Syriac, and both Arabic versions R4, and the Hebrews, as many as there are, and the more recent interpreters, as many as I have been able to see. For not one of them occurs for whom יסע here means to take away. Rather, all understand that the east wind was stirred up and sent in by God. Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae]: He stirred up the east wind in the heaven; Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot]: He made the east wind set out in the heavens; Felix Pratensis [=Felix of Prato, Latin Psalter from the Hebrew (1515)]: He moved the east wind in the heavens; Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)]: The east wind having been driven through the heaven. Add to these all the vernacular versions. Let this therefore stand: that God, according to David's meaning, moved this wind, not removed it; brought it in, not took it away. But for the matter to be understood, it must be noted that the Israelites were then in the desert of Paran, having already advanced a three days' journey beyond Mount Sinai, Num 10:12 33., and had the farthest gulf of the Arabian sea to the south; and therefore the wind that carried the quails from the Red Sea to their camp is rightly called by David תימן Theman, that is, southern. But why is the same wind called קדים kadim, that is, eastern? Aben Ezra and Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] answer that it is called by both names because it was מזרחי דרומי, that is, partly eastern, partly southern, which you might call in one word Euro-Notus R5. This is utterly false. For that wind was in no way eastern, as those acquainted with the region know. Yet it is called קדים Kadim, and I shall now say why. Although four cardinal winds are established, which are in turn subdivided into many more, nevertheless the learned b) observe that in the ancient books of the philosophers and physicians, and especially in Aristotle and Theophrastus, to designate the conditions of weather and air produced by the winds, only τὰ βόρεια [ta boreia] and τὰ νότια [ta notia] are named, never τὰ ἑσπέρια [ta hesperia] or τὰ ἑῷα [ta heōa]. Hence Aristotle, Meteorol. book 2, ch. 6, after naming eight principal winds with their side-winds, says: Ὅλως δὲ τὰ μὲν βόρεια τούτων καλεῖται, τὰ δὲ νότια [Holōs de ta men boreia toutōn kaleitai, ta de notia]. In general, of these some are called northern, others southern. That is, the western winds, as colder, are assigned to the north wind, and the eastern ones to the south wind, because they are warmer. He adds this in these words: Προστίθηται δὲ τὰ μὲν ζεφυρικὰ τῷ βορέᾳ, ψυχρότερα γὰρ, διὰ τὸ ἀπὸ δυσμῶν πνεῖν· τῷ δὲ νότῳ ἀπηλιωτικὰ, θερμότερα γὰρ, τῷ (read διὰ τὸ) ἀπὸ ἀνατολῆς πνεῖν [Prostithētai de ta men zephyrika tō borea, psychrotera gar, dia to apo dysmōn pnein; tō de notō apēliōtika, thermotera gar, tō (dia to) apo anatolēs pnein]. The eastern wind is warmer than the western for this reason, ὅτι πλείω χρόνον ὑπὸ τὸν ἥλιόν ἐστι [hoti pleiō chronon hypo ton hēlion esti], because it is longer under the sun. That is why II, 664 in Greek it is called ἀπηλιώτης [apēliōtēs], and in Latin Subsolanus. Accordingly, in the Politics, book 4, ch. 3, the same Aristotle contends that all forms of commonwealth are to be referred either to democracy or to oligarchy, καθάπερ ἐπὶ τῶν πνευμάτων λέγεται τὰ μὲν βόρεια, τὰ δὲ νότια, τάδ' ἄλλα τούτων παρεκβάσεις [kathaper epi tōn pneumatōn legetai ta men boreia, ta de notia, tad' alla toutōn parekbaseis], just as among the winds some are called northern, others southern, and the rest are deviations from these. Hence it is, he says, that they say τὸν μὲν ζέφυρον τοῦ βορέου, τοῦ δὲ νότου τὸν εὖρον [ton men zephyron tou boreou, tou de notou ton euron], that the zephyr belongs to the north wind, and the east wind to the south wind. To this belong the verses of Lucilius in the book on Cotys, king of the Thracians, which, badly corrupted in Nonius under the word Demagis, Salmasius [=Claude Saumaise (1588–1653), Plinianae exercitationes] restores thus: ¶

Rex Cotys ille duos ventos, austrum atque aquilonem, Novisse ajebat solos se demagis, istos Ex nimbo Austellos nec nosse, nec esse putare.

King Cotys used to say that he knew only two winds, the south wind and the north wind, and moreover, that those little south winds from the storm-cloud he neither knew nor thought existed. ¶

Ex his cum Eurum pateat ad australem ventum referri, potuere apud Davidem קדים Kadim, et תימן Theman, id est, Eurus et auster, haberi pro synonymis, ita, ut hoc loco Kadim non sit aliud, quam sequens Theman. Neque vero hic solum, sed et passim alibi, vento Kadim Sacri Scriptores ea tribuunt, quae potius venti australis videntur esse propria. Is enim fruges exurit, Gen. XLI, 6. Vites quoque et fructus arborum, Ezech. XVII, 10. et XIX, 12. Maris et fontium aquas exsiccat, Exod. XIV, 21. et Os. XIII, 15. Vi sua naves conterit, col. 103 Psal. XLVIII, 8. et Ezech. XXVII, 26. Omnia denique subvertit et dispergit, Iob. XXVII, 21. et Ierem. XVIII, 17. Proinde vocatur ventus durus, Es. XXVII, 8. et חרשית, strepitu suo homines surdos faciens, Ion. IV, 8. Neque a Graecis Εὖρος uspiam, sed Νότος vocatur, aut Καύσων, id est, auster, et ventus urens. Et Dei verba Deut. XXXII, 22. Ignis succensus est in furore meo, et ardebit usque ad imos inferos, consumetque terram et proventum ejus, Ionathan et Onkelos ad hunc eundem ventum referunt hac sua periphrasi, קדום תקיף כאשא, Ventus orientalis validus ut ignis a me in furore exiit, etc. A Theodoreto denique Eurus et Notus conferuntur ut venti similes, et prope iidem, cum scribit Quaestione octava in Genesin, Εὔρου ἢ Νότου πνέοντος, τὸ πεπηγὸς ὕδωρ διαλύεσθαι, Euro, aut noto flante, aquam congelatam dissolvi. Quamvis igitur רוח הקדים, proprie et praecise loquendo, ventum orientalem sonet (quod et vocis origo docet, et locus Ezech. XLII, 16. etc. ubi a vento Kadim Borealis, Australis et Occidentalis distinguuntur) tamen quia venti Kadim et Theman, id est, Eurus et Auster, quaedam habent inter se communia, Hebraeos puto has voces promiscue usurpasse. Atque inde esse, quod קדים Kadim passim a Graecis Νότος redditur: ut Exod. X, 13. et XIV, 21. Num. XXXIV, 15. Iob. XXXVIII, 24. Ezech. XXVII, 26. atque in illo ipso Psalmi loco, in quo versamur, ubi Kadim et Theman explicant Νότον καὶ Λίβα. Quocirca utraque voce II, 665 idem ventus videtur significari, Auster nimirum, aut Africus (6), qui ex intima Aegypto coturnices Israelitis advexerit (7).

Since it is clear from this that the east wind is related to the south wind, in David קדים Kadim and תימן Theman, that is, the east wind and the south wind, could be taken as synonyms, so that in this passage Kadim is nothing other than the following Theman. And not only here but frequently elsewhere the sacred writers attribute to the wind Kadim things that seem rather to be proper to the south wind. For it scorches the crops, Gen 41:6. It also scorches vines and the fruits of trees, Ezek 17:10 and XIX, 12. It dries up the waters of the sea and of springs, Exod 14:21 and Hos 13:15. By its force it shatters ships, col. 103 Ps 48:8 and Ezek 27:26. Finally, it overturns and scatters everything, Job 27:21 and Jer 18:17. Accordingly it is called a harsh wind, Isa 27:8 and חרשית, making people deaf with its roar, Jonah 4:8. Nor is it anywhere called Εὖρος [Euros] by the Greeks, but Νότος [Notos] or Καύσων [Kausōn], that is, the south wind and the burning wind. And God's words in Deut 32:22. A fire is kindled in my anger, and it shall burn to the lowest hell, and shall consume the earth and its produce, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] refer to this same wind by this paraphrase of theirs, קדום תקיף כאשא, An east wind, strong as fire, has gone out from me in anger, etc. Finally, Theodoret compares the east wind and the south wind as similar winds, almost the same, when he writes in Question 8 on Genesis, Εὔρου ἢ Νότου πνέοντος, τὸ πεπηγὸς ὕδωρ διαλύεσθαι [Eurou ē Notou pneontos, to pepēgos hydōr dialyesthai], When the east wind or the south wind blows, frozen water is dissolved. Therefore, although רוח הקדים, properly and strictly speaking, means the east wind (as both the origin of the word shows and the passage Ezek 42:16 etc., where the north, south and west winds are distinguished from the wind Kadim), nevertheless, because the winds Kadim and Theman, that is, the east wind and the south wind, have certain things in common, I think the Hebrews used these words interchangeably. And hence it is that קדים Kadim is frequently rendered Νότος [Notos] by the Greeks: as in Exod 10:13 and XIV, 21. Num 34:15. Job 38:24. Ezek 27:26 and in that very passage of the Psalm with which we are dealing, where they render Kadim and Theman as Νότον καὶ Λίβα [Noton kai Liba]. Therefore the same wind seems to be meant by both words II, 665, namely the south wind or the south-west wind R6, which brought the quails to the Israelites from the interior of Egypt R7. ¶

And Solinus: "They never set out with the south wind, for they fear the force of its swollen blast. To the same effect is the fact that the winds called Ornithiae, as if they brought birds, are described as blowing from the north, or at least from the west. Aristotle, in the book De mundo: Οἱ δὲ ὀρνιθίαι καλούμενοι, ἐαρινοί τινες ὄντες ἄνεμοι, βόρειοί εἰσι τῷ γένει [Hoi de ornithiai kaloumenoi, earinoi tines ontes anemoi, boreioi eisi tō genei]. These words, indeed, are taken not so much from this passage as from the Meteorologica, book 2, chapter 5, where the Ornithiae are said to be Ἐτησίαι ἀσθενεῖς· ἐλάττους δὲ, καὶ ὀψιαίτεροι τῶν Ἐτησίων πνέοντες [Etēsiai astheneis; elattous de, kai opsiaiteroi tōn Etēsiōn pneontes], "weak Etesians, but smaller and later than the true Etesians." In these words the Ornithiae are assigned to the Etesians. The Etesians, however, blow either from the western quarter or from the northern region. II, 667 because Moses (Num 11:31) and David (Ps 78:28) write that they were not only carried by the south wind but also thrown down onto the ground. And indeed it is utterly false that they never fly with the south wind. For they are migratory birds: just as the north winds carry them away from us in autumn, so the south winds bring them back to us in spring. Aristotle, in the place cited above: Ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ οἱ ὄρτυγες (ἀπαίρουσι)· ἐὰν μή τινες ὑπολειφθῶσι ἐν εὐηλίοις χωρίοις· [Homoiōs de kai hoi ortyges (apairousi)· ean mē tines hypoleiphthōsi en euēliois chōriois·] Likewise the quails also (depart), unless a few have remained in sunny places. Varro, Rustic. book 3, ch. 5: Thrushes fly every year across the sea into Italy around the autumn equinox, and fly back to the same place at the spring equinox. And at another time turtledoves and quails, in enormous numbers. Pliny: Quails also always arrive before the cranes. Solinus: They have their times of arrival when summer has been driven off; or rather winter, as the great commentator on Solinus shows at length. Aloysius Mundella in his Letters: To the coastal region around Pesaro a huge number of quails usually arrives, and they are caught with a thousand snares; but their arrival has been observed chiefly toward the end of spring. Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], Observat. book 2, ch. 17, on the voyage from Rhodes to Alexandria: Very many quails, flying from the north toward the south, were caught on our ship. From this I am firmly convinced that quails are migratory birds. For earlier, when I was sailing in spring from Zacynthus to Euboea, I had observed others flying in the opposite direction, from the south to the north. Gesner: Quails come to us, as I hear, at about the middle of April, and leave when the first frost sets in. From these it is clear not only that quails change their country, but also that in mid-spring they migrate from the south to the north. I note this here because at precisely that time the south wind brought quails into the camp of the Israelites, namely on the 24th day of the second month. For on the twentieth they had departed from the wilderness of Sinai; and after a three days' journey, when they had demanded meat with much murmuring, God promised that on the next day he would give it for a whole month, and he sent quails. Et immani numero fuisse, probat Moses duobus argumentis: uno, quod non per castra solum, sed et undequaque dispersae erant, ad iter unius diei, et ad al-titudinem duorum cubitorum supra terram extabant. Alterum, quod in iis aucupandis et colligendis toto biduo sic laborarunt tot hominum myriades, ut, qui minimum collegerat illarum, congesserit decem coros [Numer. XI, 31. 32.]. Haec igitur coturnicum nubes circumquaque patuit minimum quadraginta millia, (nam diurnum iter minimum est viginti millium) et duobus cubitis supra terram eminebat. Quod trifariam potest intelligi. 1. Ut sonet, coturnices in toto illo spatio ita fuisse coacervatas, ut ubique ad duos cubitos eminuerint. 2. Ut coturnicum bicubitales acervos significet hinc ita fuisse dispersos, ut vacua subinde spatia remanserint, per quae populus iter faceret, cibos, a Deo oblatos, collecturus. 3. Ut in ipso volatu significentur ita se demisisse, ut a terra non abfuerint plusquam duobus cubitis, quasi captantium manibus ultro se offerrent. Quod sequutus Hieronymus sacra verba ita reddidit: Volabantque in aëre duobus cubitis altitudine super terram. Et Ionathan, וכרום תרתין אמין הוה פרח על אנפי ארעא והוו מהלכין בהון עד פרתהון מטול דלא ילעון בזמן מכנשהון יתהון, Et ad duorum cubitorum altitudinem volabant super faciem terrae, et ibant inter illos (Israelitas) usque ad umbilicum, ne laborarent in iis colligendis. Et R. Selomo in Notis, פורחות בגובה עד שהן כנגד לבו של אדם כדי שלא יהא טורח באסיפתן לא להגביה ולא לשחות, Volabant ad humani pectoris altitudinem, ne quisquam in earum collectione se attollendo aut deprimendo fatigaret. Neque dubito, quin Philo id ipsum in animo habuerit, cum describit ὀρτυγομη-τρῶν νέφος τὰς πτήσεις προσγειοτάτας ποιούμενον εἰς τὸ εὔθηρον, coturnicum nubem terrae proxime volantem, ut facile esset aucupium. Quod coturnicis naturae non male convenit, quae cum ad nos venit, inquit Plinius, terrestris potius quam sublimis est. Itaque ab hac interpretatione non sum alienus, quamvis secundam praeferam; quia David diserte dicit, coturnices cecidisse in medio castrorum, et Moses, fusas fuisse super terram. Quibus verbis describi videntur humi plane jacentes, potius quam in infimo aëre volitantes. Eodem pertinet pluendi vox. Neque enim pluere dicuntur, nisi quae in terram usque prolabuntur. Praeterea volantes vix potuissent in biduo tam multae capi, ut quisque habuisset II, 669 decem coros (9). Corus enim Hebraeorum maxima fuit mensura, continens decem batos, ut batus tria sata, satum sex cabos, cabus quatuor logos, logus sex ova. Hebraice חומר chomer dicitur, quod ad חמור chamor, id est, asinum, revocant nonnulli Hebraeorum, quasi fuerit onus asini. Sed alii verius corum contendunt duplo fuisse graviorem, adeoque camelum oneravisse. Itaque verissimile est, in spatio, quod definit Moses, id est, per iter diurnum circa castra, non ubique, sed passim coturnicum acervos jacuisse (10), alicubi super terram ad duos cubitos eminentes. Quas longo volatu fessas vel divina vis, vel angeli a Deo missi, vel venti vehementiores hinc inde disjecerant, ut huic populo pro cibo essent.

That they were immense in number, Moses proves by two arguments. The first is that they were scattered not only through the camp but on every side, for a day's journey, and stood two cubits high above the ground. The second is that so many myriads of men laboured for two whole days in catching and gathering them that he who had gathered the least of them heaped up ten cors [Numer. XI, 31. 32.]. This cloud of quails, then, extended on every side at least forty miles (for a day's journey is at least twenty miles), and rose two cubits above the ground. This can be understood in three ways. 1. That it means the quails were so heaped up over that whole space that everywhere they rose to two cubits. 2. That it signifies heaps of quails two cubits high, scattered here and there in such a way that empty spaces were left between them, through which the people might make their way to gather the food offered by God. 3. That it signifies that in their very flight they came down so low that they were no more than two cubits from the ground, as though offering themselves of their own accord to the hands of those catching them. Following this, Jerome rendered the sacred words thus: And they flew in the air at a height of two cubits above the ground. And Jonathan: וכרום תרתין אמין הוה פרח על אנפי ארעא והוו מהלכין בהון עד פרתהון מטול דלא ילעון בזמן מכנשהון יתהון, And they flew at a height of two cubits over the face of the ground, and (the Israelites) walked among them up to the navel, so that they might not toil in gathering them. And R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in his Notes: פורחות בגובה עד שהן כנגד לבו של אדם כדי שלא יהא טורח באסיפתן לא להגביה ולא לשחות, They flew at the height of a man's breast, so that no one in gathering them should tire himself by stretching up or bending down. Nor do I doubt that Philo had this very thing in mind when he describes ὀρτυγομητρῶν νέφος τὰς πτήσεις προσγειοτάτας ποιούμενον εἰς τὸ εὔθηρον [ortygomētrōn nephos tas ptēseis prosgeiotatas poioumenon eis to euthēron], a cloud of quails flying very close to the ground, so that catching them was easy. This fits the nature of the quail not badly, which, when it comes to us, says Pliny, is a ground bird rather than a high-flying one. I am therefore not averse to this interpretation, although I prefer the second, because David says expressly that the quails fell in the midst of the camp, and Moses that they were spread out upon the ground. These words seem to describe birds lying flat on the ground rather than fluttering in the lowest air. The word raining points the same way, for only things that fall all the way to the ground are said to rain. Moreover, had they been flying, hardly so many could have been caught in two days that each man had II, 669 ten cors R9. For the cor was the largest measure of the Hebrews, containing ten baths, as the bath contained three seahs, the seah six kabs, the kab four logs, the log six eggs. In Hebrew it is called חומר chomer, which some of the Hebrews derive from חמור chamor, that is, ass, as though it had been an ass's load. But others more correctly contend that the cor was twice as heavy, and so would have loaded a camel. It is therefore most probable that in the space Moses defines, that is, a day's journey around the camp, heaps of quails lay not everywhere but here and there R10, in places rising two cubits above the ground. These, wearied by their long flight, either divine power, or angels sent by God, or rather violent winds had scattered hither and thither, to serve this people as food. ¶

Fateor interim, me ultima illa ratione, a decem coris sumpta, quos ex illo populo quisque collegisse creditur, non magnopere niti. Neque enim opus fuit, ut singuli coturnicum decem coros colligerent. Deus huic populo carnes in mensem promiserat, cui promisso fecisset satis, si unicuique pro decem coris solum dedisset cori trientem. Quod probare est in proclivi. Singulis Israelitis in diurnum cibum assignatur gomor Mannae Exod. XVI, 16. id est, ephae decima pars ibidem versu 36. [al. 35.]. Et Epha est decima pars cori Ezech. XLV, col. 106 11. Itaque in coro sunt centum mensurae gomor. Et, si gomor in diem sufficit, corus sufficit in centum dies, id est, in tres menses et amplius. Proinde, si singuli Israelitae coturnicum colligerunt decem coros, collegerunt plus trigesies quam Deus promiserat. Sed cum dicit Moses, etiam illum, qui minimum collegerat, decem coros collegisse, fortasse nonnisi ad familiarum capita hoc referendum est: neque in illo censu numerantur uxores, liberi, servi. Vel, quod puto verius, ibi loci עשרה חמרים non sunt decem cori, sed decem acervi. Nam et חמר chomer acervum sonat. Ita Habac. III, 15. חמר מים רבים, est acervus aquarum multarum. Et eodem sensu de ranis dicitur Exod. VIII, 10. ויצברו אותם חמרים חמרים, Et congregaverunt eos in acervos, acervos, id est, in multos acervos. Et Iudic. XV, 17. [al. 16.] חמרתים in Duali sunt duo acervi. Sed et hoc ipso loco עשרה חמרים; Onkelos reddit עשרא דגורים, et Arabs Parisiis editus عشرة انابير, et Erpenianus عشرة اجداس decem acervos. Quomodo si sumpseris, Moses non praecise definiverit, quam coturnicum mensuram II, 670 unusquisque collegerit. Sed solum decem acervos collectos fuisse dixerit ab eo, qui minimum. Decem acervos, id est, plurimos, phrasi Hebraeis familiari, qua decem pro multis passim usurpant. Sic Gen. XXXI, 7. Mutavit mercedem meam decies. Num. XIV, 22. Tentarunt me jam decies. Nehem. IV, 6. Iam decies nobis dixerunt. Iob. XIX, 3. Iam decies ignominia me affecistis, id est, multoties. Levit. XXVI, 26. Decem mulieres panem vestrum coquent, id est, multae. 1 Sam. I, 8. Annon melior sum tibi, quam decem filii? Eccles. VII, 19. Sapientia robur confert sapienti, plus quam decem principes, qui sunt in eadem civitate. Zachar. VIII, 23. Decem homines apprehendent alam hominis Iudaei, id est, plurimi. Ita igitur decem coturnicum acervos pro pluribus dixerit Moses (11). Et sic vertendum potius quam decem choros, praeter ea, quae jam diximus, etiam haec ratio suadet, quod כור, corus, seu חומר chomer, granorum est mensura, non carnium (12). Haec observatio non est nihili. Quia, si singuli Israelitae decem coturnicum coros collegisse dicantur, illarum numerus erit immanis, et superabit omnem fidem. Cornelius a Lapide, qui supputare conatus est in Commentariis in hunc locum, ita subducit calculos, ut probet, Israelitas collegisse circiter duodecim millia millionum coturnicum. At nos, pro decem coris, singulos statuimus ne tertiam quidem cori partem collegisse, aut absumpsisse. Non, ut minuamus Dei virtutem, sed ne fidei vim faciamus, ab illa plura extorquentes, quam Deus nobis in verbo suo revelavit (13).

Meanwhile I confess that I do not rely much on that last argument, taken from the ten cors which each of that people is believed to have gathered. For there was no need for each person to gather ten cors of quails. God had promised this people meat for a month, and he would have fulfilled this promise if, instead of ten cors, he had given each person only a third of a cor. This is easy to prove. To each of the Israelites an omer of manna is assigned as daily food, Exod 16:16, that is, the tenth part of an ephah, in the same place, verse 36 [var. 35]. And an ephah is the tenth part of a cor, Ezech. XLV, col. 106 11. Therefore a cor contains a hundred omer measures. And if an omer suffices for a day, a cor suffices for a hundred days, that is, for three months and more. Accordingly, if each of the Israelites gathered ten cors of quails, they gathered more than thirty times what God had promised. But when Moses says that even he who had gathered least gathered ten cors, perhaps this is to be referred only to the heads of families; and wives, children and slaves are not counted in that census. Or, what I think truer, in that place עשרה חמרים are not ten cors but ten heaps. For חמר chomer also means heap. Thus in Hab 3:15. חמר מים רבים is a heap of many waters. And in the same sense it is said of the frogs in Exod 8:10. ויצברו אותם חמרים חמרים, And they gathered them into heaps, heaps, that is, into many heaps. And in Judg 15:17 [var. 16.] חמרתים in the dual is two heaps. But also in this very passage עשרה חמרים, Onkelos renders עשרא דגורים, and the Arabic edited at Paris عشرة انابير [ʿashrat anābīr], and Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] عشرة اجداس [ʿashrat ajdās], ten heaps. If you take it this way, Moses will not have defined precisely what measure of quails II, 670 each person gathered, but will only have said that ten heaps were gathered by him who gathered least. Ten heaps, that is, very many, by a phrase familiar to the Hebrews, who everywhere use ten for many. Thus Gen. XXXI, 7: He has changed my wages ten times. Num 14:22: They have tempted me now ten times. Neh 4:6: They have told us now ten times. Job 19:3: These ten times you have reproached me, that is, many times. Lev 26:26: Ten women shall bake your bread, that is, many. 1 Sam 1:8: Am I not better to you than ten sons? Eccl 7:19: Wisdom gives strength to the wise more than ten rulers who are in the same city. Zech 8:23: Ten men shall take hold of the skirt of a Jew, that is, very many. So, then, Moses will have said ten heaps of quails for many R11. And that it should be translated thus rather than ten cors is suggested, besides what we have already said, also by this reason: that כור, cor, or חומר chomer, is a measure of grain, not of meat R12. This observation is not worthless. For if each of the Israelites is said to have gathered ten cors of quails, their number will be enormous and will exceed all belief. Cornelius a Lapide, who tried to compute it in his Commentaries on this passage, reckons the figures so as to prove that the Israelites gathered about twelve thousand million quails. But we, instead of ten cors, maintain that each did not gather or consume even a third part of a cor. We do this not to diminish God's power, but so as not to do violence to faith by extorting from it more than God has revealed to us in his word R13. ¶

He says that no one offers a hen for the health of a friend who is already a father and has heirs, however sick and dying he may be col. 107; not even a quail, which is the cheapest of birds and costs much less. Nor is this surprising, if what Gesner relates from Flavius Blondus is true: on the shore of Antium, when in early spring the swallows and quails cross the Tyrrhenian Sea and return to Italy, within five thousand paces sometimes a hundred thousand little birds of this kind are caught daily, and this fowling continues for about a month. The ancients report similar things R14. Thus Varro, after writing that thrushes fly away from Italy across the sea, II, 672 and fly back again about the equinoxes, says: And at another time turtledoves and quails, in enormous numbers. Hence, exhausted by flying, they sometimes fall upon ships in such numbers that they sink them by their weight. Pliny: These (quails) also fly in in a similar way, not without danger to sailors, when they have come near land. For they settle on the sails, and always at night, and sink the vessels. Solinus: When they perceive land, they gather in flocks. Then, massed together, they hasten more vehemently; and this haste generally brings destruction to sailors. For it happens at night that they settle on the sails and, weighing down the edges, overturn the hulls, elsewhere tilt, or sink, as in Pliny. To the same effect is the fact that from the abundance of quails the name Ortygia was once common to several places. What of the fact that for this very reason the ancients gave the name Ortygia to the whole of Libya? The witness is Stephanus under Λιβύῃ [Libyē]. ¶

What we have said about the chennia is proved by these passages: Athenaeus book 9, ch. 11, at the end of the treatise On quails: Τῶν δὲ καλουμένων χεννίων, μικρὸν δ᾽ ἐστὶν ὀρτύγιον, μνημονεύει Κλεομένης ἐν τῇ πρὸς Ἀλέξανδρον ἐπιστολῇ γράφων οὕτως· Φαληρίδας ταριχηρὰς μυρίας, τυλάδας πεντακισχιλίας, χέννια ταριχηρὰ μύρια. Non me latet, has appellari novam creaturam avium in Vulgata versione Sap. XIX, 10. Atque inde a multis colligi, Deum illas de novo creasse. Hesychius: Χέννιον· ὀρνιθάριόν τι κατ᾽ Αἴγυπτον ταριχευόμενον [Chennion· ornitharion ti kat᾽ Aigypton taricheuomenon]; Chennium, a certain small bird in Egypt, preserved in salt. Since, then, there was so great an abundance of chennia and quails in Egypt and in all Africa, from where were they carried on the wings of the winds into the camp of the Israelites? There is no need here, as some do, to invent new miracles for ourselves, and to say that God created or multiplied the quails, as Christ multiplied the loaves in the hands of the Apostles. col. 108 It is our part to hold fast to the sacred text, in which we do not read that the quails were created or multiplied by the power of God, but that they were brought by the winds from regions beyond the sea. I am not unaware that in the Vulgate version, Wis 19:10, they are called a new creature of birds, and that many infer from this that God created them anew. But, to say nothing of the author of the book, to whom we have not bound our faith, the Greek copy does not have κτίσιν [ktisin], creature, but γένεσιν νέαν ὀρνέων [genesin nean orneōn], a new generation of birds; the Syriac likewise has ܡܘܠܕܐ ܚܕܬܐ ܕܦܪܚܬܐ [מולדא חדתא דפרחתא], and the Arabic توالد طيور جديدة [tawālud ṭuyūr jadīda]. And it is called new not in itself, but to the Israelites, who had experienced nothing of the kind. Similar is the fact that the same author attributes to this very food ξένην γεῦσιν [xenēn geusin], a new taste, Wis 16:2, that is, one unaccustomed and unknown to those who ate it. Here, therefore, we recognize no creation of quails. But this was miraculous: that they were brought in such great abundance, and at precisely the time that God had appointed, and that they all fell down around the camp of the Israelites, so as to serve them as food divinely provided. ¶

XI, 4. 5. 6. Unde Psaltes Ps. LXXVIII, 19. Obloquentes contra Deum dixerunt, An posset Deus instruere mensam in hoc deserto? En! ita percussit rupem, ut fluxerint aquae, et torrentes exundaverint. An etiam cibum posset dare? An parabit carnem populo suo? In quibus verbis 1. mire ingratos se produnt, dum praesenti praeferunt praeteritam conditionem, id est, servitutem Aegyptiacam, e qua Deus eos asseruerat forti manu et extenso brachio, libertati, qua fruebantur. Haec audiens quis non putasset, eos in Aegypto laute vixisse et in ampla fortuna? In qua tamen misere vixerant, sub Pharaonis jugo pressi et prope oppressi: sed ingratis id solenne est, ut, licet ad majora evecti, tamen praeterita extollant, et fastidiant II, 674 praesentia, ne in melius mutati status gratias ulli se debere agnoscant. 2. Miram aperiunt ἀῤῥωστίαν τῆς διανοίας in eo, quod pisciculos, cucumeres, pepones, porros, caepe, et allia, carnalia denique praeferunt spiritualibus bonis et gloriosae Dei praesentiae, quam testatam fecerat tot signis et miraculis. 3. Non contenti Manna, unde victus necessarius abunde illis suppetebat, carnem insuper petierant, et Deum circumscripserant, ut habetur Psal. LXXVIII, 41. hoc est, potentiam ejus arbitratu suo definiverant, non solum liberari petentes, sed et hoc vel illo modo. 4. Post expertam toties Dei virtutem, tamen haec sunt verba diffidentiae, An posset Deus mensam in hoc deserto instruere? An posset etiam cibum dare? Deus igitur, ad iram merito provocatus, spondet, se daturum carnes, non solum in unum aut alterum diem, sed et in mensem integrum, quasque, non tantum ad satietatem, sed et ad nauseam et fastidium usque comesas, tandem respuant ac rejiciant. Quae verba sunt animi concitati, quique nonnisi in ira sua populi preces exaudierat. Mense igitur jam expleto, cum cibum, quo se ingurgitaverant, adhuc in ore haberent, Deus, in eos excandescens, populum percussit plaga magna. Hanc Aben Ezra pestem esse vult. Ita enim habet David Psal. LXXVIII, col. 109 21. Ideo audiens Dominus excanduit (audiens scilicet quae allata sunt ex praecedente versu, An posset Deus mensam, etc.) et ignis accensus est in Iacob. Ignem intellige, de quo Num. XI, 1. Et, cum populus quiritaretur, malum fuit in auribus Domini. Audiens igitur Dominus excanduit, et exardescens ignis Domini consumpsit extrema castrorum, etc. usque ad versum quartum, ubi breviter narrari videntur per πρόληψιν, quae postea uberius reliquo capite explicantur. Et eidem loco duplex nomen inditum: unum Tabera ab incendio, Num. XI, 3. alterum Kibrot-taava, quia ibi sepulti sunt, qui carnes concupiverant, Num. XI, 34. (15). Est et aliud exemplum in hoc eodem populo similis pervicaciae.

V. There remains the fifth question, concerning the punishment that God inflicted on the Jews who ate the quails. The sin was this: weary of their condition, they had murmured against God as follows: Who will give us meat to eat! We remember the fish that we ate in Egypt for nothing, and the cucumbers, and the melons, and the leeks, and the onions, and the garlic: but now our soul is dried up, and nothing is before our eyes except the Manna, Num 11:4–5. 6. Hence the Psalmist, Ps 78:19: Speaking against God they said, Can God furnish a table in this wilderness? Behold! he struck the rock so that waters flowed and streams overflowed. Can he also give food? Will he provide meat for his people? In these words, 1. they show themselves remarkably ungrateful, since they prefer their former condition, that is, the Egyptian servitude, from which God had rescued them with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, to the liberty they now enjoyed. Who, hearing this, would not have thought that they had lived sumptuously in Egypt and in great prosperity? Yet there they had lived wretchedly, pressed and nearly crushed under Pharaoh's yoke. But it is customary for the ungrateful, even when raised to greater things, to extol the past and disdain II, 674 the present, lest they acknowledge that they owe thanks to anyone for the improvement of their state. 2. They reveal a remarkable ἀῤῥωστίαν τῆς διανοίας [arrhōstian tēs dianoias] in that they prefer little fish, cucumbers, melons, leeks, onions and garlic, in short carnal things, to spiritual goods and to the glorious presence of God, which he had attested by so many signs and miracles. 3. Not content with the Manna, from which the necessary sustenance was abundantly supplied to them, they had demanded meat besides, and had limited God, as it says in Ps 78:41, that is, they had defined his power by their own judgement, asking not only to be delivered, but to be delivered in this or that way. 4. After having experienced God's power so many times, these are nevertheless words of distrust: Can God furnish a table in this wilderness? Can he also give food? God therefore, justly provoked to anger, promises that he will give meat, not only for one or two days but for a whole month, so that, having eaten it not only to satiety but even to nausea and loathing, they would at last spit it out and reject it. These are the words of one whose mind is stirred, and who had heard the people's prayers only in his anger. So when the month was over, while they still had in their mouths the food with which they had gorged themselves, God, blazing against them, struck the people with a great plague. Aben Ezra takes this to be pestilence. There are some who infer from Ps 106:15 that רזון, that is, leanness or consumption, is meant. Others explain it otherwise. But I side with those for whom this plague is fire, by which some of this people were consumed. For so David has it, Psal. LXXVIII, col. 109 21: Therefore the Lord heard and was enraged (hearing, that is, what is reported in the preceding verse, Can God furnish a table, etc.) and a fire was kindled in Jacob. Understand the fire spoken of in Num 11:1: And when the people complained, it was evil in the ears of the Lord. So the Lord heard and was enraged, and the fire of the Lord, blazing up, consumed the outermost parts of the camp, etc., up to the fourth verse, where the things that are afterwards explained more fully in the rest of the chapter seem to be narrated briefly by πρόληψιν [prolēpsin]. And the same place was given a double name: one, Tabera, from the burning, Num 11:3; the other, Kibrot-taava, because those who had craved meat were buried there, Num 11:34 R15. There is also another example of similar obstinacy in this same people. ¶

For to these murmurers God granted quails that very evening and Manna the next day, exacting no punishment from them, Exod 16:12–13. Indeed, God can either punish the same sins in justice or pardon and remit them in mercy. Yet many reasons can be given why he punished the latter severely for what he had kindly remitted to the former. First, if anyone falls again into sins which God has once remitted, he sins more gravely, because besides God's justice he also spurns his mercy, and so he is evil because God was good. Moreover, the former had murmured because they were pressed by hunger and the want of everything. But the latter had Manna for food, which satisfied them, so that their so greedily desiring meat and demanding it so importunately was a cry wrung not by necessity but by wicked lust. Finally, the former were still untaught, and the Law had not yet been given, immediately after the departure from Egypt; the latter murmur when already instructed, and after receiving the Law, so that the sin is graver. So it is not surprising that God, who had spared the former, dealt so severely with these. ¶

Nor are the words of Aristotle, book 8, ch. 12, quoted above, to be understood otherwise; Pliny wrongly interpreted them to mean that quails never fly when the south wind blows, and for this he is also criticized by our author in what immediately follows. Aldrovandi, Ornithol. II, p. 156, therefore rightly, after quoting the words of Belon which Bochart himself presently cites, continues thus: "For if in flying they use the north wind, and refrain from flight in a south wind, as we have said from Pliny and Aristotle, it must be declared that they fly with a contrary wind when they come to us; which plainly appears to conflict with reason and with our senses. For the north wind, which takes its name [aquilo] from the very swift flight of the eagle [aquila], is a very fast wind and also very violent; quails, by contrast, have a very weak flight because of the excessive heaviness of their body. How, then, they could come to us while this wind blows, I cannot at all persuade myself, nor would anyone else easily persuade me. Therefore I think that when leaving us they use the help of the wind, but by no means when coming to us; indeed, unless a north wind is granted for their departure, they reach the desired places with difficulty." Buffon holds the same opinion on those words of Aristotle, Hist. Nat. des Oiseaux [=Buffon, Histoire naturelle des oiseaux] vol. 2, p. 461: I believe indeed that it is the wind that helps quails make their journey, not the north wind but a favourable wind; just as it is not the south wind that delays their course but a contrary wind; and this is true in all countries where these birds have a considerable crossing to make over the seas. The Commander Godeheu has very well remarked that in spring quails land on Malta only with the north-west wind, which is contrary to them for reaching Provence, and that on their return it is the south-east wind that brings them to this island, II, 667 because with that wind they cannot land in Barbary: we even see that the Author of Nature used this means, as the most consistent with the general laws he had established, to send numerous flocks of quails to the Israelites in the desert.
