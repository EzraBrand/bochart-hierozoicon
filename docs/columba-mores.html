II, 549 Chapter IV. ¶

De Columbae simplicitate, mansuetudine, castitate et timiditate.

On the dove's simplicity, gentleness, chastity and timidity. ¶

Iam de columbarum acturi moribus, incipiemus a celebri Christi dicto Matth. X, 16. Γίνεσθε φρόνιμοι ὡς οἱ ὄφεις, καὶ ἀκέραιοι ὡς αἱ περιστεραί· Estote prudentes ut serpentes, et simplices ut columbae. A Tzetze Chiliade 9. Hist. 263. φρόνιμοι et ἀκέραιοι pro eodem sumuntur. Postquam enim prudentes eos dici asseruit, cum ut serpentes caput servant, id est, fidem in Deum, addit continuo post:

Now, as we are about to treat of the habits of doves, we shall begin with the famous saying of Christ, Matt 10:16 Γίνεσθε φρόνιμοι ὡς οἱ ὄφεις, καὶ ἀκέραιοι ὡς αἱ περιστεραί [Ginesthe phronimoi hōs hoi opheis, kai akeraioi hōs hai peristerai]· Be ye prudent as serpents, and simple as doves. By Tzetzes, Chiliad 9, Hist. 263, φρόνιμοι [phronimoi] and ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] are taken as meaning the same thing. For after he has asserted that they are called prudent when, like serpents, they guard their head, that is, their faith in God, he immediately adds: ¶

Ἀκέραιοι δὲ γίνεσθε, ὡς αἱ περιστεραί μοι· Καὶ τοῦτο πάλιν τὸ αὐτὸ δηλοῖ τε, καὶ σημαίνει. Ἀκέραιοι γὰρ λέγονται περιστεραὶ τοιώς δε, Φρόνιμοι, ὥσπερ λέγεται περιστερὰ τοῦ Νῶε.

Ἀκέραιοι δὲ γίνεσθε, ὡς αἱ περιστεραί μοι· Καὶ τοῦτο πάλιν τὸ αὐτὸ δηλοῖ τε, καὶ σημαίνει. Ἀκέραιοι γὰρ λέγονται περιστεραὶ τοιώς δε, Φρόνιμοι, ὥσπερ λέγεται περιστερὰ τοῦ Νῶε. [Akeraioi de ginesthe, hōs hai peristerai moi; Kai touto palin to auto dēloi te, kai sēmainei. Akeraioi gar legontai peristerai toiōs de, Phronimoi, hōsper legetai peristera tou Nōe.] ¶

Simplices autem mihi sitis sicut columbae. Et hoc rursus idem indicat et significat. Simplices enim dicuntur columbae, tali modo prudentes, ut dicitur columba Noe. Quae nempe ad arcam rediit, id est, ad ecclesiam, a qua non secedendum. Quo nihil absurdius fingi potuit. Nam, ut serpens et columba, ita φρόνιμος et ἀκέραιος nullo modo sunt synonyma. Neque Christus ut synonyma, sed ut opposita junxit, egregia antithesi, quali utitur Martialis, cum dicit libri decimi Epigr. 47.

But be ye simple for me as doves. And this again indicates and signifies the same thing. For doves are called simple, prudent in such a way as Noah's dove is spoken of. That is, the dove which returned to the ark, that is, to the church, from which one must not depart. Nothing more absurd could have been devised. For, like the serpent and the dove, so φρόνιμος [phronimos] and ἀκέραιος [akeraios] are in no way synonyms. Nor did Christ join them as synonyms, but as opposites, in a striking antithesis, such as Martial uses when he says, in book 10, Epigr. 47: ¶

Prudens simplicitas, pares amici.

Prudent simplicity, friends who are equals. ¶

Nempe, ut prudentia sine simplicitate in versutiam degenerat, ita simplicitas sine prudentia nihil quidquam est, quam fatuitas et stultitia. Prudentes Hebraice sunt ערומים, et simplices פתיים, vel פתאים. Quae duo Solomon saepe opponit. Sic Prov. I, 4. לתת לפתאים ערמה, ad dandum simplicibus prudentiam. Prov. VIII, 5. Intelligite simplices prudentiam. Prov. XIV, 15. פתי, simplex cre- dit omni verbo, וערום, et prudens advertit ad gressum suum. Prov. XIX, 25. ופתי יערים, Et simplex prudens fiet. Prov. XXII, 3. ערום, Prudens, cum videt malum, abscondit se, ופתיים, At simplices, transeunt et mulctantur. Atque, col. 20 ut serpens dicitur ערום, φρόνιμος, Gen. III, 1. ita columba dicitur פתה, sim- plex Os. VII, 11. Et factus est Ephraim כיונה פתה אין לב, ut columba simplex, carens corde, id est, vecors. Ad quae duo loca haud dubie respexit Christus. Si objicias, utrumque in vitio poni, et serpentem ערום dici, non quia prudens, sed quia callidus est, et versipellis: Et columbam פתה, non quia simplex, sed quia fatua est, et אין לב, vecors, ut statim sequitur. Respondeo, primo tam II, 550 ערום, quam פתי, vel פתה voces esse medias, quaeque pro subjecti ratione etiam in laudem cedant. Ita ערום non solum callidum et versutum, sed et prudentem, et ערמה prudentiam sonat, quod abunde docent loca Solomonis supra al-lata. Vicissim פתי non solum ἄφρονα fatuum, sed et ἀκέραιον simplicem significat, ut Ps. CXVI, 6. שמר פתאים יהוה. simplices servat Dominus. Eas igitur voces potuit Christus etiam in laudem usurpare. Secundo, nec serpentis prudentia, nec columbae simplicitas, laudantur a Christo solae et ab invicem divulsae, sed tum demum laudantur, cum simul conjunctae sunt, ita, ut una suppleat, quod deest in altera, aut corrigat, quod excedit. Quomodo e duobus pharmacis, quae separatim noxia sunt, tertium componitur, quod utile est et salutare, quia se mutuo temperant. Unde est, quod haec duo veteres tam saepe jungunt. Gregorius Nazianzenus de patre Oratione 19. Τὸ γὰρ τοῦ ὄφεως φρόνιμον περὶ τὸ κακὸν τῷ τῆς περιστερᾶς ἀκεραίῳ περὶ τὸ καλὸν ἀναμίξας, οὐδὲ τὸ φρόνιμον εἴασε κακοῦργον γενέσθαι. οὔτε τὸ ἁπλοῦν ἀνόητον, ἀλλ᾽ ἓν ἀρε-τῆς εἶδος, ὡς μάλιστα ἐνῆν, ἐξ ἀμφοτέρων ἀπηκριβώσατο. Cum serpentis ad malum prudentiam cum columbae ad bonum simplicitate conjunxisset, ita se comparavit, ut nec prudentiam in versutam improbitatem, nec simplicitatem in stoliditatem degenerare sineret: verum ex ambabus, quoad ejus fieri poterat, unam virtutis speciem efficeret, numeris omnibus perfectam et absolutam. Hieronymus ad Paulinum de institutione monachi, Habeto simplicitatem columbae, ne cuiquam machineris dolos, et serpentis astutiam, ne aliorum supplanteris insidiis. Isidorus Pelusiota lib. 3. epist. 175. Ἡ φρόνησις τῇ ἁπλότητι κιρνωμένη θεῖόν τι χρῆμα· φημὶ δὲ ἐντελεστάτην ἀρετὴν ἀποτελεῖ· εἰ δὲ θατέρα θατέρας χωρισθείη, ἡ μὲν εἰς πονηρίαν ἐμπίπτει, ἡ δὲ εἰς μωρίαν τελευτᾷ· Prudentia, simplicitate temperata, divinam quandam rem, hoc est, numeris omnibus absolutam virtutem parit. At si altera ab altera sejungatur, illa in perversitatem incidit, haec vero in stultitiam desinit. Faustus, De libero arbitrio lib. 2. capite ultimo, Faciamus hominem simplicem, faciamus astutum: simplicem, ne malum callidus inferat, astutum, ne incautus incurrat. Gregorius in Iobum lib. 1. cap. 2. Utraque necessario in admonitione conjunxit, ut et simplicitatem columbae astutia serpentis instrueret, et rursum serpentis astutiam columbae simplicitas temperaret. Sidonius lib. 7. Epist. 13. de Lupo, Tricassiensi episcopo, Simplicitatem columbae in ecclesia servat, in foro serpentis astutiam. Bonis prudens, malis cautus, neutris callidus judicatur. S. Bernardus Epist. 327. ad Papam Innocentium: in ecclesia ergo simplicitas, in foro astutia servanda est; hic, ne decipiaris, illic, ne decipias. Videsis plura de hoc argumento Caesarii Dial. 4. Quaest. 190. Porro simplices hic ἁπλοὶ non dicuntur, sed ἀκέραιοι. Res tamen eodem redit. Nam ex κεράω, misceo, factum ἀκέραιος proprie id significat, quod externum et peregrinum nihil habet admixtum. Sunt, quibus ἀκέραιος idem est, quod ἀκερᾶς, vel ἀκέρατος, II, 551 qui cornua non habet, adeoque est ἀβλαβὴς, et innoxius, metaphora ab iis animalibus sumta, quae cornu feriunt. Quomodo sumsisse videntur Arabs et Aethiops, quorum illi ἀκέραιοι sunt ودعا, huic የዋሃን፡ id est, mites, et mansueti. Sed prius etymon videtur magis analogicum. Nec satis convenit, ut excornis dicatur pro innoxio, cum aquilis, et leonibus, et pluribus aliis feris, col. 21 quae cornua non habent, multae sint nocendi artes, quibus in aliorum animalium perniciem abutuntur. Praeterea prudentibus simplices opponuntur, mites et mansueti non item. Itaque, si mites reddas, peribit gratia oppositionis, quae hic est manifesta inter serpentis prudentiam et columbae simplicitatem. Cujusmodi est illa Pauli Rom. XVI, 19. Vos quidem volo σοφοὺς sapientes esse in bono, ἀκεραίους δὲ, simplices vero in malo. Innocentes verterat Cl. Beza in primis editionibus. Sed in posterioribus idem in simplices recte mutavit, ut constaret oppositio (1). Eodem facit, quod in vetustissimo illo Beza exemplari pro ἀκέραιοι legebatur ἁπλούστατοι simplicissimi. Nec leve est, quod, ut ex Mose sumtum est, quod serpens vocatur prudens, ita ex Osea, quod columba vocatur simplex. At ubi mitis vocetur, Prophetarum locus nullus occurrit. Itaque Christiani Arabes videntur minus intellexisse, quid hoc loco sint ἀκέραιοι, quam Mahumetani doctores, qui بلها bulhan, id est, simplices reddunt. Ita in libro sacerdotis Achmed, cui titulus الزهد id est, abstinentia, dicit Iazid, Malzarae filius, Iesum discipulis suis praecepisse ان يكون بلها في الله مشل الحمام ut essent bulhan (id est, simplices) in Deo sicut columbae. Ita بلها bulhan reddimus cum Arabum peritissimis a Singulari ابله ablaho, cui كيس chaison opponitur, id est, versutus et callidus. Et sic sumendum docent, quae ibidem sequuntur: ليس شي ابله من الحمام وذلك انك تاخذ فرخيه من تحته فتذبحهما ثم يعود الي مكانه فيفرخ فيه Nihil est columba simplicius. Etsi enim pullos illi subtractos mactaveris, tamen ad locum suum subinde redit, et in eo pullos excludit. Quod ipsum scribunt Patres in Oseae locum, quem citavimus. Theodoretus, Ἐκείνη τῶν ἰδίων νεοττῶν ἀφαιρουμένη οὐκ ἀφίπταται, ἀλλὰ τοῖς αὐ-τοῖς προστρέχει χωρίοις. Et ex illo Theophylactus, Illa pullis sublatis non avolat, verum in iisdem locis remanet. Paullo aliter Hieronymus: Aves caeterae pullos suos etiam cum periculo vitae suae protegere festinant, et cum ad nidum II, 552 suum accipitrem, colubrum, corvum, sive cornicem accedere viderint, huc illucque volitant, et immittunt se morsu, et unguibus lacerant, et dolorem parentis querula voce testantur. Sola columba ablatos pullos non dolet, non requirit. Sed quam vere, non satis scio, cum columbae, aut palumbis genus الورشان alvvarsen describatur ab Arabibus بالحنو علي اولاده حتي انه ربما قتل نفسه اذا راها في يد القانص tanto affectu fervens erga pullos suos, ut quandoque se ipsum interimat, cum illos videt in manu aucupis. Ita ha-betur in Damire. Et in genere scriptores columbam commendant ab amore in prolem. Plinius lib. 10. cap. 34. de columba mare et foemina: Amor utrique sobolis aequalis: saepe ex hac causa castigatio, pigrius intrante foemina ad pullos. De quibus plura Aristoteles Hist. lib. 9. cap. 7. Aelianus Hist. lib. 3. cap. 45. Al-ter Aelianus Var. lib. 1. cap. 15. Athenaeus lib. 9. cap. 11. Porphyrius Περὶ ἀποχῆς, etc. lib. 3. quae non vacat hic describere. Alii columbae simplicitatem in eo patere asserunt, quod retia non evitat, neque laqueos, sibi paratos, col. 22 cum se demittit ad cibum. Et huic columbae stupori Oseam Israelitarum socordiam conferre volunt, qui pericula non praecavebant, et se ipsos in hostium potestatem conjiciebant. Utut se res habeat, hanc columbae simplicitatem agnoverunt etiam οἱ ἔξωθεν. Sic ad illud Iuvenalis,

For just as prudence without simplicity degenerates into cunning, so simplicity without prudence is nothing but foolishness and stupidity. The prudent in Hebrew are ערומים, and the simple פתיים, or פתאים. Solomon often sets these two against each other. Thus Prov 1:4. לתת לפתאים ערמה, to give prudence to the simple. Prov 8:5 O ye simple, understand prudence. Prov 14:15. פתי, the simple believes every word, וערום, but the prudent considers his step. Prov 19:25. ופתי יערים, And the simple will become prudent. Prov 22:3. ערום, The prudent, when he sees evil, hides himself, ופתיים, but the simple pass on and are punished. And, col. 20 just as the serpent is called ערום, φρόνιμος [phronimos], Gen 3:1, so the dove is called פתה, simple, Hos 7:11 And Ephraim has become כיונה פתה אין לב, like a simple dove, lacking heart, that is, senseless. To these two passages Christ no doubt alluded. You may object that both are put as vices: that the serpent is called ערום not because it is prudent but because it is crafty and wily, and the dove פתה not because it is simple but because it is foolish, and אין לב, senseless, as immediately follows. I reply, first, that both II, 550 ערום and פתי or פתה are neutral words, which according to their subject may also turn to praise. Thus ערום means not only crafty and cunning but also prudent, and ערמה prudence, as the passages of Solomon cited above amply show. Conversely פתי signifies not only ἄφρονα [aphrona], foolish, but also ἀκέραιον [akeraion], simple, as in Ps 116:6. שמר פתאים יהוה, the Lord preserves the simple. Christ could therefore use these words in praise as well. Second, neither the serpent's prudence nor the dove's simplicity is praised by Christ on its own and torn from the other; they are praised only when joined together, so that the one supplies what is lacking in the other, or corrects what is excessive. In the same way, from two drugs that are harmful separately a third is compounded that is useful and wholesome, because they temper each other. Hence the ancients so often join these two. Gregory of Nazianzus, on his father, Oration 19: Τὸ γὰρ τοῦ ὄφεως φρόνιμον περὶ τὸ κακὸν τῷ τῆς περιστερᾶς ἀκεραίῳ περὶ τὸ καλὸν ἀναμίξας, οὐδὲ τὸ φρόνιμον εἴασε κακοῦργον γενέσθαι. οὔτε τὸ ἁπλοῦν ἀνόητον, ἀλλ᾽ ἓν ἀρετῆς εἶδος, ὡς μάλιστα ἐνῆν, ἐξ ἀμφοτέρων ἀπηκριβώσατο. [To gar tou opheōs phronimon peri to kakon tō tēs peristeras akeraiō peri to kalon anamixas, oude to phronimon eiase kakourgon genesthai. oute to haploun anoēton, all᾽ hen aretēs eidos, hōs malista enēn, ex amphoterōn apēkribōsato.] Having joined the serpent's prudence regarding evil with the dove's simplicity regarding good, he so conducted himself that he let neither prudence degenerate into cunning wickedness nor simplicity into stupidity, but from both, as far as was possible, he produced a single form of virtue, perfect and complete in every respect. Jerome, to Paulinus on the training of a monk: Have the simplicity of the dove, so as not to contrive snares against anyone, and the cunning of the serpent, so as not to be tripped up by the plots of others. Isidore of Pelusium, book 3, epist. 175: Ἡ φρόνησις τῇ ἁπλότητι κιρνωμένη θεῖόν τι χρῆμα· φημὶ δὲ ἐντελεστάτην ἀρετὴν ἀποτελεῖ· εἰ δὲ θατέρα θατέρας χωρισθείη, ἡ μὲν εἰς πονηρίαν ἐμπίπτει, ἡ δὲ εἰς μωρίαν τελευτᾷ· [Hē phronēsis tē haplotēti kirnōmenē theion ti chrēma; phēmi de entelestatēn aretēn apotelei; ei de thatera thateras chōristheiē, hē men eis ponērian empiptei, hē de eis mōrian teleuta;] Prudence tempered with simplicity produces something divine, that is, a virtue complete in every respect. But if the one is separated from the other, the former falls into perversity, while the latter ends in folly. Faustus, On Free Will, book 2, last chapter: Let us make man simple, let us make him astute: simple, so that being crafty he may not inflict evil; astute, so that being incautious he may not fall into it. Gregory on Job, book 1, ch. 2: He necessarily joined both in the admonition, so that the cunning of the serpent might equip the simplicity of the dove, and in turn the simplicity of the dove might temper the cunning of the serpent. Sidonius, book 7, Epist. 13, on Lupus, bishop of Troyes: In the church he keeps the simplicity of the dove, in the forum the cunning of the serpent. To the good he is judged prudent, to the wicked wary, to neither crafty. St Bernard [=Bernard of Clairvaux], Epist. 327, to Pope Innocent: in the church, then, simplicity must be kept, in the forum cunning; here, lest you be deceived, there, lest you deceive. See more on this subject in Caesarius, Dial. 4, Quaest. 190. Further, the simple are here called not ἁπλοί [haploi] but ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi]. Yet it comes to the same thing. For ἀκέραιος [akeraios], formed from κεράω [keraō], misceo [I mix], properly signifies that which has nothing external and foreign mixed into it. Some take ἀκέραιος [akeraios] to be the same as ἀκερᾶς [akeras] or ἀκέρατος [akeratos], II, 551 that which has no horns, and hence is ἀβλαβής [ablabēs] and harmless, a metaphor taken from animals that strike with the horn. So the Arabic and Ethiopic translators seem to have taken it: in the former ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] are ودعا [wudaʿā], in the latter የዋሃን፡ [yawāhān], that is, mild and gentle. But the first etymology seems more regular. Nor is it quite fitting that hornless should be said for harmless, since eagles, lions and many other wild beasts col. 21 that have no horns possess many ways of doing harm, which they employ to destroy other animals. Moreover, the simple are opposed to the prudent, but the mild and gentle are not. So if you render mild, the grace of the opposition will be lost, which is evident here between the serpent's prudence and the dove's simplicity. Of the same kind is that of Paul, Rom 16:19: I would have you σοφούς [sophous] wise in what is good, ἀκεραίους δέ [akeraious de], but simple in what is evil. The illustrious Beza had rendered innocent in his first editions, but in later ones he rightly changed it to simple, so that the opposition might stand R1. To the same effect is the fact that in that very ancient manuscript of Beza ἁπλούστατοι [haploustatoi], most simple, was read for ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi]. Nor is it a slight point that, just as the serpent's being called prudent is taken from Moses, so the dove's being called simple is taken from Hosea. But no passage of the Prophets occurs where it is called mild. Thus the Christian Arabs seem to have understood less well what ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] means here than the Muslim doctors, who render بلها [bulhan] bulhan, that is, simple. So in the book of the priest Achmed entitled الزهد [al-zuhd], that is, abstinence, Iazid, son of Malzara, says that Jesus commanded his disciples ان يكون بلها في الله مشل الحمام [an yakūna bulhan fī Allāh mashl al-ḥamām] that they should be bulhan (that is, simple) in God like doves. Thus we render بلها [bulhan] bulhan, with the most learned of the Arabs, from the singular ابله [ablah] ablaho, to which كيس [kayyis] chaison is opposed, that is, cunning and crafty. And that it is to be so taken is shown by what follows in the same place: ليس شي ابله من الحمام وذلك انك تاخذ فرخيه من تحته فتذبحهما ثم يعود الي مكانه فيفرخ فيه [laysa shay ablah min al-ḥamām wa-dhālika annaka taʾkhudh farkhayhi min taḥtihi fa-tadhbaḥuhumā thumma yaʿūd ilā makānihi fa-yufarrikh fīhi] Nothing is simpler than the dove. For even if you kill the young taken from under it, it nevertheless soon returns to its place and hatches young in it. The very same thing the Fathers write on the passage of Hosea we have cited. Theodoret: Ἐκείνη τῶν ἰδίων νεοττῶν ἀφαιρουμένη οὐκ ἀφίπταται, ἀλλὰ τοῖς αὐτοῖς προστρέχει χωρίοις. [Ekeinē tōn idiōn neottōn aphairoumenē ouk aphiptatai, alla tois autois prostrechei chōriois.] And following him Theophylact: When its young are taken away it does not fly off, but remains in the same places. Somewhat differently Jerome: Other birds hasten to protect their young even at the risk of their own life, and when they see a hawk, a snake, a raven or a crow approach their nest, II, 552 they flutter this way and that, throw themselves at it, tear it with beak and claws, and bear witness to a parent's grief with plaintive cry. The dove alone does not grieve for its young when they are taken away, nor seek them. But how truly, I do not quite know, since a kind of dove or wood-pigeon, الورشان [al-warashān] alvvarsen, is described by the Arabs بالحنو علي اولاده حتي انه ربما قتل نفسه اذا راها في يد القانص [bi-l-ḥunuww ʿalā awlādihi ḥattā annahu rubbamā qatala nafsahu idhā rāhā fī yad al-qāniṣ] as burning with such affection for its young that it sometimes kills itself when it sees them in the fowler's hand. So it is found in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]. And in general writers commend the dove for its love of its offspring. Pliny, book 10, ch. 34, on the male and female dove: Both have equal love for their offspring; often for this reason there is chastisement, when the female is slow in going in to the young. On which more in Aristotle, Hist. book 9, ch. 7; Aelian, Hist. book 3, ch. 45; again Aelian, Var. book 1, ch. 15; Athenaeus, book 9, ch. 11; Porphyry, Περὶ ἀποχῆς [Peri apochēs], etc., book 3; which there is no room to copy out here. Others assert that the dove's simplicity is evident in the fact that it does not avoid nets, nor the snares laid for it, col. 22 when it comes down to feed. And with this stupidity of the dove they would have Hosea compare the sloth of the Israelites, who took no precaution against dangers and threw themselves into the power of their enemies. However that may be, this simplicity of the dove was recognized also by οἱ ἔξωθεν [hoi exōthen]. Thus on that line of Juvenal, ¶

— — — vexat censura columbas,

— — — censure harasses the doves, ¶

dicit vetus Scholiastes, Columbas retulit ad simplicitatem. Et in Suida ἡμένη πε-λειὰς, columba sedens proverbio dicitur ἐπὶ τῶν ἁπλουστάτων, de simplicissimis. Non tamen de omni columba usurpatur hoc proverbium: sed de iis, quae allectatrici assident, atque, ut ait Suidas, κατὰ ἀντιπαρεξέτασιν τῆς πα-λευτρίας, per oppositionem ad columbam allectatricem, id est, qua abutuntur aucupes ad caeteras alliciendas. Aristophanes in Avibus,

the old Scholiast says: He referred "doves" to simplicity. And in the Suda ἡμένη πελειὰς [hēmenē peleias], a sitting dove, is used as a proverb ἐπὶ τῶν ἁπλουστάτων [epi tōn haploustatōn], of the most simple-minded. This is, of course, διὰ τὸ εὔηθες τοῦ ζώου [dia to euēthes tou zōou], because of the creature's stupidity, as Eustathius has it on Iliad ψ΄ [psi]. The proverb, however, is not used of every dove, but of those that sit beside the decoy dove. As the Suda says, it is κατὰ ἀντιπαρεξέτασιν τῆς παλευτρίας [kata antiparexetasin tēs paleutrias], by contrast with the decoy dove, that is, the dove that fowlers misuse to lure the others. Columbasque similiter correptas coercens detinet, et ligatas in reti cogit alias alli- cere. Et ibi Scholiastes: Εἰώθασιν ἐκτυφλοῦντές τινα τῶν ὀρνέων ἱστάναι ἐν δι-κτύῳ, ὅπως τῇ φωνῇ προσκαλεῖτο τὰ ὁμοιογενῆ· Solent excaecatam avem in la- queo detinere, ut voce congeneres advocet. Et columbas in hunc usum fuisse ad-hibitas, praeter Aristophanem Philosophus quoque asserit Hist. lib. 9. cap. 7. Αἱ περιστεραὶ, inquit, ζῶσι καὶ ὀκτὼ ἔτη, αἱ τετυφλωμέναι ὑπὸ τῶν παλευτρίας τρεφόντων αὐτάς· Octo annos vivunt columbae, quae excaecatae aluntur, ut sint allectatrices. Iam, si consulas recentiores, quae dicta sunt de columbarum simplicitate et facili captura, non parum confirmabit, quod sibi contigisse narrat II, 553 Aloisius Cadamustus Navigat. cap. 40. cum in Canarias insulas primum appulit. Columbi capiebantur, ait, pro libito manu hominis. Non enim pavebant ad viri conspectum. Et capite sexto de Madera insula, Memini ab indigenis accepisse, ibi, cum primo culta est insula, copiam maximam columborum fuisse: quibus capiendis non alia arte usi sunt aucupes, quam alligato perticae uncino, ut ex arboribus, quibus illic insident, traherentur ad se, non paventibus avibus, sed mirantibus, veluti ad rem, non visam hactenus.

And likewise he seizes the doves, keeps them confined, and forces them, bound in a net, to lure others. And there the Scholiast says: Εἰώθασιν ἐκτυφλοῦντές τινα τῶν ὀρνέων ἱστάναι ἐν δικτύῳ, ὅπως τῇ φωνῇ προσκαλεῖτο τὰ ὁμοιογενῆ· [Eiōthasin ektyphlountes tina tōn orneōn histanai en diktyō, hopōs tē phōnē proskaleito ta homoiogenē] They are accustomed to keep a blinded bird in a snare, so that by its voice it may call others of its kind. That doves were used for this purpose is asserted not only by Aristophanes but also by the Philosopher, Hist. book 9, ch. 7: Αἱ περισταὶ, he says, ζῶσι καὶ ὀκτὼ ἔτη, αἱ τετυφλωμέναι ὑπὸ τῶν παλευτρίας τρεφόντων αὐτάς· [Hai peristerai … zōsi kai oktō etē, hai tetyphlōmenai hypo tōn paleutrias trephontōn autas] Doves that are blinded and kept as decoys live as long as eight years. Now, if you consult more recent writers, what has been said about the simplicity of doves and the ease of catching them will be considerably confirmed by what II, 553 Cadamosto [=Alvise Cadamosto, Navigazioni (1455-56)] relates happened to him, Navigat. ch. 40, when he first landed on the Canary Islands. The doves, he says, were caught at will by a man's hand. For they were not frightened at the sight of a man. And in chapter 6, on the island of Madeira: I remember hearing from the inhabitants that when the island was first settled there was a very great abundance of doves there. To catch them the fowlers used no other device than a hook tied to a pole, so that they might be drawn to them from the trees on which they perch there; the birds were not frightened but full of wonder, as at something never seen before. ¶

Interim, qui ἀκεραίους in Christi dicto mites et mansuetos esse volunt, praeterquam quod Grammaticorum non paucos sequuti sunt, quibus ἀκέραιον, de animali dictum, excorne significat, et benignum et innoxium, columbae naturam respexerunt, quae re vera est mitissimum et mansuetissimum animal. Proinde placidam vocat Ovidius Metam. lib. 7. Fab. 12. Et Cyrillus columbam dicit πρὸς ἄκρον ἥκειν πρᾳότητος, supremum mansuetudinis fastigium attingere, libro decimo quinto De adoratione. Quin eam felle prorsus carere multi putant. Inde ἄχολον vocat Clemens Paedag. lib. 1. cap. 5. Et Isidorus Orig. lib. 12. aves mansuetas ac sine felle. Meanwhile, those who take ἀκεραίους [akeraious] in Christ's saying to mean mild and gentle have followed a good many Grammarians, for whom ἀκέραιον [akeraion], said of an animal, means hornless, as well as kindly and harmless. Beyond this, they have had regard to the nature of the dove, which is in truth a most mild and gentle animal. Accordingly Ovid calls it placid, Metam. book 7, Fable 12. And Cyril says that the dove πρὸς ἄκρον ἥκειν πρᾳότητος [pros akron hēkein praotētos], reaches the highest summit of gentleness, in book 15 On Adoration. Indeed, many think it entirely lacks gall. Hence Clement calls it ἄχολον [acholon], Paedag. book 1, ch. 5; and Isidore, Orig. book 12, calls doves gentle birds without gall. Of the same kind is what Tertullian writes, On Baptism ch. 8, that the dove, even bodily, lacks gall itself. And Sedulius, book 2 of the Carmen, on the Baptism of Christ: ¶

Mansuetumque decet, multumque incedere mitem, Per volucrem, quae felle caret. — —

And it befits the Gentle One, the greatly Mild, to come forth through the bird that lacks gall. — — ¶

Et Galenus in libro De atra bile ridiculos eos esse ait, qui columbam felle carere putant, quoniam τὴν ἐπὶ τῷ ἤπατι κύστιν, vesicam fellis prope jecur non habeat. Neque id fugit Philosophum, cujus verba sunt Hist. lib. 2. cap. 15. Ἔνιοι πρὸς τῇ κοιλίᾳ ἔχουσιν, οἱ δὲ πρὸς τοῖς ἐντέροις τὴν χολὴν, οἷον περιστερά. Unde Plinius lib. 11. cap. 37. And Horapollo, Hieroglyph. book 1, ch. 54: Ἱστορεῖται δὲ ὅτι οὐ χολὴν ἔχει τοῦτο τὸ ζῶον [Historeitai de hoti ou cholēn echei touto to zōon], It is also reported that this animal lacks gall. Yet he himself refutes this in book 2, ch. 45, saying that the dove ἐν ὀπισθίοις τὴν χολὴν ἔχειν [en opisthiois tēn cholēn echein], has its gall at the back. And Galen, in the book On Black Bile, says those people are ridiculous who think the dove lacks gall because it does not have τὴν ἐπὶ τῷ ἤπατι κύστιν [tēn epi tō hēpati kystin], the gall bladder next to the liver. Nor did this escape the Philosopher, whose words are, Hist. book 2, ch. 15: Ἔνιοι πρὸς τῇ κοιλίᾳ ἔχουσιν, οἱ δὲ πρὸς τοῖς ἐντέροις τὴν χολὴν, οἷον περιστερά [Enioi pros tē koilia echousin, hoi de pros tois enterois tēn cholēn, hoion peristera]. Hence Pliny, book 11, ch. 37: In some the gall is attached only to the intestine, as in doves. ¶

Aliud in columbis nobis hic notandum venit, nempe conjugalis castitas, quam veteres mire depraedicant. Aristoteles Hist. lib. 9. cap. 7. Οὔτε γὰρ συν- col. 23 δυάζεσθαι θέλουσι πλείοσιν, οὔτε προσλείπουσι τὴν κοινωνίαν, πλὴν ἐὰν χῆρος, ἢ χήρα γένηται· Neque enim pluribus copulari volunt, neque mutuum consortium unquam deserunt, nisi per viduitatem. Plinius lib. 10. cap. 34. Pudicitia illis prima, et neutri nota adulteria. Conjugii fidem non violant, communemque servant domum. Nisi coelebs, aut vidua, nidum non relinquit. Propertius libro 2. Another point to be noted here in doves is their conjugal chastity, which the ancients praise remarkably. Aristotle, Hist. book 9, ch. 7: Οὔτε γὰρ συν- col. 23 δυάζεσθαι θέλουσι πλείοσιν, οὔτε προσλείπουσι τὴν κοινωνίαν, πλὴν ἐὰν χῆρος, ἢ χήρα γένηται· [Oute gar syndyazesthai thelousi pleiosin, oute proleipousi tēn koinōnian, plēn ean chēros ē chēra genētai] For they will not pair with more than one, nor do they ever abandon their partnership, except through widowhood. Pliny, book 10, ch. 34: Chastity is foremost with them, and adultery is known to neither. They do not violate conjugal fidelity, and they keep a common home. Unless unmated or widowed, a dove does not leave its nest. Propertius, book 2, Eleg. 12: ¶

II, 554 Exemplo junctae sic sint in amore columbae, Masculus et totum foemina conjugium.

II, 554 So let doves joined in love be my example, male and female, a whole marriage. ¶

De turture quoque et palumbe Philosophus idem scribit: Ἔχει δὲ τὸν ἄῤῥενα ἡ τρυγὼν τὸν αὐτὸν καὶ φάττα, καὶ ἄλλον οὐ προσίενται, Turtur quoque, et palumbus marem unum habent., neque alterum admittunt. Quin palumbos adulteria morte punire, tradunt Porphyrius, De abstin. anim. lib. 3. et Phile, De anim. cap. 19. Uterque ex Aeliano Hist. lib. 3. cap. 44. ubi turtures addit eandem legem sancte servare: sed columbas albas ex adulteriis solos mares perimere, foeminarum misertas. Clemens quoque Stromatων libro secundo animalia quaedam esse tradit, ἃ τὴν ἐπιμιξίαν οὐ ποιεῖται πρὸς πολλὰ καὶ ἀναίδην, ἀλλὰ πρὸς ἓν, καὶ ὁμόφυλον· οἷαι αἱ πελειάδες, καὶ αἱ φάσσαι, καὶ τὸ τρυγόνων γένος, καὶ ὅσα τούτοις παραπλήσια, quae cum multis et imprudenter non congrediuntur, sed cum uno, eoque ejusdem generis, ut sunt columbae, et palumbes, et turturum genus, et quae sunt his similia. Quid quod columbam nigram post conjugis obitum viduam ajunt permanere, atque ideo apud Aegyptios symbolum fuisse mulieris, quae a secundis nuptiis abhorret. Horus lib. 2. cap. 30. Γυναῖ-κα χήραν ἐπιμείνασαν ἄχρι θανάτου θέλοντες σημῆναι, περιστερὰν μέλαιναν ζωγραφοῦσιν· αὕτη γὰρ οὐ συμμίγνυται ἑτέρῳ ἀνδρὶ ἕως οὗ χηρεύει Mulierem, quae ad mortem usque vidua manet, significare cum volunt, nigram pingunt columbam: haec enim, quamdiu vidua est, alteri mari non jungitur. Proinde Tertullianus monogamiam suam columbae exemplo stabilire conatur: Avis, inquit, non tantum innocuae, sed et pudicae, quam unam unus masculus novit. At id ipsum in turtures magno consensu transtulerunt Patres posteriores, qua de causa nullus scio. Origenes in Cant. Homil. 2. Turturum ferunt hujusmodi naturam esse, ut neque masculum praeter unam foeminam adeat aliam, nec foemina plus quam unum patiatur marem. Ita, ut, si accidat, altero intercepto superesse alterum, pariter cum conjuge extinctus sit ei concubitus amor. Basilius in Hexaëmeron Homil. 8. Gregor. Nazianz. in Praeceptis ad virgines Carm. 3.

Of the turtledove and the wood pigeon the same Philosopher writes: Ἔχει δὲ τὸν ἄῤῥενα ἡ τρυγὼν τὸν αὐτὸν καὶ φάττα, καὶ ἄλλον οὐ προσίενται [Echei de ton arrhena hē trygōn ton auton kai phatta, kai allon ou prosientai], The turtledove too, and the wood pigeon, have one male, and do not admit another. Indeed, Porphyry, On Abstinence from Animal Food book 3, and Phile, On Animals ch. 19, report that wood pigeons punish adultery with death. Both take this from Aelian, Hist. book 3, ch. 44, where he adds that turtledoves keep the same law scrupulously, but that white doves kill only the males caught in adultery, taking pity on the females. Clement, too, in the second book of the Stromata, reports that there are certain animals ἃ τὴν ἐπιμιξίαν οὐ ποιεῖται πρὸς πολλὰ καὶ ἀναίδην, ἀλλὰ πρὸς ἓν, καὶ ὁμόφυλον· οἷαι αἱ πελειάδες, καὶ αἱ φάσσαι, καὶ τὸ τρυγόνων γένος, καὶ ὅσα τούτοις παραπλήσια [ha tēn epimixian ou poieitai pros polla kai anaidēn, alla pros hen, kai homophylon; hoiai hai peleiades, kai hai phassai, kai to trygonōn genos, kai hosa toutois paraplēsia], which do not mate with many and shamelessly, but with one, and that of the same kind, as are doves, and wood pigeons, and the turtledove kind, and those that are like them. What is more, they say that the black dove remains a widow after the death of its mate, and that for this reason among the Egyptians it was the symbol of a woman who shrinks from a second marriage. Horus book 2, ch. 30: Γυναῖκα χήραν ἐπιμείνασαν ἄχρι θανάτου θέλοντες σημῆναι, περιστερὰν μέλαιναν ζωγραφοῦσιν· αὕτη γὰρ οὐ συμμίγνυται ἑτέρῳ ἀνδρὶ ἕως οὗ χηρεύει [Gynaika chēran epimeinasan achri thanatou thelontes sēmēnai, peristeran melainan zōgraphousin; hautē gar ou symmignytai heterō andri heōs hou chēreuei] When they wish to signify a woman who remains a widow until death, they paint a black dove: for this bird, as long as it is widowed, does not join with another male. Accordingly Tertullian tries to establish his monogamy by the example of the dove: A bird, he says, not only harmless but also chaste, which one male alone knows. But the later Fathers, with great unanimity, transferred this very thing to turtledoves, for what reason I do not know. Origen on the Song of Songs, Homily 2: They say that the nature of turtledoves is such that the male does not approach any female besides one, nor does the female accept more than one male; so that, if it happens that one is taken away and the other survives, the love of mating is extinguished together with the mate. Basil in the Hexaemeron, Homily 8: Τὴν τρυγόνα φασὶ διαζευχθεῖσάν ποτε τοῦ ὁμόζυγος, μηκέτι τὴν πρὸς ἕτερον καταδέχεσθαι κοινωνίαν, ἀλλὰ μένειν ἀσυνδύαστον, μνήμῃ τοῦ ποτε συζευχθέντος τὴν πρὸς ἕτερον κοινωνίαν ἀπαρνημένην· [Tēn trygona phasi diazeuchtheisan pote tou homozygos, mēketi tēn pros heteron katadechesthai koinōnian, alla menein asyndyaston, mnēmē tou pote syzeuchthentos tēn pros heteron koinōnian aparnēmenēn] They say that the turtledove, once separated from its mate, never enters into union with another, but remains single without a mate, refusing marriage with another in remembrance of the partner it has lost. Gregory of Nazianzus in the Precepts to Virgins, Poem 3: ¶

Πυνθάνομ᾽ ὡς πτερόεσσα τρυγὼν τρυγόνος φιλίοιο Χηρωθεῖσ᾽ ὁμόλεκτρον ἑὸν ποθέουσα σαόφρων, Οὐ δέχεται πόσιν ἄλλον ἑῆς καθύπερθε καλιῆς·

I hear that the female of the turtledove, once widowed of her mate, restrains herself out of longing for her spouse and admits no other mate into her nest. Chrysostom, Homily II, 555 On the Turtledove, English edition, vol. 7, p. 264: Φασί τινες τὴν τρυγόνα φίλανδρον εἶναι, καὶ φίλαγνον· ὥς τε κἂν συμβῇ τὸν ταύτης ἄῤῥενα ὑπὸ ἀετοῦ ἀναλωθῆναι, ἢ ὑπὸ ἰξευτοῦ θηρευθῆναι, ταύτην ἑτέρῳ ἀνδρὶ μὴ ἐπιμίγνυσθαι, ἀλλ᾽ αὐτὸν ποθεῖν, αὐτὸν ἀναμένειν, καὶ τῇ τούτου μνήμῃ συναποθνήσκειν. [Phasi tines tēn trygona philandron einai, kai philagnon; hōs te kan symbē ton tautēs arrena hypo aetou analōthēnai, ē hypo ixeutou thēreuthēnai, tautēn heterō andri mē epimignysthai, all᾽ auton pothein, auton anamenein, kai tē toutou mnēmē synapothnēskein.] Some say that the female turtledove is held by love for her mate and is so devoted to chastity that, if her male happens to be taken by an eagle or by a fowler, she does not mate with another, but longs for and awaits him alone, and even dies together with the memory of him. So also Gregory of Nyssa, Homily 3 on the Song; Epiphanius in the Physiologus, ch. 10; Philo of Carpasia on Cant. 1.; Ambrose in the Hexaemeron, book 5, ch. 19, and on Psalm. CXVIII.; Jerome, Against Jovinian, book 1, ch. 17; Justus of Urgel, col. 24 Theodoret, Aponius and Gregory the Great on Song 1:10; Eustathius on the Hexaemeron; Ildefonsus of Toledo, Sermon on the Purification of St Mary, if it is really his; Theophylact on Luke, ch. 2; Pisides, On the Creation of the World, line 998; Geoffrey, Abbot of Vendôme, Sermon 7; Michael Glycas, Annals, section 1; Bernard on the Song, Sermons 40 and 59, and the Sermon on the Nativity of St Mary, On the Aqueduct; the Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] on Lev 12:6; R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Ps 74:19; Damir in the chapter On شفنين [shifnīn] siphnin, that is, the turtledove. All these, I say, attribute to the turtledove what the older writers had written of the dove alone: that she is so devoted to one mate that, when he dies, she does not admit another. As a result, those who have commented on the Song of Solomon have not sufficiently understood why Solomon so often designates the bride by the name of his dove. Thus Song 2:14: My dove, in the clefts of the rock, etc. And Song 5:2: Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove. And Song 6:8: My dove, my perfect one, is but one, etc. By this, clearly, he wished to signify that she was loved by him alone, and that he in turn was uniquely dear to her, just as among doves there is a remarkable love between mates and a very firm law of marriage. If this was less true in the type, that is, in Solomon, who had wives by the ἑκατοντάδας [hekatontadas], it is nevertheless most true ἐν τῷ τυπωθέντι [en tō typōthenti], that is, in Christ: just as we acknowledge him alone as bridegroom, so he has one church, which he has betrothed to himself for ever in righteousness, judgment, lovingkindness and mercy, Hos 2:19. Hence the Apostle, 2 Cor 11:2: I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ. ¶

Quamvis המה strepere et tumultuari proprie sonat, tamen Hebraea מעי המו Cant. V, 4. Graeci reddunt ἡ κοιλία μου ἐθροήθη· et Hieronymus, Venter meus intremuit. Sic Ezech. VII, 16. ubi idem habet, Erunt in montibus quasi columbae omnes trepidi: et Iunius, Erunt in ipsis montibus velut columbae vallium, trepidantes omnes: verba, omnes trepidi, vel trepidantes omnes, respondent Hebraeis כלם המות. At Os. XI, 11. pro ויחרדו in Iunio legas, trepidi accurrent, etc. ut columba e terra Assyriae. In aliis trepidabunt, aut pavebunt, aut contremiscent sicut columba, etc. Quae coluinbae ingenium prorsus exprimunt. Columba II, 556 enim est animal pavidissimum. Hinc Varro de columbis Rust. lib. 3. cap. 7. Propter timorem naturalem summa loca in tectis captant. Et paullo post, Nihil enim timidius columba. Et apud Ovidium Metam. lib. 5. Fab. 10. Arethusa Nympha de se et insequente Alpheo,

Although המה properly means to make a noise and to be in tumult, nevertheless the Hebrew מעי המו in Song 5:4 the Greeks render ἡ κοιλία μου ἐθροήθη [hē koilia mou ethroēthē], and Jerome, My belly trembled. So too in Ezek 7:16, where the same Jerome has, They shall be on the mountains like doves, all trembling, and Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], They shall be on the mountains themselves like doves of the valleys, all trembling: the words all trembling correspond to the Hebrew כלם המות. And in Hos 11:11, for ויחרדו you may read in Junius, they shall come running in trepidation, etc., like a dove from the land of Assyria. In others: they shall tremble, or they shall fear, or they tremble like a dove, etc. These expressions exactly capture the dove's nature. For the dove II, 556 is a most fearful animal. Hence Varro on doves, On Agriculture, book 3, ch. 7: Because of their natural fear they seek the highest places on roofs. And a little later: For nothing is more timid than a dove. And in Ovid, Metam. book 5, Fable 10, the nymph Arethusa says of herself and of Alpheus pursuing her: ¶

Sic ego currebam, sic me ferus ille premebat, Ut fugere accipitrem penna trepidante columba, Et solet accipiter trepidas urgere columbas.

So I ran, so that wild one pressed upon me, As the dove flees the hawk on trembling wing, And as the hawk is wont to harry trembling doves. ¶

Et Trist. lib. 1. Eleg. 1.

And Tristia, book 1, Elegy 1: ¶

Terretur minimo pennae stridore columba, col. 24 Unguibus accipiter saucia facta tuis.

The dove, once wounded by your talons, hawk, col. 24 is terrified by the slightest whirring of a wing. ¶

Et Valerius Flaccus libro octavo Argonauticων de Medea,

And Valerius Flaccus, in the eighth book of the Argonautica, of Medea: ¶

Ecce! autem pavidae virgo de more columbae, Cum semper ingenti circumdata praepetis umbra, In quemcunque tremens hominem cadit. Haud secus illa, Icta timore gravi, mediam se misit, etc.

But lo! the maiden, after the manner of a frightened dove, When, encompassed by the huge shadow of a bird of prey, She falls trembling upon whatever man she meets. Just so she, Struck with heavy fear, threw herself into the midst, etc. ¶

Ut Chorus in Ajace Sophoclis versu 139.

As the Chorus in Sophocles' Ajax, line 139: ¶

— — — πεφόβημαι Πτηνῆς ὡς ὄμμα πελειάς.

— — — πεφόβημαι Πτηνῆς ὡς ὄμμα πελειάς. [pephobēmai / Ptēnēs hōs omma peleias.] ¶

Timeo ut volucris oculus columbae. Et alius poeta apud Suidam in Πελειάδες

I fear, like the eye of the winged dove. And another poet in the Suda, under Πελειάδες [Peleiades]: ¶

Δειλαί τοι δείλοισιν ἐφεδρεύουσι πέλειαι,

Δειλαί τοι δείλοισιν ἐφεδρεύουσι πέλειαι, [Deilai toi deiloisin ephedreuousi peleiai,] ¶

Timidae timidis insident columbae. Quo pertinet, quod columba τρήρων toties ab Homero dicitur, ἐπεὶ διὰ τὴν ἀθένειαν εὐλαβὴς ἡ ὄρνις αὕτη, τρεῖν δ' ἐστὶ τὸ εὐλαβεῖσθαι, quia propter imbecillitatem avis est meticulosa, nam τρεῖν est me- col. 25 tuere. Ita Athenaeus libro nono. Neque aliter vetus Homeri Scholiastes, Eu- stathius in Odyss. μ'. Hesychius, Suidas, Etymologus, etc. Et Homerum sic imitatur Apollonius, Argonauticων libro 3. versu 541.

Timid doves sit upon timid [eggs]. To this belongs the fact that the dove is so often called τρήρων [trērōn] by Homer, ἐπεὶ διὰ τὴν ἀθένειαν εὐλαβὴς ἡ ὄρνις αὕτη, τρεῖν δ' ἐστὶ τὸ εὐλαβεῖσθαι [epei dia tēn atheneian eulabēs hē ornis hautē, trein d' esti to eulabeisthai], because on account of its weakness this bird is fearful, for τρεῖν [trein] means to col. 25 fear. So Athenaeus in book 9. Nor otherwise the old Scholiast on Homer, Eustathius on Odyssey μ' [mu], Hesychius, Suidas, the Etymologicum, etc. And Apollonius imitates Homer thus, in book 3 of the Argonautica, verse 541: ¶

Τρήρων μὲν φεύγουσα βίην κίρκοιο πελειὰς, Ὑψόθεν Αἰσονίδεω πεφοβημένη ἔμπεσε κόλποις·

Τρήρων μὲν φεύγουσα βίην κίρκοιο πελειὰς, Ὑψόθεν Αἰσονίδεω πεφοβημένη ἔμπεσε κόλποις· [Trērōn men pheugousa biēn kirkoio peleias, Hypsothen Aisonideō pephobēmenē empese kolpois] ¶

Pavida quidem fugiens vim accipitris columba, ex alto terrefacta in Aesoni-dae sinum incidit.

A timid dove, fleeing the violence of a hawk, fell terrified from on high into the bosom of Aeson's son. ¶
