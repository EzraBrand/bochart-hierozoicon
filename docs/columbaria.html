II, 545 CAP. III. col. 17

II, 545 Chapter III. col. 17 ¶

De Columbarum sedibus.

On the dwelling places of doves. ¶

Columbae in columbariis habent ארבות arubboth, id est, fenestras, seu foramina, in quibus latere et nidificare solent. Es. LX, 8. Qui sunt isti, qui ut nubes volant, et ut columbae אל ארבותיהם, ad fenestras suas? Id est, לגו שובכיהון, ad columbaria sua, ut in Chaldaeo habetur. Sed in metu latent ubivis in fossis, in petris, in montibus, etc. Hinc Ierem. XLVIII, 28. Moabitae, qui prae metu hostium relictis urbibus suis, in rupium antris delitescunt, dicuntur esse כיונה תקנן בעברי פי פחת, ut columba, quae (in simili metu praeter naturam) nidificat in lateribus oris fossae: ubi Chaldaeus, כיונא דשבקא פום שובכה ונחתא ושריא בארעית גובא, ut columba, quae foramine columbarii relicto, descendit, ut habitet in ima fossa (1). Et Cant. II, 14. per columbam, quae II, 546 latet בחגוי הסלע בסתר המדרגה, in fissuris petrae, in latibulo praecipitii, Hebraei Israelitas intelligunt, quos in exitu ex Aegypto ab una parte persequebatur Pharao, ab altera concludebat desertum, anguibus plenum. Unde Chaldaeus interpres, הות מתילא כנישתא דישראל ליונתא דסגירא בחגוי טנרא והיוא מעיק לה מגיו וגצא מעיק לה מברא, Similis erat ecclesia Israelitica columbae, quae inclusa est in fissuris petrae. Et serpens illam intrinsecus obsidet, et accipiter extrinsecus. Quod R. Selomo pluribus persequitur. Sic apud Smyrnaeum lib. 12.

In dovecotes, doves have ארבות arubboth, that is, windows or openings, in which they usually hide and nest. Isa 60:8. Who are these that fly like clouds, and like doves אל ארבותיהם, to their windows? That is, לגו שובכיהון, to their dovecotes, as the Chaldee has it. But when afraid they hide anywhere: in pits, in rocks, in mountains, etc. Hence in Jer 48:28 the Moabites, who for fear of the enemy abandon their cities and hide in caves in the cliffs, are said to be כיונה תקנן בעברי פי פחת, like the dove that (in a similar fear, contrary to its nature) nests in the sides of the mouth of a pit. There the Chaldee has כיונא דשבקא פום שובכה ונחתא ושריא בארעית גובא, like a dove that leaves the opening of the dovecote and goes down to dwell at the bottom of a pit R1. And in Song 2:14 by the dove that II, 546 hides בחגוי הסלע בסתר המדרגה, in the clefts of the rock, in the hiding place of the cliff, the Hebrews understand the Israelites, whom at the exodus from Egypt Pharaoh pursued on one side, while on the other the desert, full of snakes, hemmed them in. Hence the Chaldee translator: הות מתילא כנישתא דישראל ליונתא דסגירא בחגוי טנרא והיוא מעיק לה מגיו וגצא מעיק לה מברא, The Israelite congregation was like a dove shut up in the clefts of the rock. And the serpent besieges it from within, and the hawk from without. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] develops this at greater length. So too in Quintus of Smyrna, book 12: ¶

Ἴρηξ σεῦε πέλειαν· ἐπειγομένη δ᾽ ἄρα κείνη Κηραμον ἐς πέτρης κατεδύσατο· — —

Ἴρηξ σεῦε πέλειαν· ἐπειγομένη δ᾽ ἄρα κείνη Κηραμον ἐς πέτρης κατεδύσατο· — — [Irēx seue peleian; epeigomenē d' ara keinē Kēramon es petrēs katedysato] ¶

Accipiter columbam persequebatur: illa autem, cum urgeretur, in scopuli cavernam se subduxit. Quod ex his Homeri sumptum Iliad. φ᾽. versu 495.

A hawk was pursuing a dove; and she, hard pressed, slipped into a hollow of the rock. This is taken from these lines of Homer, Iliad φ᾽ [phi], line 495: ¶

— — — φύγεν, ὥς τε πέλεια, Ἥ ῥάθ᾽ ὑφ᾽ ἴρηκος κοίλην εἰσέπτατο πέτρην·

— — — φύγεν, ὥς τε πέλεια, Ἥ ῥάθ᾽ ὑφ᾽ ἴρηκος κοίλην εἰσέπτατο πέτρην· [phygen, hōs te peleia, Hē rhath' hyph' irēkos koilēn eiseptato petrēn] ¶

Fugit ut columba, quae ab accipitre volavit in cavam petram, in antrum. Quamvis etiam extra metum columbas in antris nidificare suadent haec Poetae:

She fled like a dove that, before a hawk, has flown into a hollow rock, into a cave. Yet these lines of the Poet suggest that doves nest in caves even apart from fear: ¶

Qualis spelunca subito commota columba, Cui domus, et dulces latebroso in pumice nidi.

As a dove suddenly startled from a cave, whose home and sweet nests are in the hollow rock. ¶

Et quod in Diodoro Semiramis exposita fuisse legitur εἰς τινας ἐρήμους, καὶ πε-τρώδεις τόπους, ἐν οἷς πολλοῦ πλήθους περιστερῶν ἐννοσσεύειν εἰωθότος, in quibusdam desertis et petrosis locis, in quibus cum magna columbarum copia nidificare soleret, etc. Si quidem ibi agatur de columbis proprie dictis. Etiam Ezech. VII, 16. qui ex magna clade evaserunt, oberrare leguntur in montibus כיוני הגיאות, ut columbae convallium. Quae nempe metu aucupis, aut accipitris, naturali sede deserta, coguntur alio secedere, ut vitae suae consulant. Columbis convallium montanae opponuntur, quomodo ferae domesticis. Sic Hesychio Semiramis est περιστερὰ ὄρειος, quod jam ante est expositum. Et in Alcamus العزف Alozpho est الحمام الطورانية columba montana.

So too we read in Diodorus that Semiramis was exposed εἰς τινας ἐρήμους, καὶ πετρώδεις τόπους, ἐν οἷς πολλοῦ πλήθους περιστερῶν ἐννοσσεύειν εἰωθότος [eis tinas erēmous, kai petrōdeis topous, en hois pollou plēthous peristerōn ennosseuein eiōthotos], in certain desert and rocky places, in which, since a great number of doves used to nest there, etc., if indeed doves in the proper sense are meant there. Also in Ezek 7:16 those who escaped a great slaughter are said to wander on the mountains כיוני הגיאות, like doves of the valleys. These, that is, for fear of the fowler or the hawk, abandon their natural home and are forced to withdraw elsewhere to save their lives. Mountain doves are contrasted with doves of the valleys, as wild animals are with domestic ones. Thus in Hesychius, Semiramis is περιστερὰ ὄρειος [peristera oreios], as has already been explained. And in the Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] العزف [al-ʿazf] Alozpho is الحمام الطورانية [al-ḥamām al-ṭūrāniyya], mountain dove. ¶

Fallor, aut etiam columbae arborum, vel lucorum memorantur in titulo col. 18 Psal. LVI. in cujus versione puto interpretes hactenus frustra desudasse. Nam, ut veteres praeteream, qui in diversissima abeunt, quis Selomonem, aut Kimchium ferat, Hebraea verba על יונת אלם רחוקים, reddentes, de columba silentii remotorum, II, 547 ut ad Davidem referant? Nempe, ut ait Selomo, David tum erat inter Philistaeos, procul a terra Israelis. Et Kimchius columbam vocari addit, quia Os. VII, 11. columba vocatur fatua, et David se fatuum esse simulabat. Et columbam silentii, id est mutam; quia non loquebatur more suo, sed tanquam insanus. Nec multo magis probo virum magnum (2) e nostris, qui, sequutus auctores τοῦ Midras, eadem verba ita exponit: De anima vim patiente a manipulo longinquorum. Quae interpretatio, ut et prior, de qua diximus, Hebraeo textui vim facit tam manifestam, ut cuivis Hebraice vel mediocriter docto pateat, cur a me rejiciatur. His igitur relictis, dicam, quod res est. Aben Ezra recte vidit, verba יונת אלם רחוקים initium esse cantici, ad cujus modulos David hunc Psalmum composuit: sed ea reliquit ἀνερμήνευτα. Nos vero hic quaerimus, quid illa verba significent. Et certum est ex iis, prout leguntur a Masorethis, nullum posse commodum sensum erui. Itaque periti videant, annon iisdem literis manentibus, sed uno puncto mutato, sit consultius אלם elim, pro אלם elem, id est, Hirec pro Segol. Quo dato, sacrae cantionis, cujus modulos in hoc Psalmo sequitur David, initium hoc fuerit, Columba nemorum remotorum. Hoc fefellit interpretes, adeoque ipsos Masorethas, quod in אלם deest Iod, nota Pluralis numeri. Sed, ut recte observat Cl. Buxtorfius, Thesauri Grammatici libri primi capite nono, Iod saepe excidit ex Plurali, ut in תנינם, dracones, Deut. XXXII, 33. תמימם, integri, Levit. IX, 2. Addo solum exemplum similis Ellipseως in hac voce, quamvis in alia notione, באלם, in Diis fortibus, pro באלים, Exod. XV, 11. Porro יונת אלים, columba arborum, seu nemorum, est columba sylvestris, id est, palumbus, qui in sylvis degit, et in arboribus nidificat. Theocritus Idyll. 5.

Unless I am mistaken, doves of the trees, or of the groves, are also mentioned in the title col. 18 of Psal. LVI., in the rendering of which I think interpreters have so far laboured in vain. For, to pass over the ancients, who go off in the most diverse directions, who could tolerate Selomo or Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], who render the Hebrew words על יונת אלם רחוקים as concerning the dove of silence of those far off, II, 547 so as to refer them to David? For, as Selomo says, David was then among the Philistines, far from the land of Israel. And Kimchi adds that he is called a dove because in Hos 7:11 the dove is called silly, and David was feigning to be a fool; and a dove of silence, that is, mute, because he did not speak in his usual manner but like a madman. Nor do I much more approve of a great man R2 from among our own, who, following the authors of the Midrash, explains the same words thus: Concerning the soul suffering violence from a band of those far off. This interpretation, like the former one we have discussed, does such manifest violence to the Hebrew text that anyone even moderately learned in Hebrew will see why I reject it. Leaving these aside, then, I shall say what the matter is. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] rightly saw that the words יונת אלם רחוקים are the opening of a song to whose melody David composed this Psalm; but he left them ἀνερμήνευτα [anermēneuta]. Here, however, we are asking what those words mean. And it is certain that no suitable sense can be drawn from them as they are read by the Masoretes. Let the experts therefore consider whether, keeping the same letters but changing one vowel point, it would not be wiser to read אלם elim instead of אלם elem, that is, Hirec for Segol. Granted this, the opening of the sacred song whose melody David follows in this Psalm would be: The dove of the distant groves. What misled the interpreters, and even the Masoretes themselves, is that אלם lacks the Iod, the mark of the plural. But, as the distinguished Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] rightly observes in Thesaurus Grammaticus, book 1, ch. 9, Iod often drops out of the plural, as in תנינם, dragons, Deut 32:33, תמימם, unblemished, Lev 9:2. I add only an example of a similar ellipsis in this very word, though in another sense: באלם, among the mighty gods, for באלים, Exod 15:11. Further, יונת אלים, the dove of the trees, or of the groves, is the wild dove, that is, the wood pigeon (palumbus), which lives in woods and nests in trees. Theocritus, Idyll 5: ¶

Κἠγὼ μὲν δώσω τᾷ παρθένῳ αὐτίκα φάσσαν, Ἐκ τᾶς ἀρκεύθω καθελών· τηνεὶ γὰρ ἐφίσδει·

Κἠγὼ μὲν δώσω τᾷ παρθένῳ αὐτίκα φάσσαν, Ἐκ τᾶς ἀρκεύθω καθελών· τηνεὶ γὰρ ἐφίσδει· [Kēgō men dōsō tai parthenōi autika phassan, Ek tas arkeuthō kathelōn; tēnei gar ephisdei] ¶

Et ego quidem mox puellae palumbum dabo, ex Iunipero abreptum: ibi enim sedet. Nemesianus Ecloga secunda,

And I will soon give the girl a wood pigeon, taken from the juniper: for there it sits. Nemesianus, Eclogue 2: ¶

Praeterea tenerum leporem, geminasque palumbes, Nuper quae potui, sylvarum praemia, misi.

Besides, I have sent a tender hare and a pair of wood pigeons, the spoils of the woods, which I lately could. ¶

Auctor Philomelae,

The author of the Philomela: ¶

Plausitat arborea clamans de fronde palumbes.

The wood pigeon claps its wings, calling from the leafy tree. ¶

Proinde ramier Galli vocant a ramis arborum, quibus insidet. Idem de liviis quoque scribitur, quae Graecis πελειάδες. Ita Herodotus in Euterpe πελειά-δα liviam describit ἰζομένην ἐπὶ φηγὸν, fago insidentem. Et de turture Origenes II, 548 in Cantica Homil. 2. Semper in montium jugis et in arborum verticibus commoratur. Theodoretus quoque in Cant. 2. turturem dicit ἐν δένδροις ἀμφιλα-φέσι τὰς καλιὰς πηγνύναι, in umbrosis arboribus nidos construere. Et Chrysostomus in Homilia De turture, Ἐπὶ τῶν στερεμνίων πρέμνων τὴν καλίαν αὐτῶν πήξασαι, καὶ ἀφόβως ἐπωάζουσαι, τοὺς νεοττοὺς ἑαυτῶν ἐκεῖθεν πετάζουσι· Firmis arborum stipitibus nidum suum defigentes et secure incubantes, pullos suos ibi extendunt. Quod columbis, proprie dictis, est infrequens, non tamen sine exemplo. Hinc Anacreon Oda nona περιστερὰν dicit πέτασθαι ὄρη τε, καὶ κατ᾽ ἄγρους, καὶ δένδρεσιν καθίζειν, per montes et agros volare, et arboribus insidere. Et Horatius libri primi Oda secunda,

Accordingly the French call it ramier, from the branches (rami) of the trees on which it perches. The same is written also of the rock doves (liviae), which the Greeks call πελειάδες [peleiades]. Thus Herodotus in Euterpe describes a πελειάδα [peleiada], a rock dove, ἰζομένην ἐπὶ φηγὸν [izomenēn epi phēgon], perching on a beech. And of the turtledove Origen II, 548 on the Song of Songs, Homily 2: It always lingers on the ridges of mountains and on the tops of trees. Theodoret also, on Cant. 2, says that the turtledove ἐν δένδροις ἀμφιλαφέσι τὰς καλιὰς πηγνύναι [en dendrois amphilaphesi tas kalias pēgnynai], builds its nests in shady trees. And Chrysostom in the homily On the Turtledove: Ἐπὶ τῶν στερεμνίων πρέμνων τὴν καλίαν αὐτῶν πήξασαι, καὶ ἀφόβως ἐπωάζουσαι, τοὺς νεοττοὺς ἑαυτῶν ἐκεῖθεν πετάζουσι· [Epi tōn steremniōn premnōn tēn kalian autōn pēxasai, kai aphobōs epōazousai, tous neottous heautōn ekeithen petazousi] Fixing their nest on firm tree trunks and brooding securely, they spread out their young there. This is infrequent in doves properly so called, yet not without example. Hence Anacreon, Ode 9, says that a περιστερὰν [peristeran] πέτασθαι ὄρη τε, καὶ κατ᾽ ἄγρους, καὶ δένδρεσιν καθίζειν [petasthai orē te, kai kat' agrous, kai dendresin kathizein], flies over mountains and fields and perches on trees. And Horace, book 1, Ode 2: ¶

Piscium et summa genus haesit ulmo, Nota quae sedes fuerat columbis.

And the race of fishes clung to the top of the elm, which had been the familiar seat of doves. ¶

Et Virgilius libro sexto de columbis, Aeneae a Venere immissis,

And Virgil in book 6, of the doves sent to Aeneas by Venus: ¶

Sedibus optatis gemina super arbore sidunt. col. 19

They settle in the longed-for seat upon the twofold tree. col. 19 ¶

Et Suetonius in Octavio capite nonagesimo quarto, ubi de palma prope Mundam, quam ut omen victoriae D. Iulius conservari jusserat. Ex ea continuo enata soboles, adeo in paucis diebus adolevit, ut non aequipararet modo matricem, verum etiam obtegeret, frequentareturque columbarum nidis: quamvis id avium genus duram et prosperam frondem maxime vitet. Ex his planum est, quid sit in Psalmi titulo Columba arborum, aut nemorum remotorum, nempe ab urbibus et locis cultis (3).

And Suetonius in the Octavius, ch. 94, on the palm near Munda which the deified Julius had ordered to be preserved as an omen of victory: From it a shoot at once sprang up and in a few days grew so much that it not only equalled the parent tree but even overshadowed it, and was frequented by the nests of doves, although that kind of bird especially avoids hard and rough foliage. From these it is plain what the dove of the trees, or of the distant groves, in the title of the Psalm is: namely, distant from cities and cultivated places R3. ¶

Sed mihi praeplacet Grotii sententia, vocabula עברי פי - פחת intelligentis de lateribus II, 546 scissae rupis. Veterum interpretum hariolationes, quae nihil iuvant sententiae intelligentiam, enumeravit I. D. MICHAE-LIS in Observatt. Critt. et Philologg. ad Ierem. p. 256.

R1 Rosenmüller, II, 545 That the dove is here said to nest in the sides of the mouth of the hole, Gussetius, in his Lexicon p. 573, refers to the fact that doves are not accustomed to place their nest directly opposite the opening, but to one side or the other of the opening; hence those who build dovecotes open the windows of the cells not in their middle but at the outer edge of the face. But I much prefer the opinion of Grotius, who understands the words עברי פי - פחת of the sides II, 546 of a split rock. The guesses of the ancient interpreters, which contribute nothing to understanding the meaning, have been listed by J. D. Michaelis [=J. D. I. L. III. cap. XVII. not. 8.); sed in verbis ipsis explicandis frustra hodie laboretur. Quis enim ex his verbis: Sey Lob und Ehr, quae multis cantilenis inscripta inveniuntur, sanum sensum elicuerit, nisi cui notus sit primus versus cantici ejus, cuius moduli praescribuntur? Sine damno igitur ignorabimus sensum trium illorum vocabulorum Hebraicorum. Conjecturae tamen, a Bocharto allatae, nemo facile sagacitatis laudem denegaverit, maxime si cum ea contuleris, quae alii interpretes de verbis vexatissimis nugati sunt, quae, si lubet, in unum congesta legi possunt in PFEIFFERI Dubiis Vexatt. p. 605., porro in SIMONIS Lex. Hebr. sub vocc. אלם et עלם, atque in I. B. KOEHLERI Animadverss. Critt. in Psal. LVI. 1. in Repertorio für bibl. und morgenl. Litterat., Eichhornii cura instructo, P. VIII. p. 252. seqq.

R3 Rosenmüller, II, 548 I wholeheartedly agree with Bochart in saying that the words יונה אלם רחוקים are the beginning of a song to whose tune David composed this Psalm (what we noted above, at vol. 1, book 3, ch. 17, note 8, about a similar custom of the Syrians in titling songs should be recalled here); but in explaining the words themselves one would labour in vain today. For who could draw a sound meaning from the words Sey Lob und Ehr, which are found written above many hymns, unless he knew the first line of the song whose tune is prescribed? We may therefore remain ignorant of the sense of those three Hebrew words without loss. Yet no one would readily deny the conjecture put forward by Bochart the praise of acuteness, especially if one compares it with the trifles other interpreters have produced about these much-vexed words, which, if one wishes, can be read gathered together in Pfeiffer's Dubia Vexata p. 605, further in Simonis' Lex. Hebr. under the words אלם and עלם, and in J. B. Koehler's Animadverss. Critt. on Ps 56:1 in the Repertorium für bibl. und morgenl. Litterat., edited by Eichhorn, vol. 8, p. 252 ff.
