Explicantur Iobi reliqua de Struthione capitis trigesimi noni versibus decimo septimo, decimo octavo, decimo nono, vigesimo et vigesimo primo.

The rest of what Job says about the ostrich in chapter 39, verses 17, 18, 19, 20 and 21, is explained. ¶

Postquam in Iobo de struthione id explicavimus, quod erat longe difficillimum, col. 250 in caeteris minus laboris, plus erit utilitatis. Et post ἀκανθολογίαν, seu spinilegium, sequetur ἀνθολογία, aut verius spicilegium. Sacra autem verba sic habent: Sed relinquit in terra ova sua, et super pulverem calefiunt. Et obliviscitur, fore, ut illa pes comprimat, et agri fera conterat. Duriter tractat pullos suos, ac si non essent sui. Frustra laboravit, absque timore est. Quia privavit eam Deus sapientia, nec prudentiam ei impertitus est. Quo tempore in altum se attollit, ridet equum et sessorem ejus. Quae singula jam exponemus.

Now that we have explained in Job what was by far the most difficult point about the ostrich, col. 250 in the rest there will be less labour and more profit. After the ἀκανθολογίαν [akanthologian], or gathering of thorns, will follow the ἀνθολογία [anthologia], or more truly the gleaning of ears. The sacred words run thus: But she leaves her eggs on the ground, and they are warmed upon the dust. And she forgets that the foot may crush them, and the beast of the field trample them. She deals harshly with her chicks, as if they were not hers. She has laboured in vain, she is without fear. For God has deprived her of wisdom, and has not imparted understanding to her. When she raises herself on high, she laughs at the horse and its rider. We shall now expound these one by one. ¶

But she leaves her eggs on the ground] In Hebrew, כי תעזוב לארץ ביציה. The connection of this verse with the preceding one is as follows: "The ostrich exults in her wings, which she has swift and beautiful; but she is also ἄστοργος [astorgos], and neglects her eggs. She has some endowments of body, but a hard and pitiless mind." Thus כי in this place means the same as but, as in Gen 45:8. It was not you who sent me here, כי האלהים, but God. She leaves, תעזוב, is an enallage of number. For the plural had preceded: The wing of the ostriches exults. But nothing is more frequent than this enallage in the book of Job. So in this very passage: She leaves her eggs on the ground, etc. And she forgets that the foot may tread on it. And a little below, of the horse: Have you given the horse strength, etc. They paw in the valley, and he rejoices in strength. The same verb תעזוב, she leaves, though feminine in gender, nevertheless refers to the noun רננים renanim, of masculine ending. But יענים, ostriches, יעלים, ibexes, רמכים, mares, רחלים, ewes, and in this very place ביצים, eggs, although of masculine ending, are nevertheless feminine in gender R1. Further, the fact that this bird is said to leave her eggs on the ground plainly shows that it is the ostrich. For in the whole class of birds no other presents itself which is truly charged with this crime. II, 855 Nor do we need to run to the farthest Indians to learn the nature of the swan; although it is common in Holland and Britain, all the Dutch and British nevertheless deny that they have observed any such thing in the swan. Of the same stamp is what Clytus, the disciple of Aristotle, writes in Athenaeus, book 14, about the bird meleagris: Ἔστι δὲ ἄστοργον πρὸς τὰ ἔκγονα τὸ ὄρνεον, καὶ ὀλιγωρεῖ τῶν νεωτέρων, ὥστε ἀνάγκη τοῖς ἱερεῦσιν ἐπιμελεῖσθαι αὐτῶν [Esti de astorgon pros ta ekgona to orneon, kai oligōrei tōn neōterōn, hōste anankē tois hiereusin epimeleisthai autōn]; This animal has little affection for its offspring, and neglects its chicks so much that the priests must take care of them. For by the name meleagris Clytus describes the hen which we call the Indian or the Barbary hen, and the ancients the Numidian: both scarcely yield to the common hen in natural φιλοστοργίᾳ [philostorgia] toward their chicks. But this properly applies to the male. For although the common proverb imputes folly to the female, yet for all her folly she loves her chicks, feeds them and teaches them to fly, just like the other animals. Someone must also have deceived Jerome when he wrote of the dove on Os. VII.: The other birds hasten to protect their chicks even at the peril of their own life, etc. The dove alone does not grieve for its chicks when they are taken away, and does not seek them. Surely there is no one who does not know that the affection of doves, both toward their chicks and toward their mates, is most tender. Male and female sit on the eggs ἐκ διαδοχῆς [ek diadochēs], that is, by turns; and in like manner they συνθερμαίνουσι [synthermainousi], warm together, the hatched chicks. And if the female returns too sluggishly after the pains of laying, the male calls her back to the nest with a light chastisement. They share the care of ψωμίζειν [psōmizein] the chicks, putting predigested food into their mouths; and when taken away from the nest, however great the distance, they return to it out of love for their offspring. Hence it has come about that some have used doves as messengers, sending them back with a note attached to the town where they had sat on eggs or chicks. Hence Oppian, book 3 [Cyneget.], verse 115. ¶

Just as among birds too the ossifrages have a constant love for their chicks, and the doves with their heavy moaning. That ravens, too, hate their chicks, though very many assert it, is nevertheless a fiction, as was amply proved above a); and the eagle b) casts one egg or chick out of its nest for no other reason than that it is unequal to feeding and warming them all. And on the dust they are warmed] In Hebrew תחמם, is warmed; supply each one of the eggs. This is an enallage of number, of which we spoke just now. Others render warms, which does not fit with what precedes II, 858: She leaves her eggs, and warms them. For the ostrich does not warm the very eggs it has left. To avoid this difficulty Saint Jerome changed the person thus: When she abandons her eggs on the ground, will you perhaps warm them in the dust? For the verb תחמם can be second person masculine as well as third person feminine. But he added you perhaps of his own, and omitted the conjunction AND, which does not allow this change of person. For it is not correct to say, When she leaves her eggs on the ground, and you will warm them in the dust, for will you warm them in the dust? I have therefore preferred to render and on the dust it is warmed, although תחמם is in Piel, a conjugation which very often has an active meaning, but sometimes also an intransitive one. Further, the eggs, deserted by the mother, are said to be warmed, not with the heat that gives life, but with the heat that corrupts. For the interpreters' view that they are hatched by the heat of the sun alone, or by the warmth of the dust alone, without the mother's help, is a mere dream, which the commentators draw from Isidore, Albertus, Cardano, Vallés, etc. But the Arabs teach that these deserted eggs are corrupted and rot, as will be noted below. Yet, if we believe Damiri, ومن اعاجيب النعامة انها تضع بيضها طولا بحيث لو مد عليه خيط لم يجد لشي منه خروجا عن الاخر ثم تعطي كل بيضة نصيبها من الحضن ان كان كل بدنها لا يشتمل علي عدد بيضها [wa-min aʿājīb al-naʿāma annahā taḍaʿu bayḍahā ṭūlan bi-ḥaythu law mudda ʿalayhi khayṭ lam yajid li-shay minhu khurūjan ʿan al-ākhar thumma tuʿṭī kull bayḍa naṣībahā min al-ḥaḍn in kāna kull badanihā lā yashtamil ʿalī ʿadad bayḍihā] Among the marvels of the ostrich is also this, that it arranges its eggs lengthwise in such a way that, if a thread were stretched over them, not a single one would be found to project beyond another. When this is done, each egg receives its share of her incubation, although her body cannot cover them all. This happens, as they would have it, through the transmission of a power, as in a chain of iron rings, if one is rubbed on a magnet, the rest follow the first. ¶

And she forgets that a foot may crush them] The Greeks have σκορπιεῖ [skorpiei], and the Chaldee תבדרנה, may scatter them; but the Hebrew תזורה is from the verb זור, to crush, not from זרה, to scatter. The Syriac, with no sense at all, has ܘܛܥܝܐ ܕܪܓܠܐ ܕܦܪܚܬܐ ܐܝܬ ܠܗ̇ [וטעיא דרגלא דפרחתא אית לה], And she forgets that she has the foot of a bird: as if for רגל תזורה he had read רגל צפור לה; which is quite beside the point, and indeed false. For the ostrich does not have a toed foot like birds, but a cloven one like quadrupeds. The Philosopher, On the Parts of Animals, book 4, chapter 14: Δίπους μέν ἐστιν, ὡς ὄρνις· διχηλὸς δὲ ὡς τετράπους· οὐ γὰρ δακτύλους ἔχει, ἀλλὰ χηλάς [Dipous men estin, hōs ornis; dichēlos de hōs tetrapous; ou gar daktylous echei, alla chēlas]. It is two-footed, like a bird, but cloven-hoofed, like a quadruped; for it does not have toes, but a cloven hoof. So it truly does not have the foot of a bird. But the Hebrew is plain col. 254: She forgets that a foot may crush them; that is, she is so stupid that she does not consider II, 859 that it may happen that her eggs, which she has left on the ground, are trodden by the feet of wild beasts or of men. As if it made no difference to her whether they are crushed or not. As if Ulysses had not turned aside his plough when, as he was ploughing, his son was laid in the furrow he was drawing. ¶

She deals harshly with her young, as if they were not hers] The Chaldee renders it very differently: שיחנא על בניא דלא דילה, She broods over young that are not hers, that is, over the eggs of others, from which chicks may emerge. The Syriac has ܐܣܓܝܬ [אסגית], she multiplies young not her own. The Arabic follows the same sense, and it describes the habits of the ostrich very well; of it Damir says: متي وجدت بيضة نعامة اخري تحضنها وتنسي بيضها [matā wajadat bayḍat naʿāma ukhrā taḥḍinuhā wa-tansā bayḍahā] If she comes upon the eggs of another ostrich, she broods them and forgets her own. Yet the Hebrew does not bear this rendering. Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae] has: He makes her forget her young, in which they will not be. This I do not understand. Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] has: God, parching, hatches her young by another's work; that is, by the heat of the sun He warms the eggs which the ostrich had laid in the sand, until the chicks are hatched, as he also says in his Notes. For since he saw this asserted about the ostrich by learned men, he tried to bend the Hebrew text in that direction. But the simplest rendering is the one we have given, and it is therefore also the most ancient: She deals harshly with her young. In Hebrew, הקשיח בניה. The verb הקשיח occurs only here and in these words of Isa 63:17. Why hast thou let us stray from thy ways, O Lord, and תקשיח לבנו מיראתך, hardened our heart from thy fear? There the Septuagint has Ἐσκλήρυνας τὰς καρδίας ἡμῶν [Esklērynas tas kardias hēmōn]; and Jerome, Thou hast hardened our heart? Nor do the Syriac and the Arabic differ. For קשח, with Cheth, is the same as הקשה, with He, except that in the former the stronger aspiration seems to intensify the meaning. So also in Arabic قسي [qasā] means to be hard, but قسح [qasaḥa] to be very hard and to be rigid. Hence قساح [qassāḥ] hyena, because it has a rigid neck, not flexible in any way. And رمح قاسح [rumḥ qāsiḥ] is a hard and rigid spear, like the spear of Lausus, of which the Poet speaks in book 10 of the Aeneid: ¶

Advenit, et rigida Dryopem ferit eminus hasta.

Hence, then, applied to the mind, قسح [qasaḥa] or קשח means to be hard and unbending in mind; and הקשיח means either to be hardened or to deal harshly with someone R3. Accordingly Jerome here has: She was hard toward her young. And Augustine: She hardened herself against her young. And the Greeks: Ἀπεσκλήρυνε τὰ τέκνα αὐτῆς [Apesklēryne ta tekna autēs], that is, τὴν περὶ τὰ τέκνα φυσικὴν στοργήν [tēn peri ta tekna physikēn storgēn]: She hardened her natural affection toward her young, as Olympiodorus rightly explains. To this belong these words of Jeremiah, Lam 4:3: The daughter of my people is cruel, II, 860 like the ostriches in the desert. So too the fact that the ostrich is called תחמס in Hebrew and ظليم [ẓalīm] in Arabic, that is, impious, unjust, as we have already shown above c). That the masculine verb הקשיח is used here for the feminine will surprise only someone who does not know how frequent this change of gender is in Hebrew idiom, especially with animals. Thus Isa 14:11. תחתיך יצע רמה ומכסיך תולעה, Beneath thee the worm is spread, and the maggot covers thee. There the nouns are feminine, but the verbs masculine. Conversely, in Exod 8:2 [var. 6.] ותעל הצפרדע, And the frog came up; and in verse 14 [var. 16], ותהי הכנים, And there was the louse: feminine verbs are joined to masculine nouns. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], on Isaiah, says this is the custom of Scripture col. 255 when it speaks not of this or that animal, but על דרך כלל, universally, of the whole species, as here of the ostrich. Because God has deprived her of wisdom, nor has he imparted understanding to her] For although wisdom and understanding, if we wish to speak properly, belong only to human beings, who are endowed with reason, they are nevertheless also attributed to brute animals in Scripture. Thus the serpent is said to be the φρονιμώτατος [phronimōtatos] of all animals, Gen 3:1. And Christ himself bids us imitate its prudence, Matt 10:16 Isaiah too sets before us the example of the ox and the ass, which know their owner and their master's crib, Isa 1:3 Jeremiah sets before us the stork, which knows her appointed times, and the turtledove, the crane and the swallow, which observe the time of their coming, Jer 8:7. And Solomon sets before us the ant, so that, having considered her ways, we may be wise, Prov. II, 862 VI, 6. Indeed, although he was by far the wisest of men, he nevertheless praises four small creatures as חכמים מחכמים, exceedingly wise: namely, besides the ants, the choerogryllii, the locusts and the stellions, Prov 30:24. But certain brute animals are said to be exceedingly wise: 1. Because they are as wise as is necessary for their own preservation. 2. Because they excel among the other brutes in this wisdom of theirs. Yet this wisdom falls short of human wisdom by many degrees, since it lacks the light of reason and is determined to a single end. But here God is said to have denied even this wisdom, such as it is, to the ostrich. He alone is the author not only of human ingenuity but also of that of the brutes. To this they refer what is found in Job 38:36 according to the Chaldee and the Vulgate: Who gave the cock understanding? that is, no one other than God. Further, the Hebrew says of the ostrich כי השה אלוה חכמה, literally because God made her forget wisdom. For from נשה, to forget, the Hiphil is השה. But the Greeks have Κατεσιώπησεν αὐτῇ ὁ Θεὸς σοφίαν [Katesiōpēsen autē ho Theos sophian], God made wisdom silent for her. And the Syriac has ܐܣܓܝ [אסגי], and the Arabic أكثر [akthara], he multiplied, in a sense not only different but entirely contrary. The Greeks, that is, read instead of השה, with He, חשה, with Cheth, he was silent or he made silent. And the Syriac, followed by the Arabic, inserting a Gimel, read השגה, he multiplied. But the Chaldee rightly has אנשיה, he made her forget wisdom. Now God is said to bring about forgetfulness of things past when a great change takes place: for instance, if prosperity follows adversity, or adversity prosperity. Thus Joseph, raised to the height of honours in Egypt, named his firstborn Manasseh, from forgetting. For God, he says, has made me forget my former toil, Gen 41:51. Conversely, in the Babylonian captivity Jeremiah laments that God has caused the solemn feasts and sabbaths to be forgotten in Zion, Lam 2:6. What is said here, that God made the ostrich forget wisdom, is not to be taken in that sense, as though she had previously remembered it and some change had occurred in her nature. For here to forget is nothing other than not to remember. This forgetfulness is not privative but negative, just as Moses calls the state before the creation of light darkness, not as a privation of light but as a mere negation, Gen 1:3. Accordingly, in the following hemistich there is added ἐξηγητικῶς [exēgētikōs], ולא חלק לה בבינה, And he did not impart to her understanding, or prudence. Thus made her forget means did not impart. חלק, commonly to divide, here means to impart, like the Greek νέμειν [nemein]. Symmachus therefore rightly has Οὐκ ἔνειμεν αὐτῇ σύνεσιν [Ouk eneimen autē synesin], where the Elders have Οὐκ ἐπεμέρισεν αὐτῇ ἐν τῇ συνέσει [Ouk epemerisen autē en tē synesei], He did not distribute to her, or He did not give her a share in understanding. As it is said to Simon Magus in Actor. VIII, 21. Οὐκ ἔστι σοι μερὶς, οὐδὲ κλῆρος ἐν τῷ λόγῳ τούτῳ· [Ouk esti soi meris, oude klēros en tō logō toutō] You have neither part col. 257 nor lot in this matter; Acts 8:21. ¶

Albertus, Struthio alta est a pedibus ad dorsum circiter quinque, vel sex pedes: collum habet longissimum, etc. Itaque Arabes هيق heic, et هيقم heicam vocant a colli longitudine, et اسقف ascaph ab eminentia. Quomodo etiam Christianorum episcopos, sed ex Graeco deflexa voce. Et de herba استرال isterala est in altum crescere ut struthionis pullus, qui Arabice رال raal dicitur. II, 869 Struthio igitur, ita in altum se erigens, ridet equum, et sessorem ejus. col. 262 Ridet, id est, spernit; ut habet Graecus Scholiastes, Καταφρονοῦσα ἵππου σὺν τῷ ἀναβάτῃ διὰ τὴν ἰσχὺν, ὥς φασι, καὶ τὸ μέγεθος. Equum cum ascensore despiciens, propter vires, ut ajunt, et magnitudinem. Recte, nisi quod equum, et equitem, struthio spernere dicendus erat, non virium, sed celeritatis fiducia. Unde Plinius, Altitudinem equi insidentis equo excedunt, celeritate vincunt. Et Xenophon, libro primo Anabaseως, Cyri equites dicit onagros et capreas in Arabia cepisse, sed struthiones nullo modo potuisse assequi. Στρουθὸν δὲ οὐδεὶς ἔλαβεν· οἱ δὲ διώξαντες τῶν ἱππέων ταχὺ ἐπαύοντο· πολὺ γὰρ ἀπέσπα απο-φεύγουσα. Struthionem nemo cepit; et, qui insequebantur, cito cessabant: nam fuga longius se proripiebat. Quod in exercitu, multis equitum millibus, instructo notabile est. Ajunt quidem, in Africa, in equorum illo genere, quos Arabicos appellant, nonnullos reperiri, qui struthionem cursu assequantur, sed paucos admodum, eosque immensi pretii. Marmolius libri primi capite vigesimo tertio, Cavallos Barbaros son llamados en toda Europa los, que vienen de Berberia: mas ay una especie de cavallos en Africa, y en Egypto, y en las Arabias, y en toda Asia, que llaman cavallos Arabes, que son de casta de cavallos salvages, que se crian por los desiertos de Arabia, etc. Son est rañamente ligeros, y la mayor experiencia, que se haze de su ligereza es quando alcançan una fiera llamada Lamte, o a un abestruz. Porque este tal cavallos es apreciado en mil ducados de oro, o en cien camellos. Mas ay pocos dellos en Berberia. Id est, In tota Europa Barbari vocantur equi, e Barbaria advecti: sed in Africa, Aegypto, Arabia, et tota Asia una est equorum species, quos Arabicos appellant, ex eorum prosapia, qui in Arabiae desertis procreantur, etc. Ii sunt mire veloces: neque majus ullum est eorum celeritatis experimentum, quam cum feram, Lamten nomine, aut struthiocamelum assequuntur. Ideo talis equus mille ducatis aureis, aut centum camelis aestimatur. Sed in Barbaria tales pauci sunt. Et certe haec est Arabum communis sententia, nullum esse animal, quod struthionem cursu superet. In quam rem diserta sunt haec Alkazuinii, لا يصبقها شي من الحيوانات Nullum animalium illam antevertit. Quod qua ratione fiat, supra f) docuimus; et alas pro velo habentem in aëre natare quasi. Unde Oppianus libri terti Cynegeticῶn versu 500.

Whose size is immense, so that on its broad back it could carry a newborn boy. Pliny: They exceed the height of a horseman sitting on a horse. Albertus: The ostrich is about five or six feet tall from the feet to the back; it has a very long neck, etc. Therefore the Arabs call it هيق [hayq] heic and هيقم [hayqam] heicam from the length of its neck, and اسقف [usquf] ascaph from its height. This is also what they call the bishops of the Christians, but there the word is derived from the Greek. And of a plant, استرال [istarāl] isterala means to grow tall like the chick of an ostrich, which in Arabic is called رال [rāl] raal. II, 869 The ostrich, then, raising itself so high, laughs at the horse and its rider. col. 262 Laughs, that is, scorns, as the Greek Scholiast has it: Καταφρονοῦσα ἵππου σὺν τῷ ἀναβάτῃ διὰ τὴν ἰσχὺν, ὥς φασι, καὶ τὸ μέγεθος [kataphronousa hippou syn tō anabatē dia tēn ischyn, hōs phasi, kai to megethos]. Despising the horse with its rider, on account of its strength, as they say, and its size. Rightly, except that the ostrich should have been said to scorn horse and horseman not in reliance on its strength but on its speed. Hence Pliny: They exceed the height of a horseman sitting on a horse, and surpass him in speed. And Xenophon, in book 1 of the Anabasis, says that the horsemen of Cyrus caught wild asses and roe deer in Arabia, but could in no way overtake the ostriches: Στρουθὸν δὲ οὐδεὶς ἔλαβεν· οἱ δὲ διώξαντες τῶν ἱππέων ταχὺ ἐπαύοντο· πολὺ γὰρ ἀπέσπα αποφεύγουσα [strouthon de oudeis elaben; hoi de diōxantes tōn hippeōn tachy epauonto; poly gar apespa apopheugousa]. No one caught an ostrich; and those who pursued it soon gave up, for in its flight it drew far ahead. This is remarkable in an army equipped with many thousands of horsemen. It is said, indeed, that in Africa, among the breed of horses called Arabian, some are found that can overtake an ostrich in running, but very few, and these of immense price. Marmol, book 1, chapter 23: Cavallos Barbaros son llamados en toda Europa los, que vienen de Berberia: mas ay una especie de cavallos en Africa, y en Egypto, y en las Arabias, y en toda Asia, que llaman cavallos Arabes, que son de casta de cavallos salvages, que se crian por los desiertos de Arabia, etc. Son est rañamente ligeros, y la mayor experiencia, que se haze de su ligereza es quando alcançan una fiera llamada Lamte, o a un abestruz. Porque este tal cavallos es apreciado en mil ducados de oro, o en cien camellos. Mas ay pocos dellos en Berberia. That is: Throughout Europe the horses brought from Barbary are called Barbary horses; but in Africa, Egypt, Arabia and all Asia there is a breed of horses which they call Arabian, descended from those bred in the deserts of Arabia, etc. They are wonderfully swift, and there is no greater proof of their speed than when they overtake the wild beast called the Lamte, or an ostrich. Therefore such a horse is valued at a thousand gold ducats, or a hundred camels. But in Barbary there are few such. And certainly this is the common opinion of the Arabs, that there is no animal which surpasses the ostrich in running. On this point these words of Alkazuinius are explicit: لا يصبقها شي من الحيوانات [lā yaṣbiquhā shay min al-ḥayawānāt] None of the animals outruns it. How this comes about we have shown above f): that, using its wings as a sail, it swims, as it were, in the air. Hence Oppian, Cynegetica book 3, verse 500: ¶

Ἠέρος ὑψιπόροισιν ἐπιπλώουσι κελεύθοις,

Ἠέρος ὑψιπόροισιν ἐπιπλώουσι κελεύθοις, [Ēeros hypsiporoisin epiplōousi keleuthois,] ¶

Aëris sublimibus natant viis. Et mox, II, 870 Αὐτοῖσιν φορέουσιν ἴσον τάχος οἰωνοῖσιν,

They swim along the lofty paths of the air. And shortly after, II, 870 Αὐτοῖσιν φορέουσιν ἴσον τάχος οἰωνοῖσιν, [Autoisin phoreousin ison tachos oiōnoisin,] ¶

Ipsis avibus aequali sunt celeritate. Itaque Arabes multa cuderunt vocabula, quibus id designarent. Sic de struthione زازا zaza est capite, alis, et cauda elatis fluere potius quam incedere: رفرف raphrapha alas motitando currere, ut Tal- mudicum רפרף riphreph: كحص chahatsa, ire tam propere, ut visum prope fugiat. Atque ob id ipsum struthio عصوف id est, turbo vocatur, et aliis nominibus, minimum decem, a cursus rapiditate ductis (6). Quin ipsa sese ad cursum incitat spiculis quibusdam osseis, qui sub alis latent, et pro calcaribus illi sunt. De quibus multo plura facile esset adducere: sed haec docere sigillatim non est praesentis locis; et Sacri Scriptoris mentem explicasse nobis sufficit.

They are of equal speed with the birds themselves. Accordingly the Arabs coined many words to express this. Thus of the ostrich, زازا [zāzā] zaza means to flow rather than walk, with head, wings and tail raised; رفرف [rafrafa] raphrapha, to run while flapping the wings, like the Talmudic רפרף riphreph; كحص [kaḥaṣa] chahatsa, to go so swiftly as almost to escape the sight. And for this very reason the ostrich is called عصوف [ʿaṣūf], that is, whirlwind, and by other names, at least ten, derived from the rapidity of its running R6. Indeed it spurs itself on to run with certain bony spikes which lie hidden under its wings and serve it as spurs. On these it would be easy to adduce much more; but to set this out point by point is not the business of the present place, and it is enough for us to have explained the meaning of the Sacred Writer. ¶
