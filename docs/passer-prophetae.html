II, 727 col. 150 CHAPTER XXII. ¶

Prophetarum loca novem, quae ad Passeres referunt nonnulli interpretes, plerumque parum certa. I. Levit. XIV. passim. II. Iob. XL, 24. III. Psal. X, 1. IV. Psal. LXXXIV, 4. V. Psal. CII, 8. VI. Psal. CIV, 16. VII. Psal. CXXIV, 7. VIII. Eccles. XII, 4. IX. Thren. III, 52.

Nine passages of the Prophets which some interpreters refer to sparrows, for the most part rather uncertain. I. Levit. XIV. passim. II. Job 40:24. III. Ps 10:1. IV. Ps 84:4. V. Ps 102:8. VI. Ps 104:16. VII. Ps 124:7. VIII. Eccl 12:4. IX. Lam 3:52. ¶

Inter interpretes non convenit satis, quibus Scripturae locis צפור tsippor sumatur pro passere. Nam ex novem locis, quae proferuntur, pleraque sunt valde dubia.

Interpreters do not sufficiently agree in which passages of Scripture צפור tsippor is taken for the sparrow. For of the nine passages that are adduced, most are very doubtful. ¶

I. Primus est Levit. XIV. initio capitis, et in fine, ubi agitur de duabus צפורים tsipporim, quarum una caesa, altera emissa purgabatur leprosus, et domus etiam lepra infecta. Ibi tsipporim esse passeres, plerique volunt, quomodo reddunt Hieronymus, Pagninus, Arias, Genevenses, alii. Sed aves in genere significari, constabit haec attente legenti versu quarto, Et accipiat qui mundandus est שתי צפורים חיות טהרות, duas tsipporim vivas mundas. Quae in passerem non quadrant, sive mundus fuisse statuatur, aut immundus. Mundus enim dici non potuit, si ex Lege fuit immundus. Contra, si fuit mundus, Lex incassum jusserit capi passeres mundos, cum immundi nulli fuerint. Mundas columbas, col. 151 aut turtures offerri nusquam jubetur; quia omnes erant mundae. Apparet igitur, צפורים tsipporim ibi esse aves in genere, quarum cum aliae mundae, aliae immundae fuerint, mundas accipi Deus jubet, puta columbas, aut turtures, quae vocantur הצפור Gen. XV, 10. aut alias quasvis, modo mundae sint et ad esum concessae juxta legem Deut. XIV, 11. כל צפור טהרה תאכלו, Omnem tsippor mundam comedetis. Itaque gallinas etiam a nonnullis hic intelligi, supra docuimus, atque eo referri posse Graecum ὀρνίθια. Nec Hebraei speciem designant, modo sint צפרים דרור טהורות, aves liberae et mundae. De hujus purificationis ritu multa habet insignia Maimonides in tractatu De lepra capite undecimo, quae non sunt hujus loci.

I. The first is Levit. XIV., at the beginning of the chapter and at the end, where it treats of two צפורים tsipporim, by which, one being killed and the other released, the leper was purified, and also a house infected with leprosy. Most hold that the tsipporim there are sparrows, as Jerome, Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], the Genevans and others render it. But that birds in general are meant will be clear to anyone who reads attentively verse four: And let him that is to be cleansed take שתי צפורים חיות טהרות, two living clean tsipporim. This does not fit the sparrow, whether it is held to have been clean or unclean. For it could not be called clean if by the Law it was unclean. Conversely, if it was clean, the Law would have commanded in vain that clean sparrows be taken, since there were no unclean ones. Nowhere is it commanded that clean doves col. 151 or turtledoves be offered, because all were clean. It appears, therefore, that צפורים tsipporim there are birds in general; and since some of these were clean and others unclean, God commands that clean ones be taken, for instance doves or turtledoves, which are called הצפור in Gen 15:10, or any others, provided they are clean and permitted as food according to the law of Deut 14:11. כל צפור טהרה תאכלו, Every clean tsippor you shall eat. Thus, as we showed above, some understand hens here too, and the Greek ὀρνίθια [ornithia] can be referred to this. Nor do the Hebrews specify a species, provided they are צפרים דרור טהורות, free and clean birds. On the rite of this purification Maimonides [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] has much that is notable in the treatise On Leprosy, chapter 11, which does not belong here. ¶

II. Secundus locus est in descriptione τοῦ Leviathan Iob. XL, 24. (al. 29.), et Hebraice sic habet: An eo ludes כצפור, ut tsippor? Et ligabis eum puellis tuis? Et in Graeca versione, Παίξῃ δὲ ἐν αὐτῷ ὥσπερ ὀρνέῳ; ἢ δήσεις αὐτὸν ὥσπερ col. 152 στρουθίον παιδίῳ; An ludes eo tanquam ave? et ligabis eum ut passerem puero? Et in Arabica, هل تضحك عليه مثل العصفور An ludes eo tanquam passere, etc. Quod ad diabolum referunt, qui mysteria consectantur, II, 728 quasi per baptismum et conversionem nobis vinctus tradatur, ut puero passerculus. Origenes de fideli homine, Ὅ γε δράκων αὐτῷ ὥσπερ στρουθίον πα-ραδέδοται παιδίῳ· Draco illi ut passer puero traditus est. Et paullo post, Τῇ χάριτι τοῦ Κυρίου, ὡς στρουθίον ὑποχείριον, ὁ Σατανᾶς δεδεμένος γεγένηται τοῖς νηπίοις, καὶ θηλάζουσιν, ὧν ἐκ στόματος κατηρτίσατο αἶνον ὁ Θεός· Domini gratia, ut passer mansuetus, Satan vinctus est pueris et lactentibus, ex quorum ore Deus perfecit laudem suam. Chrysostomus, Δήσας τὸν ἐχθρὸν ὁ Κύριος ἡμῶν δέδωκε καταπαίζειν αὐτοῦ, ὡς στρουθίου, τοῖς στραφεῖσι, καὶ γενομένοις ὡς τὰ παιδία. Conversis, et iis, qui ut infantes facti sunt, Dominus noster vinctum adversarium tanquam passerem illudendum tradidit. Quo alludens auctor vitae Pachomii capite quadragesimo nono diabolis haec tribuit, Ex quo mirabilis incarnatio Christi facta est in terris, nos sine viribus prorsus existere coepimus; Ita, ut ab his, qui credunt in nomine ejus, illudamur ut passeres. Quibus missis solum quaerimus, an Hebraea bene reddantur: An eo ludes tanquam passere? et ligabis eum pullis tuis? Et commodam hic esse mentionem passeris, suadebit Catullianum illud notissimum,

II. The second passage is in the description of the Leviathan, Job 40:24 (var. 29.), and in Hebrew it reads thus: Will you play with him כצפור, as with a tsippor? And will you bind him for your girls? And in the Greek version: Παίξῃ δὲ ἐν αὐτῷ ὥσπερ ὀρνέῳ; ἢ δήσεις αὐτὸν ὥσπερ col. 152 στρουθίον παιδίῳ; [Paixē de en autō hōsper orneō; ē dēseis auton hōsper strouthion paidiō?] Will you play with him as with a bird? and will you bind him like a sparrow for a child? And in the Arabic: هل تضحك عليه مثل العصفور [hal taḍḥaku ʿalayhi mithla al-ʿuṣfūr] Will you play with him as with a sparrow, etc. Those who pursue mysteries refer this to the devil, as though through baptism and conversion he were handed over to us bound, II, 728 like a little sparrow to a child. Origen, of the faithful man: Ὅ γε δράκων αὐτῷ ὥσπερ στρουθίον παραδέδοται παιδίῳ· [Ho ge drakōn autō hōsper strouthion paradedotai paidiō] The dragon has been handed over to him as a sparrow to a child. And shortly after: Τῇ χάριτι τοῦ Κυρίου, ὡς στρουθίον ὑποχείριον, ὁ Σατανᾶς δεδεμένος γεγένηται τοῖς νηπίοις, καὶ θηλάζουσιν, ὧν ἐκ στόματος κατηρτίσατο αἶνον ὁ Θεός· [Tē chariti tou Kyriou, hōs strouthion hypocheirion, ho Satanas dedemenos gegenētai tois nēpiois, kai thēlazousin, hōn ek stomatos katērtisato ainon ho Theos] By the grace of the Lord, like a tame sparrow, Satan has been bound for children and sucklings, out of whose mouth God has perfected his praise. Chrysostom: Δήσας τὸν ἐχθρὸν ὁ Κύριος ἡμῶν δέδωκε καταπαίζειν αὐτοῦ, ὡς στρουθίου, τοῖς στραφεῖσι, καὶ γενομένοις ὡς τὰ παιδία. [Dēsas ton echthron ho Kyrios hēmōn dedōke katapaizein autou, hōs strouthiou, tois strapheisi, kai genomenois hōs ta paidia.] To those who have been converted and have become as infants, our Lord has handed over the adversary, bound, to be mocked like a sparrow. Alluding to this, the author of the life of Pachomius, chapter 49, attributes these words to the devils: Since the wonderful incarnation of Christ took place on earth, we have begun to exist wholly without strength; so that we are mocked like sparrows by those who believe in his name. Leaving these aside, we ask only whether the Hebrew is well rendered: Will you play with him as with a sparrow? and will you bind him for your young ones? That a mention of the sparrow is fitting here is suggested by that very well-known line of Catullus, ¶

Passer deliciae meae puellae
Quicum ludere, quem in sinu tenere,
Quoi primum digitum dare adpetenti
Et acris solet incitare morsus.

Sparrow, darling of my girl, with whom she is wont to play, whom to hold in her lap, to whom, as it seeks it, to give her fingertip and to provoke its sharp bites. ¶

Unde Arnobius libro septimo, ut parvuli pusiones (ut animis parcant, abstineantque ploratibus) passerculos, populos, panes accipiunt, quibus avocare se possint. Et Arabs poeta, quem citat Ibno Rasik in libro, cui titulus غرايب garaibo, id est, Peregrinitates:

Hence Arnobius, book 7: as little boys (to spare their feelings and keep from crying) are given little sparrows, poplar toys, loaves, with which they can divert themselves. And an Arab poet, whom Ibno Rasik cites in the book entitled غرايب [gharāyib] garaibo, that is, Curiosities: ¶

كعصفورة في كف طفل يسوخها * حياض الردي والطفل يلهو ويلعب *

كعصفورة في كف طفل يسوخها * حياض الردي والطفل يلهو ويلعب * [ka-ʿuṣfūratin fī kaffi ṭiflin yasūkhuhā * ḥiyāḍa al-radī wa-l-ṭiflu yalhū wa-yalʿabu *] ¶

Ut passer foemina in manu pueri, quam is in malis luteribus demergens jocatur et ludit. Tamen in Iobo nihil necesse est praecise passerem intelligere, cam aliae aves non desint, quibus pueri et puellae aeque oblectentur. Plautus in Captivis Actu quinto,

Like a hen sparrow in a boy's hand, which he plunges into the evil troughs while he jests and plays. Yet in Job there is no need to understand the sparrow precisely, since there is no lack of other birds with which boys and girls are equally amused. Plautus in the Captivi, Act 5: ¶

— — quasi patriciis pueris aut monedulae,
Aut anates, aut coturnices dantur, qui cum lusitent.

— — as jackdaws, or ducks, or quails are given to patrician boys to play with. ¶

Plinius ad Clementem de Reguli filio libri quarti Epistola secunda, Habebat puer mannulos multos, et vinctos, et solutos, habebat canes majores minoresque, habebat luscinias, psittacos, merulas. Iulius Capitolinus in Clodio Albino, Septem aquilae parvae de nido allatae sunt, et quasi ad jocum circa cunas pueri constitutae. Pausanias in Corinthiacis, Κόκκυγα δὲ ἐπὶ τῷ σκήπτρῳ καθῆσθαί φασι, λέγοντες II, 729 τὸν Δία, ὅτε ἤρα παρθένου τῆς Ἥρας ἐς τοῦτον τὸν ὄρνιθα ἀλλαγῆναι· τὴν δὲ ἅτε παίγνιον θηρᾶσαι· Cucullum vero avem idcirco sceptro Iovis ajunt impositum, quod virginis Iunonis amore captus Iupiter in eam se avem verterit, quam puella tanquam ludicrum captarit. Unde Iupiter Coccygius, de quo idem infra meminit. Itaque Iobi locum, quem diximus, Hieronymus ita explicat: Nunquid illudes ei quasi avi? aut ligabis eum ancillis tuis? nulla passeris mentione facta. Atqui idem sequuntur plerique recentiorum.

Pliny, writing to Clemens about the son of Regulus in Letter 2 of book 4: "The boy had many ponies, both harnessed and unharnessed; he had dogs large and small; he had nightingales, parrots, blackbirds." Julius Capitolinus in Clodius Albinus: "Seven small eagles were brought from the nest and placed, as if in play, around the boy's cradle." Pausanias in the Corinthiaca: Κόκκυγα δὲ ἐπὶ τῷ σκήπτρῳ καθῆσθαί φασι, λέγοντες II, 729 τὸν Δία, ὅτε ἤρα παρθένου τῆς Ἥρας ἐς τοῦτον τὸν ὄρνιθα ἀλλαγῆναι· τὴν δὲ ἅτε παίγνιον θηρᾶσαι· [Kokkyga de epi tō skēptrō kathēsthai phasi, legontes ton Dia, hote ēra parthenou tēs Hēras es touton ton ornitha allagēnai; tēn de hate paignion thērasai] "They say the cuckoo sits on the sceptre of Jove because Jupiter, seized with love for the maiden Juno, turned himself into that bird, which the girl caught as a plaything." Hence Jupiter Coccygius, whom the same author mentions further on. Accordingly Jerome explains the passage of Job we mentioned thus: "Will you play with him as with a bird? Or will you bind him for your maidservants?", making no mention of a sparrow. And most of the more recent interpreters follow the same. ¶

III. Tertius locus est Psal. X, 1. (Hebraeis XI, 1.), ubi Graeca versio, Μεταναστεύου ἐπὶ τὰ ὄρη ὡς στρουθίον· Et Vulgata, Transmigra in montem sicut passer. Arabs quoque مثل العصفور, ut passer. Et Apollinaris in col. 153 Metaphrasi,

III. The third passage is Ps 10:1 (in the Hebrew XI, 1.), where the Greek version has Μεταναστεύου ἐπὶ τὰ ὄρη ὡς στρουθίον· [Metanasteuou epi ta orē hōs strouthion], and the Vulgate, "Flee to the mountain like a sparrow." The Arabic also has مثل العصفور [mithl al-ʿuṣfūr], like a sparrow. And Apollinaris in col. 153 his Metaphrase: ¶

Στρουθὸν ὀρεσσιβότοισιν ὅπως θρώσκοντα νομῆσι,

Στρουθὸν ὀρεσσιβότοισιν ὅπως θρώσκοντα νομῆσι, [Strouthon oressibotoisin hopōs thrōskonta nomēsi] ¶

Ut passerem montanis salientem pascuis. Et Theodoretus in Commentario, Τί δή ποτέ φησι παραινεῖτέ μοι φυγεῖν, καὶ τὰ ὄρη περινοστεῖν στρουθίου δίκην ἐπτοη-μένου; Quorsum, inquit, me hortamini, ut fugiam, et montes pererrem more passeris exterriti? Sed Hieronymus melius, ut quidem puto, Transvola in montem, ut avis, ut et plerique recentiores. Quia passer avis montana non est. Et David, de quo hic agitur, Saulem dicit se persequi, ac si quis persequeretur, non passerem, sed avem Kore in montibus, 1 Sam. XXVI, 20. id est, rusticulam, ut docuimus. Frustra objeceris, avem esse juxta Larium in Italia lacum, quam passerem montanum vocant. Neque enim illa avis vere est passer, sed e merularum genere, ut pluribus probat Gesnerus pag. 608. Nec magis ad rem est hic Galeni locus De ingenio sanitatis cap. 2. Ex avibus laudantur perdices cum montanis omnibus passeribus. Neque enim ibi intelliguntur passeres, proprie dicti, qui calidissimi sunt, et libidinem incitant, sed ex avibus minutis in genere eae, quae montanae sunt. Minutas enim aves quandoque στρουθία vocari, in confesso est. Moschopulus, Περὶ σχεδῶν pag. 178. Τρώγλη· ἡ τρύπα, ἀφ᾽ οὗ τρωγλίτης, ὃς καὶ στρουθὸς λέγεται· στρουθία δὲ οὐδετέρως πάντα τὰ μικρὰ τῶν ὀρνίθων. Ita etiam Phavorinus in Στρουθία.

"Like a sparrow leaping in mountain pastures." And Theodoret in his Commentary: Τί δή ποτέ φησι παραινεῖτέ μοι φυγεῖν, καὶ τὰ ὄρη περινοστεῖν στρουθίου δίκην ἐπτοημένου; [Ti dē pote phēsi paraineite moi phygein, kai ta orē perinostein strouthiou dikēn eptoēmenou?] "Why," he says, "do you urge me to flee and wander over the mountains like a frightened sparrow?" But Jerome does better, as I at least think, with "Fly over to the mountain like a bird", as do most of the more recent interpreters. For the sparrow is not a mountain bird. And David, who is the subject here, says that Saul is pursuing him as one would hunt, not a sparrow, but the bird Kore in the mountains, 1 Sam 26:20, that is, the woodcock, as we have shown. It would be vain to object that near Lake Larius in Italy there is a bird which they call the mountain sparrow. For that bird is not truly a sparrow, but of the blackbird kind, as Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] proves at length, p. 608. Nor is this passage of Galen, De ingenio sanitatis ch. 2, more to the point: "Among birds, partridges are praised, together with all mountain sparrows." For there not sparrows properly so called are meant, which are very hot and incite lust, but those small birds in general that live in the mountains. For it is agreed that small birds are sometimes called στρουθία [strouthia]. Moschopulus, Περὶ σχεδῶν [Peri schedōn] p. 178: Τρώγλη· ἡ τρύπα, ἀφ᾽ οὗ τρωγλίτης, ὃς καὶ στρουθὸς λέγεται· στρουθία δὲ οὐδετέρως πάντα τὰ μικρὰ τῶν ὀρνίθων. [Trōglē; hē trypa, aph᾽ hou trōglitēs, hos kai strouthos legetai; strouthia de oudeterōs panta ta mikra tōn ornithōn.] So also Phavorinus under Στρουθία [Strouthia]. ¶

IV. Quartus locus est Ps. LXXXIV, 4. גם צפור מצאה בית, pro quo Graeci, Καὶ γὰρ στρουθίον εὗρεν ἑαυτῷ οἰκίαν. Et Arabs eodem sensu, لان العصفور وجد له مسكنا Et Vulgatus, Etenim passer invenit sibi domum. Et Hieronymus, Siquidem passer invenit domum. Et Kimchius in Lexico probat hoc loco, צפור tsippor non esse nomen generale ad omnes aves, sed certae speciei peculiare, quia in hemistichio sequente דרור deror est hirundo, ut is quidem asserit. Itaque, inquit, אחר שאחרון פרט הראשון גם כן פרט, II, 730 quandoquidem posterius (id est, דרור deror) peculiaris avis nomen est, ita etiam prius (nempe צפור tsippor). Proinde in Commentariis idem statuit, צפור ibi esse aviculam, quam Hispani vocant פשׂרא paxaro, id est, passerem. Ita igitur reddendus est hic versus ex mente Kimchii: Etiam passer invenit domum, et hi- rundo nidum sibi, ubi poneret pullos suos, nempe altaria tua, o Deus exercituum. Cui sententiae nos accedimus, nisi quod דרור deror pro hirundine palumbum interpretati sumus. Et altarium nomine per senecdochen partis pro toto taberna- culum intelleximus, ad cujus trabes nidulati sint tam passeres, quam palumbi, ut jam alibi dictum.

IV. The fourth passage is Ps. LXXXIV, 4. גם צפור מצאה בית, for which the Greeks have Καὶ γὰρ στρουθίον εὗρεν ἑαυτῷ οἰκίαν [Kai gar strouthion heuren heautō oikian]. And the Arabic in the same sense: لان العصفور وجد له مسكنا [li-anna al-ʿuṣfūr wajada lahu maskanan]. And the Vulgate translator: "For the sparrow has found itself a home." And Jerome: "Since the sparrow has found a home." And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon proves from this passage that צפור tsippor is not a general name for all birds but proper to a certain species, because in the following hemistich דרור deror is the swallow, as he at least asserts. "Therefore," he says, אחר שאחרון פרט הראשון גם כן פרט, II, 730 "since the latter (that is, דרור deror) is the name of a particular bird, so also is the former (namely צפור tsippor)." Accordingly in his Commentaries he likewise holds that צפור there is the little bird which the Spaniards call פשׂרא paxaro, that is, the sparrow. So this verse is to be rendered according to Kimchi's mind: "Even the sparrow has found a home, and the swallow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young, namely your altars, O God of hosts." We agree with this view, except that we have interpreted דרור deror as the wood pigeon instead of the swallow. And by the name altars, by synecdoche of part for whole, we have understood the tabernacle, on whose beams both sparrows and wood pigeons nested, as has already been said elsewhere. ¶

V. The fifth passage is Ps 102:8 "I have kept watch, and I have become כצפור בודד על גג", where the Greeks have ὡσεὶ στρουθίον μονάζων ἐπὶ δώματι· [hōsei strouthion monazōn epi dōmati], and the Vulgate translator, "like a solitary sparrow on the roof"; and one of the Arabic versions, "I was in my solitude كالعصفور [ka-l-ʿuṣfūr] like a sparrow upon the roof"; and the other, "I have become like a sparrow that stays alone upon the roof." And Apollinaris: ¶

Ἢ στρουθόν τιν᾽ ἄϋπνον ἐόντ᾽ ἐν δώματι μοῦνον,

Ἢ στρουθόν τιν᾽ ἄϋπνον ἐόντ᾽ ἐν δώματι μοῦνον, [Ē strouthon tin᾽ aypnon eont᾽ en dōmati mounon] ¶

Aut passerem aliquem, qui insomnis est in tecto solus. Nempe Damir supra passerem nidificare dixit تحت السقوف خوف من الجوارح sub tecto, rapacium avium metu. Et Suidas in Νυκτικόραξ passerem describit tanquam δειλὸν ὄρνεον, timidam avem; quaeque ὑπὸ ἀγωνίας ἐλαύνει τὸν ὕπνον, prae sollicitudine somnum repellat. Accordingly, like many of the ancients, nearly all the more recent interpreters, Latin, French, Italian, German, Dutch and English, understand the sparrow here. Jerome, however, renders it otherwise: I was like a solitary bird upon the roof. Nor is there any of the Hebrews who points out that the sparrow is meant here, as in the preceding passage. And indeed I think nothing is further from the Prophet's mind. For these words belong entirely to the description of a nocturnal bird. I am surprised that this has not yet occurred to the interpreters, since all the circumstances of the passage openly proclaim it. For whoever speaks in this Psalm, since, worn out by heavy grief, he was fleeing the company of men and pouring out continual groans from the depths of his breast, compares himself with funereal birds, which are both solitary and have something mournful and ominous in their voice. By reason of the voice of my groaning, he says, my bones cleave to my flesh. I am become like a pelican of the wilderness. I was like an owl (or II, 731 a horned owl) of the desert places. So he speaks in the two verses immediately preceding. Then, continuing the comparison he has begun, he says: I kept watch, and was like a solitary bird upon the roof. I kept watch, that is, I spent a sleepless night. Accordingly the Chaldee has there שקדת כל ליליא, I kept watch all night. That is, like the light-shunning nocturnal birds, which, because they hate the light, after hiding by day come out of their hiding places at night to watch for prey. The Philosopher, Hist. book 9, ch. 34: Γλαῦκες δὲ, καὶ νυκτικόρακες, καὶ τὰ λοιπὰ, ὅσα τῆς ἡμέρας ἀδυνατεῖ βλέπειν, τῆς νυκτὸς μὲν θηρεύοντα τὴν τροφὴν αὐτοῖς πορίζεται· [Glaukes de, kai nyktikorakes, kai ta loipa, hosa tēs hēmeras adynatei blepein, tēs nyktos men thēreuonta tēn trophēn autois porizetai] Owls and nycticoraces, and the other birds that cannot see by day, obtain their food by hunting at night. But why should the sparrow be called solitary, since it is a gregarious bird and nests under the roofs of houses? I admit that within the sparrow kind some are wild. Hence Alexander of Myndus teaches, in Athenaeus book 9, δύο γένη εἶναι τῶν στρουθῶν, τὸ μὲν ἥμερον, τὸ δὲ ἄγριον [dyo genē einai tōn strouthōn, to men hēmeron, to de agrion], that there are two kinds of sparrows, one domestic, the other wild. But wild sparrows do not betake themselves to the roofs of houses, as does the bird of which the Psalmist speaks, whom he describes upon the roof. Therefore, if a sparrow is meant, it cannot be the wild sparrow but only the domestic one. They answer that the domestic sparrow is called solitary because it has been bereaved of its mate. This might be tolerable if the sparrow, like the turtledove or the dove, remained widowed after losing its spouse. But sparrows live by other customs: it is a most lustful animal, so given to Venus that it covers the female, if not a hundred times, as the Arabs report, at least seven times in an hour. Hence the poets yoke it to the chariot of Venus. Bound by no law, then, it rushes into promiscuous love and has no fixed spouse. So, as it was never a husband, neither does it become a widower. That is why the books of the Philosophers say nothing of a solitary sparrow. And those err by the whole breadth of heaven who derive the French name of the sparrow from the Greek μόνιος [monios]. For μόνιος [monios] is an epithet of the boar, not of the sparrow. And to us moineau is something quite different, namely μοναχίσκος [monachiskos], monachulus [little monk]. By this name the male is called, because, like monks, it seems to be hooded. Something else again is the passer solitarius [solitary sparrow] of the people of Trent. For it belongs not to the sparrows but to the blackbird kind, which the Philosopher describes as βαιὸς [baios], dusky, and ἐπὶ τῶν πετρῶν, καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν κεράμων τὰς διατριβὰς ποιῶν [epi tōn petrōn, kai epi tōn keramōn tas diatribas poiōn], living on rocks and roof-tiles. So this is nothing to the point. Now if you ask why the Psalmist describes a nocturnal bird, say the eagle-owl or the owl, upon the roof, the answer is ready. For although eagle-owls and owls nest in desert places, yet at night they are accustomed sometimes to come to towns and cultivated places and to sound their funereal song from the roofs and gables of houses. Quod probo magis, quia passeres et similes aviculae visco potius, quam laqueo capiuntur. Quod docet Baal Aruch ad vocem שפשף, qua significatur pertica, visco illita, ad aviculas capiendas.

VII. The seventh passage is Psal. CXXIV, 7: Our soul has escaped כצפור, like a tsippor, from the snare of the fowlers. The Greeks: ὡς στρυθίον [hōs strythion]; the Vulgate: like a sparrow; the Arabic likewise: مثل العصفور [mithl al-ʿuṣfūr] like the sparrow; Apollinaris: στρουθοῦ πανομοίϊος [strouthou panomoiios], entirely like a sparrow; but Jerome: as a bird. This I approve more, because sparrows and similar little birds are caught with birdlime rather than with a snare. This is taught by Baal Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh] on the word שפשף, which signifies a pole smeared with birdlime for catching little birds. ¶

VIII. Octavus Eccles. XII, 4. al. 6. ubi de sene Graeci dicunt, Καὶ ἀνα-στήσεται εἰς φωνὴν τοῦ στρουθίου, Et surget ad vocem passeris. Sed Hieronymus, Et consurget ad vocem volucris. Et Chaldaeus, Et excitaberis a somno tuo על עסק קול דעופא, propter vocem avis, id est, ad avis cantum, quam gallum esse nonnulli volunt, et gallicinium significari. Ut gallus, homines matutino cantu excitans, simpliciter ales vocatur in his Poetae Amorum lib. 1. Eleg. 6.

VIII. The eighth is Eccles. XII, 4 (var. 6), where the Greeks say of the old man: Καὶ ἀναστήσεται εἰς φωνὴν τοῦ στρουθίου [Kai anastēsetai eis phōnēn tou strouthiou], And he shall rise up at the voice of the sparrow. But Jerome: And he shall rise up at the voice of the bird. And the Chaldee: And you shall be roused from your sleep על עסק קול דעופא, because of the voice of the bird, that is, at the song of a bird, which some take to be the cock, and cockcrow to be meant. Just as the cock, which wakes people with its morning song, is simply called ales [the bird] in these lines of the Poet of the Amores, book 1, Eleg. 6: ¶

Iamque pruinosus molitur Lucifer axes,
Inque suum miseros excitat ales opus.

And now Lucifer drives forth his frosty axles,
and the bird rouses wretched men to their work. ¶

Alii malunt, passeris aut aviculae cantum intelligi, quo, quamlibet tenui, senex e somno excitetur, dum juvenes tam alte dormiunt, ut ne tonitruum quidem fragorem audiant. Nec facile est statuere, utra versio sit sanior. Eos enim abunde confutavimus, qui gallum negant, aut ullam majorum avium voce צפור significari. Potest etiam id accipi de matutino garritu avium, qui Arabice voce col. 157 maxime propria dicitur زقزقة zakzaka. Alcamus, الزقزقة صوت طير عند الصبح garritus avium circa auroram.

Others prefer to understand the song of the sparrow or of a little bird, by which, however faint, the old man is woken from sleep, while the young sleep so deeply that they do not hear even the crash of thunder. Nor is it easy to decide which version is sounder. For we have amply refuted those who deny that the cock, or any of the larger birds, is signified by the word צפור. It can also be taken of the morning chatter of birds, which in Arabic is called by a most proper word, col. 157 زقزقة [zaqzaqa] zakzaka. Alcamus: الزقزقة صوت طير عند الصبح [al-zaqzaqa ṣawt ṭayr ʿinda al-ṣubḥ] the chatter of birds about dawn. ¶

IX. Nonus locus sic habet Thren. III, 32. Me venantur כצפור, ut tsippor inimici mei merito: ubi Graeci, ὡς στρουθίον· sed Hieronymus, quasi avem. Quod acceperim potius de majoribus avibus, quibus aucupandis opera libentius insumitur, et majore cum fructu.

IX. The ninth passage reads thus, Thren. III, 32: My enemies hunt me כצפור, like a tsippor, deservedly [sic]. Here the Greeks: ὡς στρουθίον [hōs strouthion]; but Jerome: like a bird. This I would rather take of the larger birds, on the catching of which effort is more willingly spent, and with greater profit. ¶

II, 735 In Tobia Latinum exemplar a Graeco in hac voce dissidet Tob. II, 9. (al. 11.). Nam in Graeco dicit Tobias, Καὶ οὐκ ᾔδειν ὅτι στρουθία ἐν τῷ τοίχῳ ἐστί· καὶ, τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν μου ἀνεωγότων, ἀφώδευσαν τὰ στρουθία θερμὸν εἰς τοὺς ὀφθαλ-μούς μου· Et passeres in pariete esse nesciebam: et, oculis meis apertis, passeres calidum stercus in oculos meos egesserunt. At Latinum quadam die contigisse narrat, ut, cum juxta parietem Tobias obdormisset, ex nido hirundinum dormienti illi calida stercora inciderent. Quae aliter conciliari posse non video, quam si dicas, Graece στρουθία ibi non esse passeres, sed aviculas, prout supra asserebant Moschopulus et Phavorinus, aviculae omne genus Graece dici στρουθία. Aut passerum nomine hirundines significari Arabum more, qui hirundinem vocant عصفور الجنة passerem Paradisi. Quale est, quod Prov. XXVI, 2. Hebraeum דרור deror alii hirundinem reddunt: sed Graeci interpretes στρουθοὺς passeres.

II, 735 In Tobit the Latin copy disagrees with the Greek on this word, Tob 2:9 (var. 11.). For in the Greek Tobit says, Καὶ οὐκ ᾔδειν ὅτι στρουθία ἐν τῷ τοίχῳ ἐστί· καὶ, τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν μου ἀνεωγότων, ἀφώδευσαν τὰ στρουθία θερμὸν εἰς τοὺς ὀφθαλμούς μου [Kai ouk ēdein hoti strouthia en tō toichō esti; kai, tōn ophthalmōn mou aneōgotōn, aphōdeusan ta strouthia thermon eis tous ophthalmous mou]; And I did not know that there were sparrows in the wall; and, my eyes being open, the sparrows voided warm dung into my eyes. But the Latin relates that it happened one day that, when Tobit had fallen asleep beside a wall, warm dung fell upon him as he slept from a swallows' nest. I do not see how these can be reconciled otherwise than by saying that στρουθία [strouthia] in the Greek there are not sparrows but little birds, just as Moschopulus and Phavorinus asserted above that every kind of little bird is called στρουθία [strouthia] in Greek. Or else that by the name of sparrows swallows are meant, after the manner of the Arabs, who call the swallow عصفور الجنة [ʿuṣfūr al-janna] the sparrow of Paradise. Similar is the case of Prov 26:2, where some render the Hebrew דרור deror as swallow, but the Greek interpreters as στρουθοὺς [strouthous], sparrows. ¶
