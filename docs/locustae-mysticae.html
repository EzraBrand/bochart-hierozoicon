De Locustis mysticis.

On the Mystical Locusts. ¶

Haec Scriptura de locustis, si duo loca excipias: unum in Veteri Testamento, alterum in Novo, quorum sensus non est obvius; quia his putatur aliquid mysticum subesse. Prior est Eccles. XII, 7. [al. 5.] ubi e variis senectutis in commodis, quae allegoricis verbis a Solomone describuntur, unum hoc est, ויסתבל החגב, Et ingravescet (aut gravidabit) locusta. Graeci, Hieronymus, Arabs, impinguabitur locusta. Et Syrus, ܢܣܓܐ, crescet. Antecedens pro Consequente. Quia, quae impinguantur et augentur, graviora sunt. Iam, quid illud sit, nonnulli veterum anxie quaerunt. Et, obliti, de sene hic agi, multa sibi fingunt mysteria; sed ea arte, qua quidlibet fit ex quolibet. Salomius, Impinguabitur locusta] id est, Credet gentilis populus, et replebitur bonis spiritualibus. Olympiodorus, haec jungens cum iis, quae praecedunt et sequuntur, Florebit amygdalus, pinguescet locusta, et aperietur capparis, observat, illa omnia vere accidere. Vult igitur, vitam aeternam ita describi, in qua ver erit aeternum. Tamen idem paullo post, sensu non solum diverso, sed et prorsus opposito, per locustam, quae pinguescit, intelligit Inferos, qui peccatorum suppliciis aluntur, et aliquo modo pinguescunt. Τροφὴν, inquit, ἡ κόλασις δέ-χεται τοὺς ἁμαρτωλοὺς, καὶ τρόπον τινὰ παχύνεται. Nempe locustam addit suppliciorum esse symbolum, quia per eam Deus punivit Aegyptios. R. Selomoni ויסתבל, id est, in pedes erectus est. Quod plane falsum. Sed libuit illi sic reddere, ut per locustam, quae erecta est, significaretur statua alta sexaginta cubitos, quam erexit Nebucadnetsar in campo Dura, Dan. III, 1. Quam tamen toties corruisse fingit, quoties erigebatur, donec ad pedes ejus effusum est tributi vice, quidquid erat auri Hierosolymis, ut impleretur, quod dictum est Ezech. VII, 19. Aurum eorum amovebitur. Kimchio locusta gravidabit, id est, gravis erit, vel oneri erit. Quasi tanta sit in sene infirmitas, ut ne locustam quidem ferre possit, id est, ne minima quidem pondera sustinere, cum ipse sibi sit gravis (1).

This is what Scripture says about locusts, if you except two passages, one in the Old Testament and the other in the New, whose sense is not obvious, because something mystical is thought to underlie them. The first is Eccles. XII, 7. [var. 5.], where among the various discomforts of old age, which are described by Solomon in allegorical words, one is this: ויסתבל החגב, And the locust shall grow heavy (or shall become pregnant). The Greeks, Jerome and the Arabic render the locust shall be made fat. And the Syriac has ܢܣܓܐ [נסגא], shall increase. This is the antecedent put for the consequent, since things that are fattened and enlarged are heavier. Now, what this means some of the ancients investigate anxiously. And, forgetting that an old man is the subject here, they invent many mysteries for themselves, but by the art by which anything is made out of anything. Salonius, on the locust shall be made fat: that is, the Gentile people shall believe, and shall be filled with spiritual goods. Olympiodorus, joining these words with those that precede and follow, the almond tree shall flower, the locust shall grow fat, and the caper shall open, observes that all these things truly happen. He means, therefore, that eternal life is so described, in which there will be an eternal spring. Yet the same author a little later, in a sense not only different but entirely opposite, understands by the locust that grows fat the underworld, which is fed by the punishments of sinners and in a certain way grows fat. Τροφὴν [trophēn], he says, ἡ κόλασις δέχεται τοὺς ἁμαρτωλοὺς, καὶ τρόπον τινὰ παχύνεται [hē kolasis dechetai tous hamartōlous, kai tropon tina pachynetai]. He adds, namely, that the locust is a symbol of punishments, because through it God punished the Egyptians. For R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] ויסתבל means it stood erect on its feet, which is plainly false. But he chose to render it so, in order that by the locust that stood erect might be signified the statue sixty cubits high which Nebuchadnezzar set up in the plain of Dura, Dan 3:1. Yet he imagines that it collapsed as often as it was set up, until all the gold there was in Jerusalem was poured out at its feet as tribute, so that what is said in Ezek 7:19 might be fulfilled: Their gold shall be removed. For Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], the locust shall become heavy means it will be heavy, or it will be a burden; as if there were such weakness in the old man that he cannot bear even a locust, that is, cannot sustain even the smallest weights, since he is a burden to himself R1. ¶

III, 335 Recentiorum aliis: locusta ingravescet: id est, qui juvenili aetate saltabat ut locusta, nunc per aetatem ita est gravis, ut ne quidem possit incedere III, 336 (2). Aliis locusta dicitur senex, qua senex, tanquam locustae similis: quia col. 494 senis ossa exstant, et corpus est exhaustum, ita, ut sit mera mortis imago.

III, 335 For others among the moderns, the locust shall grow heavy means: he who in his youth leapt like a locust is now through age so heavy that he cannot even walk III, 336 R2. For others the old man, as an old man, is called a locust, being like a locust, because col. 494 an old man's bones stick out and his body is wasted, so that he is a mere image of death. ¶

III, 337 Proinde Tithonum poetae fingunt in extrema senectute mutatum fuisse in cicadam, animal non solum aridissimum, et siccissimum instar locustae, sed et maxime garrulum, quae senectuti conveniunt. Hinc de illo Horatius lib. 1. Oda 28.

III, 337 Accordingly the poets imagine that Tithonus in extreme old age was changed into a cicada, an animal not only extremely dry and parched like a locust, but also very talkative, qualities that suit old age. Hence Horace says of him, book 1, Ode 28: ¶

Tithonusque remotus in auras.

And Tithonus removed into the air. ¶

Et lib. 3. Oda 16.

And book 3, Ode 16: ¶

Longa Tithonum minuit senectus.

Long old age diminished Tithonus. ¶

Et Servius in lib. 1. Georgicorum, Cum optasset prolixiorem vitam, versus est in cicadam. Et in lib. 4. Aeneidos, Quem longinquitas vitae in cicadam convertit. Quod ideo fingitur, quia, ut est apud Ciceronem, (seni) sola vox remanet, et est natura loquacior (3). Hinc Iliadis 3. versu 151. duo Trojani senes, Ucalegon et Antenor, qui, ad arma jam inepti, in Scaeis portis populum sermonibus detinebant, describuntur

And Servius on book 1 of the Georgics: When he had wished for a longer life, he was turned into a cicada. And on book 4 of the Aeneid: Whom length of life turned into a cicada. This is imagined because, as Cicero says, (in an old man) only the voice remains, and his nature is more talkative R3. Hence in Iliad 3, line 151, two Trojan old men, Ucalegon and Antenor, who, already unfit for arms, detained the people with talk at the Scaean gates, are described as ¶

— — τεττίγεσσιν ἐοικότες, οἵ τε καθ᾽ ὕλην,
Δένδρεῳ ἐφεζόμενοι, ὅπα λειριόεσσαν ἱεῖσι,

— — τεττίγεσσιν ἐοικότες, οἵ τε καθ᾽ ὕλην, Δένδρεῳ ἐφεζόμενοι, ὅπα λειριόεσσαν ἱεῖσι, [tettigessin eoikotes, hoi te kath᾽ hylēn, Dendreō ephezomenoi, hopa leirioessan hieisi,] ¶

Cicadis similes, quae in sylva, arbori insidentes, vocem floridam emittunt. Nempe, ut post Plutarchum scribit Eustathius, διὰ συνέχειαν λαλιᾶς, propter continuam loquacitatem. Vide etiam Tzetzem Chiliadis 8. Historia 166.

Like cicadas, which in the forest, sitting on a tree, send forth a flowery voice. That is, as Eustathius writes following Plutarch, διὰ συνέχειαν λαλιᾶς [dia synecheian lalias], because of their continual talkativeness. See also Tzetzes, Chiliad 8, History 166. ¶

Sunt, qui locustae mentionem arbitrati hic esse intempestivam, חגב chagab hoc loco aliud esse volunt. Et nonnullis Hebraeorum, Cheth et Ajin permutatis, חגב idem est, quod עגב, id est, pudendum. Quasi hanc partem in senibus languere et remitti significetur. Quod merito rejiciunt Kimchius et Aben Ezra (4). Sed huic חגב est הקרסול, crus, vel talus. Et malleolus pedis Chaldaeo III, 338 interpreti, qui Solomonis verba sic reddit, ויתנפחון אסחורי רגליך, Et in- tumescent malleoli pedum tuorum. Alii, tali pedum tuorum. Sed אסחורי vel אסחוירי esse pedum malleolos, ex Talmudis Glossa didici, tractatu Iebamoth cap. 12. fol. 109 a. ubi אסחוירא definitur קבילא המפסקת בין שוק לרגל, Malleolus dividens crus a pede. קבילא, non est vox Hebraea, ut putant; sed Gallica che- ville, qua significatur pedis malleolus. Nempe, qui ea utitur, R. Selomo, Gal- lus erat. Rursus in tractatu Menachoth fol. 33. a. legitur esse מקום חבור השוק והרגל הוי השוק זקוף מלמעלי והרגל שוכב בזה, Locus conjunctionis cruris cum pede, crure nempe supra elato, et pede infra jacente. Unde אסחורא pro gressu a Chaldaeo paraphraste saepe usurpatur. Et respondet Hebraeo אשור, a quo deductum dicerem, si Hebraeum ש in ס Chaldaei permutarent, quomodo solent Arabes. Nunc, ut dicam, quod sentio, iis accedo, quibus חגב chagab hoc loco non est locusta, sed pars cruris, prout cruris nomen etiam femur complectitur. Et, quae pars illa sit, ex Arabibus puto me didicisse, quibus حجبة chagaba est caput femoris. Golius, حجبة (Chagaba) caput coxae ut inque prominens, quae duo حجبتان chagabathani dicuntur. Giggeius, الحجبتان (Alchagabathan) utraque extremitas femoris et anchae super inguina. Intelligo femoris geminum caput, a quo anatomici illud vocant ἀμ-φικέφαλον. A quo femoris capite cum incipiat motus cruris, merito de sene dicit Solomon, ויסתבל חגב, Et ingravescit chagab; ut doceat, illi graviorem et tardiorem esse incessum (5).

There are some who, thinking a mention of the locust out of place here, hold that חגב chagab in this passage is something else. For some of the Hebrews, interchanging Cheth and Ajin, חגב is the same as עגב, that is, the pudendum, as though it were meant that this part grows languid and slack in old men. Kimchi and Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] rightly reject this R4. But for the latter חגב is הקרסול, the leg or the ankle. And for the Chaldee III, 338 interpreter it is the ankle-bone of the foot; he renders Solomon's words thus: ויתנפחון אסחורי רגליך, And the ankle-bones of your feet shall swell. Others: the ankles of your feet. But that אסחורי or אסחוירי are the ankle-bones of the feet I have learned from the Gloss on the Talmud, tractate Iebamoth ch. 12, fol. 109a, where אסחוירא is defined as קבילא המפסקת בין שוק לרגל, the ankle-bone dividing the leg from the foot. קבילא is not a Hebrew word, as people think, but the French cheville, which means the ankle-bone of the foot. For the one who uses it, R. Selomo, was a Frenchman. Again in tractate Menachoth fol. 33a, we read that it is מקום חבור השוק והרגל הוי השוק זקוף מלמעלי והרגל שוכב בזה, the place where the leg joins the foot, namely with the leg raised above and the foot lying below. Hence אסחורא is often used by the Chaldee paraphrast for a step. And it corresponds to the Hebrew אשור, from which I would say it was derived, if the Chaldeans changed the Hebrew ש into ס, as the Arabs are accustomed to do. Now, to say what I think, I side with those for whom חגב chagab in this passage is not the locust but a part of the leg, inasmuch as the term leg also includes the thigh. And which part that is I think I have learned from the Arabs, for whom حجبة [ḥajaba] chagaba is the head of the thigh-bone. Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: حجبة [ḥajaba] (Chagaba), the head of the hip, projecting on either side; the two are called حجبتان [ḥajabatān] chagabathani. Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)]: الحجبتان [al-ḥajabatān] (Alchagabathan), each extremity of the thigh-bone and of the haunch above the groin. I understand the twin head of the thigh-bone, on account of which anatomists call it ἀμφικέφαλον [amphikephalon]. Since the movement of the leg begins from this head of the thigh-bone, Solomon rightly says of the old man, ויסתבל חגב, And the chagab grows heavy, to show that his gait is heavier and slower R5. ¶

Alter locus est Apocalypseos capite nono, ubi de locustis agitur, quae quaedam habent ex verae locustae descriptione petita. Sed his addi videas multa peculiaria, quaeque ad mysterium pertinent. Ad prius illud refero, quod col. 495 eas locustas fumus in terram dejicit. Ascendit enim fumus ex puteo abyssi, ὡς καπνὸς καμίνου μεγάλης, ut fumus fornacis magnae, vel, ut alii legunt, καιο-μένης, ardentis; unde Syrus, ܐܝܟ ܬܢܢܐ ܕܐܬܘܢܐ: et Arabs Parisiis editus, تنور ملتهب. Sequitur, Et obtenebratus est sol, et aër a fumo pu- tei: et ex fumo prodierunt locustae in terram. Qua ipsa ratione locustas venantur III, 339 Acridophagi, de quibus Agatharchides; Καπνῷ δὲ ταύτας θηρῶσιν, ἐκ τοῦ ἀέρος ἐπὶ γῆν καταφέροντες· Fumo autem illas venantur ex aëre in terram dejectas. Et Strabo, Ἐν ταῖς χαράδραις δὲ ἐμβάλλοντες ὕλην κα-πνώδη, καὶ ὑφάψαντες μικρόν· ὑπερπετάμεναι γὰρ τὸν καπνὸν σκοτοῦνται, καὶ πίπτουσι. Fumosam materiam, in voragines injectam, incendunt: illae autem, supervolantes, fumo tenebras offundente, decidunt. Plura qui volet, Diodorum legat lib. 3. ubi profunda vorago describitur, per multa stadia patens, et accensa materie plena, quae in illis locis abunde suppetit: Ἐγειρομένου δὲ καπνοῦ πολλοῦ, καὶ δριμέος, αἱ μὲν ἀκρίδες ὑπερπετόμεναι τὴν χαράδραν, καὶ διὰ τὴν τοῦ καπνοῦ δριμύτητα πνιγόμεναι, καταπίπτουσιν ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν, ὀλίγον διαπετασθεῖσαι τόπον· Excitato autem multo et acri fumo, locustae, voraginem supervolantes, et propter fumi acrimoniam praefocatae, in terram decidunt, locum illum parum praetergressae. Ad eam Acridophagorum historiam sacer scriptor videtur allusisse (6). Ita quod versu 5. et 10. nocent hominibus per quinque menses, videtur ideo dici; quia locustae, vere natae, sub finem aestatis obeunt, nec supra quinque menses vivere solent. Et quod versu 7. describuntur equis similes, paratis ad praelium, petitum est ex Ioelis II, 4. Sicut aspectus equorum aspectus earum, et sicut equites current. Et vers. 8. Dentes earum ut leonum erant: ita habetur Ioelis I, 6. Dentes ejus dentes leonis, et leaenae molares. Sed et haec versu 9. Habebant etiam loricas, ut loricas ferreas, ad locustarum naturam aliquo modo pertinet, de quibus Claudianus:

The other passage is in chapter nine of the Apocalypse, which deals with locusts that have some features taken from the description of the real locust. But you may see many peculiar features added to these, which pertain to the mystery. To the former I assign the fact that col. 495 smoke casts these locusts down onto the earth. For smoke rises from the pit of the abyss, ὡς καπνὸς καμίνου μεγάλης [hōs kapnos kaminou megalēs], as the smoke of a great furnace, or, as others read, καιομένης [kaiomenēs], of a burning one; hence the Syriac ܐܝܟ ܬܢܢܐ ܕܐܬܘܢܐ [איך תננא דאתונא], and the Arabic edited at Paris تنور ملتهب [tannūr multahib]. There follows: And the sun and the air were darkened by the smoke of the pit; and out of the smoke came locusts upon the earth. It is in exactly this way that III, 339 the Acridophagi hunt locusts, of whom Agatharchides says: Καπνῷ δὲ ταύτας θηρῶσιν, ἐκ τοῦ ἀέρος ἐπὶ γῆν καταφέροντες· [Kapnō de tautas thērōsin, ek tou aeros epi gēn katapherontes] They hunt them with smoke, bringing them down out of the air onto the ground. And Strabo: Ἐν ταῖς χαράδραις δὲ ἐμβάλλοντες ὕλην καπνώδη, καὶ ὑφάψαντες μικρόν· ὑπερπετάμεναι γὰρ τὸν καπνὸν σκοτοῦνται, καὶ πίπτουσι. [En tais charadrais de emballontes hylēn kapnōdē, kai hyphapsantes mikron; hyperpetamenai gar ton kapnon skotountai, kai piptousi.] They throw smoky material into the ravines and set it alight; and the locusts, flying over, are blinded by the darkness the smoke spreads, and fall. Whoever wants more should read Diodorus, book 3, where a deep ravine is described, extending over many stadia and filled with kindled material, which is abundantly available in those parts: Ἐγειρομένου δὲ καπνοῦ πολλοῦ, καὶ δριμέος, αἱ μὲν ἀκρίδες ὑπερπετόμεναι τὴν χαράδραν, καὶ διὰ τὴν τοῦ καπνοῦ δριμύτητα πνιγόμεναι, καταπίπτουσιν ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν, ὀλίγον διαπετασθεῖσαι τόπον· [Egeiromenou de kapnou pollou, kai drimeos, hai men akrides hyperpetomenai tēn charadran, kai dia tēn tou kapnou drimytēta pnigomenai, katapiptousin epi tēn gēn, oligon diapetastheisai topon] When much acrid smoke has been raised, the locusts, flying over the ravine and suffocated by the acridity of the smoke, fall to the ground after flying only a little way past that spot. The sacred writer seems to have alluded to this account of the Acridophagi R6. Likewise, the statement in verses 5 and 10 that they harm men for five months seems to be made because locusts, born in spring, die toward the end of summer and do not usually live beyond five months. And their description in verse 7 as like horses prepared for battle is taken from Ioelis II, 4. Their appearance is as the appearance of horses, and like horsemen they shall run. And in verse 8, Their teeth were as the teeth of lions: so we have in Ioelis I, 6. His teeth are the teeth of a lion, and he has the molars of a lioness. But also the statement in verse 9, They also had breastplates, as it were breastplates of iron, pertains in some way to the nature of locusts, of which Claudian says: ¶

— — cognatus dorso durescit amictus,
Armavit natura cutem. — —

— — a covering grown with it hardens on its back;
nature has armed its skin. — — ¶

Arabs Erpenianus, ad alas referens, ita reddidit, واجنحتهم مثل اتراس حديد Et alae earum erant ut clypei ferrei. Alter autem ita ad pectus, وكانت صدورها مزردة كزرد حديد Et erant pectora earum tanquam loricis ferreis loricata. Quod sequitur, sonitum alarum fuisse ut sonitum curruum, multis equis ad bellum currentibus, sumptum est ex Ioelis II, 5. Tanquam sonitu curruum super montium verticem salient, etc. sicut populus validus, ad praelium instructus. Itaque ex locustarum historia petiit Iohannes, quae hactenus observata sunt. Sed jam plura sequuntur, his peculiaria. Quorum illud primum esto, quod fumus ille, quo dejiciuntur III, 340 in terram, ex Acridophagorum voragine non prodit, sed ex abyssi puteo, quae inferorum est vorago. 2. Quod neque terrae gramen, neque arbores agri laedunt, sed solum homines, qui non habent Dei signum in frontibus suis. 3. Quod in caudis habent scorpionum aculeos, quibus homines torquent cruciatu, qui similis est cruciatui scorpionis, cum percussit hominem. 4. His adduntur coronae in capitibus auri specie. 5. Et hominum facies. 6. Et muliebres capilli, quae omnes notae in veris locustis desunt. Denique verae locustae regem non habent, Prov. XXX, 27. At hae regem habent, eumque non sui generis, sed abyssi angelum, qui Hebraice אבדון Abaddon dicitur; et Graece Ἀπολλύων, non Ἀπόλλων, ut habent Syrus et Aethiops; utrumque a perdendo sumptum. Nam אבד abad Verbum est ἀπολλύειν, et אבדון abaddon est ἀπώλεια. Et locustae, si regem haberent, illi conveniret hoc nomen; quia col. 496 locustarum proprium est perdere. Hinc Ioel I, 11. Hebraice אבד קציר שרה, Periit agri messis. Et Graece, Ἀπώλωλε τρύγητος ἐξ ἀγροῦ. Periit ex agro vindemia, a locustis scilicet perdita. Sed hic ut aliae locustae, ita etiam alia perditio intelligitur, ab ea longe diversa, quam vulgares locustae inferunt. Cujus auctor abyssi angelus, id est, diabolus, qui, ipse pridem perditus, in id solum incumbit, ut nos quoque in idem perditionis barathrum devolvat, atque detrudat. Quod cum faciat locustarum ministerio, quae a Iohanne designantur, operae pretium esset, docere, quaenam sint illae locustae, et suis notis illas insignire; sed ab hac disquisitione certis de causis abstinemus, jam ad alia transituri.

Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)], referring it to the wings, rendered it thus: واجنحتهم مثل اتراس حديد [wa-ajniḥatuhum mithl atrās ḥadīd] And their wings were like iron shields. The other, however, referred it to the breast: وكانت صدورها مزردة كزرد حديد [wa-kānat ṣudūruhā muzarrada ka-zard ḥadīd] And their breasts were mailed as with breastplates of iron. What follows, that the sound of their wings was as the sound of chariots, of many horses running to battle, is taken from Ioelis II, 5. Like the sound of chariots they shall leap upon the tops of the mountains, etc. as a strong people set in battle array. Thus what has been noted so far John drew from the natural history of locusts. But now more features follow that are peculiar to these. Let the first of them be that the smoke by which they are cast down III, 340 onto the earth does not come out of the chasm of the Acridophagi, but from the pit of the abyss, which is the chasm of the underworld. 2. That they harm neither the grass of the earth nor the trees of the field, but only those men who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads. 3. That they have in their tails the stings of scorpions, with which they torment men with a torment like the torment of a scorpion when it strikes a man. 4. To these are added crowns on their heads like gold. 5. And the faces of men. 6. And the hair of women. All these marks are lacking in real locusts. Finally, real locusts have no king, Prov 30:27. But these have a king, and not one of their own kind, but the angel of the abyss, who in Hebrew is called אבדון Abaddon, and in Greek Ἀπολλύων [Apollyōn], not Ἀπόλλων [Apollōn], as the Syriac and the Ethiopic have it; both are taken from destroying. For אבד abad is the verb ἀπολλύειν [apollyein], and אבדון abaddon is ἀπώλεια [apōleia]. And if locusts had a king, this name would suit him, because col. 496 destroying is the proper work of locusts. Hence Joel 1:11, in Hebrew אבד קציר שרה, The harvest of the field has perished. And in Greek, Ἀπώλωλε τρύγητος ἐξ ἀγροῦ [Apōlōle trygētos ex agrou], The vintage has perished from the field, destroyed, that is, by locusts. But here, as other locusts are meant, so also another destruction, far different from that which common locusts bring. Its author is the angel of the abyss, that is, the devil, who, himself long since lost, is bent on this alone: to roll and thrust us too down into the same pit of perdition. Since he does this through the agency of the locusts that John describes, it would be worthwhile to show what those locusts are and to mark them out by their characteristics; but for certain reasons we refrain from this inquiry, being about to pass on to other matters. ¶

G. A new interpretation was recently proposed by Henr. Frid. Pfannkuche in his Exercitationes in Ecclesiastae, Salomoni vulgo tributi, locum vexatissimum Cap. XI, 7.—XII, 7., Göttingen 1794. In this booklet he tried to show that that portion of Ecclesiastes contains not, as is commonly thought, a description of old age, but of every kind of misfortune and calamity, under the image of a storm with rains, gales and thunder, which is presented and variously embellished from verse 2 of Chapter XII onward. To this description of the storm also belongs, he says, the statement that small locusts (for he understands these by חגב, following Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde]; see above, note 5 to Chapter I) are swept away by whirlwinds, or carried off; with the vowel points changed, of course, he reads יִסָּתֵבַל החגב, the locust is carried off, is swept away (by the winds, which are hostile to locusts). Whether this arbitrary change of reading, devised in favour of a hypothesis that is hardly firm, will win everyone's approval, we very much doubt.
