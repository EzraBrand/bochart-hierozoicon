Loca quaedam de Apum mellificio, quae tanquam ex Scriptura proferunt Graeci Patres.

Some passages on the honey-making of bees which the Greek Fathers cite as if from Scripture. ¶

Apes commendat inprimis mellis conficiendi artificium, de quo in Hebraeorum col. 511 sacro textu hoc solum reperias Iud. XIV, 8. Et, ecce examen apum in corpore leonis, et mel! Examen apum, et mel: id est, examen apum mellificarum, aut mel conficientium, per figuram ἓν διὰ δυοῖν. Sed Graeci Patres e penu suo tres alios textus proferunt. Primus est Psalmi CXVII, (Hebraeis CXVIII,) 12. Ἐκύκλωσάν με, ὡσεὶ μέλισσαι κηρίον· Circumdederunt me, sicut apes favum. Ita ex Graeca versione citant Augustinus, Hieronymus, Theodoretus. Neque aliud habent nostrae editiones. Aethiops quoque idem sequitur. Et Arabs, quem habeo manuscriptum, احتاطوا بي مثل النحل بالشهد Circumdederunt me, ut apes favum. Sed pro الشهد, id est, favo, editi habent الشمع ceram. Quasi Arabes κηρὸν Graece legerint, non κηρίον. At in Hebraeo solum legitur, סבוני כדבורים, Circumdederunt me, ut apes. Et illud κηρίον videtur perperam additum. Agit enim David de hostibus suis: nec eos confert cum apibus, quae favum circumstant, cum in eo nihil sit hostile, sed cum apibus, quae hominem aut brutum agminatim circumdant, ut infestis aculeis pungant. Quomodo apud Statium Thebaidos lib. 10.

Bees are commended above all for their skill in making honey. On this, in the sacred text of the Hebrews, col. 511 you will find only Iud. XIV, 8: And behold, a swarm of bees in the body of the lion, and honey! A swarm of bees, and honey: that is, a swarm of honey-making bees, or of bees producing honey, by the figure ἓν διὰ δυοῖν [hen dia dyoin]. But the Greek Fathers bring out three other texts from their own store. The first is Psalmi CXVII, (Hebraeis CXVIII,) 12: Ἐκύκλωσάν με, ὡσεὶ μέλισσαι κηρίον· [Ekyklōsan me, hōsei melissai kērion] They surrounded me, as bees a honeycomb. So Augustine, Jerome and Theodoret cite it from the Greek version. Nor do our editions have anything else. The Ethiopic also follows the same. And the Arabic, which I have in manuscript, reads احتاطوا بي مثل النحل بالشهد [iḥtāṭū bī mithl al-naḥl bi-l-shahd] They surrounded me, as bees a honeycomb. But for الشهد [al-shahd], that is, honeycomb, the printed editions have الشمع [al-shamʿ] wax, as if the Arabs had read κηρὸν [kēron] in the Greek, not κηρίον [kērion]. In the Hebrew, however, one reads only סבוני כדבורים, They surrounded me, like bees. And that κηρίον [kērion] seems to have been wrongly added. For David is speaking of his enemies; and he does not compare them with bees that surround a honeycomb, since there is nothing hostile in that, but with bees that surround a man or a beast in a body, to sting with hostile stings. So in Statius, Thebaid book 10: ¶

Sic, ubi pumiceo pastor rapturus ab antro
Armatas exegit apes, fremit aspera nubes,
Inque vicem sese stridore hortantur, et omnes
Hostis in ora volant. — — —

So, when a shepherd, about to plunder them, has driven the armed bees from their pumice cave, the fierce cloud roars, and they urge one another on with buzzing, and all fly into the face of the foe. — — — ¶

Quae in versione sua Castalio sic expressit, Aut apem adi, et disce, quam operosa sit, quamque augustum opus exerceat: cujus laboribus et reges, et privati vescuntur salubriter. Eaque omnibus est amabilis et illustris: et, quamvis viribus infirma sit, tamen sapientiae studio promovit. Et labores ejus deferuntur ad reges. Et vulgus iis utitur ad tuendam sanitatem. Estque apud omnes homines excellens et nobilis. Etiamsi debilis sit viribus, tamen antecellit; quia commendatur sapien- col. 512 tia, et praestans est. Eundem locum citant multi veteres: ut Clemens Alexandrinus lib. 1. Stromatων; Origenes in Numeros Homilia 27. et in Esaiam Homilia 2. Basilius in Hexaëmeron Homilia 8. Ambrosius in Hexaëmeron lib. 5. cap. 21. Hieronymus in Ezechielem cap. 3. Theodoretus De Providentia Oratione 5. An-tiochus, Abbas Sabae, Homilia 36. Iohannes Damascenus lib. 2. Parall. cap. 89. Tamen, ut vere observat Hieronymus ubi supra, hoc Hebraea non habent exemplaria. Itaque nec in Chaldaea, nec in Syra, nec in Vulgata versione ullum est hujus loci vestigium. Et saepe mirari subiit, unde id sumpserint Graeci interpretes, ut et quaedam alia, quae variis locis passim inserunt. Neque enim credibile est, illos de suo haec fuisse addituros, nisi in quibusdam Hebraeorum exemplaribus jam inserta reperissent.

VI, 8, where, after the much-celebrated (πολυθρύλλητον [polythryllēton]) passage on the ant, Go to the ant, O sluggard, etc., the Greek edition reads: Ἢ πορεύθητι πρὸς τὴν μέλισσαν, καὶ μάθε, ὡς ἐργάτις ἐστι, τήν τε ἐργασίαν ὡς σεμνὴν ποιεῖται· ἧς τοὺς πόνους βασιλεῖς καὶ ἰδιῶται πρὸς ὑγίειαν προσφέρονται. ποθεινὴ δέ ἐστι πᾶσι, καὶ ἐπίδοξος· καίπερ οὖσα τῇ ῥώμῃ ἀσθενὴς, τὴν σοφίαν τιμήσασα προήχθη. [Ē poreuthēti pros tēn melissan, kai mathe, hōs ergatis esti, tēn te ergasian hōs semnēn poieitai; hēs tous ponous basileis kai idiōtai pros hygieian prospherontai. potheinē de esti pasi, kai epidoxos; kaiper ousa tē rhōmē asthenēs, tēn sophian timēsasa proēchthē.] Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] rendered this in his version as follows: Or go to the bee, and learn how industrious she is, and how majestic a work she carries on: by whose labours both kings and private persons are wholesomely fed. And she is beloved and renowned among all; and, although weak in strength, she has nevertheless advanced by her zeal for wisdom. The Arabic version also: او انهب الي النحلة واعرف انها مثل الفاعل هي تعمل صناعتها طرفة عجيبة * واتعابها تحمل الي الملوك * والعامة يستعملوها لحفظ الصحة * وهي عند جميع الناس ماثورة مشرفة * مع ضعيفة في قوتها قدمت لانها اكرمت بالحكمة وفضلت * [aw inhab ilá al-naḥla wa-iʿrif annahā mithl al-fāʿil hiya taʿmal ṣināʿatahā ṭurfa ʿajība * wa-atʿābuhā tuḥmal ilá al-mulūk * wa-al-ʿāmma yastaʿmilūhā li-ḥifẓ al-ṣiḥḥa * wa-hiya ʿinda jamīʿ al-nās maʾthūra musharrafa * maʿa ḍaʿīfa fī quwwatihā qaddamat li-annahā ukrimat bi-l-ḥikma wa-fuḍḍilat *] Or go to the bee, and know that, like a workman, she fashions her work with wonderful art. And her labours are carried to kings, and the common people use them to preserve health. And she is excellent and noble among all men. Although she is weak in strength, she nevertheless excels, because she is commended for wisdom, col. 512 and is outstanding. Many ancient writers cite the same passage: thus Clement of Alexandria, book 1 of the Stromata; Origen on Numbers, Homily 27, and on Isaiah, Homily 2; Basil on the Hexaemeron, Homily 8; Ambrose on the Hexaemeron, book 5, ch. 21; Jerome on Ezekiel, ch. 3; Theodoret, On Providence, Oration 5; Antiochus, Abbot of Saba, Homily 36; John of Damascus, book 2 of the Parallels, ch. 89. Yet, as Jerome rightly observes in the place cited, the Hebrew copies do not have this. Accordingly there is no trace of this passage in the Chaldee, the Syriac or the Vulgate version. And I have often wondered where the Greek translators took it from, as also certain other things which they insert here and there in various places. For it is not credible that they would have added these things of their own accord, unless they had found them already inserted in some copies of the Hebrews. ¶

However that may be, certain points should be noted in this passage about the bee. First: the bee is joined with the ant, as is common among writers. Thus the Philosopher, History book 1, ch. 1, after saying that the bee and the ant are political and gregarious animals, adds: τὸ τῶν μελιττῶν γένος εἶναι ὑφ᾽ ἡγεμόνα· μύρμηκες δὲ, καὶ μυρία ἄλλα ἄναρχα [to tōn melittōn genos einai hyph᾽ hēgemona; myrmēkes de, kai myria alla anarcha], the race of bees is under a leader, but ants and countless others are without a leader. And book 9, ch. 38: Τῶν δ᾽ ἐντόμων ζώων ἐργατικώτατα σχεδόν ἐστι, καὶ πρός τ᾽ ἄλλα συγκρίνεσθαι πάντα, τότε τῶν μυρμήκων γένος, καὶ τὸ τῶν μελιττῶν [Tōn d᾽ entomōn zōōn ergatikōtata schedon esti, kai pros t᾽ alla synkrinesthai panta, tote tōn myrmēkōn genos, kai to tōn melittōn]: Of insect III, 367 animals the most industrious, and to be preferred to all the rest, are the races of ants and of bees. Cicero, Academic Questions book 4: Whose (God's) majesty you bring down even to the perfection of bees and ants. Plutarch, in the book Whether an Old Man Should Engage in Public Affairs: Τὸ κοινωνικὸν, καὶ πολιτικὸν, ὃ καὶ μύρμηξιν ἄχρι τέλους παραμένει καὶ μελίτταις [To koinōnikon, kai politikon, ho kai myrmēxin achri telous paramenei kai melittais]: The communal and political life, in which both ants and bees remain to the very end. The airy, industrious bee also labors. But she who is born of the bee is well versed in household management and knows how to work. Hence in Xenophon's Oeconomicus, chapter 13, ἡ τῶν μελισσῶν ἡγεμῶν [hē tōn melissōn hēgemōn], the leader of the bees, is compared at length with the mother of a household. Accordingly, even illustrious women did not disdain to be called by the name of bee: as among the Hebrews there are two named DEBORA: one, the nurse of Rebecca, Gen. XXXV, 8, and the other, the prophetess who judged Israel, Iudicum IV, 4. And among the Greeks there is MELISSA, the nymph who nursed Jupiter; and MELISSA, daughter of Epidamnus, of whom Stephanus speaks under Δυῤῥάχιον [Dyrrhachion]; and MELISSA, wife of Periander, king of the Corinthians; and a later MELISSA, whose Letter Aldus published in the volume of Greek Letters. 4. Quid si σμήνων legas pro σεμνὴν, hoc modo, τήν τε ἐργασίαν σμήνων ὡς ποιεῖται, et quomodo faciat opus alvearium, id est, mirabile illud opus, quod fit in alvearibus? Quod ingens naturae arcanum est, neque dum satis perspectum. Philosophus ubi supra, Ὅταν δὲ εἰς σμῆνος ἀφίκωνται ἀπο-σείονται, καὶ ἀκολουθοῦσιν ἑκάστῃ τρεῖς, ἢ τέτταρες. τὸ δὲ λαμβανόμενον οὐ ῥᾴδιόν ἐστιν ἰδεῖν, οὐδὲ τὴν ἐργασίαν, ὅν τινα τρόπον ποιοῦνται, οὐκ ὦπται· Cum autem in alveare (a florilegio) redeunt, se excutiunt, et unamquamque apem tres aut quatuor sequuntur. Sed quid accipiant, videre facile non est. Neque dum observatum est, quomodo opus suum faciant. On this, Tzetzes, Chiliad 8, History 208: III, 370 Τοῦτο τὸ σχῆμα γίνωσκε τυγχάνειν εἰρωνείας. Καὶ τίς γὰρ, ὃς ἠγνόηκε πόνον μελίσσης μέλι; [Touto to schēma ginōske tynchanein eirōneias. Kai tis gar, hos ēgnoēke ponon melissēs meli?] ¶

Hanc figuram scito esse ironiae. Quis enim est, qui nesciat, laborem apis esse mel? Porro mel πρὸς ὑγίειαν, ad sanitatem adhiberi, vere dicitur. Ita enim statuunt veteres. Hippocrates capite quinquagesimo De Affectionibus, Μέλι, ξὺν ἑτέροις ἐσθιόμενον, καὶ τρέφει, καὶ εὔχροιαν παρέχει· Mel, cum aliis comestum, et nutrit, et bonum colorem exhibet. Et capite quinquagesimo quarto, Οἶνος καὶ μέλι κάλλιστα κέκριται ἐν ἀνθρώποισι, ἢν πρὸς φύσιν καὶ ὑγιαίνουσι, καὶ ἀσθενέουσι, σὺν καιρῷ, καὶ μετριότητι, προσφέρονται· Vinum et mel optima judicata sunt in hominibus, si juxta naturam et sanis, et debilibus, cum temporis opportunitate ac mediocritate, exhibeantur. Diophanes libro decimo quinto, in Geoponicis, Οὐ μόνον ἡδὺ τοῖς χρωμένοις, ἀλλὰ καὶ μακροβίους ποιεῖ· οἱ οὖν γήρᾳ μέλιτι μετὰ ἄρτου μόνου τρεφόμενοι, ἐπὶ πλεῖστον βιοῦσι· καὶ τὰς αἰσθήσεις δὲ διαφυλάττει πάσας ἐῤῥωμένας. Mel utentibus non solum suave est, sed et eos longaevos facit. Qui igitur in senectute melle cum solo pane vescuntur, diu vivunt; et sensus vegetos facit ille cibus: Proinde Λύκος πολυχρονίους φησὶν εἶναι τοὺς Κυρνίους, διὰ τὸ μέλιτι ἀεὶ χρῆσθαι, longaevos esse Corsos, tradit Lycus, quia melle semper utuntur. Ita Athenaeus libri secundi capite septimo. Et ibidem, Τῶν Πυθαγο-ρικῶν τροφὴ ἄρτος μετὰ μέλιτος, ὥς φησι Ἀριστόξενος, τοὺς προσφερομένους αὐτὰ ἀεὶ ἐπ᾽ ἀρίστῳ λέγων ἀνόσως διατελεῖν. Pythagoricorum nutrimentum panem cum melle fuisse, scribit Aristoxenus, addens, sine morbo vivere, qui ea ad prandium semper adhibent. Quod videntur ex praeceptore suo, Pythagora, didicisse, de quo Eustathius in Iliad λ᾽. Ὡς δὲ καὶ τρόφιμον τὸ μέλι Πυθαγορᾶς μαρτυρεῖ, ὃς, εὐτελέστατα διαβιοὺς, πολλάκις μέλιτι μόνῳ ἠρ-κεῖτο. Mel etiam posse alere, testatur Pythagoras, qui, frugaliter admodum vivens, plerumque solo melle contentus erat. Eodem facit vetus de Glauco proverbium, Γλαῦκος πινὼν μέλι ἀνέστη· Glaucus, poto melle, revixit: id est, si Paroemiastae mentem capio, e gravi morbo convaluit. Et quod Democritum scribit Athenaeus, cum de eo jam esset conclamatum, διαζῆσαι ἡμέ-ρας ἱκανὰς, τῇ ἀπὸ τοῦ μέλιτος ἀναφορᾷ μόνῃ τρεφόμενον, in dies aliquot vitam prorogasse, solo mellis odore nutritum. Nempe, ut ibidem sequitur, mellis usu maxime delectabatur. Itaque Πρὸς τὸν πυθόμενον, Πῶς ἂν ὑγιῶς τις διάγοι; ἔφη, Εἰ τὰ μὲν ἐντὸς μέλιτι βρέχοι, τὰ δὲ ἐκτὸς ἐλαίῳ· col. 515 percontanti, qua ratione vitam quis sanus degeret? respondit: si corporis interiora melle rigarentur, exteriora oleo. Nec multo aliter Diophanes, Δημό-κριτος δὲ, ἐρωτηθεὶς, Πῶς ἂν ἄνοσοι καὶ μακραίωνες γίγνοιντο οἱ ἄνθρω-ποι; εἶπεν, Εἰ τὰ μὲν ἔξωθεν ἐλαίῳ τοῦ σώματος, τὰ δὲ ἔνδοθεν μέλιτι III, 371 χρήσαιντο. Pro quo malim χρίσαιντο. Indidem petitum Pollionis apophthegma, de quo Plinius libri vigesimi secundi capite vigesimo quarto, Multi senectam longam mulsi tantum nutritu toleravere, neque alio ullo cibo, celebri Pollionis Romuli exemplo. Centesimum annum excedentem eum Divus Augustus hospes interrogavit, quanam ratione maxime vigorem illum animi corporisque custodisset? At ille respondit: intus mulso, foris oleo. Mulso, id est, τῷ οἰνομέλιτι. Patet igitur, mel poni merito inter regum et magnatum dapes. Quod facile sit probare exemplis, ex Scriptura petitis. Sic enim ad Iosephum, Aegypti pro-regem, pro eximio munere mel mittitur, Gen. XLIII, 11. et regi Davidi, cum esset in oppido Mahanajim, mel obtulerunt vicini nobiles, 2 Sam. XVII, 29. Solomon quoque ita loquitur Cant. V, 1. Comedi favos cum melle meo. Ut Ionathanem taceam, regis filium, cujus oculi et vires mellis esu refectae sunt, 1 Sam. XIV, 27. Quibus si profana jungimus, Iliad. λ΄. versu 630. Hecamedem reperiemus Nestori et Patroclo in convivio μέλι χλωρὸν, mel flavum, aut mel recens apposuisse. Et Odyss. υ΄. versu 69. Venerem Pandari filias nutrivisse τυρῷ, καὶ μέλιτι γλυκερῷ, caseo et dulci melle. Sic Suetonius de Nerone capite vigesimo septimo, Indicebat et familiaribus coenas, quarum uni mellita quadragies sestertium constiterunt: id est, centena aureorum millia, ex Budaei supputatione. Sed vocem mellita, quam habent vulgares editiones, mutavit doctissimus Casaubonus in mitellita, quia sic legunt codices vetustissimi. Interim mitellita quid sint, ingenue fatetur se ignorare. Hodie, post saccharum inventum, multum de mellis pretio decessisse, nemo mirabitur. 7. Additur, apem esse apud omnes ποθεινὴν καὶ ἐπίδοξον, amabilem et claram. Mel enim ubique nascitur, ut notat Plinius libri vigesimi secundi capite vigesimo tertio, et ubique est in pretio, ab ultima Scythia usque ad Afros et Aethiopes. Augustinus, aut quisquis est, Sermone decimo quinto Ad fratres in eremo, scribit, Philosophum, Aristodemum, annis multis insudasse in natura apis investiganda. Sed pro Aristodemo Aristomachum libens scripserim, de quo Plinius libri undecimi capite nono, Ne quis miretur amore earum (apum) captos Aristomachum Solensem duodesexaginta annis nihil aliud egisse: Philiscum vero Thasium, in desertis apes colentem, Agrium cognominatum. 8. Conclusio denique haec est: Quamvis (apis) sit infirma viribus, τὴν σοφίαν τιμήσασα προήχθη, quia sapientiam coluit (id est, sapientiae studio) provecta est. Nempe, ut serpenti prudentia, sic api sapientiae studium tribuitur καταχρηστικῶς, quae naturae instinctu id facit, quae si facerent homines, sapientum catalogo merito inscriberentur. Qua καταχρήσει sapientem esse apem, non pauci scribunt. Ita Lucianus in Halcyone apem appellat σοφὴν θείου μέλιτος ἐργάτιν, sapientem divini mellis opificem. Et Plutarchus Περὶ φιλοστοργίας, Τὴν μέλιτταν ἡμεῖς σοφὴν καλοῦμεν, καὶ νομίζομεν, ξανθὸν μέλι μηδομένην· Apem nos sapientem III, 372 vocamus et censemus, quia mel flavum operatur. Phile capite vigesimo septimo de ape,

Know that this figure is irony. For who is there who does not know that the labour of the bee is honey? Further, it is truly said that honey is used πρὸς ὑγίειαν [pros hygieian], for health. For so the ancients hold. Hippocrates, chapter 50 of On Affections: Μέλι, ξὺν ἑτέροις ἐσθιόμενον, καὶ τρέφει, καὶ εὔχροιαν παρέχει [Meli, xyn heterois esthiomenon, kai trephei, kai euchroian parechei]; Honey, eaten with other things, both nourishes and gives a good colour. And in chapter 54: Οἶνος καὶ μέλι κάλλιστα κέκριται ἐν ἀνθρώποισι, ἢν πρὸς φύσιν καὶ ὑγιαίνουσι, καὶ ἀσθενέουσι, σὺν καιρῷ, καὶ μετριότητι, προσφέρονται [Oinos kai meli kallista kekritai en anthrōpoisi, ēn pros physin kai hygiainousi, kai astheneousi, syn kairō, kai metriotēti, prospherontai]; Wine and honey have been judged best for human beings, if they are given in accordance with nature to both the healthy and the weak, at the right time and in moderation. Diophanes, book 15 of the Geoponica: Οὐ μόνον ἡδὺ τοῖς χρωμένοις, ἀλλὰ καὶ μακροβίους ποιεῖ· οἱ οὖν γήρᾳ μέλιτι μετὰ ἄρτου μόνου τρεφόμενοι, ἐπὶ πλεῖστον βιοῦσι· καὶ τὰς αἰσθήσεις δὲ διαφυλάττει πάσας ἐῤῥωμένας [Ou monon hēdy tois chrōmenois, alla kai makrobious poiei; hoi oun gēra meliti meta artou monou trephomenoi, epi pleiston biousi; kai tas aisthēseis de diaphylattei pasas errōmenas]. Honey is not only pleasant to those who use it, but also makes them long-lived. So those who in old age eat honey with bread alone live a long time; and that food keeps the senses vigorous. Accordingly, Λύκος πολυχρονίους φησὶν εἶναι τοὺς Κυρνίους, διὰ τὸ μέλιτι ἀεὶ χρῆσθαι [Lykos polychronious phēsin einai tous Kyrnious, dia to meliti aei chrēsthai]: Lycus reports that the Corsicans are long-lived, because they always use honey. So Athenaeus, book 2, chapter 7. And in the same place: Τῶν Πυθαγορικῶν τροφὴ ἄρτος μετὰ μέλιτος, ὥς φησι Ἀριστόξενος, τοὺς προσφερομένους αὐτὰ ἀεὶ ἐπ᾽ ἀρίστῳ λέγων ἀνόσως διατελεῖν [Tōn Pythagorikōn trophē artos meta melitos, hōs phēsi Aristoxenos, tous prospheromenous auta aei ep' aristō legōn anosōs diatelein]. Aristoxenus writes that the food of the Pythagoreans was bread with honey, adding that those who always take these at their midday meal live free of disease. They seem to have learned this from their teacher, Pythagoras, of whom Eustathius on Iliad λ᾽ [l']: Ὡς δὲ καὶ τρόφιμον τὸ μέλι Πυθαγορᾶς μαρτυρεῖ, ὃς, εὐτελέστατα διαβιοὺς, πολλάκις μέλιτι μόνῳ ἠρκεῖτο [Hōs de kai trophimon to meli Pythagoras martyrei, hos, eutelestata diabious, pollakis meliti monō ērkeito]. Pythagoras also testifies that honey can nourish; living very frugally, he was mostly content with honey alone. To the same effect is the old proverb about Glaucus, Γλαῦκος πινὼν μέλι ἀνέστη [Glaukos pinōn meli anestē]: Glaucus, having drunk honey, came back to life; that is, if I grasp the meaning of the Paroemiographer, he recovered from a grave illness. And Athenaeus writes of Democritus that, when he had already been given up for dead, διαζῆσαι ἡμέρας ἱκανὰς, τῇ ἀπὸ τοῦ μέλιτος ἀναφορᾷ μόνῃ τρεφόμενον [diazēsai hēmeras hikanas, tē apo tou melitos anaphora monē trephomenon], he prolonged his life for some days, nourished only by the smell of honey. For, as follows in the same place, he took the greatest delight in the use of honey. So Πρὸς τὸν πυθόμενον, Πῶς ἂν ὑγιῶς τις διάγοι; ἔφη, Εἰ τὰ μὲν ἐντὸς μέλιτι βρέχοι, τὰ δὲ ἐκτὸς ἐλαίῳ [Pros ton pythomenon, Pōs an hygiōs tis diagoi? ephē, Ei ta men entos meliti brechoi, ta de ektos elaiō]; col. 515 to one who asked how a person might live his life in health, he replied: if the inner parts of the body were moistened with honey, the outer with oil. Not much differently Diophanes: Δημόκριτος δὲ, ἐρωτηθεὶς, Πῶς ἂν ἄνοσοι καὶ μακραίωνες γίγνοιντο οἱ ἄνθρωποι; εἶπεν, Εἰ τὰ μὲν ἔξωθεν ἐλαίῳ τοῦ σώματος, τὰ δὲ ἔνδοθεν μέλιτι [Dēmokritos de, erōtētheis, Pōs an anosoi kai makraiōnes gignointo hoi anthrōpoi? eipen, Ei ta men exōthen elaiō tou sōmatos, ta de endothen meliti] III, 371 χρήσαιντο [chrēsainto]. For this I would prefer χρίσαιντο [chrisainto]. From the same source is drawn the saying of Pollio, of which Pliny, book 22, chapter 24: Many have sustained a long old age on the nourishment of mead alone, with no other food at all, as in the famous example of Pollio Romulus. When he was past his hundredth year, the Divine Augustus, his guest, asked him by what means above all he had preserved that vigour of mind and body. And he replied: within by mead, without by oil. Mulsum [mead], that is, τῷ οἰνομέλιτι [tō oinomeliti]. It is clear, then, that honey is rightly placed among the dishes of kings and great men. This is easily proved by examples taken from Scripture. Thus honey is sent as a choice gift to Joseph, viceroy of Egypt, Gen 43:11 and to King David, when he was in the town of Mahanaim, neighbouring nobles offered honey, 2 Sam 17:29 Solomon too speaks thus, Song 5:1. I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey. Not to mention Jonathan, the king's son, whose eyes and strength were restored by eating honey, 1 Sam 14:27. If we add profane sources to these, in Iliad λ΄ [l'], line 630, we shall find that Hecamede set before Nestor and Patroclus at a banquet μέλι χλωρὸν [meli chlōron], yellow honey, or fresh honey. And in Odyssey υ΄ [y'], line 69, that Venus nourished the daughters of Pandarus τυρῷ, καὶ μέλιτι γλυκερῷ [tyrō, kai meliti glykerō], with cheese and sweet honey. So Suetonius on Nero, chapter 27: He also imposed dinners on his friends, of which one cost four million sesterces for the honeyed dishes; that is, one hundred thousand gold pieces, by Budé's reckoning. But the word mellita [honeyed dishes], which the common editions have, the most learned Casaubon changed into mitellita, because the oldest manuscripts read so. Yet he candidly admits that he does not know what mitellita are. Today, since the invention of sugar, no one will be surprised that honey has lost much of its value. 7. It is added that the bee is ποθεινὴν καὶ ἐπίδοξον [potheinēn kai epidoxon], beloved and renowned, among all. For honey is produced everywhere, as Pliny notes, book 22, chapter 23, and it is valued everywhere, from farthest Scythia to the Africans and Ethiopians. Augustine, or whoever the author is, in Sermon 15 To the Brothers in the Desert, writes that the philosopher Aristodemus toiled for many years in investigating the nature of the bee. But for Aristodemus I would gladly write Aristomachus, of whom Pliny, book 11, chapter 9: Let no one wonder that men have been captivated by love of them (the bees), that Aristomachus of Soli did nothing else for fifty-eight years, while Philiscus of Thasos, who kept bees in the wilderness, was surnamed Agrius [the Wild]. 8. Finally, the conclusion is this: Although (the bee) is weak in strength, τὴν σοφίαν τιμήσασα προήχθη [tēn sophian timēsasa proēchthē], because she honoured wisdom (that is, through the pursuit of wisdom) she has been advanced. That is, as prudence is ascribed to the serpent, so the pursuit of wisdom is ascribed to the bee καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs], since she does by natural instinct what, if human beings did it, would rightly enrol them in the catalogue of the wise. By this καταχρήσει [katachrēsei] not a few write that the bee is wise. Thus Lucian in the Halcyon calls the bee σοφὴν θείου μέλιτος ἐργάτιν [sophēn theiou melitos ergatin], the wise maker of divine honey. And Plutarch, Περὶ φιλοστοργίας [Peri philostorgias]: Τὴν μέλιτταν ἡμεῖς σοφὴν καλοῦμεν, καὶ νομίζομεν, ξανθὸν μέλι μηδομένην [Tēn melittan hēmeis sophēn kaloumen, kai nomizomen, xanthon meli mēdomenēn]; We call and consider the bee wise III, 372 because she produces yellow honey. And the wise one is wont to live a pure life. Pisides, De mundi opificio, verse 137: ¶

Who induced the wise, craftsmanlike bee to practise geometry, and to raise houses of three storeys in hexagonal construction? And further on, ¶

Μή που σε κέντρον τῆς σοφῆς διδασκάλου

Μή που σε κέντρον τῆς σοφῆς διδασκάλου Πλήξῃ μελίττης, καὶ μαθεῖν ἀναγκάσῃ· [Mē pou se kentron tēs sophēs didaskalou plēxē melittēs, kai mathein anankasē;] ¶

Ne sapientis magistrae apis aculeus te percutiat et discere cogat. Didymus libro decimo quinto Geoponicῶn, Ἡ μέλιττα πάντων τῶν λοιπῶν ζώων ἐστὶ σοφωτά-τη, καὶ εὐμηχανωτάτη, καὶ σύνεγγυς ἀνθρώπῳ κατὰ τὸ συνετόν: Apis est omnium animalium sapientissima, et artificiosissima, et ad hominis solertiam maxime accedens. Poeta denique, poetico more rem in majus efferens, voluit apes partem habere divinae mentis. Verba sunt,

Lest the sting of the bee, that wise teacher, strike you and compel you to learn. Didymus, in book 15 of the Geoponica: Ἡ μέλιττα πάντων τῶν λοιπῶν ζώων ἐστὶ σοφωτάτη, καὶ εὐμηχανωτάτη, καὶ σύνεγγυς ἀνθρώπῳ κατὰ τὸ συνετόν [Hē melitta pantōn tōn loipōn zōōn esti sophōtatē, kai eumēchanōtatē, kai synengys anthrōpō kata to syneton]: The bee is the wisest and most ingenious of all animals, and comes closest to the shrewdness of man. Finally the Poet, magnifying the matter in poetic fashion, held that bees have a share of the divine mind. His words are: ¶

Following these signs and these examples, some have said that bees have a share of the divine mind and draughts of the ether. — — — — ¶

Tertius de apum mellificio locus ex eo libro sumtus est, qui neque Hebraice exstat, neque ab Hebraeis pro canonico agnoscitur. Sed verba Graeca sic habent Ecclesiastici XI, 3. Μικρὰ ἐν πετεινοῖς μέλισσα, καὶ ἀρχὴ γλυκασμά-των ὁ καρπὸς αὐτῆς· Parva in volucribus est apis, et principium dulcedinum est fructus ejus. Parva in volucribus; id est, una e minimis, non absolute minima volucrium, ut hic habet Drusius. Nam ape minores sunt musca et culex. Sic Beth-lehem parva fuit in millibus Iuda, non tamen minima omnium, Matth. II, 6. Principium dulcedinum: id est, summe dulcis, primas tenet inter dulcia. Ut Amos VI, 6. ראשית שמנים, principio unguentorum ungunt se, id est, unguento primario. Nempe inter dulcia primas tenet apis fructus, qui est mel. Unde Hebraeum proverbium, Dulce ut mel, de quo infra. Iungendus est hic de ape versus cum proxime praecedente, Μὴ αἰνέσῃς ἄνδρα ἐν κάλλει αὐτοῦ, καὶ μὴ βδελύξῃ ἄνθρωπον ἐν ὁράσει αὐτοῦ. Non laudaveris virum ob pulchritudinem III, 373 ejus, neque hominem dedignare propter speciem ejus. Quibus verbis non significat, homini a forma nihil accedere commendationis; nam

The third passage on the honey-making of bees is taken from a book which neither survives in Hebrew nor is acknowledged as canonical by the Hebrews. The Greek words of Ecclesiasticus XI, 3. run thus: Μικρὰ ἐν πετεινοῖς μέλισσα, καὶ ἀρχὴ γλυκασμάτων ὁ καρπὸς αὐτῆς· [Mikra en peteinois melissa, kai archē glykasmatōn ho karpos autēs;] The bee is small among flying creatures, and her fruit is the beginning of sweet things. Small among flying creatures: that is, one of the smallest, not absolutely the smallest of flying creatures, as Drusius has it here. For the fly and the gnat are smaller than the bee. Thus Bethlehem was small among the thousands of Judah, yet not the least of all, Matt 2:6 The beginning of sweet things: that is, supremely sweet, holding first place among sweet things. As in Amos 6:6. ראשית שמנים, they anoint themselves with the beginning of ointments, that is, with the chief ointment. For the fruit of the bee, which is honey, holds first place among sweet things. Hence the Hebrew proverb, Sweet as honey, of which below. This verse about the bee must be joined with the one immediately preceding: Μὴ αἰνέσῃς ἄνδρα ἐν κάλλει αὐτοῦ, καὶ μὴ βδελύξῃ ἄνθρωπον ἐν ὁράσει αὐτοῦ [Mē ainesēs andra en kallei autou, kai mē bdelyxē anthrōpon en horasei autou]. Do not praise a man for his beauty III, 373, nor disdain a man because of his appearance. By these words he does not mean that a man gains no recommendation from his looks; for ¶

Gratior est pulchro veniens e corpore virtus:

Virtue is more pleasing when it comes from a handsome body: ¶

Solum igitur vult, viri laudem in eo potissimum non esse sitam, quod forma sit eximius; cum vera laus non tam in corporis, quam in animi bonis consistat. Proinde neque quisquam propter externam speciem spernendus est. Puta, si sit deformior, aut statura brevior. Saepe enim

He means only that a man's praise does not chiefly lie in his being outstanding in form, since true praise consists not so much in goods of the body as in goods of the mind. Accordingly, no one is to be despised for his outward appearance, say, if he is rather ugly or short of stature. For often ¶

Major in exiguo regnavit corpore virtus.

Greater virtue has reigned in a slight body. ¶

Quod probatur apis exemplo, quae, quamlibet parva, tamen producit fructum insignis dulcedinis. Cum haec auctoris sit vera mens, tamen Epiphanium, in Physiologo capite vigesimo primo, eundem locum multo aliter intellexisse, probant illius verba, A sapiente dicitur, parva in volucribus est apis; et principium dulcedinum fructus illius. Sic etiam Dei opera sunt ἀνεξιχνίαστα ἐν ἀνθρώποις, καὶ θαυμαστὰ ἐν ὑψηλοῖς, καὶ γλυκύτερα ὑπὲρ μέλι, inscrutabilia in hominibus, et mirabilia in excelsis, et melle dulciora. Quae ex parte petita sunt ex ejusdem Sirachidis versu quinto, Ὅτι θαυμαστὰ τὰ ἔργα Κυρίου, καὶ κρυπτὰ τὰ ἔργα αὐτοῦ ἐν ἀνθρώποις· quasi quintus ille versus cum tertio cohaereret, qui est de ape: cum cohaereat cum quarto proxime praecedenti, quo homini dixerat, non esse gloriandum, nec esse, cur se efferat, cum ab aliis honoratur. Nam Deus, cujus opera (id est, judicia,) mira sunt et inscrutabilia, potest superbos uno momento de statu suo dejicere, et in ruinam impellere: ut Nabuchodonozori obtigit, Dan. IV, 31. et Herodi, Act. XII, 23. Quem verum esse loci sensum, confirmant sequentia versu sexto et septimo de tyrannis, solo afflictis, et potenti-bus, ignominia affectis, et illustribus, traditis in manus aliorum.

This is proved by the example of the bee, which, however small, yet produces a fruit of remarkable sweetness. Although this is the true meaning of the author, nevertheless Epiphanius, in the Physiologus, chapter 21, understood the same passage quite differently, as his words show: It is said by the wise man: the bee is small among flying creatures, and her fruit is the beginning of sweet things. So too the works of God are ἀνεξιχνίαστα ἐν ἀνθρώποις, καὶ θαυμαστὰ ἐν ὑψηλοῖς, καὶ γλυκύτερα ὑπὲρ μέλι [anexichniasta en anthrōpois, kai thaumasta en hypsēlois, kai glykytera hyper meli], inscrutable among men, and wonderful in the heights, and sweeter than honey. This is partly drawn from verse 5 of the same Sirach: Ὅτι θαυμαστὰ τὰ ἔργα Κυρίου, καὶ κρυπτὰ τὰ ἔργα αὐτοῦ ἐν ἀνθρώποις· [Hoti thaumasta ta erga Kyriou, kai krypta ta erga autou en anthrōpois;] as if that fifth verse were connected with the third, which is about the bee. But it is connected with the fourth, immediately preceding, in which he had told man not to boast, and that there is no reason for him to exalt himself when he is honoured by others. For God, whose works (that is, judgements) are wonderful and inscrutable, can in a single moment cast the proud down from their station and drive them to ruin, as befell Nebuchadnezzar, Dan 4:31 and Herod, Acts 12:23. That this is the true sense of the passage is confirmed by what follows in verses 6 and 7 about tyrants brought down to the ground, the mighty covered with disgrace, and the illustrious delivered into the hands of others. ¶
