De Gallinae affectu erga pullos suos.

On the hen's love for her chicks.

Superest, ut aliquid dicamus de gallinae affectu erga pullos suos ad insignem Christi locum Matth. XXIII, 37. et Luc. XIII, 34. Ierusalem, Ierusalem, quoties volui congregare filios tuos, quomodo congregat gallina pullos suos sub alas, et noluistis? Gallina Graece est ὄρνις, quae vox avem in genere significabat apud Graecos vetustiores. Tamen Platoni lib. 7. De Legibus ὀρνίθων θρέμ-ματα putantur esse gallinarum pulli. Et Aristophani in Vespis ὀρνίθων γάλα, lac gallinarum. Et Aristoteli οἱ ὄρνιθες sunt galli, cum scribit Hist. lib. 9. cap. 50. Ἐκτέμνονται δὲ οἱ ὄρνιθες κατὰ τὸ ὀῤῥοπύγιον, id est, Castrantur galli prope uropygium. Et αἱ ὄρνιθες, gallinae, De generat. anim. lib. 1. cap. 21. ubi quosdam tradit περὶ τὰς ὄρνιθας τὰς γενναίας σπουδάζειν, operam dare, ut generosae gallinae procreentur. Neque aliud significatur in his Theophrasti, De causis lib. 2. cap. 15. Τὰ ὅλως εὔτοκα βραχύβια· καὶ τὰ ὑπερτοκήσαντα πολλάκις ἀπόλλυται, καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῶν τε συνανθρωπευομένων αἱ ὄρνιθες. Quae omnino sic reddenda: Foecundissima quaeque animalia brevis vitae sunt: et quorum foetura exuberat saepe pereunt, et gallinae potissimum inter ea, quae cum hominibus degunt. Neque in his Chrysippi apud Athenaeum lib. 9. cap. 4. Καθά-περ τινὲς τὰς λευκὰς ὀρνίθας τῶν μελανῶν ἡδίους εἶναι μᾶλλον· Ut candidas gallinas nonnulli volunt nigris esse suaviores. Sed et Comicos veteres Menandrum, Cratinum et Antiphanem pro gallinis usurpasse, exemplis allatis idem probat. Et apud eundem lib. 1. cap. 15. narrat Philarchus, τινὰς δυνάμεις στυτικὰς a Sandrocotto, Indorum rege, ad Seleucum missas, ὁρμὰς ἐμποιεῖν ὀρνίθων δίκην, Veneris appetentiam excitasse, qualis est in gallis gallinaceis. Perperam edi στυ-πτικὰς, notavit vir maximus ad illum locum; et Sandrocotto ab interprete substitui Sandropycton, et Antiochum Seleuco, et gallis passerculos, ita, ut in hoc loco nihil supersit sani. Cum igitur ὄρνις Homero et Hesiodo nihil quidquam sit, quam avis in genere; Platonis coepit aevo usurpari pro gallinaceo genere, et per aliquot saecula tam de gallo, quam de gallina dici: donec obtinuit usus, ut proprium esset gallinae nomen. Unde apud Athenaeum lib. 9. Myrtilus, Ἀλλὰ II, 696 μὴν καὶ ὄρνιθας καὶ ὀρνίθια νῦν μόνον ἡ συνήθεια καλεῖ τὰς θηλείας· Nunc quidem fert consuetudo, ut solae (gallorum) foeminae ὄρνιθες dicantur et ὀρνίθια. Itaque in Matthaeo et Luca ὄρνις non est طير vel طاير avis in genere, ut Vulgatus et Arabum unus in Luca, alter utroque loco reddit, sed دجاجة gallina, ut in Matthaeo habent Vulgatus et Erpenianus Arabs. Proinde Syrus etiam constanter explicat ܬܪܢܓܘܠܬܐ, et Aethiops ዶርሆ፡ id est, galli- nam. Ita etiam Cyprianus, et Hilarius, et Hieronymus, et Augustinus, et plerique veteres, praeter Ambrosium in Lucam, ubi hic locus ita redditur: Quoties volui congregare filios tuos, ut avis nidum suum sub pennis? Sed id gallinae maxime proprium est, quod non solum ovis incubat, quomodo reliquae aves, sed et pullos jam editos sub alis congregat, ut materno calore foveantur et vegeten- col. 126 tur. Unde est, quod Arabice cognominatur ام حفصة mater congregatio- nis. Ita diserte scribunt insignes duo Lexicographi Giauhari et Alcamus, et e naturae scriptoribus saepe a nobis citatus Damir. Hujus gallinae φιλοστοργίας ecce illustre exemplum ex Alpheo Mitylenaeo Anthologiae lib. 1. cap. 87.

It remains for us to say something about the hen's love for her chicks, in connection with the notable passage of Christ, Matt 23:37 and Luke 13:34: "Jerusalem, Jerusalem, how often would I have gathered your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you would not!" Hen in Greek is ὄρνις [ornis], a word which among the older Greeks signified bird in general. Yet in Plato, Laws book 7, ὀρνίθων θρέμματα [ornithōn thremmata] are thought to be the chicks of hens. And in Aristophanes, in the Wasps, ὀρνίθων γάλα [ornithōn gala] is hens' milk. And in Aristotle οἱ ὄρνιθες [hoi ornithes] are cocks, when he writes in Hist. book 9, ch. 50: Ἐκτέμνονται δὲ οἱ ὄρνιθες κατὰ τὸ ὀῤῥοπύγιον [ektemnontai de hoi ornithes kata to orrhopygion], that is, Cocks are castrated near the rump. And αἱ ὄρνιθες [hai ornithes] are hens in De generat. anim. book 1, ch. 21, where he reports that some people περὶ τὰς ὄρνιθας τὰς γενναίας σπουδάζειν [peri tas ornithas tas gennaias spoudazein], take pains to breed well-bred hens. Nor is anything else meant in these words of Theophrastus, De causis book 2, ch. 15: Τὰ ὅλως εὔτοκα βραχύβια· καὶ τὰ ὑπερτοκήσαντα πολλάκις ἀπόλλυται, καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῶν τε συνανθρωπευομένων αἱ ὄρνιθες [ta holōs eutoka brachybia; kai ta hypertokēsanta pollakis apollytai, kai malista epi tōn te synanthrōpeuomenōn hai ornithes]. This should certainly be rendered thus: All the most prolific animals are short-lived; and those whose breeding is excessive often perish, and hens above all among those that live with humans. Nor in these words of Chrysippus in Athenaeus, book 9, ch. 4: Καθάπερ τινὲς τὰς λευκὰς ὀρνίθας τῶν μελανῶν ἡδίους εἶναι μᾶλλον [kathaper tines tas leukas ornithas tōn melanōn hēdious einai mallon]; As some hold that white hens are tastier than black ones. And he likewise proves, by adducing examples, that the old comic poets Menander, Cratinus and Antiphanes used it for hens. And in the same author, book 1, ch. 15, Phylarchus relates that τινὰς δυνάμεις στυτικὰς [tinas dynameis stytikas] sent by Sandrocottus, king of the Indians, to Seleucus, ὁρμὰς ἐμποιεῖν ὀρνίθων δίκην [hormas empoiein ornithōn dikēn], excited sexual desire, such as is found in cocks. A very great man noted on that passage that στυπτικὰς [styptikas] is wrongly printed; and that the translator substituted Sandropyctus for Sandrocottus, Antiochus for Seleucus, and little sparrows for cocks, so that nothing sound remains in this passage. Since, then, ὄρνις [ornis] in Homer and Hesiod is nothing other than bird in general, in Plato's age it began to be used for the gallinaceous kind, and for some centuries was said of the cock as well as of the hen, until usage prevailed that it was the proper name of the hen. Hence Myrtilus in Athenaeus, book 9: Ἀλλὰ II, 696 μὴν καὶ ὄρνιθας καὶ ὀρνίθια νῦν μόνον ἡ συνήθεια καλεῖ τὰς θηλείας [alla mēn kai ornithas kai ornithia nyn monon hē synētheia kalei tas thēleias]; Now indeed custom has it that only the females (of cocks) are called ὄρνιθες [ornithes] and ὀρνίθια [ornithia]. Therefore in Matthew and Luke ὄρνις [ornis] is not طير [ṭayr] or طاير [ṭāyir], bird in general, as the Vulgate translator renders it in Luke, and as one of the Arabic versions renders it in Luke and the other in both places, but دجاجة [dajāja], hen, as the Vulgate translator and Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] have it in Matthew. Accordingly the Syriac too consistently renders ܬܪܢܓܘܠܬܐ [תרנגולתא], and the Ethiopic ዶርሆ፡, that is, hen. So also Cyprian, Hilary, Jerome, Augustine, and most of the ancients, except Ambrose on Luke, where this passage is rendered thus: How often would I have gathered your children, as a bird its nest under its feathers? But this is most especially proper to the hen, that she not only sits on her eggs, like the other birds, but also gathers her chicks, once hatched, under her wings, so that they may be warmed and invigorated col. 126 by their mother's heat. Hence it is that in Arabic she is surnamed ام حفصة [umm ḥafṣa], mother of gathering. So the two distinguished lexicographers Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] expressly write, and, among the writers on nature, Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], often cited by us. Here is a striking example of this hen's φιλοστοργία [philostorgia] from Alpheus of Mytilene, Anthology book 1, ch. 87.

Χειμερίαις νιφάδεσσι παλυνομένα τιθὰς ὄρνις Τέκνοις εὐναίας ἀμφέχεε πτέρυγας· Μέσφά μιν οὐράνιον κρύος ὤλεσεν. ἦ γὰρ ἔμεινεν Αἰθέρος οὐρανίων ἀντίπαλος νεφέων. Πρόκνη, καὶ Μήδεια, κατ᾽ ἀΐδος αἰδέσθητε, Μητέρες, ὀρνιθίων ἔργα διδασκόμεναι.

Χειμερίαις νιφάδεσσι παλυνομένα τιθὰς ὄρνις Τέκνοις εὐναίας ἀμφέχεε πτέρυγας· Μέσφά μιν οὐράνιον κρύος ὤλεσεν. ἦ γὰρ ἔμεινεν Αἰθέρος οὐρανίων ἀντίπαλος νεφέων. Πρόκνη, καὶ Μήδεια, κατ᾽ ἀΐδος αἰδέσθητε, Μητέρες, ὀρνιθίων ἔργα διδασκόμεναι. [Cheimeriais niphadessi palynomena tithas ornis teknois eunaias amphechee pterygas; mespha min ouranion kryos ōlesen. ē gar emeinen aitheros ouraniōn antipalos nepheōn. Proknē, kai Mēdeia, kat᾽ aidos aidesthēte, mēteres, ornithiōn erga didaskomenai.]

Hyemalibus nivibus aspersa mitis gallina pullis soporiferas circumfudit alas: donec illam perdidit coeleste frigus. Certe permansit coelestium oppugnatrix nubium. O matres, Progne et Medea, in Inferis erubescite, gallinarum opera discentes. Et eximius scriptor Plutarchus in libro De philostorgia ob oculos rem ita ponit, Τὰς δὲ ἀλεκτορίδας ἐν τοῖς ὄμμασι καθ᾽ ἡμέραν ἔχομεν, ὃν τρόπον τὰ νεοττία περιέπουσι, τοῖς μὲν ἐνδῦναι χαλῶσαι τὰς πτέρυγας, τὰ δὲ ἐπιβαίνοντα τῶν νώτων, καὶ προσέρχονται πανταχόθεν ἀναδεχόμεναι, μετὰ τοῦ γεγηθὸς, ἢ καὶ προσφιλὲς ἐπιφθέγγεσθαι. κυνέας δὲ, καὶ δρακοντίας (lege κύνας δὲ, καὶ δράκοντας, vel δρακόντια) ἂν περὶ αὐτῶν φοβηθῶσι φεύγουσιν· ἂν δὲ περὶ τῶν τέκνων, ἀμύνονται, καὶ διαμάχονται παρὰ δύναμιν. Gallinae autem, quae quotidie oculis nostris obversantur, quanta cura pullos suos tuentur, aliis qui- dem alas, quas subeunt remittentes, aliis autem dorsum conscendentibus? Et acce- dunt undequaque suscipientes, ita, ut gaudium et amorem voce testentur. Canes autem et dracones, si sibi metuant, fugiunt: at pullis si metuant, propulsant, et supra vires dimicant. Quod ipsum his verbis observat Augustinus in Quaestioni- bus Evangelicis lib. 1. quaest. 36. Quod difficilius in caeteris animantibus invenies, II, 697 alis suis filios protegens contra milvum pugnat. Sed et idem saepe asserit, gallinae id esse peculiare, quod pullorum suorum infirmitate sic afficitur, ut et ipsa infirma et aegra fiat. Ita in Psalmum 58. Infirmatus est (Christus) usque ad mortem, et assumpsit infirmitatis carnem, ut pullos Ierusalem colligeret sub alas suas, tanquam gallina infirmatur cum parvulis. Et paullo post, Certe notam rem dico, quae in conspectu nostro quotidie versatur. Quomodo raucescit vox? quomodo hispidum fit totum corpus, demittuntur alae, laxantur plumae? Et in Psalmum XC. Multae hic aves ante nos foetant, calefaciunt pullos suos: nulla sic avis infirmatur cum pullis suis, quomodo gallina. Attendat charitas vestra hirundines, passeres et ciconias. Videmus extra nidos suos, nec cognoscimus, utrum foetum habeant. At vero gallinam cognoscimus in infirmitate vocis et in relaxatione plumarum: tota mutatur affectu pullorum; quia illi infirmi sunt, infirmam se facit. Et De Verbis Domini Sermone 29. Illa gallina Domini sapientia est. Sed assumpsit carnem, ut pullis congrueret. Videte gallinam hispidam plumis, dimissis alis, voce fracta, et quassa, ac lassa, et languida congruere pullis suis. Et in Quaestionibus ubi supra, Hoc genus animantis magnum affectum in filios habet, ita, ut eorum infirmitate affecta et ipsa infirmetur. Hinc apud Euripidem, in Hercule Furente, Megara de liberis, quos ex Hercule habuerat, maxime sollicita, eos sub alis servare se dictitat, ut pullos gallina incubans. Verba sunt:

Sprinkled with the winter snows, the gentle hen spread her sleep-bringing wings over her chicks, until the cold of the sky killed her. Truly she held out as a fighter against the clouds of heaven. O mothers, Procne and Medea, blush in the underworld, as you learn the works of hens. And the excellent writer Plutarch, in the book On Affection for Offspring, sets the matter before our eyes thus: Τὰς δὲ ἀλεκτορίδας ἐν τοῖς ὄμμασι καθ᾽ ἡμέραν ἔχομεν, ὃν τρόπον τὰ νεοττία περιέπουσι, τοῖς μὲν ἐνδῦναι χαλῶσαι τὰς πτέρυγας, τὰ δὲ ἐπιβαίνοντα τῶν νώτων, καὶ προσέρχονται πανταχόθεν ἀναδεχόμεναι, μετὰ τοῦ γεγηθὸς, ἢ καὶ προσφιλὲς ἐπιφθέγγεσθαι. κυνέας δὲ, καὶ δρακοντίας [Tas de alektoridas en tois ommasi kath᾽ hēmeran echomen, hon tropon ta neottia periepousi, tois men endynai chalōsai tas pterygas, ta de epibainonta tōn nōtōn, kai proserchontai pantachothen anadechomenai, meta tou gegēthos, ē kai prosphiles epiphthengesthai. kyneas de, kai drakontias] (read κύνας δὲ, καὶ δράκοντας [kynas de, kai drakontas], or δρακόντια [drakontia]) ἂν περὶ αὐτῶν φοβηθῶσι φεύγουσιν· ἂν δὲ περὶ τῶν τέκνων, ἀμύνονται, καὶ διαμάχονται παρὰ δύναμιν [an peri autōn phobēthōsi pheugousin; an de peri tōn teknōn, amynontai, kai diamachontai para dynamin]. And hens, which are before our eyes every day: with what care they protect their chicks, letting down their wings for some to creep under, while others climb onto their backs! And they come up from every side to receive them, so as to testify their joy and love with their voice. Dogs and serpents, if they fear for themselves, flee; but if they fear for their young, they drive off the attacker and fight beyond their strength. Augustine observes this very thing in these words in the Gospel Questions, book 1, question 36: What you will find with greater difficulty in other animals, protecting her young with her wings she II, 697 fights against the kite. But he also often asserts that it is peculiar to the hen that she is so affected by the weakness of her chicks that she herself becomes weak and sick. Thus on Psalm 58: He (Christ) was made weak even unto death, and took on the flesh of weakness, in order to gather the chicks of Jerusalem under his wings, as the hen becomes weak with her little ones. And a little later: Surely I speak of a well-known thing, which is before our eyes every day. How her voice grows hoarse! How her whole body becomes bristly, her wings droop, her feathers loosen! And on Psalm XC.: Many birds here breed before our eyes and warm their chicks; no bird becomes weak with her chicks as the hen does. Let your charity consider swallows, sparrows and storks. We see them outside their nests, and we do not know whether they have young. But the hen we recognize by the weakness of her voice and the loosening of her feathers: she is wholly changed by affection for her chicks; because they are weak, she makes herself weak. And in On the Words of the Lord, Sermon 29: That hen is the wisdom of the Lord. But it took on flesh in order to be suited to the chicks. See the hen, bristling in her feathers, wings lowered, voice broken, shaken, weary and languid, suiting herself to her chicks. And in the Questions cited above: This kind of animal has great affection for its young, so that, affected by their weakness, it too becomes weak. Hence in Euripides, in the Madness of Heracles, Megara, greatly anxious about the children she had borne to Hercules, repeatedly says that she keeps them under her wings, like a hen brooding her chicks. The words are:

Οἵ θ᾽ Ἡράκλειοι παῖδες, οὓς ὑποπτέρους Σώζω νεοσσοὺς, ὄρνις ὡς ὑφειμένη· Et quos sub alis servo natos Herculis, Pullis ut incubare gallina assolet.

Οἵ θ᾽ Ἡράκλειοι παῖδες, οὓς ὑποπτέρους Σώζω νεοσσοὺς, ὄρνις ὡς ὑφειμένη· [Hoi th᾽ Hērakleioi paides, hous hypopterous Sōzō neossous, ornis hōs hypheimenē] And the sons of Hercules, whom under my wings I keep, as a hen is wont to brood her chicks.

Quae ideo hic affero, quia sunt Christi verbis quam simillima, quibus suam φιλανθρωπίαν confert cum gallinae φιλοστοργίᾳ, pullos suos summo studio ad se alliciente, ut ab imminente milvo, aut accipitre sartos tectos sub alis tueatur.

I adduce these things here because they are very similar to the words of Christ, in which he compares his φιλανθρωπίαν [philanthrōpian] with the φιλοστοργίᾳ [philostorgia] of the hen, which with the greatest eagerness draws her chicks to herself, in order to keep them safe and sound under her wings from the threatening kite or hawk.
