De providentia Dei circa Passeres et aviculas, in Matth. X, 29. et Luc. XII, 6.

On the providence of God concerning sparrows and little birds, in Matt 10:29 and Luke 12:6. ¶

Christus Matth. X, 29. dicit, duos passeres assario vendi, nec tamen eorum ul-lum in terram cadere sine Deo. Et Luc. XII, 6. quinque passeres duobus assariis venire, nec tamen eorum ullum in oblivione esse apud Deum. Itaque singuli passeres semisse vendebantur, aut paullo minus. Falluntur enim, qui as-sarium semissem esse volunt. Quin assarium est idem, quod as. Glossae Latino-Graecae, Asse, Ἀσσάριον, lege Ἀσσαρίου. Et alibi, Sesquas, Ἓι ἥμισυ ἀσσάριον. Lege, Ἓν ἥμισυ καὶ ἀσσάριον. Glossae Graeco-latinae Phloxeni, Ἀσσάριον, Hic as. Ἀσσαρίου ἥμισυ, Sesquas, lege Ἓν ἀσσάριον καὶ ἥμισυ. As, ex Budaei supputatione, quaternos denariolos nostros valuit et pauxillo plus. Syrus interpres habet ܐܣܪ assar, qua voce pro assario Hebraei etiam saepe utuntur. A Davide Cohen de Lara, in libro De convenientia Rabbinismi col. 158 cum Graeco, אסר assar Hispanice definitur moneda de tres maravedis, quod ad Budaei λογισμὸν proxime accedit. Oboli pondus, qui gera dicitur, est sedecim granorum hordei. Pondus assarii quatuor granorum, peruta dimidii grani. Similem locum citat Drusius ex libro Mandatorum. Pro assario فلس follem, et pro duobus assariis فلسين duos folles habet Arabs interpres, et Hieronymus dipondium. Sed Matth. V, 26. in eodem Arabe follis est pro quadrante. Et Marc. Christ, in Matt 10:29, says that two sparrows are sold for an assarion, and yet not one of them falls to the ground without God. And in Luke 12:6 he says that five sparrows are sold for two assaria, and yet not one of them is forgotten before God. Thus individual sparrows were sold for half an as, or a little less. For those are mistaken who hold that the assarion is half an as. Rather, the assarion is the same as the as. The Latin-Greek Glosses: Asse, Ἀσσάριον [assarion]; read Ἀσσαρίου [assariou]. And elsewhere: Sesquas, Ἓι ἥμισυ ἀσσάριον [hei hēmisy assarion]. Read Ἓν ἥμισυ καὶ ἀσσάριον [hen hēmisy kai assarion]. The Greek-Latin Glosses of Philoxenus: Ἀσσάριον [assarion], Hic as.; Ἀσσαρίου ἥμισυ [assariou hēmisy], Sesquas; read Ἓν ἀσσάριον καὶ ἥμισυ [hen assarion kai hēmisy]. The as, by Budé's reckoning, was worth four of our little deniers and a trifle more. The Syriac translator has ܐܣܪ [אסר] assar, a word which the Hebrews also often use for the assarion. David Cohen de Lara, in his book De convenientia Rabbinismi col. 158 cum Graeco, defines אסר assar in Spanish as moneda de tres maravedis, which comes very close to Budé's λογισμὸν [logismon]. In Maimonides' [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] treatise On Shekels, ch. 1. 3.: הסלע ארבעה דינרין והדינר שש מעין ומעה היא היקראת בימי משה רבינו גרה ומאה היא שני פונדיונין ופונדיון שני אסרין ופרוטה אחת משמונה באסר נמצא משקל המעה והיא הגרה שש עשרה שעורות ומשקל האסר ארבע שעורות ומשקל פרוטה חצי שעורה, The shekel is worth four denarii. The denarius six obols. The obol was called a gerah in the days of Moses our master. The obol is worth two II, 736 pondia, and the pondion two assaria. And the perutah is the eighth part of an assarion. The weight of the obol, which is called a gerah, is sixteen barley grains. The weight of the assarion four grains, of the perutah half a grain. Drusius cites a similar passage from the Book of Commandments. For assarion the Arabic translator has فلس [fals] follis, and for two assaria فلسين [falsayn] two folles; and Jerome has dipondium. But in Matt 5:26 the same Arabic version has follis for quadrans. And in Mark 14:42 فلسين [falsayn] two folles stands for two mites, that is, two פרוטות perutoth, which together make one quadrans. It is evident, therefore, that this translator, caring little about the true value of the coins, was content to put one cheap coin for another cheap coin, though of different value. And indeed, to be sold for an assarion or an as means nothing other than to be sold off at a low price. For whatever is sold or valued at an as must be cheap. Hence Catullus to his Lesbia: ¶

Rumoresque senum severiorum,
Omnes unius aestimemus assis.

And the gossip of stern old men, let us value it all at a single as. ¶

Et in Vivennios,

And against the Vibennii: ¶

Fili, non potes asse venditare.

Son, you cannot sell it for an as. ¶

Et in quendam,

And against a certain man: ¶

Non assis facis? O lutum, lupanar!

You think it not worth an as? O filth, O brothel! ¶

Et Cicero pro Quintio, Assem sese negat daturum, nisi prius de rebus rationibusque socitatis omnibus decidisset. Et pro Roscio Comoedo, Dic LLS. CCCIↃↃↃ dedisse, qui assem nullum dedit. Et Iuvenalis Satyra 10.

And Cicero, for Quinctius: He says he will not pay an as unless he has first settled all the affairs and accounts of the partnership. And for Roscius the Comedian: Say he gave LLS. CCCIↃↃↃ, he who gave not a single as. And Juvenal, Satire 10: ¶

Quisquis adhuc uno partam colit asse Minervam.

Whoever still worships Minerva, acquired for a single as. ¶

Et Misna in fine tractatus Cholin, ubi de praecepto, quo jubentur Israelitae matrem dmittere, cum ova et pullos in nido capiunt, ut illis bene sit, et prorogenturdies, Deut. XXII, 6. 7. אם מצוה קלה שהיא באסר אמרה תורה למען ייטב לך והארכת ימים קל וחומר על מצות חמורות שבתורה, Si in praecepto leviore, de re, quae vix assarii est, Lex ait, „Ut bene sit tibi, et proroget dies „tuos:“ quanto magis (idem sperandum) de gravioribus praeceptis, qui sunt in Lege? And the Mishnah at the end of the tractate Chullin, concerning the precept by which the Israelites are commanded to let the mother go when they take eggs and young from a nest, that it may be well with them and their days be prolonged, Deut 22:6–7.: אם מצוה קלה שהיא באסר אמרה תורה למען ייטב לך והארכת ימים קל וחומר על מצות חמורות שבתורה, If concerning a lighter precept, about a thing that is scarcely worth an assarion, the Law says, “That it may be well with thee, and that thou mayest prolong thy days,” how much more (is the same to be hoped) for the weightier precepts that are in the Law? For assarion the older Greeks would have said obol. Thus in the Birds of Aristophanes the birds complain bitterly of a certain Philocrates, who, ¶

— συνείρων τοὺς σπίνους, πωλεῖ καθ᾽ ἑπτὰ ὀβολοῦ,

— συνείρων τοὺς σπίνους, πωλεῖ καθ᾽ ἑπτὰ ὀβολοῦ, [syneirōn tous spinous, pōlei kath᾽ hepta obolou,] ¶

Fringillos connectens, septem vendit uno obolo. Obolo, id est, duobus assariis, sive assibus. Quod docent haec Polybii Historiarum lib. 2. Ὡς μὲν οὖν ἐπιτοπο-λὺ παρίενται τοὺς καταλύτας οἱ πανδοχεῖς ἡμιασσαρίου, τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐστὶ τέταρ-τον μέρος ὀβολοῦ. Hospites suos plerumque caupones accipiunt semisse, seu dimidio assario, quod est oboli quarta pars. Cum igitur quinque passeres duobus II, 737 assariis venire dicit Christus, idem est, ac si dixisset, obolo vendi. Unde colligis, Athenis aevo Aristophanis aliquanto viliorem fuisse annonam, quam in Iudaea Christi aetate. Nam quo pretio Athenienses septem fringillos, nonnisi quinque passeres Iudaei comparabant. Latinus porro Aristophanis interpres pro fringillis habet passeres; quia passer et fringillus sunt aves simillimae. Hinc Glos- col. 159 sae Philoxeni, Σπίνος· ὁ στρουθὸς, Frincillus, fringillus. Quasi σπίνος idem esset, quod στρουθὸς, seu passer. Fringillus, Gallis pinson, olim forte pintion, ut Hesychio σπίνθιον. Tale chanson ex cantione, maison ex mansione, raison ex ratione, etc. Sed, sive quinque, vel septem hujusmodi aviculae obolo distrahantur, et matrem solam, aut matrem cum pullis, Misnae auctor assem aestimaverit, vilissima oportet esse animalia, cum tam levi pretio prostent. Tamen hic Christus nos docet, etiam haec animalcula Deo ita esse curae, ut eorum nullum absque eo in terram cadat, id est, eo non permittente pereat. Quibus haec Lucae consona: Ἓν ἐξ αὐτῶν οὐκ ἔστιν ἐπιλελησμένον ἐνώπιον τοῦ Θεοῦ· Unus ex illis non est in oblivione coram Deo. Phrasi sumpta ex Psal. IX, 19. ubi de paupere dicitur, Οὐκ εἰς τέλος ἐπιλησθήσεται, Non in aeternum oblivioni erit. Hebraice לא לנצח ישכח. Unde regius Psaltes tam de pusillis, quam de magnis animalibus Psal. CIV, 27. Omnia ista a te exspectant, ut des illis escam in tempore suo. Tamen, ut de Iudaeis taceam in praesens, quorum plerique in diversa sunt sententia, Hieronymus in Habacucum cap. 1. haec habet: Absurdum est, ad hoc Dei deducere majestatem, ut sciat, per momenta singula quot nascantur culices, quotve moriantur: quae cimicum, et pulicum, et muscarum sit in terra multitudo: quanti pisces in aqua natent: et qui de minoribus majorum praedae cedere debeant. Ne simus tam fatui adulatores Dei, ut, dum potentiam ejus ad ima detrahimus, in nos ipsos injuriosi simus; eandem rationabilium quam irrationabilium providentiam esse dicentes. Quae duriora videri poterunt, nisi benigna interpretatione molliantur; ita, ut intelligamus, haec comparate dici (quod sub finem innuit), ne putetur Deus brutorum eandem habere rationem, quam hominum, quos creavit ad suam imaginem: cum bruta servet praecipue propter homines, in quorum usum creata sunt. Diversos igitur agnoscimus divinae providentiae gradus, neque Deum curare boves eodem modo, quo homines, quod docet Paulus 1 Cor. IX, 9. Tamen tam boves, quam homines, et vilis- col. 160 simas etiam creaturas Deo subesse, certum est. Neque is regere et conservare dedignatur, quae creare dignatus est. Et cavendum, ne fingamus limites illius providentiae, quam, ut nec maxima onerant, sic nec minima effugiunt, aut ab ea negliguntur. Quis credat, Deum ignorasse numerum coturnicum, quibus Israelitas in deserto pavit, aut piscium, qui natabant in lacu Genesareth, cum Christi jussu Petrus jecit retia? Aut nescivisse, quot ranis aut pediculis usurus esset in Aegyptios; aut locustas illas, quae vocantur Dei copiae Ioel. II, 11. ipsi II, 738 non fuisse curae, et summum illum ducem fugisse, quot milites in exercitu suo conscripserit? Denique quid Christi verbis disertius, nullum ex passeribus in terram cadere sine Deo, aut in oblivione esse coram illo? In quem locum haec observanda, quae ex Talmude Hierosolymitano profert doctissimus Buxtorfius Lexici Talmudici pag. 553. Rabbi Simeon Ben Iochai latebat in quadam spelunca. Die quodam sedens ad ostium speluncae, et videns aucupem, aves capientem et rete extendentem, audivit filiam vocis (oraculum), quae cum dicebat דימוס dimos (id est, liberatio), tum evadebat avis. Quando vero filia vocis dicebat ספיקולא, spicula (id est, sententia mortis), tum avis capiebatur. Proinde dixit oraculum, צפור מבלברי שמיא לא יבדא כל שכן בר נשא, Avicula sine coelo (id est, sine Dei voluntate et providentia) non perit: quanto minus homo! Pro לא יבדא lege לא יאבד. His etiam ethnici non dissimilia scribunt. Sic Plutarchus alicubi ex Homero probat, veteres in ea fuisse sententia, Dei numine curari οὐ μόνον βόας, ἀλλὰ καὶ πάντα τὰ ἄλλα ζῶα, non solum boves, sed et omnia alia animalia. Et Aelianus Hist. lib. 11. cap. 31. Ἴδιον δὲ ἄρα τῶν ζώων καὶ ἐκεῖνο ἀγαθὸν, πρόνοιαν αὐτῶν καὶ οἱ Θεοὶ ποιοῦνται, καὶ οὔτε αὐτῶν καταφρονοῦσιν, οὐδὲ μὴν ὀλιγώρως ἔχουσιν· Etiam brutis competit hoc bonum, quod iis Dii provident, neque illa despiciunt, aut susque deque habent.

Tying finches together, he sells seven for one obol. For an obol, that is, for two assaria, or asses. This is shown by these words of Polybius, Histories book 2: Ὡς μὲν οὖν ἐπιτοπολὺ παρίενται τοὺς καταλύτας οἱ πανδοχεῖς ἡμιασσαρίου, τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐστὶ τέταρτον μέρος ὀβολοῦ [Hōs men oun epitopoly parieнтai tous katalytas hoi pandocheis hēmiassariou, touto d᾽ esti tetarton meros obolou]. Innkeepers generally receive their guests for half an as, or half an assarion, which is a quarter of an obol. So when Christ says that five sparrows are sold for two II, 737 assaria, it is the same as if he had said they are sold for an obol. From this you may infer that at Athens in the time of Aristophanes provisions were somewhat cheaper than in Judaea in the time of Christ. For at the price for which the Athenians bought seven finches, the Jews bought only five sparrows. Further, the Latin translator of Aristophanes has passeres [sparrows] for fringilli [finches], because the sparrow and the finch are very similar birds. Hence the Glosses col. 159 of Philoxenus: Σπίνος· ὁ στρουθὸς [Spinos; ho strouthos], Frincillus, fringillus, as if σπίνος [spinos] were the same as στρουθὸς [strouthos], or passer [sparrow]. Fringillus is in French pinson, formerly perhaps pintion, like σπίνθιον [spinthion] in Hesychius. So too chanson from cantione, maison from mansione, raison from ratione, etc. But whether five or seven such little birds are sold for an obol, and whether the author of the Mishnah valued the mother alone, or the mother with her young, at an as, they must be very cheap animals, since they are on sale for so slight a price. Yet here Christ teaches us that even these little creatures are such a concern to God that none of them falls to the ground without him, that is, perishes without his permission. With this agrees Luke: Ἓν ἐξ αὐτῶν οὐκ ἔστιν ἐπιλελησμένον ἐνώπιον τοῦ Θεοῦ [Hen ex autōn ouk estin epilelēsmenon enōpion tou Theou]; Not one of them is forgotten before God. The phrase is taken from Ps 9:19, where it is said of the poor man: Οὐκ εἰς τέλος ἐπιλησθήσεται [Ouk eis telos epilēsthēsetai], He shall not be forgotten for ever; in Hebrew לא לנצח ישכח. Hence the royal Psalmist, of small and great animals alike, Ps 104:27: These all wait upon you, that you may give them their food in due season. Yet, to say nothing for the present of the Jews, most of whom hold a different opinion, Jerome on Habakkuk ch. 1 says this: It is absurd to lower the majesty of God to this, that he should know at every moment how many gnats are born and how many die; how great is the multitude of bugs, fleas and flies on the earth; how many fish swim in the water; and which of the smaller must fall prey to the larger. Let us not be such foolish flatterers of God that, while we drag his power down to the lowest things, we do injury to ourselves, saying that the providence over rational and irrational creatures is the same. These words might seem rather harsh unless softened by a kindly interpretation, so that we understand them to be said comparatively (as he hints toward the end): lest God be thought to have the same regard for brutes as for men, whom he created in his own image, since he preserves the brutes chiefly for the sake of men, for whose use they were created. We therefore recognize different degrees of divine providence, and that God does not care for oxen in the same way as for men, as Paul teaches, 1 Cor 9:9. Yet it is certain that oxen as well as men, and even the meanest col. 160 creatures, are subject to God. Nor does he disdain to govern and preserve what he deigned to create. And we must beware of inventing limits to that providence, which, as the greatest things do not burden it, so the smallest do not escape it or go neglected by it. Who would believe that God did not know the number of the quails with which he fed the Israelites in the desert, or of the fish that swam in the lake of Gennesaret when Peter cast his nets at Christ's command? Or that he did not know how many frogs or lice he would use against the Egyptians; or that those locusts which are called God's army, Joel 2:11, were of no II, 738 concern to him, and that this supreme commander did not know how many soldiers he had enlisted in his army? Finally, what could be plainer than Christ's words, that none of the sparrows falls to the ground without God, or is forgotten before him? On this passage note what the most learned Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] cites from the Jerusalem Talmud in his Lexicon Talmudicum, p. 553: Rabbi Simeon ben Yochai was hiding in a cave. One day, sitting at the mouth of the cave and seeing a fowler catching birds and spreading his net, he heard a daughter of the voice (an oracle); when it said דימוס dimos (that is, release), the bird escaped. But when the daughter of the voice said ספיקולא, spicula (that is, sentence of death), the bird was caught. Then the oracle said, צפור מבלברי שמיא לא יבדא כל שכן בר נשא, A little bird does not perish without heaven (that is, without God's will and providence): how much less a man! For לא יבדא read לא יאבד. The pagans too write things not unlike these. Thus Plutarch somewhere proves from Homer that the ancients held the opinion that by the divine power are cared for οὐ μόνον βόας, ἀλλὰ καὶ πάντα τὰ ἄλλα ζῶα [ou monon boas, alla kai panta ta alla zōa], not only oxen, but all other animals too. And Aelian, Hist. book 11, ch. 31: Ἴδιον δὲ ἄρα τῶν ζώων καὶ ἐκεῖνο ἀγαθὸν, πρόνοιαν αὐτῶν καὶ οἱ Θεοὶ ποιοῦνται, καὶ οὔτε αὐτῶν καταφρονοῦσιν, οὐδὲ μὴν ὀλιγώρως ἔχουσιν [Idion de ara tōn zōōn kai ekeino agathon, pronoian autōn kai hoi Theoi poiountai, kai oute autōn kataphronousin, oude mēn oligōrōs echousin]; The brutes too have this good, that the gods provide for them, and neither despise them nor treat them with indifference. ¶
