III, 194 Chapter 11. col. 408 ¶

Probatur, voce קפוז kippoz, Es. XXXIV, 15. neque Hericium, ut veteribus, neque avis speciem significari, ut recentioribus visum: sed serpentem Acontiam, sive Iaculum.

It is proved that the word קפוז kippoz, Isa 34:15, signifies neither the hedgehog, as the ancients thought, nor a kind of bird, as the moderns thought, but the serpent acontias, or iaculus. ¶

Bostram et Idumaeam ferro et igne ita vastandam interminatur Esaias capite trigesimo quarto, ut, omnibus incolis vel pulsis, vel extinctis, feris tantum et solivagis animalibus sit deinceps habitabilis. In his nominatur קפוז kippoz, de quo sic agit divinus Vates versu decimo quinto, Ibi nidificabit kippoz, et liberabitur, et rumpet, et congregabit in umbra sua. Sic Hebraea sonant ad verbum. Sed, verbis תמלט, et בקעה, et דגרה, ex generali significatione translatis ad specialem, recentiores Hebraei hunc versum recte reddunt: Ibi nidificabit kippoz, et pariet, et excludet, et fovebit in umbra sua. Nempe מלט malat in genere est liberare, vel liberari; hic parere, ut Es. LXVI, 7. המליטה זכר, ubi nostra versio ad verbum, Elle a est è deliuree d' un masle, phrasi tam Hebraica, quam Gallica. Idem verbum ملط malata etiam Arabes de partu usurpant, sed de eo proprie, qui abortivus et intempestivus est. Assyria Venus, quam praesedisse partui, atque eandem fuisse cum Lucina et Ilithya, multis contendit Seldenus in libello De Diis Syris, ab hoc Verbo videtur nomen habuisse Μυλίττας, ut col. 409 est in Herodoto; vel Μυλήτας et Μιλίτης, ut est in Hesychio; potius quam a Verbo ילד, generare, prout conjicit Scaliger: cum ut verbi sonus, ita etiam significatio congruat magis. Lucina enim in partu juvat, non in generatione. Etiam בקע in genere est findere, vel rumpere, hic ovi ruptione parvulos excludere: ut Esa. LIX, 5. Ova basilisci בקעו, ruperunt, id est, excluserunt. Sic Latine dicitur pullus ex ovo erumpere. Denique דגר apud Chaldaeos, a quibus desumtum est hoc Verbum, in genere est congregare, hic congregare sub se ova vel pullos, ut calore materno foveantur. Non aliter sumitur Ier. XVII, 11. ubi Propheta dicit, קרא דגר ולא ילד, Rusticula congregat (id est, fovet) quae non peperit. Nec a Chaldaeo interprete, cum Hebraeum תחמם, calefaciet, de struthionis ovis, reddit תדגר, congregabit, id est, fovebit, Iob. XXXIX, 14. (al. 17.). Insuper in haec verba, בקעה ודגרה, excludet et fovebit, notandum, conjunctionem et idem esse quod cum, vel postquam: ac si Propheta scripsisset, Excludet ova, postquam ea foverit. Sic Exod. XIV, 21. Redegit mare in siccum, et divisae sunt aquae: id est, postquam divisae sunt aquae. Et Esa. LXIV, 5. Iratus es, et peccavimus: id est, cum peccavimus, vel quia peccavimus. Ita Kimchius. III, 195 Sed haec nemini non sunt obvia: quid sit קפוז kippoz, non item; Cum alibi nullum occurrat animal hujus nominis, nec in Scriptura, nec in Talmude, nec in Syrorum aut Chaldaeorum libris. Dicam tamen de ea voce quid senserint cum veteres, tum recentiores interpretes. Graeci, Chaldaeus, Syrus, Arabs, Vulgatus, Hieronymus, Pomarius, Schindlerus reddunt hericium, ac si קפוז kippoz sit idem, quod קפוד kippod, paullo ante versu 11. Ita suadente scilicet soni vicinitate, et literarum Z et D facili permutatione. Kimchius item, et Selomo, et Mardochaeus, et Aquinas, et Hebraei fere quotquot sunt, kippod et kippoz pro eodem habent. Sed kippod, adeoque et kippoz, alius testudinem exponit, alius noctuam, alius in genere avem solitariam. Solus Aben Ezra, quod sciam, kippod et kippoz diversas aves esse vult, sed quales, non explicat. Sed et eorum interpretum, qui nostro aevo in novis versionibus elucubrandis utilem operam navarunt, non parva hic est dissensio. Santes exponit hericium, Castalio bubonem, Iunius merulam, Deodatius il gufo, id est, scopem; Angli the great Owe, id est, magnam ululam; Galli, post Mercerum, le martinet, id est, cypselum; Arias ipsam vocem Hebraeam in Latinum textum inserit, ingenue professus, eam a se non intelligi.

In chapter 34 Isaiah threatens that Bozrah and Idumaea are to be laid waste by sword and fire, so that, all the inhabitants being either driven out or destroyed, it will thereafter be habitable only for wild and solitary animals. Among these is named קפוז kippoz, of which the divine Prophet speaks thus in verse 15: There shall kippoz nest, and shall be delivered, and shall break, and shall gather in its shadow. So the Hebrew runs word for word. But, taking the verbs תמלט, בקעה and דגרה from their general meaning to a special one, the more recent Hebrews rightly render this verse: There shall kippoz nest, and lay, and hatch, and brood in its shadow. For מלט malat in general is to deliver or to be delivered; here it is to bring forth, as in Es. LXVI, 7, המליטה זכר, where our version has, word for word, Elle a est è deliuree d' un masle, a phrase as much Hebrew as French. The Arabs also use the same verb ملط [malaṭa] malata of birth, but properly of one that is abortive and untimely. The Assyrian Venus, who, as Selden argues at length in his little book De Diis Syris, presided over childbirth and was the same as Lucina and Ilithyia, seems to have taken from this verb the name Μυλίττας [Mylittas], as it is in Herodotus, col. 409 or Μυλήτας [Mylētas] and Μιλίτης [Militēs], as it is in Hesychius, rather than from the verb ילד, to beget, as Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] conjectures; for the meaning, like the sound of the word, agrees better. Lucina, after all, helps in childbirth, not in begetting. Likewise בקע in general is to split or to break; here it is to hatch the young by breaking the egg, as in Isa 59:5: The eggs of the basilisk בקעו, they broke, that is, they hatched. So in Latin a chick is said to break out of the egg. Finally, דגר among the Chaldeans, from whom this verb was taken, is in general to gather; here it is to gather eggs or chicks under oneself, so that they may be kept warm by the mother's heat. It is taken no otherwise in Ier. XVII, 11, where the Prophet says קרא דגר ולא ילד, The partridge gathers (that is, broods) what it did not lay. Nor otherwise by the Chaldee translator, when he renders the Hebrew תחמם, she will warm, said of the ostrich's eggs, by תדגר, she will gather, that is, she will brood, Job 39:14 (var. 17). Moreover, on the words בקעה ודגרה, will hatch and brood, it should be noted that the conjunction and means the same as when or after, as if the Prophet had written, She will hatch the eggs after she has brooded them. So in Exod 14:21: He made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided, that is, after the waters were divided. And in Isa 64:5: You were angry, and we sinned, that is, when we sinned, or because we sinned. So Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]. III, 195 But these things are obvious to everyone; what קפוז kippoz is, is not, since no animal of this name occurs anywhere else, neither in Scripture, nor in the Talmud, nor in the books of the Syrians or Chaldeans. I shall, however, state what both the ancient and the more recent interpreters thought of this word. The Greeks, the Chaldee, the Syriac, the Arabic, the Vulgate translator, Jerome, Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] and Schindler render it hedgehog, as if קפוז kippoz were the same as קפוד kippod a little before, in verse 11; persuaded, of course, by the closeness of sound and the easy interchange of the letters Z and D. Kimchi likewise, and Solomon, and Mordecai, and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)], and nearly all the Hebrews there are, take kippod and kippoz for the same thing. But kippod, and therefore also kippoz, one explains as the tortoise, another as the little owl, another in general as a solitary bird. Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra] alone, as far as I know, holds that kippod and kippoz are different birds, but of what kind he does not explain. And among those interpreters who in our own age have done useful work in producing new versions there is no small disagreement here. Santes explains it as hedgehog, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] as eagle-owl, Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] as blackbird, Diodati as il gufo, that is, scops owl; the English as the great Owe, that is, the great owl; the French, following Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs], as le martinet, that is, the swift; Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] inserts the Hebrew word itself into the Latin text, candidly confessing that he does not understand it. ¶

Nunc, ut modeste aperiam, sed libere, quid in singulis desiderem: Primo hoc haberi velim pro certo, קפוד kippod et קפוז kippoz, utut sono similia sint, tamen esse nomina diversorum animalium. 1. Quia in eadem serie contextus ab Esaia proponuntur ut diversa. Eam, inquit, possidebunt pelecanus et KIPPOD. Et, paucis interjectis, Ibi nidificabit KIPPOZ. Quae inanis est repetitio, si kippod et kippoz idem essent (1). 2. Hoc potissimum, quod, cum קפוד kippod hericium esse, suo loco a) probaverim, id est, animal quadrupes viviparum: contra describitur קפוז kippoz ut animal oviparum (2): Ibi nidificabit kippoz, et pariet, et excludet (ova scilicet), postquam (illa) foverit in umbra sua. Quid expressius? Itaque Santes et Schindlerus in Lexicis suis, voce בקע, plane videntur ignorasse, quod animal sit hericius: alioqui nunquam dicturi, quod faciunt in hujus loci expositione, nidificare hericium, et rumpere ova, et, iis ruptis, pullos suos excludere. III, 196 Secundo, quidquid sit קפוז kippoz, cum a saltu nomen habeat, neque te- studo est, neque cypselus avis. Neutri enim convenit saltus. Non testudini, quae ne graditur quidem aliter, quam reptitando. Sed neque avi cypselo, quam alii apodem vocant, quia pedum usu caret, et incessus fere est expers. Plinius col. 410 lib. 10. cap. 39. Plurimum volant, quae apodes; quia carent usu pedum: ab aliis cypseli appellantur hirundinum specie. Item, His quies, nisi in nido, nulla. Aut pendent, aut jacent. Eadem etiam alii scribunt. Praeterea, cum cypselus marina sit avis, aut riparia, quorsum nidificare dicatur in Idumaeae aridis locis? Frustra Mercerus urget, cypseli vocem ab Hebraea קפוז kippoz non abludere. Neque enim id tanti est, cum caeterae notae desint. Ut taceam, ab Hebraea lingua male repeti vocem, quae palam est Graecae originis. Cypseli enim dicuntur ἀπὸ τῶν κυψελίδων, ab alveolis, quos e luto sibi construunt. Philoso- phus Historiae libri 9. cap. 30. Οἱ δὲ ἄποδες, οὓς καλοῦσί τινες κυψέλους, νεοτ-τεύουσιν ἐν κυψελίσιν ἐν πυλοῦ πεπλασμένας μακραῖς. Sic, apud Herodotum in Terpsichore, Eëtionis filio Cypseli nomen inditur, ἀπὸ τῆς κυψέλης, in qua latens ex periculo mortis evaserat.

Now, to state modestly but freely what I find lacking in each: first, I would have this held as certain, that קפוד kippod and קפוז kippoz, however similar in sound, are nevertheless names of different animals. 1. Because in the same sequence of the text they are presented by Isaiah as different. The pelican, he says, and the KIPPOD shall possess it. And, a few words later, There shall the KIPPOZ make its nest. This is an empty repetition if kippod and kippoz were the same R1. 2. Above all this: whereas I have proved in its place a) that קפוד kippod is the hedgehog, that is, a viviparous quadruped, קפוז kippoz, on the contrary, is described as an oviparous animal R2: There shall the kippoz make its nest, and lay, and hatch (the eggs, that is), after it has (them) warmed in its shadow. What could be more explicit? Thus Santes and Schindlerus in their Lexicons, under the word בקע, seem plainly not to have known what kind of animal the hedgehog is; otherwise they would never have said, as they do in expounding this passage, that the hedgehog builds a nest, and breaks its eggs, and, when they are broken, hatches its young. III, 196 Secondly, whatever קפוז kippoz may be, since it takes its name from leaping, it is neither the tortoise nor the bird cypselus. For leaping suits neither. Not the tortoise, which does not even walk except by crawling. Nor the bird cypselus, which others call apus, because it lacks the use of its feet and is almost incapable of walking. Pliny col. 410 book 10, ch. 39: Those called apodes fly most of all, because they lack the use of their feet; by others they are called cypseli, of the swallow kind. Also: They have no rest except in the nest. They either hang or lie. Others too write the same. Moreover, since the cypselus is a sea bird, or a bird of the riverbanks, why should it be said to nest in the dry places of Idumaea? Mercerus urges in vain that the word cypselus is not far from the Hebrew קפוז kippoz. For that counts for little when the other marks are lacking. Not to mention that a word plainly of Greek origin is wrongly derived from the Hebrew language. For cypseli are so called ἀπὸ τῶν κυψελίδων [apo tōn kypselidōn], from the little cells which they build for themselves out of mud. The Philosopher, History, book 9, ch. 30: Οἱ δὲ ἄποδες, οὓς καλοῦσί τινες κυψέλους, νεοττεύουσιν ἐν κυψελίσιν ἐν πυλοῦ πεπλασμένας μακραῖς [Hoi de apodes, hous kalousi tines kypselous, neotteuousin en kypselisin en pylou peplasmenas makrais]. So in Herodotus, in Terpsichore, the son of Eëtion is given the name Cypselus, ἀπὸ τῆς κυψέλης [apo tēs kypselēs], in which he had lain hidden and escaped the danger of death. ¶

Tertia interpretatio, quae קפוז kippoz merulam exponit, plus habet verisimilitudinis. 1. Merula enim Graece est κόσσυφος, quod ex Hebraeo, kipphoz, ut efferebant veteres, factum videri queat per literarum metathesim. 2. Et cum solitariis animalibus videtur posse censeri, cum merulam dici velint Varro b), Festus c), Isidorus d), eo quod mera, (id est, sola), volitet. 3. Eadem avis קפוז kippoz recte dicatur a saltu, cum subsultet potius, quam ambulet. Plinius lib. 10. cap. 38. Avium ambulant aliquae, ut cornices; saliunt aliquae, ut passe- res, merulae. Has rationes habuit Iunius, cur kippoz merulam redderet. Sed vir doctissimus non cogitavit, Prophetam ibi agere de φιλερήμοις animalibus, quorum inhabitatione cultus antea locus vastus atque horridus fiat. In quo numero merulam esse, nemo dixerit, quae avis est innoxia, habitu corporis decens, voce suavi, sapore grato, fimo ad laetificandos agros utilissimo, quamque adeo in Ornithotrophiis cum lusciniis vult ali Varro lib. 3. De Re Rustica cap. 5. Merulam quidem dici volunt, quia fere vagatur sola, nec nisi propter sobolem cum avibus sui generis solet inire societatem. Sed hanc originationem, cum aliis Varronis deridet Quinctilianus lib. 1. cap. 5. Cui, inquit, non post Varronem sit venia? qui agrum, quod in eo agatur aliquid; et graculos, quia gregatim volent, dictos; Ciceroni persuadere voluit (ad eum enim scribit): cum alterum ex Graeco sit manifestum dici, alterum ex vocibus avium. Sed hoc tanti III, 197 fuit vertere, ut merula, quia sola volat, quasi mera volans nominaretur. Et merulam quidem dici, quasi merivolam, merum commentum: merula tamen pro mera, id est, sola, dici potuit diminutiva voce. Verum, undecunque nomen traxerit, eam in habitatis locis degere, nemo est, qui nesciat, atque unam merulae speciem plerumque ἐπὶ τῶν κεράμων, super tectorum tegulas versari, ut observat Philosophus Historiae libri noni capite decimo nono. Unde est, quod huc nonnulli referunt illud Psaltis, Fui sicut passer solitarius super tectum.

The third interpretation, which explains קפוז kippoz as the blackbird, has more probability. 1. For blackbird in Greek is κόσσυφος [kossyphos], which could seem to have been formed from the Hebrew kipphoz, as the ancients pronounced it, by a metathesis of letters. 2. And it seems it can be reckoned among the solitary animals, since Varro b), Festus c) and Isidore d) would have merula [blackbird] so called because it flies mera (that is, alone). 3. The same bird could rightly be called קפוז kippoz from leaping, since it hops rather than walks. Pliny book 10, ch. 38: Some birds walk, like crows; some hop, like sparrows and blackbirds. These were Junius's reasons for rendering kippoz as blackbird. But that most learned man did not consider that the Prophet is there dealing with φιλερήμοις [philerēmois] animals, desert-loving ones, by whose habitation a place formerly cultivated becomes waste and dreadful. No one would say the blackbird is of that number: it is a harmless bird, comely in form, sweet of voice, pleasant in taste, its dung most useful for enriching fields, so much so that Varro book 3, On Agriculture, ch. 5, wants it kept in aviaries together with nightingales. They do indeed say it is called merula because it usually wanders alone and does not usually associate with birds of its own kind except for the sake of its brood. But this derivation, along with others of Varro's, is ridiculed by Quintilian book 1, ch. 5: Who, he says, would not be pardoned after Varro? who wanted to persuade Cicero (for he writes to him) that ager [field] was so called because something agitur [is done] in it, and graculi [jackdaws] because they fly gregatim [in flocks]; although it is manifest that the one comes from the Greek, the other from the cries of the birds. But it was worth so much to him to make the turn, that merula, because it flies alone, was named as if mera volans [flying alone] III, 197. And indeed that merula is said as if merivola is pure invention; yet merula could have been said for mera, that is, alone, as a diminutive. But wherever it got its name, there is no one who does not know that it lives in inhabited places, and that one species of blackbird mostly frequents ἐπὶ τῶν κεράμων [epi tōn keramōn], upon the tiles of roofs, as the Philosopher observes in History, book 9, chapter 19. Hence some refer here that saying of the Psalmist: I was like a solitary sparrow upon the roof. ¶

Hoc vitavit incommodum Deodatius ὁ μακαρίτης, qui קפוז kippoz, Italice gufo, id est, scopem, interpretatur; avem non modo solitariam, et φιλέρη-μον, sed et nocturnam, et deformem, et ominosam, vocisque absurdae et absonae (3). Homeri vetus Scholiastes, Σκῶπες· ὄρνεα νυκτίνομα, κακόνομα, κακόφωνα, οἱονεὶ σκαιόπές τινες. 2. Accedit, quod merito קפוז kippoz, id est, saltatricula, nominari posse videtur avis, quae saltatione tam delectatur, ut, aucupe saltante, capi legatur ἀντορχουμένη, id est, ex adverso saltitans. Quocirca scopem Athenaeus e) iis accenset animalibus, quae saltantem intuentia νευ-ροσπαστεῖται, id est, moventur ad instar sigillariorum νευροσπαστῶν (Gallice des marionettes) et ut — nervis alienis mobile lignum. Unde σκὼψ f) appellatur saltationis quoddam genus, varietate motuum et gesticulationum insignis. Et σκώ-πτειν proprie est, scopis instar, imitatione ridicula aliquid effingere. 3. Quid, col. 411 quod pro scope veteres fere copem g) scribebant, sine Sigma praefixo. In his Epicharmus, Speusippus, Metrodorus et Alexander Myndius, qui Homerum etiam et Aristotelem asserit κῶπες scripsisse, non σκῶπες. Quod ideo observamus, quia κῶψ magis accedit ad Hebraeum קפוז kippoz. Et vox Italica gufo videtur ex utroque posse deduci: cum gli cufi, vel gufi, sint veterum copes, vel scopes. Phavorinus, καὶ δοκοῦσι σκῶπες εἶναι, οὓς κούφους ἢ γούφους οἱ Ῥωμαῖοί φασι. Idem est in Eustathio, nisi quod, pro κούφους ἢ γούφους, nostra exemplaria perperam habent κουκούβας. Videt cordatus lector, ultimam hanc interpretationem maxime esse probabilem. Neque aliud eos voluisse puto, qui bubonem, aut magnam ululam, eandem vocem reddiderunt. Tamen hoc certum est, scopem III, 198 non potuisse dici קפוז kippoz, eo quod sit ὀρχηστικὴ, et ab aucupe saltante capiatur ἀντορχουμένη. Neque enim Hebraeum קפז est ὀρχεῖσθαι, sed ἅλλε-σθαι. Proprium vocis usum didicimus ex Arabibus, qui tria verba prope synonyma زج zaggia, طفر taphara (Chaldaice טפז tephaz) et قفز kaphaza (Chaldaice קפז kephaz) ita promiscue usurpant, ut زج zaggia plerumque significet prosilire in plano, طفر taphara, e sublimi loco in inferiorem desilire, قفز kaphaza, sursum exsilire ex inferiore loco. Hinc in Avicennae capite h) De speciebus exercitii, القفز Alcaphzo fit الي شي ليتعلق به ad rem aliquam, ut ab ea quis suspendatur. Et in Sacris paraphrasibus قفز kaphaza occurrit passim pro verbis Hebraeis דלג, et זנק, et נתר, quibus ἅλ-μα, et πήδημα, non ὄρχησιν, significari, notum est. Ergo קפוז kippoz, ut res nomini respondeat, oportet esse animal ἁλτικὸν, non ὀρχηστικὸν (Gallice propre a sauter, non a dancer). Quae duo quam differant, nemo non videt. Neque id omissum a Scaligero, qui, Aristotelis locum explicans, quo capite le-gitur otus ἀντορχούμενος Historiae lib. 8. cap. 12. Ἀντορχούμενος, inquit, vicissim gesticulans. Ὄρχησις aliud est quam ἅλμα.

This difficulty was avoided by the late Diodati (ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs]), who renders קפוז kippoz in Italian as gufo, that is, scops [scops-owl]: a bird not only solitary and φιλέρημον [philerēmon], but also nocturnal, ugly and ill-omened, with a harsh and discordant voice R3. The old Scholiast of Homer: Σκῶπες· ὄρνεα νυκτίνομα, κακόνομα, κακόφωνα, οἱονεὶ σκαιόπές τινες [Skōpes· ornea nyktinoma, kakonoma, kakophōna, hoionei skaiopes tines]. 2. Add that the name קפוז kippoz, that is, little leaper, seems fitly applicable to a bird that so delights in dancing that, when the fowler dances, it is reported to be caught ἀντορχουμένη [antorchoumenē], that is, dancing in return opposite him. Accordingly Athenaeus e) counts the scops among the animals which, watching a dancer, νευροσπαστεῖται [neurospasteitai], that is, are moved like puppets νευροσπαστῶν [neurospastōn] (in French des marionettes), and like — a piece of wood moved by another's strings. For the Hebrew קפז is not ὀρχεῖσθαι [orcheisthai], but ἅλλεσθαι [hallesthai]. We have learned the proper use of the word from the Arabs, who use three nearly synonymous verbs, زج [zj] zaggia, طفر [ṭafara] taphara (in Chaldee טפז tephaz) and قفز [qafaza] kaphaza (in Chaldee קפז kephaz), so interchangeably that زج [zj] zaggia mostly means to leap forward on level ground, طفر [ṭafara] taphara to leap down from a high place to a lower one, قفز [qafaza] kaphaza to leap upward from a lower place. And in the sacred paraphrases قفز [qafaza] kaphaza occurs everywhere for the Hebrew verbs דלג, זנק and נתר, which are known to signify ἅλμα [halma] and πήδημα [pēdēma], not ὄρχησιν [orchēsin]. Therefore קפוז kippoz, if the thing is to answer to the name, must be an animal ἁλτικὸν [haltikon], not ὀρχηστικὸν [orchēstikon] (in French propre a sauter, non a dancer). Ὄρχησις [Orchēsis] is something other than ἅλμα [halma]. ¶

Iam in Esaia quae sequuntur, Ibi nidulabitur acontias, et pariet, et ova excludet, quae foverit umbra sua, ipsissima verba sunt, quae in hoc argumento naturae consulti usurpant. Philosophus de serpentibus, Historiae lib. 5. in fine; Ὠοτοκοῦσιν ἔξω· ὅταν δὲ τέκῃ, εἰς τὴν γὴν ἐπωάζει. ἐκλέπε-ται δὲ καὶ ταῦτα τῷ ὑστέρῳ ἔτει. Ova foris edunt: quae cum pepererunt, in terra fovent. Ea autem excluduntur anno sequente. Unde Plinius libri 10. cap. 62. Ova pariunt serpentes, etc. Et contexta in terra incubant, et foetum sequente excludunt anno. Et Celsus lib. 5. cap. 27. Perniciosissimi sunt (serpentes), cum incubant. Ergo in Esaia מלט, est ὠοτοκεῖν, ova parere: דגר, ἐπωάζειν, fovere, incubare: בקע, ἐκλέπειν, excludere. Atque ipsemet Esaias alibi eodem Verbo ovorum basilisci exclusionem exprimit, ut supra l) notavimus.

Now what follows in Isaiah, There shall the acontias nest, and lay, and hatch out the eggs, which it has kept warm in its shadow, are the very words which the naturalists use on this subject. The Philosopher, on serpents, History book 5, at the end: Ὠοτοκοῦσιν ἔξω· ὅταν δὲ τέκῃ, εἰς τὴν γὴν ἐπωάζει. ἐκλέπεται δὲ καὶ ταῦτα τῷ ὑστέρῳ ἔτει [Ōotokousin exō; hotan de tekē, eis tēn gēn epōazei. eklepetai de kai tauta tō hysterō etei]. They lay their eggs outside; when they have laid them, they keep them warm in the ground. And these are hatched in the following year. Hence Pliny, book 10, ch. 62: Serpents lay eggs, etc. And they incubate them woven together in the ground, and hatch the young in the following year. And Celsus, book 5, ch. 27: They (serpents) are most deadly when they are incubating. Therefore in Isaiah מלט is ὠοτοκεῖν [ōotokein], to lay eggs; דגר is ἐπωάζειν [epōazein], to keep warm, to incubate; בקע is ἐκλέπειν [eklepein], to hatch out. And Isaiah himself elsewhere expresses with the same verb the hatching of the eggs of the basilisk, as we noted above l). ¶

Etiam Verbum דגר, congregare proprie, vel ova congregata et connexa vovere, etsi tribuatur avibus, tamen in serpentem maxime quadrat, cujus ova non modo sunt simul collecta, sed et mutuo nexu cohaerent monilis III, 204 instar. Aristoteles ubi supra, Τὰ δ' ὠὰ ἀλλήλοις συνεχῆ ἐστιν, ὥσπερ αἱ τῶν γυναικῶν ὑποδερίδες· Ova sunt sibi mutuo connexa ut mulierum monilia, id est, ut margaritae in monilibus. Damir etiam, postquam serpentis ova descripsit longa, coloris fusci, viridis, nigri, albi, varii, punctis quibusdam distincti, lucidi, cum tantae varietatis causa non appareat satis, atque egit etiam de sanie intus latente, وهو في جوفها منضد طولا علي خط واحد Per ventrem, inquit, in longitudinem super lineam unam connexa sunt. Et Scaliger, Ea ova vidimus nos agglutinata inter se, globulorum modo, quibus preces numerant mulieres. Sunt, qui Hebraice id dici putant שלל של ביצים, sed proprie שלל של ביצים est, ovorum in gallinae ventre conglobatio, cum ad maturitatem vergunt. Ut אשכול של ביצים botrus ovorum, dicuntur ova minora, ex semine primum conglobata. Vide Talmudicos in tractatu Betsa fol. 7. a.

Also the verb דגר, properly to gather together, or to keep warm eggs gathered and joined together, although it is applied to birds, nevertheless fits a serpent best of all, whose eggs are not only collected together but also cling to one another by a mutual bond, like a necklace. III, 204 Aristotle, in the place cited above: Τὰ δ' ὠὰ ἀλλήλοις συνεχῆ ἐστιν, ὥσπερ αἱ τῶν γυναικῶν ὑποδερίδες [Ta d' ōa allēlois synechē estin, hōsper hai tōn gynaikōn hypoderides]; The eggs are joined to one another like women's necklaces, that is, like the pearls in necklaces. Damir too, after describing the eggs of the serpent as long, of a dusky, green, black, white or variegated colour, marked with certain spots, shining (though the cause of such great variety is not sufficiently apparent), and after treating also of the fluid hidden inside, says: وهو في جوفها منضد طولا علي خط واحد [wa-huwa fī jawfihā munaḍḍad ṭūlan ʿalī khaṭṭ wāḥid] Through the belly they are joined lengthwise along a single line. And Scaliger: We have seen these eggs glued to one another, in the manner of the little beads with which women count their prayers. There are some who think that this is called in Hebrew שלל של ביצים, but properly שלל של ביצים is the clustering of the eggs in the hen's belly, when they are approaching maturity. Just as the smaller eggs, first clustered together from the seed, are called אשכול של ביצים, a cluster of eggs. See the Talmudists in the tractate Betsa, fol. 7a. ¶

Neque id omittendum, quod acontiam ova בצלה, umbra sua fovere dicit Propheta, dicturus potius sub alis, aut sub pennis, aut sub alarum umbra, si de ave res esset. Forte etiam his ipsis verbis insinuat divinus vates, hoc serpentis genus de suis ovis magis esse sollicitum. Amphisbaena saltem, quam Avicenna jaculi speciem esse suspicatur, legitur apud Albertum serpens esse vigil, et sollicitus de suis ovis valde, cum ea incubat (5).

Nor should it be passed over that the Prophet says the acontias keeps its eggs warm בצלה, in its shadow, whereas he would rather have said under its wings, or under its feathers, or under the shadow of its wings, if the subject were a bird. Perhaps also by these very words the divine seer hints that this kind of serpent is especially solicitous for its eggs. At least the amphisbaena, which Avicenna suspects is a species of iaculus, is described in Albertus as a serpent that is watchful, and very solicitous for its eggs when it incubates them R5. ¶
