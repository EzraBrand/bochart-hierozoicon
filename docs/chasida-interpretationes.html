III, 78 col. 321 CHAPTER 28. ¶

Variae interpretationes vocis חסידה chasida, vel asida.

Various interpretations of the word חסידה chasida, or asida. ¶

Ut a misericordia רחם racham; sic a pietate dicta est, quae sequitur, חסידה hasida, vel asida, ut quidam scribunt, aspiratione demta. Cujus vocis interpretamenta aliquammulta reperio. I. Primo enim Septuaginta interpretes ἐρωδιόν· et Vulgatus, herodium, vel herodionem, id est, ardeam, reddunt in Lege, et Psalmi centesimi tertii (Hebraeis centesimi quarti) versu decimo nono. Aquila etiam constanter ἐρωδιόν· ut et Zach. V, 9. Symmachus et Theodotion. Sed et Arabica Psalteria manuscripta duo habeo, quorum unum ubi supra ha-bet الهيرودي alheiraudi τὸν ἐρωδιόν. Aethiopicum quoque ሄሮድዮስ፡ herodios. Quo eodem refero, quod in Parisiensi Psalmorum editione est, البلشوم albalsum. Nam avis بلشوم balsum, vel بلشون balsun, quae et مالك الحزين malich alhazin, omnino videtur esse ardea. Quod colligo ex Arabum descriptionibus, quae sic habent. Damir, البلشون هو مالك الحزين Abalsun est Malich alhazin. Giauhari, مالك col. 322 الحزين هو طير الما Malich alhazin est avis aquatica. Ibno Bari in Marginalibus Notis, هو طير طويل العنق والرجلين Avis est longo collo et pedibus. Alkazuinius itidem, طير طويل الرقبة والرجلين يقال له بالفارسية بوتيما Avis longo collo et cruribus, quae Persice vocatur BUTHIMA. Idem, et Damir ex Algiahid, من اعاجيب الدنيا امر مالك الحزين لانه لا يزال يقعد بقرب الما ومواضع نبعها من الانهار وغيرها فاذا انقصت حزن عليها ويبقي حزينا كييبا وربما ترك الشرب حتي يموت عطشا خوفا من زيادة نقصها بشرب منها E mundi miraculis est, quod ajunt de malich alhazin, eum ad aquas semper degere, sive sint fluviatiles, vel aliunde ortae. Quas cum videt imminui, hoc illum torquet, ita, ut moestus tristisque maneat, et quandoque a potu tam diu abstineat, ut prae siti moriatur, ne plus nimio imminuantur, si ex iis bibat. Sequitur in Damire non absimile commentum de quadam cicindelae specie, quam noctilucam, alatam, viridem et laevem esse dicunt Arabes, et prope amnes volitare, et vesci terrae pulvere, sed ita parce et tenuiter, ut nunquam satietur, et quandoque fame pereat, verita, ne deficiat terrae pulvis, si III, 79 ad satietatem usque ederet. Quae a poetis conficta sunt, ut essent typi avari hominis, qui, quae aliis abstulit, sibi ipsi negat, et suum defraudat genium. Sed veriora sunt, quae de eadem ave referuntur ab Attaugide in libro الامتناع والموانسة Antipathiae et Sympathiae, مالك الحزين ينشل الحيتان من الما فياكلها وهي طعامها ولا يحسن السباحة فان اخطاه الانتشال وجاع طرح نفسه علي شاطي البحر وفي بعض ضحضاحاته فاذا اجتمع اليه السمك الصغار اسرع في خطف ما استطاع منها Malich alhazin ex aquis pisces extrahit, quibus vestatur, eosque habet pro cibo, quamvis natandi imperitus. Et, cum esurit, hac piscatione non succedente, e littore maris in vada se conjicit, et ex accedentibus pisciculis magna festinatione rapit, quodcunque potest. Unde ardeam esse conjicio, de qua Oppiani paraphrastes [Ixeut. L. II. c. 8.] apud Gesnerum; Ab ardeis victum ex aquis primum inventum ajunt, reliquas ab iis didicisse, cum victus unus col. 323 initio omnibus esset avibus. Deinde, cum gloriarentur hoc invento, et ne ipsum quidem Neptunum natando cum eis contendere posse jactarent, hanc artem, irato deo, amisisse, etc. Hinc adeo reliquae omnes aves captandis piscibus merguntur in aquam: at ardeae, natandi facultatis expertes, in littore stantes venantur. Haec nota est certissima, cum in aquaticis avibus alia nulla sit, quae natare nesciat. Ecce aliam notam in Damire, quamvis minus expressam! وهو لا يحتاج الي تزواج الاسفاد Copulationem et coitum non appetit. Nempe quia sine dolore non coit. Philosophus Historiae libri noni capite primo, Τῶν δ’ ἐρωδιῶν ἐστι τρία γένη· ὅ τε πέλλος, καὶ ὁ λευκὸς, καὶ ὁ ἀστερίας καλούμενος. Τούτων ὁ πέλλος χαλεπῶς εὐνάζει τε, καὶ ὀχεύει. κράζει τε γὰρ, καὶ αἷμα, ὥς φασιν, ἀφίησιν ἐκ τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν ὀχεύων. Et rursus capite decimo octavo, Τῶν δ’ ἐρωδιῶν ὁ μὲν πέλλος, ὥσπερ εἴρηται, ὀχεύει μὲν χαλεπῶς. Unde Plinius libri decimi capite sexagesimo, Ardeolarum tria genera, leucon, asterias, pellos. Hi in coitu anguntur. Mares quidem cum vociferatu sanguinem ex oculis profundunt. Proinde Etymologus, et Eustathius in Iliad. κ. ἐρωδιὸν dici volunt παρὰ τὸ ἔαρ, vel ἔωρ, ὃ σημαίνει τὸ αἷμα, καὶ ἰδίειν τὸ ἱδροῦν. ἐν γὰρ ταῖς μίξεσιν (ὥς φασιν) ἡ ἐρωδιὸς αἷμα ἱδροῖ, ab ἔαρ, aut ἔωρ, quod sanguinem significat, et ἰδίειν, quod sudare est. Ardea enim, ut ajunt, in coitu sanguinem sudat. Sed iis assentiri malo, quibus ἐρωδιὸς dicitur quasi ἐλω-διὸς, palustris: φιληδεῖ γὰρ τοῖς ἑλώδεσι τόποις, locis enim palustribus delectatur. Iam tertia sequitur nota. Mahumetani Theologi permittunt esum τοῦ balsun, seu malich alhazin: sed, ut asserunt Medicorum filii, لحمه غليظ بارد III, 80 يولد ادمان اكله البواسير Crassa et frigida caro ejus, et stercora ex illius esu haemorrhoides generant. Quod ipsum de ardea his verbis docet Arnoldus de Villa-nova apud Gesnerum, Quicunque haemorrhoides crebro experiuntur praesertim pulsantes, inter alia grues et agrones (id est, ardeas) praecipue caveant. Omnino igitur بلشوم balsum, vel بلشون balsun, est ardea (1), quae a fabula, quam diximus, alio nomine dicitur مالك الحزين malich alhazin, aquator vel potor tristis. Nam ملك malch apud Arabes inter caetera aquam sonat. Quod ideo hic observo, quia vocem بلشوم vel بلشون lexicographi omiserunt.

As רחם racham is named from mercy, so the bird that follows, חסידה hasida, or asida, as some write it with the aspiration dropped, is named from piety. I find a great many interpretations of this word. I. First, the Septuagint translators render it ἐρωδιόν [erōdion], and the Vulgate translator herodium or herodionem, that is, heron, in the Law and in verse nineteen of Psalm one hundred and three (one hundred and four in the Hebrew). Aquila too consistently has ἐρωδιόν [erōdion], as do Symmachus and Theodotion at Zech 5:9. I also have two manuscript Arabic Psalters, one of which, in the place cited above, has الهيرودي [al-hīrūdī] alheiraudi, τὸν ἐρωδιόν [ton erōdion]. The Ethiopic likewise has ሄሮድዮስ፡ [hērodəyos] herodios. To the same I refer what stands in the Paris edition of the Psalms, البلشوم [al-balshūm] albalsum. For the bird بلشوم [balshūm] balsum, or بلشون [balshūn] balsun, also called مالك الحزين [mālik al-ḥazīn] malich alhazin, certainly seems to be the heron. This I gather from the descriptions of the Arabs, which run thus. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: البلشون هو مالك الحزين [al-balshūn huwa mālik al-ḥazīn] Abalsun is Malich alhazin. Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ]: مالك col. 322 الحزين هو طير الما [mālik al-ḥazīn huwa ṭayr al-māʾ] Malich alhazin is a water bird. Ibno Bari in his Marginal Notes: هو طير طويل العنق والرجلين [huwa ṭayr ṭawīl al-ʿunuq wa-l-rijlayn] It is a bird with a long neck and legs. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] likewise: طير طويل الرقبة والرجلين يقال له بالفارسية بوتيما [ṭayr ṭawīl al-raqaba wa-l-rijlayn yuqāl lahu bi-l-fārisiyya būtīmā] A bird with a long neck and legs, which in Persian is called BUTHIMA. The same author, and Damir from Algiahid: من اعاجيب الدنيا امر مالك الحزين لانه لا يزال يقعد بقرب الما ومواضع نبعها من الانهار وغيرها فاذا انقصت حزن عليها ويبقي حزينا كييبا وربما ترك الشرب حتي يموت عطشا خوفا من زيادة نقصها بشرب منها [min aʿājīb al-dunyā amr mālik al-ḥazīn li-annahu lā yazālu yaqʿudu bi-qurb al-māʾ wa-mawāḍiʿ nabʿihā min al-anhār wa-ghayrihā fa-idhā anqaṣat ḥazina ʿalayhā wa-yabqā ḥazīnan kayīban wa-rubbamā taraka al-shurb ḥattā yamūta ʿaṭashan khawfan min ziyādat naqṣihā bi-shurbihi minhā] Among the wonders of the world is what they say of malich alhazin: that it always lives by the waters, whether of rivers or springing from elsewhere. When it sees them diminish, this torments it so that it remains sad and sorrowful, and sometimes abstains from drinking so long that it dies of thirst, lest they be diminished too much if it drinks from them. There follows in Damir a not dissimilar fiction about a kind of glow-worm, which the Arabs say shines by night, is winged, green and smooth, flits near rivers and feeds on the dust of the earth, but so sparingly and meagrely that it is never sated and sometimes dies of hunger, fearing the dust of the earth would run out if III, 79 it ate its fill. These things were invented by the poets as types of the avaricious man, who denies himself what he has taken from others and cheats his own appetite. But truer is what Attaugides reports of the same bird in the book الامتناع والموانسة [al-imtināʿ wa-l-muwānasa] Of Antipathy and Sympathy: مالك الحزين ينشل الحيتان من الما فياكلها وهي طعامها ولا يحسن السباحة فان اخطاه الانتشال وجاع طرح نفسه علي شاطي البحر وفي بعض ضحضاحاته فاذا اجتمع اليه السمك الصغار اسرع في خطف ما استطاع منها [mālik al-ḥazīn yanshilu al-ḥītān min al-māʾ fa-yaʾkuluhā wa-hiya ṭaʿāmuhu wa-lā yuḥsinu al-sibāḥa fa-in akhṭaʾahu al-intishāl wa-jāʿa ṭaraḥa nafsahu ʿalā shāṭiʾ al-baḥr wa-fī baʿḍ ḍaḥḍāḥātihi fa-idhā ijtamaʿa ilayhi al-samak al-ṣighār asraʿa fī khaṭf mā istaṭāʿa minhā] Malich alhazin draws fish out of the waters, on which it feeds, and has them for its food, although it cannot swim. And when it is hungry and this fishing does not succeed, it throws itself from the seashore into the shallows, and from the little fish that approach it snatches with great haste whatever it can. Hence I infer that it is the heron, of which Oppian's paraphrast [Ixeut. book 2, ch. 8] says, in Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium]: They say that getting food from the waters was first discovered by herons, and that the rest learned it from them, since at the beginning all birds had one food. col. 323 Then, since they gloried in this discovery and boasted that not even Neptune could compete with them in swimming, they lost this art through the god's anger, etc. Hence all other birds plunge into the water to catch fish; but herons, lacking the faculty of swimming, hunt standing on the shore. This mark is most certain, since among water birds there is no other that cannot swim. Here is another mark in Damir, though less explicit: وهو لا يحتاج الي تزواج الاسفاد [wa-huwa lā yaḥtāju ilā tazwāj al-isfād] It does not desire copulation and coition. Namely, because it does not mate without pain. The Philosopher, History, book 9, ch. 1: Τῶν δ’ ἐρωδιῶν ἐστι τρία γένη· ὅ τε πέλλος, καὶ ὁ λευκὸς, καὶ ὁ ἀστερίας καλούμενος. Τούτων ὁ πέλλος χαλεπῶς εὐνάζει τε, καὶ ὀχεύει. κράζει τε γὰρ, καὶ αἷμα, ὥς φασιν, ἀφίησιν ἐκ τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν ὀχεύων. [Tōn d’ erōdiōn esti tria genē; ho te pellos, kai ho leukos, kai ho asterias kaloumenos. Toutōn ho pellos chalepōs eunazei te, kai ocheuei. krazei te gar, kai haima, hōs phasin, aphiēsin ek tōn ophthalmōn ocheuōn.] And again in ch. 18: Τῶν δ’ ἐρωδιῶν ὁ μὲν πέλλος, ὥσπερ εἴρηται, ὀχεύει μὲν χαλεπῶς. [Tōn d’ erōdiōn ho men pellos, hōsper eirētai, ocheuei men chalepōs.] Hence Pliny, book 10, chapter 60: Of herons there are three kinds, leucon, asterias, pellos. These suffer distress in coition. The males indeed pour blood from their eyes as they cry out. Accordingly the Etymologicum, and Eustathius on Iliad κ, would have ἐρωδιὸν [erōdion] named παρὰ τὸ ἔαρ, vel ἔωρ, ὃ σημαίνει τὸ αἷμα, καὶ ἰδίειν τὸ ἱδροῦν. ἐν γὰρ ταῖς μίξεσιν (ὥς φασιν) ἡ ἐρωδιὸς αἷμα ἱδροῖ [para to ear, vel eōr, ho sēmainei to haima, kai idiein to hidroun. en gar tais mixesin (hōs phasin) hē erōdios haima hidroi], from ἔαρ [ear] or ἔωρ [eōr], which means blood, and ἰδίειν [idiein], which is to sweat. For the heron, as they say, sweats blood in coition. But I prefer to agree with those who say ἐρωδιὸς [erōdios] is as it were ἐλωδιὸς [elōdios], marsh bird: φιληδεῖ γὰρ τοῖς ἑλώδεσι τόποις [philēdei gar tois helōdesi topois], for it delights in marshy places. Now a third mark follows. The Muslim theologians permit eating the balsun, or malich alhazin; but, as the sons of the physicians assert, لحمه غليظ بارد III, 80 يولد ادمان اكله البواسير [laḥmuhu ghalīẓ bārid yuwallidu idmān akalihi al-bawāsīr] Its flesh is coarse and cold, and the excrements from eating it produce haemorrhoids. This very thing Arnoldus de Villa-nova teaches about the heron, in Gesner, in these words: Whoever frequently suffers from haemorrhoids, especially throbbing ones, should above all beware, among other things, of cranes and agrones (that is, herons). Certainly, then, بلشوم [balshūm] balsum, or بلشون [balshūn] balsun, is the heron R1, which from the fable we have related is called by another name مالك الحزين [mālik al-ḥazīn] malich alhazin, the sad waterer or sad drinker. For ملك [mlk] malch among the Arabs means, among other things, water. I note this here because the lexicographers have omitted the word بلشوم [balshūm] or بلشون [balshūn]. ¶

II. Secundo, חסידה hasida, fulica redditur a vetusto interprete, quem sequuntur Augustinus et Arnobius in Psalmos. Sed haec interpretatio ex praecedente nata. Utut enim ardea, et fulica, vel fulix, multum differant: tamen etiam e doctissimis reperti sunt, qui has aves promiscue sumerent. Ita in Arati versione Avienus τὸν ἐρωδιὸν, modo fulicem, modo reddit ardeam. Et in Glossis Philoxeni legas, ἐρωδιὸς, Erodius, fulica. Plinius quoque libri undecimi capite trigesimo septimo, in eundem inpingens lapidem, Et fulicarum generi, inquit, natura dedit cirros; quod ardearum est proprium. Et libri decimi capite quadragesimo quarto, Diomedeas aves fulicarum similes facit. Unde Solinus capite secundo, Forma illis pene, quae fulicis. Et Isidorus libri duodecimi capite septimo, Diomedeas aves etc. forma fulicae similes. Sed, ut observat in Solinum vir magnus, fulicarum nomine Plinius erodios voluit. Nam Graeci Diomedeas illas aves non tantum erodiis similes esse jactant, sed et αὐτόχρημα ἐρω-διοὺς, reapse erodios, sive ardeas. Aelianus Historiae statim initio, καλεῖταί τις Διομήδεια νῆσος, καὶ ἐρωδιοὺς ἔχει πολλούς. Insula quaedam, Diomedea nomine, col. 324 multos habet erodios, seu multas ardeas. Stephanus, Νῆσος ἡ Διομήδεια, ἐν ᾗ οἱ ἐρωδιοὶ χειροήθεις προσπετόμενοι, καὶ εἰς τοὺς κόλπους δύοντες. Insula Diomedea, in qua mansueti erodii advolantes, et sinum subeuntes. Servius in Aeneidos librum undecimum, Hinc eae aves hodieque Latine Diomedeae vocantur, quas Graeci ἐρωδιοὺς dicunt. Cujus generis avem auspicio dixit Homerus Diomedi et Ulyssi speculatoribus apparuisse:

II. Second, חסידה hasida is rendered fulica [coot] by the old translator, whom Augustine and Arnobius follow on the Psalms. But this interpretation arose from the preceding one. For however much the heron and the fulica, or fulix, differ, there have nevertheless been found even among the most learned some who took these birds indiscriminately. Thus in his version of Aratus, Avienus renders τὸν ἐρωδιὸν [ton erōdion] now as fulix, now as ardea. And in the Glosses of Philoxenus you may read: ἐρωδιὸς [erōdios], Erodius, fulica. Pliny too, book 11, chapter 37, stumbling on the same stone, says: "And to the kind of the coots nature has given crests"; which is proper to herons. And in book 10, chapter 44, he makes the Diomedean birds "like coots." Hence Solinus, chapter 2: "Their form is almost that of coots." And Isidore, book 12, chapter 7: "The Diomedean birds" etc. "in form like the coot." But, as a great man observes on Solinus, by the name fulicae Pliny meant erodii. For the Greeks declare that those Diomedean birds are not merely like erodii, but αὐτόχρημα ἐρωδιοὺς [autochrēma erōdious], actually erodii, that is, herons. Aelian, at the very beginning of the History: καλεῖταί τις Διομήδεια νῆσος, καὶ ἐρωδιοὺς ἔχει πολλούς [kaleitai tis Diomēdeia nēsos, kai erōdious echei pollous]. "A certain island, named Diomedea, col. 324 has many erodii, that is, many herons." Stephanus: Νῆσος ἡ Διομήδεια, ἐν ᾗ οἱ ἐρωδιοὶ χειροήθεις προσπετόμενοι, καὶ εἰς τοὺς κόλπους δύοντες [Nēsos hē Diomēdeia, en hē hoi erōdioi cheiroētheis prospetomenoi, kai eis tous kolpous dyontes]. "The island Diomedea, in which tame erodii fly up and slip into one's lap." Servius on book 11 of the Aeneid: "Hence those birds are to this day called Diomedeae in Latin, which the Greeks call ἐρωδιοὺς [erōdious]. A bird of this kind, he said, Homer reports appeared as an omen to Diomedes and Ulysses as they went scouting:" ¶

Τοῖσι δὲ δεξιὸν ἧκεν ἐρωδιὸν ἔγγυς ὁδοῖο Παλλὰς Ἀθηναίη. — — [Toisi de dexion hēken erōdion engys hodoio Pallas Athēnaiē] ¶

Et his omnibus longe vetustior Antigonus Historia quadragesima octava, quae medica manu eget; Περὶ δὲ τῶν Τώων Λύκον μὲν ἐν τῇ Διομηδείᾳ τῇ νήσῳ φησὶν III, 81 ἱϛορεῖν τοὺς ἐρωδιοὺς ὑπὸ μὲν τῶν Μήδων, ὅτ᾽ ἂν παραβάλλῃ τις εἰς τοὺς τόπους, οὐ μόνον ψαυομένους ὑπομένειν, ἀλλὰ καὶ προσπετομένους εἰς τοὺς κόλπους ἐνδύ-νειν, καὶ σαίνειν φιλοφρόνως. Quae sic vertenda: Lycum de Troibus (id est, in Troicis) narrare dicit, in Diomedea insula ardeas ab advenis, si quis ad ea loca perveniat, non modo tangi se pati, sed et devolantes in sinum delabi, et blande adu- lari. Itaque pro Τώων scribendum Τρώων, non Κώων, ut vult Xylander. Ly- cus Butheras Rheginus, philosophus Peripateticus, Lycophronis pater, circa Ptolemaei Lagidae tempora historias multas scripserat, quarum meminerunt Agatharchides, Clemens Alexandrinus, Porphyrius, Suidas, uterque Tzetzes, etc. In his Troica, quae hic citantur, unde illi nata est occasio agendi de Dio- mede et Diomedis avibus, quas ὑπὸ τῶν Μήδων, a Medis, ad ea loca devenientibus, tangi non dixit. Quod fuisset ridiculum, cum eo Medi nunquam venerint, neque cum Diomede commune quidquam habuerint. Itaque, pro ὑπὸ τῶν Μήδων, restitue ὑπὸ τῶν ἀποδήμων, vel ἐκδήμων, a peregrinis: quibus statim opponuntur ἐγχώριοι, indigenae. Sequitur enim, Λέγεσθαι δέ τι τοιοῦ-τον ὑπὸ τῶν ἐγχωρίων, ὡς τῶν τοῦ Διομήδου ἑταίρων εἰς τὴν τῶν ὀρνέων τούτων φύσιν μετασχηματισθέντων, referre indigenas Diomedis socios in harum avium naturam fuisse transformatos. Sed haec obiter.

And far older than all these is Antigonus, History 48, which needs a physician's hand: Περὶ δὲ τῶν Τώων Λύκον μὲν ἐν τῇ Διομηδείᾳ τῇ νήσῳ φησὶν III, 81 ἱϛορεῖν τοὺς ἐρωδιοὺς ὑπὸ μὲν τῶν Μήδων, ὅτ᾽ ἂν παραβάλλῃ τις εἰς τοὺς τόπους, οὐ μόνον ψαυομένους ὑπομένειν, ἀλλὰ καὶ προσπετομένους εἰς τοὺς κόλπους ἐνδύνειν, καὶ σαίνειν φιλοφρόνως [Peri de tōn Tōōn Lykon men en tē Diomēdeia tē nēsō phēsin historein tous erōdious hypo men tōn Mēdōn, hot' an paraballē tis eis tous topous, ou monon psauomenous hypomenein, alla kai prospetomenous eis tous kolpous endynein, kai sainein philophronōs]. This should be translated thus: "He says that Lycus, concerning the Trojans (that is, in the Troica), relates that on the Diomedean island the herons, when anyone comes to those places, not only allow themselves to be touched by strangers, but also fly down and slip into their lap, and fawn on them caressingly." Therefore for Τώων [Tōōn] one must write Τρώων [Trōōn], not Κώων [Kōōn], as Xylander would have it. Lycus Butheras of Rhegium, a Peripatetic philosopher, father of Lycophron, had written many histories around the time of Ptolemy son of Lagus, which are mentioned by Agatharchides, Clement of Alexandria, Porphyry, Suidas, both Tzetzes, etc. Among them are the Troica cited here, from which he took occasion to treat of Diomedes and the birds of Diomedes, which he did not say were touched ὑπὸ τῶν Μήδων [hypo tōn Mēdōn], by the Medes, coming to those places. That would have been ridiculous, since the Medes never came there, nor had anything in common with Diomedes. Therefore, for ὑπὸ τῶν Μήδων [hypo tōn Mēdōn], restore ὑπὸ τῶν ἀποδήμων [hypo tōn apodēmōn], or ἐκδήμων [ekdēmōn], by foreigners; to whom are at once opposed the ἐγχώριοι [enchōrioi], the natives. For there follows: Λέγεσθαι δέ τι τοιοῦτον ὑπὸ τῶν ἐγχωρίων, ὡς τῶν τοῦ Διομήδου ἑταίρων εἰς τὴν τῶν ὀρνέων τούτων φύσιν μετασχηματισθέντων [Legesthai de ti toiouton hypo tōn enchōriōn, hōs tōn tou Diomēdou hetairōn eis tēn tōn orneōn toutōn physin metaschēmatisthentōn], "the natives relate that the companions of Diomedes were transformed into the nature of these birds." But this in passing. ¶

III. Tertio nonnulli veterum πελαργὸν ciconiam transtulerunt, quos Olympiodorus in Iobum conatur sic refellere: Ἡ ἀσίδα δὲ ὁ ἐρωδιός ἐστι κατὰ Ἀκύλαν, ἢ ὁ πελαργὸς, ὥς τινες ἔφασαν, οὐ καλῶς νοήσαντες· οὐ γὰρ εἰς γῆν τίκτει ὁ πελαργὸς, ἀλλ᾽ εἰς τὰ ὑψηλὰ τῶν δένδρων. Καὶ οἱ ὀρνιθιακὰ γρά-ψαντες ἕτερον γένος ἔφασαν εἶναι παρὰ τὴν ἀσίδα τὸν πελαργόν. Asida au- tem est ἐρωδιὸς, ardea juxta Aquilam, aut ciconia, ut quidam ajunt, non recte sentientes: neque enim humi ciconia, sed in celsis arboribus ova ponit. Et qui de avium natura scripserunt, ciconiam ab asida diversum genus esse tradiderunt. Suidae tamen ἐρωδιὸς est εἶδος ὀρνέου ὁ πελαργὸς λεγόμενος, ἢ ὅμοιος αὐτῷ, Erodios genus est avis, quae ciconia dicitur, vel ipsi similis. Quod ferri potuisset, si solum similem dixisset: re vera enim ardea et ciconia sunt in multis similes. Utut sit, haec interpretatio maxime arrisit Hebraeis recentioribus, quorum plerisque hasida est ciconia.

III. Third, some of the ancients translated it πελαργὸν [pelargon], stork, whom Olympiodorus on Job tries to refute thus: Ἡ ἀσίδα δὲ ὁ ἐρωδιός ἐστι κατὰ Ἀκύλαν, ἢ ὁ πελαργὸς, ὥς τινες ἔφασαν, οὐ καλῶς νοήσαντες· οὐ γὰρ εἰς γῆν τίκτει ὁ πελαργὸς, ἀλλ᾽ εἰς τὰ ὑψηλὰ τῶν δένδρων. Καὶ οἱ ὀρνιθιακὰ γράψαντες ἕτερον γένος ἔφασαν εἶναι παρὰ τὴν ἀσίδα τὸν πελαργόν [Hē asida de ho erōdios estin kata Akylan, ē ho pelargos, hōs tines ephasan, ou kalōs noēsantes; ou gar eis gēn tiktei ho pelargos, all' eis ta hypsēla tōn dendrōn. Kai hoi ornithiaka grapsantes heteron genos ephasan einai para tēn asida ton pelargon]. "The asida is the ἐρωδιὸς [erōdios], the heron, according to Aquila, or the stork, as some say, who do not judge rightly: for the stork lays its eggs not on the ground but in lofty trees. And those who have written on the nature of birds have taught that the stork is a kind different from the asida." For Suidas, however, ἐρωδιὸς [erōdios] is εἶδος ὀρνέου ὁ πελαργὸς λεγόμενος, ἢ ὅμοιος αὐτῷ [eidos orneou ho pelargos legomenos, ē homoios autō], "Erodios is a kind of bird which is called the stork, or one like it." This could have been tolerated if he had said only like: for in truth the heron and the stork are alike in many respects. Be that as it may, this interpretation pleased the later Hebrews most, for most of whom the hasida is the stork. ¶

IV. Quarto, a Symmacho חסידה hasida in Iobo redditur ὁ κύκνος, cygnus, avis, ardeae, fulicae, et ciconiae aliqua ex parte similis. Proinde Diomedeas aves, quas alii cum ardeis, alii cum fulicis, Ovidius cum cygnis confert Meta- morphoseως libri decimi quarti Fabula decima,

IV. Fourth, Symmachus renders חסידה hasida in Job as ὁ κύκνος [ho kyknos], cygnus [swan], a bird that in some respect resembles the heron, the coot and the stork. Accordingly, the Diomedean birds, which some compare with herons and others with coots, Ovid compares with swans, Metamorphoses book 14, fable 10: ¶

Si volucrum quae sit dubiarum forma requiris, Ut non cygnorum, sic albis proxima cygnis. III, 82 Et Graecus auctor apud Phavorinum, Οἱ δὲ φίλοι αὐτοῦ θρηνοῦντες αὐτὸν μετε-βλήθησαν εἰς ὄρνεα ὅμοια κύκνοις· Eum flentes amici ejus mutati sunt in aves, cygnis similes. Et Isidorus, ut varias sententias aliquo modo conciliaret, has aves describit forma fulicae similes, magnitudine cygnorum, colore candido, etc. col. 325 Tamen dubito, an, quae avis ab aliis ardea, et fulica, et ciconia dicta est, a Symmacho in Iobo cygnus appellata sit propter formae similitudinem. Et suspicor, vocem esse corruptam, et pro κύκνος legendum esse ἰκτῖνος, id est, milvus, quomodo alibi reddidisse Symmachum jam dicemus.

If you ask what the form of these doubtful birds is, Though not that of swans, it is very close to white swans. III, 82 And a Greek author in Phavorinus: Οἱ δὲ φίλοι αὐτοῦ θρηνοῦντες αὐτὸν μετεβλήθησαν εἰς ὄρνεα ὅμοια κύκνοις [Hoi de philoi autou thrēnountes auton metheblēthēsan eis ornea homoia kyknois]· His friends, as they wept for him, were changed into birds like swans. And Isidore, in order to reconcile the various opinions in some way, describes these birds as similar in form to the coot, of the size of swans, white in colour, etc. col. 325 Yet I doubt whether the bird which others called heron, coot and stork was called a swan by Symmachus in Job because of a likeness of form. And I suspect that the word is corrupt, and that instead of κύκνος [kyknos] we should read ἰκτῖνος [iktinos], that is, kite, as Symmachus rendered it elsewhere, as we shall presently say. ¶

V. Quinto חסידה hasida, ab Hieronymo milvus redditur Psalm. CIV, 17. Ierem. VIII, 7. et Zachar. V, 9. Et Epistola centesima trigesima quinta, ad Suniam et Fretellam, profitetur, se hac in parte Symmachum sequi, et sextam editionem. „Pro herodio (inquit), quod in Hebraico dicitur חסידה hasida, „Symmachus ἰκτῖνον, id est, milvum interpretatus est. Denique et nos ita ver-„timus in Latinum, Milvi abies domus est. Quod scilicet semper in excelsis et ar-„duis arboribus nidos facere consueverit. Unde et sexta editio manifestius inter-„pretata est. Milvo cupressus ad nidificandum.“ Et rursus in Ierem. VIII. Pro milvo, quem interpretatus est Symmachus“ etc. Et in Zachar. V. „Hasidam Hebraei milvum putant, avem rapacissimam, et semper domesticis avibus insidiantem.“

V. Fifth, חסידה hasida is rendered by Jerome as kite in Psalm. CIV, 17. Ierem. VIII, 7. and Zachar. V, 9. And in Letter 135, to Sunnia and Fretela, he declares that in this point he follows Symmachus and the sixth edition. "For herodius (he says), which in Hebrew is called חסידה hasida, "Symmachus translated ἰκτῖνον [iktinon], that is, kite. And so we too have rendered it in Latin: The fir is the house of the kite. Because, namely, it has always been accustomed to make its nests in lofty and steep trees. Hence the sixth edition also translated more plainly: The cypress for the kite to nest in." And again on Ierem. VIII: "For kite, which Symmachus translated" etc. And on Zachar. V: "The Hebrews think the hasida is the kite, a most rapacious bird, always lying in wait for domestic fowl." ¶

VI. Sexto, Hebraei vetustiores דיה לבנה deja lebana explicant, quod milvum cum Hieronymo non interpretor, sed vulturem album. Nam דיה daja vulturem esse, non milvum, patet ex Es. XXXIV, 15. ubi דיות dajoth congregari dicuntur. Quod non convenire milvo, sed e rapacium numero soli vulturi, suo loco a) docuimus. Praeterea a Talmudicis et Chaldaeis paraphrastis דיה daja, in nigrum et album distinguitur, quomodo milvos nemo distinguit; quia milvus albus nullus est. Sed de nigro et albo vulture multum b) egimus: quorum ille supra דיה daja simpliciter; hic רחם racham a Mose dictus est. Res fiet manifestior ex Talmudici loci lectione, qui sic habet in tractatu Cholim cap. 3. fol. 63. a. אמר רב יהודה החסידה זו דיה לבנה למה נקרא שמה חסידה שעושה חסידות עם חברותיה, Dixit Rabbi Iuda, Hasida est daja alba. Quorsum vocatur hasida? Quia facit hasidoth (benignitates, id est, benigne agit) cum sociis suis. Nempe, ut ibi Glossa, מחלקת מזונותיה, cibos suos ipsis impertiens. Nihil videt, qui non videt, haec de milvo non posse dici, qui in cibi societatem alios milvos non admittit; sed de vulturibus, qui gregatim in idem cadaver involant. Proinde III, 83 דיה a Selomone diserte vultur explicatur in Notis ad Zachar. V, 9. Chal- daei etiam interpretes idem sequuntur, quod Talmudici. Et in lege Iona- than et Ierosolymitanus habent דיותא חווריתא, , vulturem album. Et Onkelos חווריתא , solum, id est, album, supple vulturem. Quomodo etiam occurrit in Chaldaea paraphrasi Iobi et Ieremiae. At Rabbi Ioseph Caecus in Psalm. CIV, 17. habet ותיא חוריתא . In quibus verbis quid sit ותיא , dicat, qui potest.

VI. Sixth, the older Hebrews explain it as דיה לבנה deja lebana, which I do not interpret with Jerome as kite, but as white vulture. For that דיה daja is the vulture, not the kite, is clear from Es. XXXIV, 15, where the דיות dajoth are said to gather together. This does not suit the kite, but, among birds of prey, the vulture alone, as we showed in its place a). Moreover, the Talmudists and the Chaldee paraphrasts divide דיה daja into black and white, a distinction nobody makes for kites, since there is no white kite. But we have dealt at length b) with the black and the white vulture: of these the former was called above simply דיה daja, the latter רחם racham by Moses. The matter will become clearer from reading the Talmudic passage, which runs thus in the tractate Cholim ch. 3, fol. 63a: אמר רב יהודה החסידה זו דיה לבנה למה נקרא שמה חסידה שעושה חסידות עם חברותיה, Rabbi Judah said: Hasida is the white daja. Why is it called hasida? Because it does hasidoth (kindnesses, that is, acts kindly) with its companions. Namely, as the Gloss there says, מחלקת מזונותיה, sharing its food with them. He sees nothing who does not see that this cannot be said of the kite, which does not admit other kites to share its food, but of vultures, which fly in flocks onto the same carcass. Accordingly III, 83 דיה is expressly explained as vulture by Solomon [Rashi] in his Notes on Zech 5:9. The Chaldee interpreters too follow the same as the Talmudists. And in the Law, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and the Jerusalem Targum have דיותא חווריתא, white vulture. And Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] has חווריתא alone, that is, white, supply vulture. So it also occurs in the Chaldee paraphrase of Job and Jeremiah. But Rabbi Joseph the Blind on Psalm. CIV, 17 has ותיא חוריתא. What ותיא means in these words, let him say who can. ¶

VII. Septimo, Zach. V, 9. pro verbis, Habebant alas ככנפי החסידה , ut alas hasidae, Chaldaeus habet, Et populi celeres transferebant illos velociter כמא דמשתרי נשרא , sicut volat aquila: ac si hasida sit aquilae genus; nempe ex eorum sententia, qui aquilas et vultures promiscue sumunt. Pagni- nus quoque, in majore Lexico, aquilam herodianam reddit, sed longe diversa de causa. Apud barbaros scriptores erodius, vel herodius, non est ardea, sed aquila major, et γνήσιος, aut etiam hiero-falco, quem vocant. Alber- tus, Herodius est aquila praegrandis, tota nigra, licet in senecta color in dorso, et super alas in cinereum vertatur, etc. Vincentius in Doctrinali li-bri decimi sexti capite centesimo sexagesimo secundo, Herodius, qui et girfalchus appellatur, est avis inter omnes nobilissima, colore coeruleo, etc. Adeo fortis, ut aquilam capiat, etc. Glossa in Deuteronomii caput decimum quartum, Herodius vulgo girfalco dicitur, et rapit aquilam, etc. Et in Psalmum centesimum tertium, Herodius est avis rapacissima omnium avium majorum, col. 326 et vincit aquilam. Albertus ita dici sibi fingit quasi heroëm avium. Itaque herodii, aut herodionis vocabulo in Vulgata versione, vel majorem aquilam, vel girifalconem intelligebant illorum temporum scriptores (2). Quorum torrente abreptus Sanctes Pagninus in eundem errorem incidit.

VII. Seventh, at Zech 5:9, for the words They had wings ככנפי החסידה, like the wings of the hasida, the Chaldee has And swift peoples carried them over quickly כמא דמשתרי נשרא, as the eagle flies: as if the hasida were a kind of eagle; that is, following the view of those who take eagles and vultures indiscriminately. Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae] too, in his larger Lexicon, renders it aquila herodiana [herodian eagle], but for a quite different reason. In the barbarous writers erodius or herodius is not the heron, but the larger and γνήσιος [gnēsios] eagle, or even the so-called hiero-falco. Albertus: The herodius is a very large eagle, entirely black, although in old age the colour on the back and over the wings turns ash-grey, etc. Vincent in the Doctrinale, book 16, chapter 162: The herodius, which is also called girfalchus, is the noblest of all birds, blue in colour, etc. So strong that it catches the eagle, etc. The Gloss on Deuteronomy chapter 14: The herodius is commonly called girfalco, and it seizes the eagle, etc. And on Psalm 103: The herodius is the most rapacious of all the larger birds, col. 326 and overcomes the eagle. Albertus imagines that it is so called as if the hero of birds. Thus by the word herodius or herodio in the Vulgate version the writers of those times understood either the larger eagle or the gyrfalcon R2. Carried away by their torrent, Pagninus fell into the same error. ¶

VIII. Caeteras hujus vocis interpretationes mihi erat animus pari filo persequi. Sed vereor, ne lectori sim taediosus, si in singulis immorer. Utut enim multas jam attulerim, tamen plures supersunt. De Samaritano scilicet III, 84 nondum actum, cui hasida est ࠔࠍࠅࠍࠉࠕࠀ hirundo (3). Nec de iis Arabum, qui in Lege الصقر sacrum explicant accipitrem: et in Psalmis الهام noctuam: in Prophetis القعقع alkoko; quod Gabriel Sionita in uno loco pro grue, in altero pro pica sumit, sed utrobique perperam, ut infra docebitur. Neque de Syro, qui alibi ܚܣܝܕܐ, ciconiam interpretatur, sed in Iobo ܐܛܐ otum. Neque de Graecis in Zacharia, quibus כנפי החסידה, sunt πτέρυγες ἔποπος, alae upupae. Neque de Olympiodoro in Ieremiam, ubi Ἰνδικόν ἐστι γένος ὀρνέου ἡ ἀσίδα, Indicum avis genus est asida. Neque de Polychronio in Iobum, ubi Τινὲς ἔφασαν τὴν Ἀραβίδα εἶναι ὄρνιν, ἥτις ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ εὑρίσκεται, Arabicam esse avem, nonnulli tradiderunt, quae in Mesopotamia reperitur. Neque de Lexicographis, Cyrillo et Phavorino: quibus ἀσίδα est στρουθοκάμηλος. Quae semel et simul monuisse sufficiat, ut jam inter tot veterum fluctuationes pedem conemur in firmo loco sistere.

VIII. I had intended to pursue the remaining interpretations of this word in the same manner. But I fear I may weary the reader if I dwell on each one. For although I have already brought forward many, more still remain. Nothing has yet been said, namely, of the Samaritan III, 84, for whom the hasida is ࠔࠍࠅࠍࠉࠕࠀ [שנוניתא], the swallow R3. Nor of those Arabs who in the Law explain it as الصقر [al-ṣaqr] sacrum, the hawk; in the Psalms as الهام [al-hām], the little owl; in the Prophets as القعقع [al-qaʿqaʿ] alkoko, which Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)] takes in one place for the crane, in another for the magpie, but wrongly in both, as will be shown below. Nor of the Syriac, which elsewhere renders it ܚܣܝܕܐ [חסידא], the stork, but in Job ܐܛܐ [אטא], the otus. Nor of the Greeks in Zechariah, for whom כנפי החסידה are πτέρυγες ἔποπος [pteryges epopos], the wings of the hoopoe. Nor of Olympiodorus on Jeremiah, where we read Ἰνδικόν ἐστι γένος ὀρνέου ἡ ἀσίδα [Indikon esti genos orneou hē asida], the asida is an Indian kind of bird. Nor of Polychronius on Job, where we read Τινὲς ἔφασαν τὴν Ἀραβίδα εἶναι ὄρνιν, ἥτις ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ εὑρίσκεται [Tines ephasan tēn Arabida einai ornin, hētis en tē Mesopotamia heurisketai], some have held that it is an Arabian bird, which is found in Mesopotamia. Nor of the lexicographers, Cyril and Phavorinus, for whom ἀσίδα [asida] is στρουθοκάμηλος [strouthokamēlos]. Let it suffice to have noted these once and together, so that amid so many waverings of the ancients we may now try to set our foot on firm ground. ¶
