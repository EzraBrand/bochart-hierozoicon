II, 787 col. 197 CAP. X.

II, 787 col. 197 Chapter X. ¶

Corvi nomina, vox, color, sedes, victus, species, etc.

The names of the raven, its voice, colour, abode, food, kinds, etc. ¶

Corvus, de quo jam agendum, duo habet, quae in sensus maxime se inge- col. 198 runt, crocitationem et nigredinem. Crocitatio, Graece κρωγμὸς, est vox rauca et aspera, ex imis ducta faucibus, quam corvi, quasi certatim, magna contentione ingeminant; inprimis, cum pluvias et tempestates praesagiunt. Hinc Nicander Theriacῶν versu 406.

The raven, which we must now treat, has two features that most force themselves upon the senses: col. 198 its croaking and its blackness. Croaking, in Greek κρωγμὸς [krōgmos], is a hoarse and harsh sound drawn from the depths of the throat, which ravens repeat over and over with great effort, as if in competition; above all when they foretell rains and storms. Hence Nicander, Theriaca, line 406: ¶

Αἰγυπιοὶ, γῦπές τε, κόραξ τ᾽ ὀμβρήρεα κρώζων,

Αἰγυπιοὶ, γῦπές τε, κόραξ τ᾽ ὀμβρήρεα κρώζων, [Aigypioi, gypes te, korax t᾽ ombrērea krōzōn,] ¶

Aegypii, et vultures, et corvus pluvia crocitans, id est, pluvium tempus futurum crocitatione praenuntians. Lucretius libri quinti versu 1082.

Aegypii, and vultures, and the raven croaking of rain, that is, announcing rainy weather to come by its croaking. Lucretius, book 5, line 1082: ¶

Et partim mutant cum tempestatibus una Raucisonos cantus cornicum, et saec'la vetusta, Corvorumque greges, ubi aquas dicuntur et imbres Poscere, et interdum ventos aurasque vocare.

And in part they change together with the weather The hoarse-sounding songs of the crows, and the long-lived generations, And the flocks of ravens, when they are said to call for Waters and rains, and at times to summon winds and breezes. ¶

Nigredo quoque corvi propria, eaque intensissima, et per totum corpus diffusa. Non tamen usquequaque verum est, quod a Porphyrio scribitur capite quinto Isagoges: Τὸ δὲ μέλαν εἶναι ἀχωρίστως τῷ κόρακι καὶ τῷ Αἰθίοπι συμ-βέβηκε· Nigredo corvo et Aethiopi sic inest, ut separari non possit. Quippe etiam albi corvi visi sunt. Aristoteles, De generatione animalium libri quinti capite sexto, Ὦπται καὶ πέρδιξ λευκὴ, καὶ κόραξ· Perdix visa est alba, et corvus. Heraclides in Republica Cyren. Ἀρκεσιλάου δὲ βασιλεύοντος λευκὸς κόραξ ἐφά-νη· Regnante Arcesilao, corvus albus apparuit. Quales hodieque in Suecia et Norvegia reperiuntur, ubi frigus est intensius. Tamen in confesso est, corvum alvum raro inveniri. Hinc Iuvenalis Satyra septima,

Blackness, too, is proper to the raven, and that most intense, and spread over its whole body. Yet what Porphyry writes in chapter 5 of the Isagoge is not true without exception: Τὸ δὲ μέλαν εἶναι ἀχωρίστως τῷ κόρακι καὶ τῷ Αἰθίοπι συμβέβηκε· [To de melan einai achōristōs tō koraki kai tō Aithiopi symbebēke;] Blackness is so present in the raven and the Ethiopian that it cannot be separated. For white ravens too have been seen. Aristotle, On the Generation of Animals, book 5, chapter 6: Ὦπται καὶ πέρδιξ λευκὴ, καὶ κόραξ· [Ōptai kai perdix leukē, kai korax;] A white partridge has been seen, and a raven. Heraclides in the Constitution of Cyrene: Ἀρκεσιλάου δὲ βασιλεύοντος λευκὸς κόραξ ἐφάνη· [Arkesilaou de basileuontos leukos korax ephanē;] When Arcesilaus was king, a white raven appeared. Such are found even today in Sweden and Norway, where the cold is more intense. Yet it is admitted that a white raven is rarely found. Hence Juvenal, Satire 7: ¶

Felix ille tamen, corvo quoque rarior albo.

Yet he is fortunate, rarer even than a white raven. ¶

Et Ammianus Anthologiae libri secundi capite quadragesimo sexto, Epigrammate decimo septimo,

And Ammianus in the Anthology, book 2, chapter 46, Epigram 17: ¶

Θᾶττον ἔην λευκοὺς κόρακας, πτηνάς τε χελώνας Εὑρεῖν, ἢ δόκιμον ῥήτορα Καππαδόκην·

Θᾶττον ἔην λευκοὺς κόρακας, πτηνάς τε χελώνας Εὑρεῖν, ἢ δόκιμον ῥήτορα Καππαδόκην· [Thatton eēn leukous korakas, ptēnas te chelōnas heurein, ē dokimon rhētora Kappadokēn;] ¶

Facilius est, albos corvos et testudines volantes reperire, quam probum rhetorem Cappadocem. Itaque, ut haec duo, crocitatio, inquam, et nigredo, sunt corvo maxime propria: ita pleraque corvi nomina ex alterutro sumpta sunt. Sic a crocitatione ܢܥܒܐ neab; et Arabice نعاب noab, a verbo نعب naa- ba scilicet, quod crocitare significat. Et in Aethiopico corvi nomine ቋዕ፡ II, 788 koe manifesta est imitatio corvinae vocis. Indidem natum Graecum κόραξ, ut κρώζειν· et Romana crocire, et crocitare. Nam frustra sunt, qui κόρακα dici volunt παρὰ τὸν κόρον, ὃ σημαίνει τὸ μέλαν· cum κόρος pro nigro apud veteres nusquam occurrat. At Hebraicum עורב oreb, Chaldaicum ערבא orba, Arabicum غراب gorab (unde Romani corvum dixere), palam ad colorem pertinent. Hebraice enim ערב ereb est vespera, et ערב arab, Verbum advesperascere, obtenebrescere. Unde Arabice غربيب garbib est niger (Ain initiali mutata in Gain propter punctum appositum); itaque hic idem accidit, quod Graecis vocibus δνοφερὸς, ὄρφνιος et ὄρφνινος, quae a tenebris ad nigredinem significandam trans- col. 199 feruntur. Et a tenebroso, vel, quod idem est, nigro colore corvum Hebraei עורב oreb, et Arabes غراب gorab dixerunt. Interim agnoscendum est, colorem κοράκινον, seu corvinum, aliquid habere luminis, cum color pullus sit ἄστιλπνος. Unde est, quod coracinum a pullo his verbis distinguit Vitruvius libri octavi capite tertio: Quamvis sint alba, procreant aliis locis leucophaea, aliis locis pulla, aliis coracino colore. Et κοράκινον χρῶμα docti definiunt colorem cum aliquo splendore nigerrimum; qualis est gagatis lapidis, et nostri serici villosi. Quem colorem lanis tribuit Strabo lib. duodecimo circa Laodiceam Phrygiae, illam regionem ferre dicens προβάτων ἀρετὰς, οὐκ εἰς μα-λακότητας μόνον τῶν ἐρίων, ᾗ καὶ τῶν Μιλησίων διαφέρει, ἀλλὰ καὶ εἰς τὴν κόραξιν χρόαν, praestantes oves, non solum mollitie lanarum, qua Milesias etiam superant, sed et colore, quali in corvis. Legendum enim τὴν ἐν κόραξιν χρόαν· sed post τὴν omissum illud ἐν, quia τὴν et ἐν sunt voces fere ὁμοιόπτωτοι. Placet etiam is color in capillis. Hinc Apuleji libro secundo laudatur capillus, corvina nigredine coerulus: coerulus, id est, niger, ut Graece κυάνεος. Itaque cum corvo confertur etiam, qui arte nigrum habet capillitium. Martialis libri tertii Epigrammate quadragesimo tertio,

It is easier to find white ravens and flying tortoises than a good orator from Cappadocia. Therefore, as these two things, croaking, I say, and blackness, are most proper to the raven, so most of the raven's names are taken from one or the other. Thus from croaking comes ܢܥܒܐ [נעבא] neab; and in Arabic نعاب [nuʿāb] noab, namely from the verb نعب [naʿaba] naaba, which means to croak. And in the Ethiopic name of the raven, ቋዕ፡ II, 788 koe, there is a manifest imitation of the raven's voice. From the same source arose the Greek κόραξ [korax], as well as κρώζειν [krōzein]; and the Roman crocire and crocitare. For those are mistaken who would have κόραξ [korax] so called παρὰ τὸν κόρον, ὃ σημαίνει τὸ μέλαν [para ton koron, ho sēmainei to melan], since κόρος [koros] in the sense of black occurs nowhere among the ancients. But the Hebrew עורב oreb, the Chaldee ערבא orba, and the Arabic غراب [ghurāb] gorab (whence the Romans said corvus) plainly relate to colour. For in Hebrew ערב ereb is evening, and ערב arab, the verb, is to grow towards evening, to grow dark. Hence in Arabic غربيب [ghirbīb] garbib is black (the initial Ain being changed into Gain by the added dot); so here the same thing happens as with the Greek words δνοφερὸς [dnopheros], ὄρφνιος [orphnios] and ὄρφνινος [orphninos], which are transferred from darkness to signify col. 199 blackness. And from the dark, or, what is the same, black colour, the Hebrews called the raven עורב oreb, and the Arabs غراب [ghurāb] gorab. Meanwhile it must be acknowledged that the κοράκινον [korakinon], or raven-coloured, has some lustre, whereas the dusky colour is ἄστιλπνος [astilpnos]. That is why Vitruvius, book 8, chapter 3, distinguishes raven-black from dusky in these words: Though they are white, in some places they produce grey-white, in other places dusky, in others raven-black. And learned men define κοράκινον χρῶμα [korakinon chrōma] as a deepest black with some sheen, such as that of jet and of our velvet. Strabo, book 12, attributes this colour to the wool around Laodicea in Phrygia, saying that region bears προβάτων ἀρετὰς, οὐκ εἰς μαλακότητας μόνον τῶν ἐρίων, ᾗ καὶ τῶν Μιλησίων διαφέρει, ἀλλὰ καὶ εἰς τὴν κόραξιν χρόαν [probatōn aretas, ouk eis malakotētas monon tōn eriōn, hē kai tōn Milēsiōn diapherei, alla kai eis tēn koraxin chroan], excellent sheep, not only in the softness of their wool, in which they surpass even the Milesian, but also in colour, such as is in ravens. For one must read τὴν ἐν κόραξιν χρόαν [tēn en koraxin chroan]; but after τὴν [tēn] that ἐν [en] was omitted, because τὴν [tēn] and ἐν [en] are nearly ὁμοιόπτωτοι [homoioptōtoi]. This colour is also pleasing in hair. Hence in Apuleius, book 2, hair is praised as dark-blue with raven blackness: coerulus, that is, black, as in Greek κυάνεος [kyaneos]. So even one who has hair made black by art is compared with the raven. Martial, book 3, Epigram 43: ¶

Mentiris juvenem tinctis, Lentine, capillis, Tam subito corvus, qui modo cygnus eras.

You feign youth with dyed hair, Lentinus, so suddenly a raven, you who were lately a swan. ¶

Et de Theoro balbutiens dicit Alcibiades apud Aristophanem in Vespis,

And Alcibiades, lisping, says of Theorus in Aristophanes, in the Wasps: ¶

Ὁλᾷς Θέωλον τὴν κεφαλὴν κόλακος ἔχει,

Ὁλᾷς Θέωλον τὴν κεφαλὴν κόλακος ἔχει, [Holas Theōlon tēn kephalēn kolakos echei,] ¶

κόλακος, id est, adulatoris, caput illi tribuens pro κόρακος, id est, corvi, capite; quia R non poterat aliter efferre, quam per L. Sed sequitur,

κόλακος [kolakos], that is, of a flatterer, attributing to him the head of a flatterer instead of κόρακος [korakos], that is, of a raven; because he could not pronounce R otherwise than as L. But there follows, ¶

Ὀρθῶς γε τοῦτ᾽ Ἀλκιβιάδης ἐθραύλισεν,

Ὀρθῶς γε τοῦτ᾽ Ἀλκιβιάδης ἐθραύλισεν [Orthōs ge tout᾽ Alkibiadēs ethraulisen], ¶

Hoc recte balbutiit Alcibiades; quia re vera Theorus adulator erat insignis. Haec eo pertinent, ut doceamus, quorsum de sponso dicatur Cant. V, 2. קוצותיו תלתלים שחורות כעורב, Antiae ejus pendulae nigrae ut corvus. Ita enim interpretamur: II, 789 quia Arabice قُصَّة Kutssa est comae portio, quae fronti imminet, aut ex anteriore capitis parte propendet. Et תלתלים, pendulas cum Selomone explicamus, tanquam geminatum a verbo תלה, quod pendere est. Sic ex יפה geminato factum יפיפית pulcher es, Ps. XLV, 3. Illae igitur antiae, quae in frontem, aures et genas pendulae sunt, nigrae dicuntur esse ut corvus, id est, coracini coloris, qui splendore suo est conspicuus. Neque obscurum est, cur solum antiarum meminerit. Agitur de rege coronato: proinde dicitur Cant. III, 11. Videte, filiae Sion, regem Solomonem cum corona, qua mater illum coronavit, in die sponsaliorum ejus. Et in hoc ipso versu, Capite ejus auro optimo (supple coronato) antiae ejus pendulae, nigrae ut corvus. Quae capillitii pars fere sola apparet, cum caput est corona cinctum. Porro ad formae decus id pertinere, ut coracino colore crines fulgeant, omnes sciunt, ac proinde id a nonnullis summo studio affectari, atque ab ipso corvo remedia a curiosis expeti, quibus hoc consequantur. Hinc Plinius libri vigesimi noni capite sexto, Corvi ovum, in aereo vase permixtum, illitumque deraso capite, nigritiam capillis affert, etc. Et paullo post, Alii sanguine et cerebro ejus utuntur cum vino nigro. Alii excoquunt ipsum, et nocte concubia in plumbeum vas condunt. Et Aelianus libri primi capite quadragesimo octavo Hist. Προσακούω δὲ καὶ ὠὰ κόρακος με-λαίνειν τρίχας· Corvi ova audio nigros efficere pilos. Quae eadem praescribunt Sextus, et Marcellus, et Rasis, et Arabum non pauci alii. Meritò igitur Solomonis species ex eo in Cantico commendatur, quod comam nigram habuerit, adeoque corvi instar nigram, id est coracini coloris. Si Solomonem objicias in col. 200 hoc libro nobis proponi ut Christi typum, qui contra Dan. VII, 9. et Apoc. I, 14. senex et canis capillis describitur; respondeo, utrumque Christo convenire diverso respectu. Nempe, ut nigri capilli juvenilem vigorem arguunt, ita cani senilem gravitatem et sapientiam. Corvi nigredinem Arabes حلك chalac, aut حنك chanac vocant, voce maxime propria. Alcamus, حلك الغراب حنكه او سواده Chalac corvi, id est, chanac, aut nigredo ejus. Et alibi, حنك الغراب منقاره او سواده Chanac corvi, rostrum, aut nigredo ejus. Item, اسود حانك حالك Niger, chanic, chalic, id est, nigerrimus, ut corvus, cujus nigredo est intensissima.

This Alcibiades lisped rightly; because in truth Theorus was a notorious flatterer. All this serves to show why it is said of the bridegroom in Cant. V, 2: קוצותיו תלתלים שחורות כעורב, His locks are hanging, black as a raven. For so we interpret it: II, 789 because in Arabic قُصَّة [quṣṣa] Kutssa is the portion of the hair that overhangs the forehead, or hangs down from the front part of the head. And we explain תלתלים, with Solomon, as hanging, as if a reduplicated form of the verb תלה, which means to hang. Thus from יפה reduplicated comes יפיפית, thou art fair, Ps 45:3. Those locks, then, which hang down over the forehead, ears and cheeks, are said to be black as a raven, that is, of raven colour, which is conspicuous for its sheen. Nor is it obscure why he mentions only the locks. The subject is a crowned king: accordingly it is said in Song 3:11. Behold, daughters of Zion, King Solomon with the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his espousals. And in this very verse: His head of finest gold (supply crowned), his locks hanging, black as a raven. This is almost the only part of the hair that is visible when the head is encircled with a crown. Moreover, everyone knows that it belongs to beauty of appearance that the hair should shine with a raven colour; hence some strive after it with the greatest zeal, and the curious seek remedies from the raven itself by which to obtain it. Hence Pliny, book 29, chapter 6: A raven's egg, mixed in a bronze vessel and smeared on the shaven head, gives blackness to the hair, etc. And a little later: Others use its blood and brain with dark wine. Others boil the bird itself down and at dead of night store it in a lead vessel. And Aelian, Hist. book 1, chapter 48: Προσακούω δὲ καὶ ὠὰ κόρακος μελαίνειν τρίχας [Prosakouō de kai ōa korakos melainein trichas]; I hear that raven's eggs make the hair black. The same things are prescribed by Sextus, Marcellus, Rhazes, and not a few other Arabs. Rightly, therefore, Solomon's appearance is praised in the Song for his having black hair, indeed black like a raven, that is, of raven colour. If you object that in col. 200 this book Solomon is set before us as a type of Christ, who on the contrary in Dan 7:9 and Rev 1:14 is described as old and with white hair, I reply that both befit Christ in different respects. For as black hair signifies youthful vigour, so white hair signifies the gravity and wisdom of age. The Arabs call the blackness of the raven حلك [ḥalak] chalac, or حنك [ḥanak] chanac, a most proper term. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: حلك الغراب حنكه او سواده [ḥalak al-ghurāb ḥanakuhu aw sawāduhu], Chalac of the raven, that is, chanac, or its blackness. And elsewhere: حنك الغراب منقاره او سواده [ḥanak al-ghurāb minqāruhu aw sawāduhu], Chanac of the raven: its beak, or its blackness. Likewise, اسود حانك حالك [aswad ḥānik ḥālik], Black, chanic, chalic, that is, very black, like the raven, whose blackness is most intense. ¶

Corvum solitudinis amicum esse animal, in confesso est. Proinde Hesychius, Σκορακίζει· εἰς ἔρημον πέμπει, καὶ ἀρᾶται, ἀπὸ τοῦ εἰς κόρακας πέμ-πειν. Sensus est, ad corvos mittere, idem esse, quod alicui imprecari, ut in solitudinem amandetur et relegetur. Quale est, quod ad proverbium, Βάλλ᾽ ἐς κόρ κας, Scholiastes Aristophanis in Nubibus corvos asserit ἐν τοῖς ἐρημοτέροις τόποις ἐπιτηρεῖν τὰ σώματα, in solitariis locis observare cadavera. Huc quoque II, 790 pertinet, quod praedicitur Es. XXXIV, 10. fore, ut post vastatam Idumaeam in Bosrae metropolis ruderibus noctua et corvus habitent. Additur ex Sophon. II, 14. ubi de vastatione Ninives, Vox cantantis in fenestra, corvus in superliminari. Ita enim habet Hieronymus. Et Graecorum versio, Θηρία φωνήσει ἐν τοῖς διορύγμασιν αὐτῶν, καὶ κόρακες ἐν τοῖς πυλῶσιν αὐτῆς· Bestiae clamabunt in eorum (Ninivitarum) fossis, et corvi in portis ejus (Ninives). Et a Theodoreto ad hunc locum censentur inter vastitatis signa κοράκων νεοττίαι, corvorum nidi. Et a Cyrillo per corvos intelliguntur nycticoraces, qui in ipsis urbis portis non habitarent, nisi plurimum ibi quietis et otii esset, eorum naturae gratissimi: Οὐδ᾽ ἂν ἐμφιλοχωρήσειαν αὐτοῖς πυλῶσιν οἱ νυκτικόρακες, εἰ μὴ πλείστη τις ὅση καὶ ἁνδάνουσα λίαν τοῖς ὧδε φύσεως ἔχουσιν ὑπάρχῃ σχολή. Sed Hebraea vox חרב choreb, quam corvum explicant (1), siccitatem et vastitatem significat: aut, si chereb legas, gladium. Proinde Syrus interpres gladium reddit, quomodo etiam Aquila. Et Graecorum alii siccitatem, ut testatur Hieronymus, qui cur maluerit corvum reddere, nullus capio. In Hebraeo (inquit) ponitur חרב, quod secundum lectionis diversitatem vel siccitas, vel gladius, vel corvus accipitur. Quasi idem sint, חרב, per Cheth, et ערב, per Ajin. Quod Hieronymo nullus concesserit. Nam ערב, per Ajin et Cholem, corvum sonat: sed חרב, per Cheth et Cholem, siccitatem et vastitatem; aut gladium, si sit Segol. Hic igitur cum חרב per Cheth et Cholem scribant, Sophoniae verba sic reddenda sunt: Vox resonabit e fenestra: vastitas erit in limine, cum cedrus nudata fuerit, id est, cedrinum tabulatum. Vocem hic intelligo, qualem Es. XIII, 21.

That the raven is an animal fond of solitude is generally agreed. Accordingly Hesychius has: Σκορακίζει· εἰς ἔρημον πέμπει, καὶ ἀρᾶται, ἀπὸ τοῦ εἰς κόρακας πέμπειν [Skorakizei; eis erēmon pempei, kai aratai, apo tou eis korakas pempein]. The sense is that to send to the ravens is the same as to curse someone, wishing that he be banished and relegated to the wilderness. Of the same kind is the remark of the Scholiast on Aristophanes in the Clouds, on the proverb Βάλλ᾽ ἐς κόρ κας [Ball᾽ es kor kas], that ravens ἐν τοῖς ἐρημοτέροις τόποις ἐπιτηρεῖν τὰ σώματα [en tois erēmoterois topois epitērein ta sōmata], watch for corpses in solitary places. Here too II, 790 belongs the prediction in Es. XXXIV, 10, that after Idumaea has been laid waste, the owl and the raven will dwell in the ruins of its metropolis Bosra. To this is added Sophon. II, 14, on the devastation of Nineveh: The voice of one singing in the window, the raven on the lintel. For so Jerome has it. And the version of the Greeks: Θηρία φωνήσει ἐν τοῖς διορύγμασιν αὐτῶν, καὶ κόρακες ἐν τοῖς πυλῶσιν αὐτῆς [Thēria phōnēsei en tois diorygmasin autōn, kai korakes en tois pylōsin autēs]; Beasts will cry out in their (the Ninevites') ditches, and ravens in her gates (Nineveh's). And Theodoret, on this passage, counts among the signs of devastation κοράκων νεοττίαι [korakōn neottiai], nests of ravens. And Cyril understands by the ravens night-ravens, which would not dwell in the very gates of the city unless there were a great deal of quiet and leisure there, which is most agreeable to their nature: Οὐδ᾽ ἂν ἐμφιλοχωρήσειαν αὐτοῖς πυλῶσιν οἱ νυκτικόρακες, εἰ μὴ πλείστη τις ὅση καὶ ἁνδάνουσα λίαν τοῖς ὧδε φύσεως ἔχουσιν ὑπάρχῃ σχολή [Oud᾽ an emphilochōrēseian autois pylōsin hoi nyktikorakes, ei mē pleistē tis hosē kai handanousa lian tois hōde physeōs echousin hyparchē scholē]. But the Hebrew word חרב choreb, which they explain as raven R1, means dryness and devastation; or, if you read chereb, sword. Accordingly the Syriac translator renders it sword, as does Aquila too. And others among the Greeks render it dryness, as Jerome attests; why he preferred to render it raven, I cannot grasp at all. In the Hebrew (he says) is written חרב, which, according to the difference of reading, is taken as either dryness, or sword, or raven. As if חרב with Cheth and ערב with Ajin were the same. No one would grant Jerome that. For ערב, with Ajin and Cholem, means raven; but חרב, with Cheth and Cholem, means dryness and devastation, or sword if it has Segol. Since here, then, they write חרב with Cheth and Cholem, the words of Zephaniah are to be rendered thus: A voice will resound from the window; devastation will be on the threshold, when the cedar has been laid bare, that is, the cedar panelling. By voice here I understand one such as in Es. XIII, 21. ¶

Here it may be asked why Solomon calls ravens ערבי נחל, ravens of the valley, or, if you prefer, ravens of the torrent, Prov 30:17. For נחל means both valley and torrent R2. I would take it as ravens of the valley, with the Greeks, who have κόρακες ἐκ τῶν φαράγγων [korakes ek tōn pharangōn], that is, ravens from the valleys, or those that like to nest in secluded valleys, which in Hebrew you might rightly call נחלי הבתות, valleys of the wastes II, 791, with the Prophet Isa 7:19. One may also understand some particular valley frequented by ravens and named after them. Isaiah could be understood of some such valley, were it not for the Masoretes, when he says of the riches of the Moabites, Es. XV, 7: על נחל הערבים ישאום, that is, as it is commonly rendered, They will carry them to the valley of the Arabs, or to the torrent of willows. And that indeed rightly, since the vowels supplied by the Masoretes col. 201 do not allow us to interpret otherwise. But if they were absent, one could just as well read, They will carry them to the valley of the ravens. In this sense it is said in Greek Βάλλ᾽ ἐς κόρακας [Ball᾽ es korakas], that is, εἰς ἀπώλειαν, καὶ εἰς φθοράν [eis apōleian, kai eis phthoran]. And in Isaiah himself, II, 20, to cast idols to the moles and the bats is to destroy and cut them off. Now, if one may indulge in conjecture, that valley of the ravens was the very one that is called the valley of corpses in Jer 31:40. And the same valley was called by both names because ravens, flocking together from every side, consumed the corpses of the condemned thrown into it. Granting this, when Solomon says that the ravens of the valley will pick out the eye that mocks its father, it will be as if he had said that one guilty of so foul a crime, having suffered an infamous punishment, is to be thrown into the valley of corpses, to be consumed there by ravens. For it counts as part of the penalty for the guilty that their corpses lie unburied or hang, to be torn apart by wild beasts or by carnivorous birds. Hence in Aristophanes' Θεσμοφορούσαις [Thesmophorousais] Mnesilochus begs to be spared punishment on this account, that he, an old man, should not be laughed at by the women τοῖς κόραξιν ἑστιῶν [tois koraxin hestiōn], providing a banquet for ravens. Et Alkazuinius in capite غراب gorab, seu Ce Corvo, يجتمع علي كل الحيوانات الكبار البادية كالجمل والفرس وكذا الادمي ويقصد قلع عينها ولا يمتنع بالدفع والضرب لشدة جوعه Versatur super omnia magna animalia, quae sunt in agris, ut super camelos et equos, adeoque super homines, et eorum oculos conatur evellere: nec propellendo, aut verberando prohibetur propter vim famis. Quanquam id corvo non sit peculiare, si vere scribit Carduanus lib. 7. capite 35. De rerum varietate: Nullum ge- col. 202 nus avium magnarum, seu gallinae, seu pavones, aut corvi sint, vel grues, aut ciconiae, ita mansuescit, ut, si propius sint, non conentur tibi oculos eruere. Interim veteri proverbio jactatur, Corvum corvi oculum non eruere. Gregor. Sed, ut video, consentis iniquitati, et in te impletur proverbium illud, quod „Corvus oculum corvi non eruit“. Quo proverbio innuebat, Gregorium episcopum in alterum episcopum, laesae majestatis reum, noluisse quidquam pronuntiare.

Again, let the ravens pluck out the eyes of the yoked oxen with which they plough the land, and of the sheep. And Gregory of Nyssa, Homily 13, calls the raven σαρκοβόρον τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν ἀφανιστικὸν [sarkoboron tōn ophthalmōn aphanistikon], a flesh-eater, a destroyer of eyes. And Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt], in the chapter غراب [ghurāb] gorab, or On the Raven: يجتمع علي كل الحيوانات الكبار البادية كالجمل والفرس وكذا الادمي ويقصد قلع عينها ولا يمتنع بالدفع والضرب لشدة جوعه [yajtamiʿ ʿalī kull al-ḥayawānāt al-kibār al-bādiya ka-l-jamal wa-l-faras wa-kadhā al-ādamī wa-yaqṣid qalʿ ʿaynihā wa-lā yamtaniʿ bi-l-dafʿ wa-l-ḍarb li-shiddat jūʿihi] It settles on all the large animals that are in the open country, such as camels and horses, and even on men, and tries to pluck out their eyes; nor is it kept off by driving away or beating, because of the violence of its hunger. Although this is not peculiar to the raven, if Cardanus writes truly in book 7, chapter 35 of De rerum varietate: No kind of large bird, whether hens, or peacocks, or ravens, or cranes, or col. 202 storks, becomes so tame that, if they are rather close, they do not try to tear out your eyes. I have heard the same about ostriches from those who lead them about. And Pliny gives this reason, book 11, ch. 37: And so perfect is the mirror-like power in the eyes that that tiny pupil gives back the whole image of a man. This is the reason why most birds, when in men's hands, go chiefly for the eyes: seeing their own likeness in them, they make for them as if toward the objects of their natural longing. Meanwhile an old proverb is bandied about, A raven does not pluck out a raven's eye. Gregory of Tours on King Chilperic (History, book 5, ch. 18): And on seeing me the king said, "O bishop, you ought to bestow justice on all; and, behold! I do not receive justice from you. But, as I see, you consent to iniquity, and in you is fulfilled that proverb, 'A raven does not pluck out a raven's eye.'" By this proverb he was hinting that Bishop Gregory had been unwilling to pronounce anything against another bishop accused of high treason. ¶

Ex iis, quae dicta sunt, aperte constat, τὸ σκορακίζειν, seu ad corvos mittere, aut abjicere, non solum id esse, quod supra definiebat Hesychius, secessum, aut relegationem in solitudinem alicui imprecari, sed et ad necem eum devovere, et ultima quaeque illi exoptare. Tamen apud sacros scriptores σκορα-κίζειν et ἀποσκορακίζειν solum est relinquere, repellere, increpare. Et σκορα-κισμὸς, vel ἀποσκορακισμὸς, repulsio et increpatio. Sic Eccles. XLI, 19. (Αἰ-σχύνεθε) ἀπὸ σκορακισμοῦ λήψεως, καὶ δόσεως· Vos pudeat ob repulsionem dati II, 793 et accepti. Septuaginta Psal. XXVI, 9. Μὴ ἀποσκορακίσῃς με (3)· Ne me derelinquas: Hebr. אל תטשני. Esa. XVII, 13. Καὶ ἀποσκορακιεῖ αὐτόν· Hebr. וגער בו, Et increpavit eum. Es. LXVI, 15. Καὶ ἀποσκορακισμὸν ἐν φλογὶ πυ-ρός· Hebraice וגערתו בלהבי אש, Et increpationem ejus in flamma ignis. Sed ibi increpatio pro perditione sumitur; quomodo saepe apud Hebraeos. Tamen Cyrillus ἀποβολὴν abjectionem explicat; quia Deus dicet reprobis, Discedite a me, maledicti, in ignem aeternum.

From what has been said it is plainly evident that τὸ σκορακίζειν [to skorakizein], or to send or cast to the ravens, is not only what Hesychius defined above, to wish on someone withdrawal or banishment into the wilderness, but also to devote him to death and to wish him every extremity. Yet among the sacred writers σκορακίζειν [skorakizein] and ἀποσκορακίζειν [aposkorakizein] mean only to abandon, to repel, to rebuke. And σκορακισμὸς [skorakismos], or ἀποσκορακισμὸς [aposkorakismos], means repulse and rebuke. Thus Sir 41:19 (Αἰσχύνεθε [Aischynethe]) ἀπὸ σκορακισμοῦ λήψεως, καὶ δόσεως· [apo skorakismou lēpseōs, kai doseōs] Be ashamed on account of the rejection of what is given II, 793 and received. The Septuagint, Ps 26:9 Μὴ ἀποσκορακίσῃς με R3· [Mē aposkorakisēs me] Do not forsake me: Hebrew אל תטשני. Isa 17:13 Καὶ ἀποσκορακιεῖ αὐτόν· [Kai aposkorakiei auton] Hebrew וגער בו, And he rebuked him. Isa 66:15 Καὶ ἀποσκορακισμὸν ἐν φλογὶ πυρός· [Kai aposkorakismon en phlogi pyros] in Hebrew וגערתו בלהבי אש, And his rebuke in a flame of fire. But there rebuke is taken for destruction, as often among the Hebrews. Yet Cyril explains it as ἀποβολὴν [apobolēn], rejection, because God will say to the reprobate, Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire. ¶

Corvum Iudaeis prohibet Moses למינו, secundum genus suum, aut secundum speciem suam: ut et alias quasdam aves in immundarum censu. Et, ut notat R. Selomo, cum ad nomen avis additur juxta genus suum, יש באותו המין שאין דומין זה לזה לא כמראיהם ולא בשמותיהם וכולן מין אחד, Species habet figura et nomine dissimiles, quae tamen ad unum genus revocantur. Sed haec infra docebimus parum esse solita. Tamen, si corvorum species quaeras, eo referri queant cornix, graculus, monedula, pica, sturnus etiam, si creditur Baal Hatturim, Cabalistica ratione fretus; ista nimirum, quod verba עורב למינו, corvus secundum genus suum, et vox זרזר, id est, sturnus, sunt ἰσόψηφα. Utraque enim valent 414. Corvi species in Damire Arabes ita describunt: والغربان اربعة اجناس اسود حالك وابلق ومطرف ببياض لطيف الجرم ياكل الحب واسود طاووسي براق الريش ورجلاه بلون المرجان يعرف بالزاغ, Corvorum sunt quatuor species. 1. Perfecte niger (is est corvus, proprie dictus.). 2. Varius, seu ex nigro et albo mixtus (cornicem intelligo, quam vernaculo sermone palliatam vocamus). 3. Qui extrema habet alba, corpore gracilis, granis vescens (id est, cornix σπερμολόγος). 4. Niger pavoninus, lucentibus plumis, pedibus corallinis, qui pica vocatur. Ara- col. 203 bes cornicem referre ad corvinum genus, ex eo maxime constat, quod herbam κορωνοπόδα constanter appellant رجل الغراب pedem corvi.

Moses forbids the raven to the Jews למינו, after its kind, or after its species, as he does certain other birds in the list of the unclean. And, as R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] notes, when after its kind is added to the name of a bird, יש באותו המין שאין דומין זה לזה לא כמראיהם ולא בשמותיהם וכולן מין אחד, it has species unlike in form and name, which are nevertheless referred to one kind. But we shall show below that this is hardly well founded. Still, if you ask for the species of ravens, there may be referred to it the crow, the graculus [jackdaw], the jackdaw, the magpie, and even the starling, if one believes Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah], who relies on a Cabbalistic argument: namely, that the words עורב למינו, the raven after its kind, and the word זרזר, that is, starling, are ἰσόψηφα [isopsēpha]. For both amount to 414. The Arabs in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] describe the species of the raven thus: والغربان اربعة اجناس اسود حالك وابلق ومطرف ببياض لطيف الجرم ياكل الحب واسود طاووسي براق الريش ورجلاه بلون المرجان يعرف بالزاغ [wa-l-ghirbān arbaʿat ajnās aswad ḥālik wa-ablaq wa-muṭarraf bi-bayāḍ laṭīf al-jirm yaʾkul al-ḥabb wa-aswad ṭāwūsī barrāq al-rīsh wa-rijlāhu bi-lawn al-marjān yuʿraf bi-l-zāgh], There are four species of ravens. 1. The perfectly black (this is the raven properly so called). 2. The pied, or mixed of black and white (I understand the crow which in our vernacular we call the palliata [hooded]). 3. The one that has white extremities, slender in body, feeding on grains (that is, the σπερμολόγος [spermologos] crow). 4. The peacock-like black one, with shining plumage and coral-coloured feet, which is called the magpie. That the Arabs col. 203 refer the crow to the raven kind is clearest from the fact that they consistently call the plant κορωνόπους [korōnopous] رجل الغراب [rijl al-ghurāb] raven's foot. ¶
