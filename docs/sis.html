De Hirundine.

On the Swallow. ¶

Cum תור thor, id est turture, junguntur סיס sis et עגור agor, ut aves pariter migratoriae, in his Ierem. VIII, 7. Et thor, et sis, et agor custodierunt tempus adventus sui. Et de turture cum sit actum, sequitur, ut agamus de sis et agor. Quae voces rursus occurrunt in Ezechiae, regis, cantico Es. XXXVIII, 14. Ut sis (inquit) et agor pipiebam, gemebam ut columba. Sed harum alibi nulla mentio. Et cum utroque loco סוס in textu scribatur per Vau, tamen in Ieremia Hebraei סיס sis, per Iod legunt. Quam lectionem maxime probo, cum ut col. 60 סיס sis, a voce סוס sus, differat, qua significatur equus, tum quia סיס sis videtur magis exprimere naturam rei, quae hoc nomine significatur (1). Quod ipsum, quia in quaestione est, jam disquirendum. Pagninus, Munsterus, Buxtorfius, Schindlerus, Iunius, et Tremellius, Angli, Itali, etc. et plerique alii novi interpretes סיס sis gruem constanter exponunt. Ita nimirum statuunt Hebraeorum doctissimi, Iarchius, Kimchius et Pomarius, Ionathanem sequuti in Chaldaica Paraphrasi, quae pro סיס sis habet כורכיא kurkeja. Porro כורכיא kurkeja gruem esse, tam ex Talmude constat, quam ex Arabum scriptoribus, qui scribunt كركي kurki. Pro כורכיא kurkeja quandoque reperias כרוכיא krukeja, ut in tractatu Kiddusim cap. 2. fol. 44. a. Cui voci simillima est vox כרוכא kruka, quae pro חגב chagab, seu cicada, apud Ionathanem occurrit Levit. XI, 22. Extabat etiam olim in Paraphrasi Ierusalem, ex qua citatur ab Elia in Methurgeman. Unde natus jocularis error in Symmacho, qui, promiscue sumens voces כרוכא kruka et כרוכיא krukeja, ex grue in Ieremia cicadam fecit. Hinc Hieronymus in Ieremiam: Symmachus cicadam transtulit, quae Hebraice dicitur סיס sis. Et ibidem Theodoretus, Τρυγὼν, καὶ τέττιξ, καὶ χελιδὼν, ἀγροῦ στρουθία, ἐφύλαξαν καιροὺς εἰσόδων αὐτῶν· Turtur, et cicada, et hirundo, II, 603 agri aviculae, observarunt tempus adventus sui. Quae ipsa verba postquam citavit Chrysostomus in Homilia De turture, tanquam ex Ieremia, addit pro commentario, Τρυγόνα λέγων τὴν πάνσεμνον ἐκκλησίαν, χελιδόνα τὸν φιλέρημον Ἰωάννην, τέττιγα τὸν εὔλαλον Παῦλον. Turturem vocans venerabilem ecclesiam, hirundinem deserti incolam, Ioannem, cicadam eloquentem Paulum. Qui er-ror ex alio errore ortus. Tam enim falluntur, qui סיס sis gruem, quam qui cicadam reddunt: cum סיס sis neque grus, neque cicada sit, sed hirundo. Id probat primo consensus vetustiorum interpretum, qui apud me multum potest, cum ei non repugnat necessaria ratio, qualis hoc loco nulla se offert. Vetustiores hic appello, 1. Graecos Seniores, a quibus סיס sis χελιδὼν utroque loco exponitur; 2. Theodotionem; 3. Hieronymum, qui id ipsum sequuntur; 4. Il-lum ipsum Symmachum, cui סיס sis in Ieremia est cicada. Ab eodem enim Ezechiae verba כסוס עגור redduntur ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλεισθεῖσα, ut hirundo inclusa. 5. His adde Arabem, cui סיס sis est سنونوه sununua, id est, hirundo. In Abi Ubid, Cent. 1. Prov. 32. سنونية senunia scribitur, et hirundinem recte explicaverat Erpenius ὁ μακαρίτης, sed solum ex conjectura. Avis, inquit, nomen esse, satis liquet: at cujusmodi nescio. Verti hirundinem, quod sic fere verti soleat סנוניתא senunitha Chaldaicum. Alioqui hirundo خطاف chottaph vocatur, etc. His viri, in Arabicis literis longe doctissimi, et de me olim optime meriti, dubiis ut occurratur, libet vocem explicare accuratius. خطاف chottaph est hirundo in genere, a volatus rapiditate sic dicta, ut Chaldaice חטפיתא chatphitha. Sed hujus plures sunt species, quas in Damire sic definiunt Arabes: الخطاطيف انواع منها نوع يالف السواحل عند البحر يحفر بيتها هناك ويعشش هناك وهو صغير الجثة دون عصغور الجنة ولونه رمادي col. 61 والناس يسمونه سنونوا بضم السين المهملة ونونين ومنها نوع لخضرته علي ظهرة بعض حمرة اصغر من الدرة تسميه اهل مصر الخضري لخضرته يغتات الغراش والذباب ونحو ذلك ومنها طويل الاجنحة رقيقها يالف الجبال وياكل النمل وهذا النوع يقال له السايم مغرب سامه ومنهم من يسمي هذا النوع السنونوا الوحدة سنونوة وهو كثير في المسجد الحرام بمكة يعشش في سقف المسجد في مقام ابراهيم وباب بني شيبة وبعض الناس II, 604 يزعم انه الطير الابابيل الذي عذب الله به اصحاب الغيل Hirundinum variae sunt species: quarum una degit in maris littoribus, et ibi effossa domo sua nidulatur. Haec corpore minor est ave Paradisi, et cinerei coloris. Vocatur sununu per Sin Semol dammatum et duo Nun. Est et species viridis cum aliqua rubedine super dorsum, minor psittaco, quam Aegyptii ratione coloris vocant chadra, id est, viridem. Vescitur papilionibus, muscis atque aliis similibus animalculis. Est et alia species longis alis, et tenuibus, incola montium, formicas edens. Has semaim appellant a Singulari semama. Sed et nonnulli sununu vocant. Singulare unitatis est sununua. Hujus speciei multae sunt in templo Charam Mecchanae urbis, quae in templi tecto nidificant, in Abrahami statione, in porta filiorum Seibae. Quidam asserunt, aves esse ababil dictas, per quas Deus castigavit socios elephantis: jactis scilicet in eorum capita حجارة من سجيل lapidibus ex siggil. Ita illi vocant lapides, in inferorum igne excoctos et induratos, cum eorum, in quos cadebant, nominibus inscriptis. Il-lorum dux Abraheth, rex Abasenorum in Arabia Felici intima, eo ipso anno, quo natus est Mahometh, magno elephante vectus cum multis copiis venerat, ut Mecchanum templum exscinderet. Quod pseudo-propheta obiter tangit Azoara cxv, cui titulus الغيل Elephas. Sed haec sunt alterius loci. Hic notasse sufficiat, constare ex iis, quae allata sunt, سنونوة sununua proprie dici ripariam hirundinem (2), de qua Plinius lib. 10. cap. 33. Tertium est earum genus, quae ripas excavant, atque ita internidificant. Et lib. 30. cap. 4. Multo tamen efficacissimi ripariarum (hirundinum) pulli. Ita vocant in riparum cavis nidificantes. Riparia Graece dicitur δρεπανὶς a falcatis pedibus. Ita vocat Philosophus Hist. col. 62 lib. 1. cap. 1. Sed et eodem nomine سنونوة sununua illas hirundines significari addunt, quae in montibus et templis nidificant. Hae sunt rusticae, seu agrestes Plinii hirundines, de quibus Gesnerus pag. 565. (3). Interim certum est, hoc nomen quandoque patere latius, et pro quavis hirundine sumi. Ut, cum lapis اليرقان icterias, id est, quo sanantur icterici, alio nomine vocatur حجر السنونوة lapis sununuae, id est, hirundinis; quia in ejus nido reperitur. Nam hirundinis pullos croco tingunt Arabes; et pater, eos ictero laborare suspicatus, lapidem illum ex India in nidum affert et pullis supponit. Parvum describit Damir, lineis rubris striatum, quae ad nigredinem vergunt. Ita etiam qui scripsit De Naturae mirabilibus, ubi صنونو sununu per Sad perperam legi notat Damir. Et Baruch. VI, 21. Graeca haec verba, Ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν II, 605 αὐτῶν ἐφίπτανται χελιδόνες, Arabs ita reddidit: السنونو تلمسهم علي راسهم Assununu (seu hirundines) tangunt eos super capita sua. Tangunt, id est, ἐφάπτονται, quomodo legit pro ἐφίπτανται involant.

With תור thor, that is, the turtledove, are joined סיס sis and עגור agor, as birds that are likewise migratory, in these words of Jer 8:7. Both thor and sis and agor have kept the time of their coming. And since the turtledove has been dealt with, it follows that we treat of sis and agor. These words occur again in the song of King Hezekiah, Isa 38:14. Like sis (he says) and agor I chirped, I moaned like a dove. But elsewhere there is no mention of them. And although in both places סוס is written in the text with Vau, yet in Jeremiah the Hebrews read סיס sis, with Iod. This reading I strongly approve, both so that סיס sis may differ from the word סוס sus, which means horse, and because סיס sis seems better to express the nature of the thing signified by this name R1. This very point, since it is in question, must now be examined. Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Munster, Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], Schindler, Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], the English, the Italians, etc., and most other modern interpreters consistently render סיס sis as crane. So, of course, the most learned of the Hebrews hold, Jarchi [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac), called Jarchi by the Christian Hebraists], Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], following Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] in the Chaldee Paraphrase, which has כורכיא kurkeja for סיס sis. Further, that כורכיא kurkeja is the crane is clear both from the Talmud and from the Arab writers, who write كركي [kurkī] kurki. For כורכיא kurkeja you sometimes find כרוכיא krukeja, as in the tractate Kiddusim, ch. 2, fol. 44a. Very similar to this word is the word כרוכא kruka, which occurs in Jonathan at Lev 11:22 for חגב chagab, or cicada. It once also stood in the Jerusalem Paraphrase, from which it is cited by Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] in the Methurgeman. Hence arose an amusing error in Symmachus, who, taking the words כרוכא kruka and כרוכיא krukeja indiscriminately, made a cicada out of the crane in Jeremiah. Hence Jerome on Jeremiah: Symmachus translated it 'cicada', which in Hebrew is called סיס sis. And in the same place Theodoret: Τρυγὼν, καὶ τέττιξ, καὶ χελιδὼν, ἀγροῦ στρουθία, ἐφύλαξαν καιροὺς εἰσόδων αὐτῶν· [Trygōn, kai tettix, kai chelidōn, agrou strouthia, ephylaxan kairous eisodōn autōn] The turtledove, and the cicada, and the swallow, II, 603 little birds of the field, have observed the time of their coming. After Chrysostom quoted these very words in the Homily On the Turtledove, as if from Jeremiah, he adds by way of commentary: Τρυγόνα λέγων τὴν πάνσεμνον ἐκκλησίαν, χελιδόνα τὸν φιλέρημον Ἰωάννην, τέττιγα τὸν εὔλαλον Παῦλον [Trygona legōn tēn pansemnon ekklēsian, chelidona ton philerēmon Iōannēn, tettiga ton eulalon Paulon]. Calling the venerable church the turtledove, the dweller in the desert, John, the swallow, and the eloquent Paul the cicada. This error arose from another error. For those who render סיס sis as crane are as mistaken as those who render it cicada: since סיס sis is neither the crane nor the cicada, but the swallow. This is proved first by the agreement of the older interpreters, which carries great weight with me when no compelling reason opposes it, and here none presents itself. As older interpreters I here name: 1. the Greek Elders, by whom סיס sis is rendered χελιδὼν [chelidōn] in both places; 2. Theodotion; 3. Jerome, who follow the same; 4. that very Symmachus for whom סיס sis in Jeremiah is the cicada. For by him Hezekiah's words כסוס עגור are rendered ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλεισθεῖσα [hōs chelidōn enkleistheisa], like a swallow shut in. 5. Add to these the Arabic translator, for whom סיס sis is سنونوه [sunūnūh] sununua, that is, swallow. In Abi Ubid, Century 1, Proverb 32, it is written سنونية [sunūniya] senunia, and the late Erpenius ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs] had rightly explained it as swallow, but only by conjecture. That it is the name of a bird, he says, is clear enough; but of what kind I do not know. I translated it swallow, because the Chaldee סנוניתא senunitha is usually so translated. Otherwise the swallow is called خطاف [khuṭṭāf] chottaph, etc. To meet these doubts of a man by far the most learned in Arabic letters, and once most deserving of my gratitude, I wish to explain the word more precisely. خطاف [khuṭṭāf] chottaph is the swallow in general, so called from the swiftness of its flight, like the Chaldee חטפיתא chatphitha. But of this there are several species, which the Arabs in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] define thus: الخطاطيف انواع منها نوع يالف السواحل عند البحر يحفر بيتها هناك ويعشش هناك وهو صغير الجثة دون عصغور الجنة ولونه رمادي [al-khaṭāṭīf anwāʿ minhā nawʿ yaʾlaf al-sawāḥil ʿinda al-baḥr yaḥfir baytahā hunāka wa-yuʿashshish hunāka wa-huwa ṣaghīr al-jutha dūna ʿaṣghūr al-janna wa-lawnuhu ramādī] col. 61 والناس يسمونه سنونوا بضم السين المهملة ونونين ومنها نوع لخضرته علي ظهرة بعض حمرة اصغر من الدرة تسميه اهل مصر الخضري لخضرته يغتات الغراش والذباب ونحو ذلك ومنها طويل الاجنحة رقيقها يالف الجبال وياكل النمل وهذا النوع يقال له السايم مغرب سامه ومنهم من يسمي هذا النوع السنونوا الوحدة سنونوة وهو كثير في المسجد الحرام بمكة يعشش في سقف المسجد في مقام ابراهيم وباب بني شيبة وبعض الناس [wa-al-nās yusammūnahu sunūnū bi-ḍamm al-sīn al-muhmala wa-nūnayn wa-minhā nawʿ li-khuḍratihi ʿalā ẓahrih baʿḍ ḥumra aṣghar min al-durra tusammīhi ahl Miṣr al-khuḍarī li-khuḍratihi yaghtāt al-gharāsh wa-al-dhubāb wa-naḥw dhālika wa-minhā ṭawīl al-ajniḥa raqīquhā yaʾlaf al-jibāl wa-yaʾkul al-naml wa-hādhā al-nawʿ yuqāl lahu al-sāyim mughrib sāmih wa-minhum man yusammī hādhā al-nawʿ al-sunūnū al-waḥda sunūnūa wa-huwa kathīr fī al-masjid al-ḥarām bi-Makka yuʿashshish fī saqf al-masjid fī maqām Ibrāhīm wa-bāb banī Shayba wa-baʿḍ al-nās] II, 604 يزعم انه الطير الابابيل الذي عذب الله به اصحاب الغيل [yazʿum annahu al-ṭayr al-abābīl alladhī ʿadhdhaba Allāh bihi aṣḥāb al-ghīl] There are various species of swallows. One of them lives on the shores of the sea, and there digs out its home and nests. It is smaller in body than the bird of Paradise, and ash-grey in colour. It is called sununu, with Sin Semol given damma and two Nun. There is also a green species with some redness on the back, smaller than a parrot, which the Egyptians on account of its colour call chadra, that is, green. It feeds on butterflies, flies and other similar small creatures. There is also another species, with long and slender wings, a dweller in the mountains, which eats ants. These they call semaim, from the singular semama. But some also call them sununu. The singular of unity is sununua. Of this species there are many in the Charam temple of the city of Mecca, which nest in the roof of the temple, at the station of Abraham and at the gate of the sons of Seiba. Some assert that these are the birds called ababil, by which God punished the companions of the elephant: namely by throwing on their heads حجارة من سجيل [ḥijāra min sijjīl] stones of siggil. So they call stones baked and hardened in the fire of hell, inscribed with the names of those on whom they fell. Their leader Abraheth, king of the Abyssinians in the interior of Arabia Felix, in the very year in which Muhammad was born, had come riding a great elephant with large forces in order to destroy the temple of Mecca. The pseudo-prophet touches on this in passing in Azoara 115, entitled الغيل [al-ghīl] The Elephant. But these matters belong elsewhere. Here it suffices to have noted that it is clear from what has been adduced that سنونوة [sunūnūa] sununua properly denotes the sand martin [hirundo riparia] R2, of which Pliny, book 10, ch. 33: The third kind of them is those which hollow out banks and so nest within. And book 30, ch. 4: Yet by far the most efficacious are the young of the bank (swallows). So they call those that nest in hollows of riverbanks. The riparia [bank swallow] is called in Greek δρεπανὶς [drepanis], from its sickle-shaped feet. So the Philosopher calls it, Hist. col. 62 book 1, ch. 1. But they add that by the same name سنونوة [sunūnūa] sununua are also signified those swallows which nest in mountains and temples. These are the rustic or field swallows of Pliny, on which see Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], p. 565 R3. Meanwhile it is certain that this name sometimes extends more widely and is used for any swallow. Thus the stone اليرقان [al-yaraqān] icterias, that is, by which jaundice sufferers are healed, is by another name called حجر السنونوة [ḥajar al-sunūnūa] the stone of the sununua, that is, of the swallow, because it is found in its nest. For the Arabs dye the swallow's young with saffron; and the father, suspecting that they suffer from jaundice, brings that stone from India into the nest and places it under the young. Damir describes it as small, streaked with red lines that verge on black. So too the author On the Wonders of Nature, where, as Damir notes, صنونو [ṣunūnū] sununu is wrongly read with Sad. And at Bar 6:21 the Arabic translator rendered these Greek words, Ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν II, 605 αὐτῶν ἐφίπτανται χελιδόνες [Epi tēn kephalēn autōn ephiptantai chelidones], thus: السنونو تلمسهم علي راسهم [al-sunūnū talmisuhum ʿalā raʾsihim] Assununu (or swallows) touch them upon their heads. Touch, that is, ἐφάπτονται [ephaptontai], as he read instead of ἐφίπτανται [ephiptantai], fly upon. ¶

Probatum est hactenus, plerisque veterum סיס sis esse hirundinem. Quibus Ionathan frustra opponitur, quasi כורכיא, id est, gruem, reddiderit. Ille enim duas voces סיס sis, et עגור agor, recte explicavit per כורכיא kurkeja, et סנוניתא sununitha, id est, gruem et hirundinem. Nam, ut סיס sis est hirundo, sic עגור agor est grus, ut sequenti capite docebitur. Sed invertit ordinem, et hirundinem, quae in Hebraeo textu prior est, posteriore loco posuit. Quod interpretibus saepe accidit. Et, ne longius exempla petantur, in loco Baruch, quem nuper citavimus, pro Graecis verbis νυκτερίδες, χελιδόνες, vespertiliones et hirundines, Arabs habet, inverso ordine, السنونو والوطاويط hirundines et vespertiliones. Proinde Chaldaeum etiam, quem Syrus sequutus est, iis veterum addimus, quibus סיס sis est hirundo. Ita, ut priscorum interpretum jam nullus sit, qui non assentiatur versioni, quam asserimus.

It has been proved so far that for most of the ancients סיס sis is the swallow. Against them Jonathan is cited in vain, as though he had rendered it כורכיא, that is, crane. For he correctly explained the two words סיס sis and עגור agor by כורכיא kurkeja and סנוניתא sununitha, that is, crane and swallow. For as סיס sis is the swallow, so עגור agor is the crane, as will be shown in the following chapter. But he inverted the order, and put the swallow, which comes first in the Hebrew text, in the second place. This often happens with translators. And, not to seek examples further afield, in the passage of Baruch which we cited a little earlier, for the Greek words νυκτερίδες, χελιδόνες [nykterides, chelidones], bats and swallows, the Arabic has, in inverted order, السنونو والوطاويط [al-sunūnū wa-l-waṭāwīṭ] swallows and bats. Accordingly we add the Chaldee also, whom the Syriac followed, to those of the ancients for whom סיס sis is the swallow. Thus there is now none of the early translators who does not agree with the rendering we maintain. ¶

Ad hanc veterum auctoritatem accedit manifesta ratio. Vox סיס sis est ὀνοματοποιητικὴ, qua Hebraeus fuit animus hirundinis ψιθυρισμὸν utcunque imitari. Ut Italis circa Venetiam, quibus zisilla est hirundo, et zisillare hirundinis more τιτυβίζειν, vel ψιθυρίζειν. Zinzilulare dixit auctor Philomelae,

To this authority of the ancients is added a clear reason. The word סיס sis is ὀνοματοποιητικὴ [onomatopoiētikē], by which the Hebrew intended to imitate, after a fashion, the swallow's ψιθυρισμὸν [psithyrismon]. So with the Italians around Venice, for whom zisilla is the swallow, and zisillare means to τιτυβίζειν [titybizein] or ψιθυρίζειν [psithyrizein] in the manner of a swallow. The author of the Philomela said zinzilulare: ¶

Regulus, atque merops, et rubro pectore Progne, Consimili modulo zinzilulare sciunt.

The wren and the bee-eater, and Procne with red breast, Know how to zinzilulare with a like measure. ¶

Quia ὄρνιθι μὲν, εἰ ἐποίησεν, οὐκ αἰσχρὸν, παρθένῳ δὲ αἰσχρόν· εὖ οὖν ἐλοιδόρησεν, εἰπὼν ὃ ἦν ἀλλ᾽ οὐχ᾽ ὅ ἐστιν, avi quidem, si fecerat, id turpe non est, sed puellae turpe est. Recte igitur eam convitiatus est, id dicens, quod fuerat, non autem, quod tum erat. Nec est, quod quis miretur, hirundini indi Philomelae nomen, cum vulgo sit persuasissimum, Philomelam esse lusciniam. Res enim est in ambiguo. Et multi veterum Prognen in lusciniam, ac Philomelam in hirundinem mutatam fuisse diserte tradunt. Quod nullo negotio probaverim, si haec res ageretur. Sed alio jam properamus.

I add one point: in the Greek copy of Tobit, chapter 2, verse 11, we read that τὰ στρουθία [ta strouthia], little birds, dropped dung into his eyes. Jerome rightly saw that this refers to swallows, and renders it thus: It happened that, as he slept, warm dung fell from a nest of swallows onto his eyes. For this is peculiar to the swallow: the mother herself either throws the droppings of the chicks out of the nest while they are small, or teaches them to void outside the nest when they are somewhat bigger. Aristotle, History of Animals, book 9, chapter 7: Καὶ κόπρον τὸ πρῶτον αὐταὶ ἐκβάλλουσιν· ὅταν δὲ αὐξηθῶσι μεταστρέφοντας ἔξω διδάσκουσι τοὺς νεοττοὺς προΐεσθαι [Kai kopron to prōton autai ekballousin; hotan de auxēthōsi metastrephontas exō didaskousi tous neottous proiesthai]. This is rendered by Pliny, book 10, chapter 33, as follows: With remarkable cleanliness they carry out the excrement of the chicks; and when these are older they teach them to turn round and discharge their fullness outside. Nor is this statement of the Arabs in Damir unlike it: ولا يلقي في عشه زبلا بل يلقيه الي خارج فانا اكبر فراخه علمها ذلك [wa-lā yulqī fī ʿushshihi ziblan bal yulqīhi ilā khārij fa-anā akbar firākhihi ʿallamahā dhālik] It does not void its dung into the nest but outside, and it teaches this very thing to its chicks when they have grown up. Something similar is recorded to have happened to Gorgias, for a swallow dropped dung on him. Plutarch, Table Talk, book 8, chapter 7: col. 68 Γοργίας δὲ ὁ Σοφιστὴς, χελιδόνος ἀφείσης ἐπ᾽ αὐτὸν ἀπόπατον, ἀναβλέψας πρὸς αὐτὴν, Οὐ καλὰ ταῦτα (εἶπεν) ὦ Φιλομῆλα [Gorgias de ho Sophistēs, chelidonos apheisēs ep᾽ auton apopaton, anablepsas pros autēn, Ou kala tauta (eipen) ō Philomēla]· Gorgias the Sophist, when a swallow had let fall its dung on him, turned his eyes towards it and said, "This is not becoming II, 614, Philomela." The Philosopher praises this as an apt saying in the Rhetoric, book 3, chapter 3, because ὄρνιθι μὲν, εἰ ἐποίησεν, οὐκ αἰσχρὸν, παρθένῳ δὲ αἰσχρόν· εὖ οὖν ἐλοιδόρησεν, εἰπὼν ὃ ἦν ἀλλ᾽ οὐχ᾽ ὅ ἐστιν [ornithi men, ei epoiēsen, ouk aischron, parthenō de aischron; eu oun eloidorēsen, eipōn ho ēn all᾽ ouch ho estin], for a bird, if it had done it, it is not shameful, but for a maiden it is shameful. So he rebuked her well, saying what she had been, not what she then was. Nor should anyone be surprised that the swallow is given the name Philomela, although it is very commonly believed that Philomela is the nightingale. For the matter is uncertain, and many of the ancients state explicitly that Procne was changed into a nightingale and Philomela into a swallow. I could prove this without any trouble, if that were the subject at hand. But we now hasten to other matters. ¶
