II, 800 Chapter XII. col. 209 ¶

De Corvo, quem ex arca emisit Noë.

On the Raven that Noah Sent Out of the Ark. ¶

De corvo in sacro textu duae habentur historiae: una de corvo, a Noa ex arca emisso; altera de corvis, qui Eliam in deserto paverunt. Prior sic habet juxta Hebraeos Gen. VIII, 7. Et emisit corvum, et is exivit exeundo, et redeundo, donec aquae exaruissent a superficie terrae. Causam emissionis corvi Graeca editio sic effert in quibusdam exemplaribus, Καὶ ἀπέστειλε τὸν κόρακα ἰδεῖν, εἰ κεκόπακε τὸ ὕδωρ· Et corvum emisit, ut videret, an cessavissent aquae. Quod ex versu sequenti sumptum, ubi de columba habetur, Emisit quoque columbam a se, ut videret, an extenuatae fuissent aquae a superficie terrae. Et vero Noam eodem animo corvum, quo columbam emisisse constat. Sed, ut repetitionis taedium vitaret Moses, id solum de columba dixit, cujus historiam ideo expressit disertius; quia est majoris momenti. Die igitur prima decimi mensis, cum aquae diluvii non parum decrevissent, apparere jam inceperant celsissimorum montium summi vertices, Gen. VIII, 5. Et post quadraginta dies elapsos, id est, undecimi mensis die undecima, Noë, aperta arcae fenestra, corvum emisit; cum terram aquis obruissent quadragesimales pluviae, Gen. VII, 12. Noë speraverat fore, ut pari numero dierum aquae exsiccarentur. Quod cum non successisset, corvum emisit, quem sciebat odore cadaverum allici posse, ut longius evolaret, si jam animalibus patuisset terra. At corvus, arcam circumvolitans, ab ea non abscessit longius. Exivit (inquit Moses) exeundo, et redeundo. Emissus enim, non nisi foedas paludes cernens, statim receptum quaesiit vel in arcam, aut super arcam. A qua subinde rursus avolans redibat identidem: neque II, 801 aliud quidquam praestitit, donec aquae exaruissent. Hebraei nugantur in Agudda, corvum ideo non desiisse arcam circumvolitare, nec ivisse quo missus fuerat: שהיה חושדו על בת זוגו, quia de conjuge (seu foemina) sua sollicitus erat; ne scilicet, se absente, ab alio iniretur. Quod cum de alio corvo non possit intelligi, cum Noë solum duos servasset, nolo attexere, quae monstra sibi fingant impuri homines, et mortalium insulsissimi. In Beresith Rabba sect. 33. Noë et corvus colloquuntur invicem: et, hoc quaerente, cur ex omnibus pecudibus, col. 210 feris, et avibus solus mitteretur, respondet Noë, mundum corvis posse facile carere, cum neque לאכילה, ad esum, neque לקרבן, ad sacrificia sint utiles. Sed intercedit Deus, monens, futuris saeculis magnum fore corvorum usum, nempe Eliae tempore, qui pane et carne, a corvis allata, aliquamdiu pastus fuit. Atque ob id ipsum scilicet noluit Deus, ut corvus ab arca longius secederet. Et illi dixit, אתה מזומן לשליחות אחרת בימי אליהו, Tu Eliae diebus aliam (majoris momenti) legationem obire debes: ac proinde in illa tempora servandus es. Quod ipsa Mosis verba volunt obscure subinnuere, cum dicit עד יבשת, donec exaruissent. Quia vox יבשת easdem habet literas, quas vox תשבי Thisbi. Itaque עד יבשת, id est, עד תשבי, Ad usque Thisbitem, supple Eliam, quod observat Baal Hatturim; et literas יבשת המים, Exaruerunt aquae, per Gematriam idem valere, quod נחל כרית, torrentem Cerith, vbi Eliam corvi paverunt: quod tamen verum non est.

Concerning the raven, the sacred text contains two narratives: one about the raven sent out of the ark by Noah; the other about the ravens that fed Elias in the desert. According to the Hebrews, the former reads as follows, Gen 8:7: And he sent out the raven, and it went out, going forth and returning, until the waters had dried up from the face of the earth. In some copies the Greek edition states the reason for sending out the raven thus: Καὶ ἀπέστειλε τὸν κόρακα ἰδεῖν, εἰ κεκόπακε τὸ ὕδωρ [Kai apesteile ton koraka idein, ei kekopake to hydōr]; And he sent out the raven, to see whether the waters had ceased. This is taken from the following verse, which concerns the dove: He also sent out the dove from him, to see whether the waters had abated from the face of the earth. And indeed it is evident that Noah sent out the raven with the same intention as the dove. But Moses, to avoid the tedium of repetition, stated this only of the dove, whose story he therefore set out more explicitly, because it is of greater importance. So on the first day of the tenth month, when the waters of the flood had decreased considerably, the topmost peaks of the highest mountains had already begun to appear, Gen 8:5. And after forty days had passed, that is, on the eleventh day of the eleventh month, Noah opened the window of the ark and sent out the raven. Since forty days of rain had overwhelmed the earth with waters, Gen 7:12, Noah had hoped that the waters would dry up in the same number of days. When this did not happen, he sent out the raven, which he knew could be lured by the smell of carcasses to fly farther off, if the earth were already open to animals. But the raven, flying round the ark, did not go far from it. It went out (says Moses) going forth and returning. For once sent out, seeing nothing but foul swamps, it at once sought refuge either in the ark or on top of the ark. Flying off from it again and again, it kept returning; and it did nothing II, 801 else until the waters had dried up. The Hebrews talk nonsense in the Agudda, saying that the raven did not stop circling the ark, nor go where it had been sent, שהיה חושדו על בת זוגו, because he was anxious about his mate (or female); that is, lest in his absence she be covered by another. Since this cannot be understood of another raven, as Noah had kept only two, I will not add the monstrosities that these impure men, the most senseless of mortals, invent for themselves. In Bereshit Rabbah, section 33, Noah and the raven converse with each other. When the raven asks why, out of all the cattle, col. 210 wild beasts and birds, he alone was sent, Noah replies that the world could easily do without ravens, since they are useful neither לאכילה, for eating, nor לקרבן, for sacrifices. But God intervenes, warning that in future ages ravens would be of great use, namely in the time of Elias, who was fed for some time on bread and meat brought by ravens. And for that very reason, of course, God did not want the raven to go far from the ark. And He said to it, אתה מזומן לשליחות אחרת בימי אליהו, In the days of Elias you must undertake another mission (of greater importance); and therefore you must be preserved for those times. This, they say, the very words of Moses obscurely hint at when he says עד יבשת, until they had dried up, because the word יבשת has the same letters as the word תשבי Tishbi. Thus עד יבשת means עד תשבי, Until the Tishbite, supply Elias, as Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] observes; and he adds that the letters of יבשת המים, The waters dried up, by Gematria are equivalent to נחל כרית, the brook Cherith, where the ravens fed Elias: which, however, is not true. ¶

Haec de corvo Noae juxta Hebraeos: sed multo aliter Graeci; quorum versio in speciem id ipsum negat, quod diserte asserit Moses, nempe rediisse corvum. Habet enim, Ἐξελθὼν οὐκ ἀνέστρεψεν, Egressus (ex arca corvus) non reversus est, pro Hebraeis ויצא יצוא ושוב, Et egressus est egrediendo, et redeundo. Syrus itidem, ܘܢܦܩ ܡܦܩ ܘܠܐ ܗܦܟ, Et egressus est egrediendo, et non rediit. Et Hieronymus, Qui egrediebatur, et non revertebatur. Et Arabes manuscripti duo, quos in Suecia vidi, لم يعون non rediit, aut فلم يعد et non rediit. Sed ille ex Graeca, hic ex Syra versione palam expressi. Quod ipsum sequuntur prisci doctores, Hilarius, Arnobius et Pseudo-Hieronymus in Psalmum centesimum quadragesimum sextum, verus Hieronymus in Dialogo contra Luciferianos capite octavo, Ambrosius libri De Noë et arca capite decimo septimo, Sulpitius Severus Historiae libro primo, Augustinus in Genesim libro primo, Quaestione decima tertia, et passim, Chrysostomus in Genesim li-bro secundo. Eutychius quoque Alexandrinus pag. 40. فذهب الغراب ولم يرجع Et abiit corvus, nec reversus est. Omnes denique veteres, in quos incidere licuit, praeter unum Procopium, qui negationem non admittit. Unde nata duplex quaestio: prior, unde ortum sumpserit haec lectionum diversitas; II, 802 altera, utra lectio vera sit, atque alteri praeferenda. Atque ad illam multi respondent, Hebraeos et Graecos posse optime conciliari; utrumque enim vere dici, et corvum esse reversum, et corvum non esse reversum. Cum ad arcam reversus sit, vel super arcam, sed non reversus sit in arcam. Quae responsio est inanis. Cum neutra lectio corvum dicat in arcam, aut super arcam rediisse, vel non rediisse: sed harum una simpliciter corvum rediisse habet, altera non rediisse; quae omnino sunt contraria. Mosis verba Gen. XXII, 1. Deus tentavit Abrahamum, si quis ita reddiderit, Deus non tentavit Abrahamum, nonne esset falsus interpres; etiamsi verum sit aliquo modo, Deum non tentasse Abrahamum, nempe quomodo dixit Iacobus a), Deum neminem tentare? Praeterea, licet in Hebraeo textu non diserte dicatur corvus rediisse in arcam, tamen palam subintelligitur. Ita enim habent verba: Egressus est egrediendo, et redeundo, donec, etc. Id est, saepe egressus est, et saepe rediit. Ex arca porro ut saepe egrederetur, necesse illi fuit in eandem saepe regredi. Neque enim e domo quisquam egreditur, qui in eam non sit ingressus. Aliis Hebraeum שוב col. 211 non solum redire, sed et recedere est: ut hujus ipsius capitis versu tertio, וישבו המים מעל הארץ הלוך ושוב, Et redierunt aquae a facie terrae, eundo et redeundo, id est, recesserunt recedendo. Et pari ratione, inquiunt, corvus ex arca egressus est exeundo, ושוב, et redeundo, id est, recedendo. Ex arca porro recedere idem est, ut asserunt, quod ad eam non reverti. Sed neque haec responsio satis est idonea. Nam, quidquid dicant, שוב proprie redire est. Etiam cum dicuntur aquae a facie terrae rediisse, ideo rediisse dicuntur, quia prius eam demerserant, et a facie terrae se conferebant in naturalem suum locum, id est, in mare, etc. Praeterea שוב in recedendi notione non sumitur, nisi addita particula מן, id est, ab, vel ex, quae hic nusquam comparet. Denique haec non cohaerent, Corvus exiit exeundo et recedendo, donec exaruissent aquae. Exiit enim corvus mensis undecimi die undecima, Gen. VIII, 5. 6. Et aquae demum exaruerunt anni sequentis initio, Gen. VIII, 13. id est, diebus quinquaginta post corvi exitum. Quo toto tempore corvum semper ex arca recessisse, nemo dixerit. Dato tamen, non concesso, Hebraea verba sic posse reddi: Corvus exiit exeundo et recedendo, ab iis valde diversa esset Graeca versio, Corvus egressus non rediit. Abire enim et recedere alicunde per quinquaginta dierum iter, non idem est, quod inde abire, nec redire ante diem quinquagesimum. Alii Iudaeos volunt negationem ex Hebraeo textu exemisse, quos refellit Samaritani codicis cum Hebraico consensus. Praeterea Chaldaei duo, Ionathan et Onkelos, hic habent, ונפק מפק ותאיב. Et Samaritanus interpres, ࠅࠍࠐࠒ ࠌࠐࠅࠒ ࠅࠕࠁ Et egressus est egrediendo et revertendo. Et Arabs Parisiis editus, II, 803 فجعل يخرج ويرجع Et exibat, et redibat. Et Erpenianus, وخرج خارجا وراجعا Et exiit exiens, et rediens. Iosephus quoque Antiqu. lib. 1. cap. 4. πρὸς Νῶχον ἐπανῆλθε, Ad Noam rediit. Sed et in Beresith Rabba, et in Agudda, qui Hebraeorum libri sunt vetustissimi, legitur iste locus plane ut in nostris codicibus. Hieronymum denique sic legisse, mihi persuasissimum est, et in Quaestionibus Hebraicis haberi perperam: „Et de corvo aliter (in Hebraeo textu) dicitur, Et emisit corvum, et egressus est exiens, et non revertens“. Le-gendum enim, Exiens et revertens. Nam, si legas, et non revertens, quorsum asserit, in Hebraeo textu aliter, quam in Graeco dici? Quae Chisamensis episcopi observatio est verissima. Neque vero causam habuere Iudaei, cur illum locum corrumperent, cum eorum nihil intersit, utrum corvus rediisse, vel non rediisse credatur. Sunt denique, qui Graecis codicibus, vel certo consilio, vel per scribarum incuriam putant insertam fuisse negationem: quorum neutrum est verissimile. Non prius; quia ut reliquis Hebraeorum causa non fuit, cur negationem ex Hebraeo textu eximerent, ita nec Graecis interpretibus causa fuit, cur illam suae versioni adderent: neque posterius; quia ex Graecis verbis (Ἐξελ-θὼν οὐκ ἀνέστρεψεν, ἕως τοῦ ξηρανθῆναι τὸ ὕδωρ, Egressus non rediit, donec aquae exaruissent) negationem si demas, dissolvetur orationis tota compages. Et haec erunt sine ullo sensu, Egressus rediit, donec aquae exaruissent. Cum igitur haec versio iis verbis concepta sit, ex quibus negatio nullo modo potest abesse, eam patet ab ipsis auctoribus esse adscriptam, non scribarum errore insultam, atque in textum insertam.

So much on Noah's raven according to the Hebrews. The Greeks, however, put it very differently: their version appears to deny the very thing that Moses expressly asserts, namely that the raven came back. For it has Ἐξελθὼν οὐκ ἀνέστρεψεν [Exelthōn ouk anestrepsen], Having gone out (from the ark, the raven) did not return, for the Hebrew ויצא יצוא ושוב, And it went out, going out and returning. The Syriac likewise has ܘܢܦܩ ܡܦܩ ܘܠܐ ܗܦܟ [ונפק מפק ולא הפך], And it went out, going out, and did not return. And Jerome: Which went out and did not return. And two Arabic manuscripts which I saw in Sweden have لم يعون [lam yaʿūn] it did not return, or فلم يعد [fa-lam yaʿud] and it did not return. But the former is plainly rendered from the Greek version, the latter from the Syriac. The early doctors follow the same reading: Hilary, Arnobius and Pseudo-Jerome on Psalm 146; the genuine Jerome in the Dialogue against the Luciferians, chapter 8; Ambrose, On Noah and the Ark, chapter 17; Sulpicius Severus, History, book 1; Augustine on Genesis, book 1, Question 13, and elsewhere; Chrysostom on Genesis, book 2. Also Eutychius of Alexandria, p. 40: فذهب الغراب ولم يرجع [fa-dhahaba al-ghurāb wa-lam yarjiʿ] And the raven went away, and did not return. In short, all the ancients whom I have been able to come across, except Procopius alone, who does not admit the negation. Hence a twofold question arises: first, where this difference of readings originated; II, 802 second, which reading is true and to be preferred to the other. To the first many reply that the Hebrews and the Greeks can very well be reconciled, since both statements are truly made: that the raven returned and that the raven did not return. For it returned to the ark, or onto the ark, but did not return into the ark. This answer is empty. For neither reading says that the raven returned or did not return into the ark or onto the ark; rather, one of them says simply that the raven returned, the other that it did not return, which are flatly contrary. If someone rendered the words of Moses, Gen 22:1. God tested Abraham, as God did not test Abraham, would he not be a false translator, even though it is in some sense true that God did not test Abraham, namely in the sense in which James said a) that God tempts no one? Moreover, although the Hebrew text does not expressly say that the raven returned into the ark, it is nevertheless plainly implied. For the words run thus: It went out, going out and returning, until, etc. That is, it often went out and often returned. And in order to go out of the ark often, it had necessarily to go back into it often. For no one goes out of a house who has not gone into it. According to others the Hebrew שוב col. 211 means not only to return but also to recede, as in verse 3 of this very chapter: וישבו המים מעל הארץ הלוך ושוב, And the waters returned from the face of the earth, going and returning, that is, they receded steadily. And in the same way, they say, the raven went out, going out from the ark, ושוב, and returning, that is, receding. And to recede from the ark, they assert, is the same as not returning to it. But neither is this answer adequate. For whatever they say, שוב properly means to return. Even when the waters are said to have returned from the face of the earth, they are said to have returned because they had previously submerged it and were withdrawing from the face of the earth to their natural place, that is, into the sea, etc. Moreover, שוב is not taken in the sense of receding unless the particle מן, that is, from or out of, is added, which nowhere appears here. Finally, these words do not hang together: The raven went out, going out and receding, until the waters had dried up. For the raven went out on the eleventh day of the eleventh month, Gen 8:5–6., and the waters dried up only at the beginning of the following year, Gen 8:13, that is, fifty days after the raven went out. No one would say that the raven kept receding from the ark during that whole time. But granting, without conceding, that the Hebrew words could be rendered thus, The raven went out, going out and receding, the Greek version, The raven having gone out did not return, would still differ greatly from them. For to go away and recede from somewhere over a fifty days' journey is not the same as to go away from there and not return before the fiftieth day. Others hold that the Jews removed the negation from the Hebrew text; they are refuted by the agreement of the Samaritan codex with the Hebrew. Moreover the two Chaldees, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], have here ונפק מפק ותאיב. And the Samaritan translator has ࠅࠍࠐࠒ [ונפק] ࠌࠐࠅࠒ [מפוק] ࠅࠕࠁ [ותב] And it went out, going out and returning. And the Arabic edited at Paris, II, 803 فجعل يخرج ويرجع [fa-jaʿala yakhruju wa-yarjiʿu] And it kept going out and coming back. And Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)], وخرج خارجا وراجعا [wa-kharaja khārijan wa-rājiʿan] And it went out, going out and returning. Josephus also, Antiqu. book 1, ch. 4: πρὸς Νῶχον ἐπανῆλθε [pros Nōchon epanēlthe], It returned to Noah. Moreover, in Bereshit Rabbah and in the Agudda, which are very ancient books of the Hebrews, this passage reads exactly as in our codices. Finally, I am entirely convinced that Jerome read it thus, and that the Hebrew Questions read wrongly: „And of the raven it is said otherwise (in the Hebrew text): And he sent out the raven, and it went out, going out and not returning“. For one should read going out and returning. For if you read and not returning, why does he assert that the Hebrew text says something different from the Greek? This observation of the bishop of Chisamus is entirely true. Nor indeed did the Jews have any reason to corrupt that passage, since it makes no difference to them whether the raven is believed to have returned or not. Finally, there are those who think the negation was inserted into the Greek codices, either deliberately or through the carelessness of scribes; neither of these is at all likely. Not the former, because just as the Hebrews had no reason to remove the negation from the Hebrew text, so the Greek translators had no reason to add it to their version. Nor the latter, because if you take the negation out of the Greek words (Ἐξελθὼν οὐκ ἀνέστρεψεν, ἕως τοῦ ξηρανθῆναι τὸ ὕδωρ [Exelthōn ouk anestrepsen, heōs tou xēranthēnai to hydōr], Having gone out it did not return until the waters had dried up), the whole structure of the sentence falls apart, and it will make no sense at all: Having gone out it returned until the waters had dried up. Since, therefore, this version is framed in words from which the negation can in no way be absent, it is clear that it was written by the translators themselves, not slipped in and inserted into the text by scribal error. ¶

Nempe, si recte conjicimus, Hebraea verba, יצוא ושוב, Egrediendo et redeundo, Graeci interpretes sic legerunt, ולוא ישוב, Et non reversus est. Ad col. 212 verbum, Et non revertetur. Sed Futurum pro Praeterito tam frequenter occurrit, ut in uno Psalmo centesimo septimo huius καταχρήσεως viginti minimum exempla reperire sit. Neque scriptura multum differunt יצוא ושוב, et ולוא ישוב. Nam Vau et Iod in veteribus manuscriptis literae sunt tam similes, ut saepissime permutentur. Et Lamed a Tsade fere solo cornu differt: quod Tsade dextrum habet, et demissius; Lamed sinistrum, et elatius. Sciunt quid velim, qui Rabbinorum lectioni assueverunt. Hinc igitur, nisi fallor, diversae lectionis et versionis horum verborum vera est origo. Neque facile dixerim, utra sit praeferenda. Et quamvis Hebraicam lectionem venerari soleam, ut ab ea nunquam discedam nisi valde invitus: tamen Graecam fateor hoc loco esse perspicuam magis, Egressus est corvus, neque rediit donec etc. id est, nunquam ad arcam rediit. At, inquiunt, si non rediit; quid de illo factum est? Respondet Augustinus in Iohannem Tractatu sexto, Mortuus est in his aquis. Et II, 804 In Faustum libri duodecimi capite vigesimo, Emissus corvus non est reversus, aut aquis utique interceptus, aut aliquo supernatante cadavere illectus. Et Cyrillus in Glaphyris libro secundo, Ἐνεπνίγη δὲ, οἶμαι, τοῖς ὕδασιν, ἔρεισμά τε, καὶ στάσιν οὐχ εὑρηκώς· Submersus est, ut arbitror, aquis, cum, ubi subsisteret, non invenisset. Quod potest sine periculo concedi. Et frustra objicitur, ex uno corvo superstite non potuisse post diluvium corvorum speciem reparari. Poterat enim ille corvus foeminam inivisse, antequam emitteretur. Et vero sunt ex Hebraeis, qui ob id ipsum volunt a Noa fuisse emissum, quod arcam intempestiva libidine polluisset, atque incestasset. Potuit etiam superesse corvus, etsi in arcam non redierit, sive cadaveri aquis innatanti insederit, aut montium verticibus, qui aquis nudari jam inceperant diebus quadraginta, antequam fuisset emissus, Gen. VIII, 5. 6. 7. Cur igitur columba, septiduo post emissa, non reperiens, ubi pedem figeret, in arcam redire coacta est, quia aquae adhuc universae terrae incumbebant, Gen. VIII, 8? Respondeo: Primo, gratis id dici, columbam septiduo post corvum fuisse emissam. Illam quidem emisit Noë ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ post eum, id est, post corvum, si Graecos et Hieronymum sequimur: sed ne id quidem habet Moses, cuius verba sunt, Et emisit columbam מאתו a se: quomodo recte reddunt Ionathan, et Onkelos, et Syrus, et Samaritanus, et uterque Arabs. Itaque potuerunt corvus et columba simul emitti. Pone tamen, columbam post corvum fuisse emissam; quid opus est hanc emissionem in septiduum differre, cum, corvo non reverso, Noë potuerit illam emittere die proxime sequente? Secundo, columba rediit, quia in humidis et uliginosis locis pedem non figit libenter. At corvum, utpote temperamenti siccissimi, aquis maxime gaudere notum. Unde est, quod corvi, futuram pluviam praesentientes, multa edunt laetitiae signa, tam voce, quam motu alarum: quae videre est in Arato versu 963. etc. Quia φιληδοῦσι τοῖς ὑγροῖς, humidis oblectantur, ut ibi habet Theon.

That is, if we conjecture rightly, the Greek translators read the Hebrew words יצוא ושוב, going out and returning, as ולוא ישוב, and it did not return. Literally col. 212 and it will not return. But the future for the past occurs so frequently that in Psalm 107 alone one can find at least twenty examples of this κατάχρησις [katachrēsis]. Nor do יצוא ושוב and ולוא ישוב differ much in writing. For in old manuscripts the letters Vau and Iod are so similar that they are very often interchanged. And Lamed differs from Tsade almost only by its horn: Tsade has it on the right and lower, Lamed on the left and higher. Those who are accustomed to reading the Rabbis know what I mean. Here, then, if I am not mistaken, is the true origin of the different reading and rendering of these words. Nor would I readily say which is to be preferred. And although I am accustomed to revere the Hebrew reading, so that I never depart from it except very reluctantly, I nevertheless admit that in this passage the Greek is clearer: The raven went out, and did not return until etc., that is, it never returned to the ark. But, they say, if it did not return, what became of it? Augustine answers in his sixth Tractate on John: It died in these waters. And II, 804 in Against Faustus, book 12, chapter 20: The raven, when sent out, did not return, being either overtaken by the waters or lured by some floating corpse. And Cyril in the Glaphyra, book 2: Ἐνεπνίγη δὲ, οἶμαι, τοῖς ὕδασιν, ἔρεισμά τε, καὶ στάσιν οὐχ εὑρηκώς· [Enepnigē de, oimai, tois hydasin, ereisma te, kai stasin ouch heurēkōs] It was drowned, I think, in the waters, since it had not found where to stand. This can be granted without risk. And it is objected in vain that the species of ravens could not have been restored after the flood from a single surviving raven. For that raven could have mated with the female before it was sent out. Indeed, there are some among the Hebrews who hold that it was sent out by Noah for precisely this reason, that it had polluted and defiled the ark with untimely lust. The raven could also have survived even though it did not return to the ark, whether by perching on a corpse floating on the waters, or on the mountain tops, which had already begun to be laid bare of the waters forty days before it was sent out, Gen 8:5–6. 7. Why then was the dove, sent out seven days later, forced to return to the ark, not finding where to set its foot, because the waters still lay over the whole earth, Gen 8:8? I answer: First, it is asserted without grounds that the dove was sent out seven days after the raven. Noah did indeed send it out ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ [opisō autou] after it, that is, after the raven, if we follow the Greeks and Jerome; but Moses does not even say this. His words are: And he sent out the dove מאתו from himself, as Jonathan, Onkelos, the Syriac, the Samaritan and both Arabic versions rightly render it. So the raven and the dove could have been sent out at the same time. But suppose the dove was sent out after the raven: what need was there to delay this sending for seven days, when, the raven not having returned, Noah could have sent it out on the very next day? Second, the dove returned because it does not willingly set its foot in wet and marshy places. But the raven, being of a very dry temperament, is known to delight greatly in water. Hence ravens, sensing coming rain, give many signs of joy, both by their voice and by the movement of their wings, as can be seen in Aratus, line 963 etc. Because φιληδοῦσι τοῖς ὑγροῖς [philēdousi tois hygrois], they delight in moist things, as Theon puts it there. ¶

Sed et Graecorum lectioni favere videtur Ethnica fabula de corvo, qui, ab Apolline aquatum missus, non rediit, nisi post ficus maturas. Ovidius libro secundo Fastorum,

The pagan fable of the raven also seems to favour the Greek reading: sent by Apollo to fetch water, it did not return until the figs were ripe. Ovid, Fasti, book 2: ¶

Immemor imperii sedisse sub arbore fertur,
Dum fierent tarda dulcia poma mora.

Forgetful of its orders, it is said to have sat beneath a tree,
Until the fruit grew sweet with slow delay. ¶

Hyginus Astronomicῶn libro secundo in Hydra; Corvus, Apollinis tutela usus, eo sacrificante missus a fonte aquam puram petitum, vidit arbores complures ficorum immaturas: eas expectans, dum maturescerent, in arbore quadam earum consedit. Et Germanicus in Aratum capite De Hydra, Corvus ideo inter astra col. 213 collocatus dicitur, eo quod fuerit in tutela Apollinis, a quo missus ad fontem, etc. cum vidisset arbores, grossos ficus habentes, volans consedit in eis, donec maturae II, 805 fierent, et aquam deferre distulit. A quo parum diversus Arati Scholiastes nondum editus, quem habeo manuscriptum; Τιμὴν γὰρ ἔχει ὁ κόραξ παρὰ τῷ Ἀπόλλωνι· ἑκάστῳ γὰρ τῶν Θεῶν ὄρνεόν ἐστι ἀνακείμενον. Θυσίας δὲ γενομέ-νης τοῖς Θεοῖς, σπονδὴν πεμφθεὶς ἐνέγκαι ἀπὸ κρήνης τινος, ἰδὼν παρὰ τὴν κρήνην συκὴν ὀλύνθους ἔχουσαν, ἔμεινεν, ὡςb) πεπανθῶσιν. Sed paulo aliter Aelianus Hist. lib. 1. cap. 47. Ὁ Ἀπολλὼν αὐτὸν θεράποντα ὄντα ὑδρευσάμενον ἀποπέμπει· ὁ δὲ ἐντυγχάνει λήϊῳ βαθεῖ μὲν, ἔτι δὲ χλωρῷ, καὶ μένει ἕως ἂν αὖον γένηται, τῶν πυρῶν περαχναῦσαιc) βουλόμενος, καὶ τοῦ προστάγματος ὠλιγώρησεν. Cum ad aquationem eum famulum suum misisset Apollo, ille in magnam segetem, adhuc viridem, cum incidisset, expectavit dum maturuisset, granis frumenti frui volens, et mandatum neglexit. Et aliter rursus apud Gesnerum Oppiani Scholiastes in librum primum Ἰξευτικῶν, Cum Coronis in Tricca Aesculapium pareret, Corax (servus) aquari jussus, re neglecta ἐλαγνεύετο lasciviebat: quamobrem iratus Apollo convertit eum in avem atram, etc. Sed haec ita diversa sunt, ut appareat, ex eodem fonte esse orta. Ut et vetus Proverbium de cessatore apud Hesychium, et Suidam, Κόραξ ὑδρεύει, Corvus aquatur. Et plerique, ut corvi moram longissimam fuisse significent, illum expectasse fingunt, donec ficus maturuisset; quia ficus serotina est, nec nisi cum uvis maturescit, ut constat ex Cant. II, 13. et Num. XIII, 24. Unde Epictetus apud Arrianum libri primi capite decimo quinto, Ἄν μοι λέγῃς, ὅτι θέλω σῦκον, ἀποκρινοῦμαί σοι, ὅτι χρόνου δεῖ· ἄφες ἀνθήσῃ πρῶτον, εἶτα προβαλεῖ τὸν καρπὸν, εἶτα πε-πανθῇ. εἶτα συκῆς μὲν καρπὸς ἄφνω, καὶ μίᾳ ὥρᾳ οὐ τελειοῦται. Si mihi dicas, te velle ficum, respondebo tibi, tempore opus esse. Permitte, ut primum floreat, deinde fructum emittat, deinde maturescat. Neque enim fici fructus cito et una hora ad maturitatem perducitur. Proinde ficus apta visa est ad designandam corvi moram, et cunctationem ad aquas. Quod ipsum natum videtur ex historia de corvo Noae, qui, ad aquas emissus, non rediit, donec, etc. Atque eodem fortasse pertinet, quod tradunt veteres apud Servium, corvos obliviosos esse, et plerumque minime ad nidos suos reverti. Neque enim id corvo tribuunt philosophorum libri. Sed fama de corvo Noae, qui reditus fuit immemor, causa potuit esse, cur vulgus corvino generi affingeret, quod huic fuit peculiare. Hae conjecturae si verae sunt, ut saltem habent aliquam veri speciem, vetustissima est illa lectio, qua corvus, ex arca emissus, ad Noam rediisse negatur.

Hyginus, in the second book of the Astronomica, under Hydra: The raven, which was under the protection of Apollo, was sent by him, while he was sacrificing, to fetch pure water from a spring. It saw a number of fig trees with unripe fruit; waiting for them to ripen, it settled on one of those trees. And Germanicus on Aratus, in the chapter On the Hydra: The raven is said to have been placed among the stars col. 213 because it was under the protection of Apollo, by whom it was sent to a spring, etc. When it had seen trees bearing unripe figs, it flew and settled on them until they II, 805 became ripe, and put off bringing the water. Little different from him is the still unpublished Scholiast on Aratus, whom I have in manuscript: Τιμὴν γὰρ ἔχει ὁ κόραξ παρὰ τῷ Ἀπόλλωνι· ἑκάστῳ γὰρ τῶν Θεῶν ὄρνεόν ἐστι ἀνακείμενον. Θυσίας δὲ γενομένης τοῖς Θεοῖς, σπονδὴν πεμφθεὶς ἐνέγκαι ἀπὸ κρήνης τινος, ἰδὼν παρὰ τὴν κρήνην συκὴν ὀλύνθους ἔχουσαν, ἔμεινεν, ὡςb) πεπανθῶσιν [Timēn gar echei ho korax para tō Apollōni; hekastō gar tōn Theōn orneon esti anakeimenon. Thysias de genomenēs tois Theois, spondēn pemphtheis enenkai apo krēnēs tinos, idōn para tēn krēnēn sykēn olynthous echousan, emeinen, hōs pepanthōsin]. But Aelian puts it somewhat differently, Hist. book 1, ch. 47: Ὁ Ἀπολλὼν αὐτὸν θεράποντα ὄντα ὑδρευσάμενον ἀποπέμπει· ὁ δὲ ἐντυγχάνει λήϊῳ βαθεῖ μὲν, ἔτι δὲ χλωρῷ, καὶ μένει ἕως ἂν αὖον γένηται, τῶν πυρῶν περαχναῦσαιc) βουλόμενος, καὶ τοῦ προστάγματος ὠλιγώρησεν [Ho Apollōn auton theraponta onta hydreusamenon apopempei; ho de entynchanei lēïō bathei men, eti de chlōrō, kai menei heōs an auon genētai, tōn pyrōn perachnausai boulomenos, kai tou prostagmatos ōligōrēsen]. When Apollo had sent it, his servant, to fetch water, it came upon a large field of grain, still green, and waited until it had ripened, wishing to enjoy the grains of wheat, and it neglected its errand. And differently again the Scholiast on Oppian, cited by Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], on the first book of the Ἰξευτικά [Ixeutika]: When Coronis was giving birth to Asclepius in Tricca, Corax (a servant), ordered to fetch water, neglected the task and ἐλαγνεύετο [elagneueto] was wanton; for this reason the angry Apollo turned him into a black bird, etc. But these accounts differ in such a way that it is evident they arose from the same source. So too the old proverb about an idler in Hesychius and the Suda: Κόραξ ὑδρεύει [Korax hydreuei], The raven is fetching water. And most writers, to indicate that the raven's delay was very long, imagine that it waited until the fig had ripened; for the fig is late and ripens only with the grapes, as is clear from Song 2:13 and Num 13:24. Hence Epictetus in Arrian, book 1, chapter 15: Ἄν μοι λέγῃς, ὅτι θέλω σῦκον, ἀποκρινοῦμαί σοι, ὅτι χρόνου δεῖ· ἄφες ἀνθήσῃ πρῶτον, εἶτα προβαλεῖ τὸν καρπὸν, εἶτα πεπανθῇ. εἶτα συκῆς μὲν καρπὸς ἄφνω, καὶ μίᾳ ὥρᾳ οὐ τελειοῦται [An moi legēs, hoti thelō sykon, apokrinoumai soi, hoti chronou dei; aphes anthēsē prōton, eita probalei ton karpon, eita pepanthē. eita sykēs men karpos aphnō, kai mia hōra ou teleioutai]. If you tell me that you want a fig, I shall answer you that it takes time. Let it first blossom, then put forth its fruit, then ripen. For the fruit of the fig tree is not brought to ripeness quickly and in a single hour. Accordingly the fig seemed suitable for denoting the raven's delay and its dawdling at the water. This very idea seems to have sprung from the story of Noah's raven, which, sent out to the waters, did not return until, etc. And perhaps to the same thing belongs what the ancients report in Servius, that ravens are forgetful, and for the most part do not return to their nests at all. For the books of the philosophers do not attribute this to the raven. But the report of Noah's raven, which forgot to return, may have been the reason why the common people ascribed to the whole raven kind what was peculiar to this one. If these conjectures are true, as at least they have some appearance of truth, then that reading by which the raven sent out of the ark is denied to have returned to Noah is very ancient. ¶

Quid quod ex verbis Hebraeis, prout hodie leguntur, non facile est se expedire, Exiit exeundo, et redeundo, donec aquae exaruissent? Illud enim, Exiit exeundo, est Ἑβραϊκώτατον. Sed quod sequitur, Exiit redeundo, poterit II, 806 videri durius, et esse oppositum in apposito. Et dubito, an alibi dicatur animal exire redeundo. Nec patet, quorsum Noë corvum, in arcam semel regressum, passus sit ex ea toties egredi, et per quinquaginta dies divagari nullo successu: maxime, postquam ex columba didicit, quod scire avebat. Has quippe volitationes clausa fenestra potuisset prohibere. Nam, quod ajunt, corvum ad arcam, non in arcam rediisse, jam supra confutavimus. Itaque non displicet Graeca lectio, quae non laborat his incommodis. Tamen haec pono, non statuo, et peritiorum judicio non illibenter submitto (0).

What of the fact that it is not easy to make sense of the Hebrew words as they are read today: It went out going out, and returning, until the waters had dried up? For It went out going out is thoroughly Hebraic. But what follows, It went out returning, may II, 806 seem rather harsh, and a contradiction in terms. And I doubt whether an animal is anywhere else said to go out returning. Nor is it clear why Noah should have allowed the raven, once it had come back into the ark, to go out of it so many times and wander about for fifty days to no purpose, especially after he had learned from the dove what he was eager to know. For he could have prevented these flights by shutting the window. As for the claim that the raven returned to the ark, not into the ark, we have already refuted it above. Therefore the Greek reading, which does not suffer from these difficulties, does not displease me. Yet I put this forward, I do not decide it, and I not unwillingly submit it to the judgement of those more expert R0. ¶
