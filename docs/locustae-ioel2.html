De Locustis quid habeat Ioel capite secundo.

What Joel has about the locusts in chapter two. ¶

In Ioelis prophetia cap. II, 1. et 2. haec habentur, Commoveantur omnes incolae col. 473 terrae: quia advenit dies Domini; quia propinquus est. Dies tenebrarum et caliginis; dies nubis et obscuritatis; ut aurora super montes expansa: populus multus et robustus. Cui similis non fuit a saeculo, nec post ipsum amplius erit us- que ad annos cujusque aetatis.] Ut antea vocavit locustas gentem validam et innu- merabilem; ita hic populum multum et robustum appellat: quae metaphora jam explicata est. His populum similem aut fuisse, aut futurum esse, negat; ut supra cap. I, 2. Tempus, quo venturae sunt, dies Domini dicitur: quia tum reis diem dicet Dominus, et de iis poenas reposcet. Et dies tenebrarum et caliginis; quia, ut lux res secundas, ita tenebrae in Scripturis adversas notant. Et confertur cum aurora, super montes expansa; ut significetur, fore, ut hoc malum et late et cito diffundatur. Utrumque enim est aurorae proprium, cujus lumen ubique spargitur puncto temporis, Eodem sensu dixit Esaias LVIII, 8. Erumpet ut aurora lumen tuum. Nempe οἷον ἀςραπῆς τινος δίκην, σὺν ὁρμῇ καὶ δρόμῳ καθιέμενον παρὰ Θεοῦ, ut in fulguris modum a Deo cum impetu et cursu dimis- sum, ut ibi habet Cyrillus. De caetero auroram expandi, vel spargi, etiam poe- tae dicunt. Homerus Iliad ϑ'. initio,

In the prophecy of Joel, ch. II, 1. and 2., we read: Let all the inhabitants of the col. 473 land tremble: for the day of the Lord is coming; for it is near. A day of darkness and gloom; a day of cloud and obscurity; like the dawn spread upon the mountains: a people great and strong. The like of it has never been from of old, nor shall be again after it, to the years of every generation.] As he earlier called the locusts a strong and innumerable nation, so here he calls them a great and strong people: this metaphor has already been explained. He denies that a people like them either has been or will be, as above in ch. I, 2. The time at which they will come is called the day of the Lord, because the Lord will then summon the guilty to trial and exact punishment from them. And it is a day of darkness and gloom, because in Scripture, just as light denotes prosperity, so darkness denotes adversity. And it is compared with the dawn spread upon the mountains, to signify that this evil will spread both widely and swiftly. For both are proper to the dawn, whose light is scattered everywhere in an instant. In the same sense Isaiah LVIII, 8. said: Your light shall break forth like the dawn. That is, οἷον ἀςραπῆς τινος δίκην, σὺν ὁρμῇ καὶ δρόμῳ καθιέμενον παρὰ Θεοῦ [hoion astrapēs tinos dikēn, syn hormē kai dromō kathiemenon para Theou], sent forth by God with force and speed like lightning, as Cyril has it there. Moreover, the poets too speak of the dawn being spread or scattered. Homer, at the beginning of Iliad ϑ' [8], ¶

Ἠὼς μὲν κροκόπεπλος ἐκίδνατο πᾶσαν ἐπ' αἶαν.

Ἠὼς μὲν κροκόπεπλος ἐκίδνατο πᾶσαν ἐπ' αἶαν. [Ēōs men krokopeplos ekidnato pasan ep' aian.] ¶

Which Virgil rendered thus in book 4 of the Aeneid: ¶

Et jam prima novo spargebat lumine terras
Tithoni croceum linquens Aurora cubile.

And now early Dawn, leaving the saffron couch of Tithonus, was scattering fresh light upon the earth. ¶

Et Aeneidos lib. 12.

And in book 12 of the Aeneid: ¶

Postera vix summos spargebat lumine montes.

The next day was scarcely scattering the mountain-tops with light. ¶

Ubi montes lumine suo spargere legitur, ut in Propheta, Sicut aurora super mon- tes expansa: quia lux in montibus primum apparet (1).

Here we read of scattering the mountains with its light, as in the Prophet, Like the dawn spread upon the mountains: because light appears first on the mountains R1. ¶

III, 306 Verse 3. Before it fire shall consume, and behind it a flame shall burn. The land was like the garden of Eden before it; but behind it shall be a vast desert; nor shall anything escape it.] Some hold that the locusts themselves are compared here, and above in ch. I, 19. 20., with fire and flame, because, on the authority of Pliny a), they scorch many things by their touch. Hence in the life of Aphraates the monk, Theodoret describes locusts as πυρὶ παραπλησίως ἅπαντα προσδαπανώσας [pyri paraplēsiōs hapanta prosdapanōsas], consuming everything like fire. So too the grammarians would have locustae named a locis ustis [from burnt places]. But, to say this in passing, to me col. 474 locusta seems named from nothing other than locutio [speech], since it is as loquax [talkative] as the cicada, which they hold to be the symbol of talkativeness. Thus in the Pythagorist of Aristophon, in Athenaeus, book 6, a certain parasite boasts that he is λαλεῖν τέττιγα [lalein tettiga], a cicada in talkativeness. And Aelian, Historia book 6, ch. 19, calls τὸ τεττίγων φῦλον [to tettigōn phylon], the race of cicadas, λάλον [lalon], talkative. Hence Alexis in the Thraso, in Athenaeus, book 4: ¶

— — — σοῦ δ' ἐγὼ λαλιστέραν
Οὐ πώποτ' εἶδον, οὔτε κερκώπην, γύναι,
Οὐ κίτταν, οὐκ ἀηδόνα, οὐ τρυγόνα, οὐ τέττιγα.

— — — σοῦ δ' ἐγὼ λαλιστέραν Οὐ πώποτ' εἶδον, οὔτε κερκώπην, γύναι, Οὐ κίτταν, οὐκ ἀηδόνα, οὐ τρυγόνα, οὐ τέττιγα. [— — — sou d' egō lalisteran ou pōpot' eidon, oute kerkōpēn, gynai, ou kittan, ouk aēdona, ou trygona, ou tettiga.] ¶

Ego, o mulier, te loquaciorem nunquam vidi, non cercopem, non picam, non lusciniam, non cicadam. Hesychio quoque Ἀχέτης est ὁ ἄῤῥην τέττιξ ὁ λαλί-στατος, cicada mas maxime loquax. Et, Servio si credimus, longaevus ille Tithonus ideo in cicadam fingitur esse versus, quia senes sunt natura loquaciores. Tzetzes denique de cicadarum loquacitate multa habet lib. 10. Historia 301. Haec III, 307 inprimis, in aestu eas esse loquaciores: et alas frustra comprimi, ut taceant: quia eo magis loquuntur. Ac proinde loquacibus vulgari proverbio dici, Τέττι-γα δόξεις τῶν πτερῶν τάχα κεκρατηκέναι, Cicadam videris alis fortasse cohibuisse. Iam, ut de locustis eadem jactari scias, lege haec Theocriti Idyllio 5.

I, woman, have never seen anyone more talkative than you, not a cercops, not a magpie, not a nightingale, not a cicada. For Hesychius too Ἀχέτης [Achetēs] is ὁ ἄῤῥην τέττιξ ὁ λαλίστατος [ho arrhēn tettix ho lalistatos], the male cicada, most talkative. And, if we believe Servius, the long-lived Tithonus is fabled to have been turned into a cicada because old men are by nature more talkative. Finally, Tzetzes has much on the talkativeness of cicadas in book 10, History 301. This III, 307 above all: that they are more talkative in the heat, and that their wings are pressed in vain to silence them, since they then chatter all the more. And therefore of talkative people the common proverb says, Τέττιγα δόξεις τῶν πτερῶν τάχα κεκρατηκέναι [Tettiga doxeis tōn pterōn tacha kekratēkenai], You will seem perhaps to have held a cicada by the wings. Now, that you may know the same is said of locusts, read these words of Theocritus, Idyll 5: ¶

— — — Καὶ ἀκρίδες ὧδε λαλεῦνται,

— — — Καὶ ἀκρίδες ὧδε λαλεῦνται, [— — — Kai akrides hōde laleuntai,] ¶

Etiam hic loquuntur locustae. Et Meleagri de locusta, Anthologiae lib. 3. cap. 24.

Here too the locusts talk. And Meleager on the locust, Anthology book 3, ch. 24: ¶

Ἐκκρέουσα φίλας ποσσὶ λάλους πτέρυγας,

Ἐκκρέουσα φίλας ποσσὶ λάλους πτέρυγας, [Ekkreousa philas possi lalous pterygas,] ¶

Pedibus concutiens charas loquaces alas: quasi locusta loquatur alis. Sed eo maxime pertinet ἀπὸ τῶν ἀκρίδων, a locustis formatum Verbum κατακριδεύειν, quod significat, loquacitate sua aliquem obtundere, quasi locustare dixeris. Hesychius, Κατακριδεύσει· καταλήσει (lege καταλαλήσει·) ἐπεὶ καὶ αἱ ἀκρίδες πολύφωνοί εἰσιν· Locustabit (si verbo datur venia), loquacitate obruet; quia locustae sunt loquacissimae. Sed, ut ad rem redeamus, cum de venturo locustarum populo dicit Propheta, Ante eum consumet ignis, et post eum exuret flamma: utrobique agi puto de summa siccitate, quae locustarum adventum praecesserit et sequuta sit. Ac proinde a Propheta dici, Ante eum (locustarum populum) consumet, etc. Terra Chanaan cum horto Edenis confertur; ut Aegyptus et Sodomaeorum ager ante cladem, a Deo immissam, Gen. XIII, 10. Ante eum, id est, ante adventum ejus: ut post eum, id est, post ejus discessum; ut Zachar. VII, 14. Terra desolata est post eos, id est, post eorum migrationem (2).

Striking with its feet its dear chattering wings: as though the locust spoke with its wings. But most to the point here is the verb κατακριδεύειν [katakrideuein], formed ἀπὸ τῶν ἀκρίδων [apo tōn akridōn], from locusts, which means to deafen someone with one's chatter, as if you were to say locustare [to locust]. Hesychius: Κατακριδεύσει· καταλήσει (read καταλαλήσει·) ἐπεὶ καὶ αἱ ἀκρίδες πολύφωνοί εἰσιν· [Katakrideusei; katalēsei (read katalalēsei;) epei kai hai akrides polyphōnoi eisin;] He will locust (if the word may be pardoned), he will overwhelm with chatter; for locusts are most loquacious. But, to return to the matter: when the Prophet says of the coming people of locusts, Before it fire shall consume, and behind it a flame shall burn, I think that in both places he is speaking of an extreme drought, which preceded and followed the arrival of the locusts. And therefore the Prophet says, Before it (the people of locusts) shall consume, etc. The land of Canaan is compared with the garden of Eden, as Egypt and the land of the Sodomites before the disaster sent by God, Gen 13:10. Before it, that is, before its arrival; as behind it, that is, after its departure; as in Zech 7:14. The land was desolate after them, that is, after their migration R2. ¶

Nec erit, quod ei evadat.] Nempe in terrae proventu. Omnia enim ita absument, ut nihil plane sit residuum.

And nothing shall escape it.] That is, of the produce of the land. For they will consume everything so that absolutely nothing is left. ¶

Hoc Hebraei referunt ad cursus aut volatus velocitatem, qua sint equis prope pares: nos insuper ad caput aut faciem, equinae non absimilem. A qua locustae ab Italis vocantur cavallette. Hinc Albertus lib. 26. Caput habent figura equi. Et Al-drovandus, Equinum quodammodo caput habent. Et multo ante Theodoretus in Ioelem, Εἴ τις ἀκριβῶς κατίδοι τὴν κεφαλὴν τὴν ἀκρίδος, σφόδρα τῇ τοῦ ἵππου III, 308 ἐοικυῖαν εὑρήσει· Locustae caput si quis attente consideret, id equi capiti reperiet valde simile. Verse 4. Its appearance is like the appearance of horses, and like horsemen so shall they run] As in Rev 9:7. And the shapes of the locusts were like horses prepared for battle. The Hebrews refer this to the speed of their running or flight, in which they are nearly equal to horses; we refer it in addition to the head or face, which is not unlike a horse's. From this the Italians call locusts cavallette. Hence Albertus, book 26: They have a head with the shape of a horse. And Aldrovandi [=Ulisse Aldrovandi, Ornithologia (1599–1603)]: They have a head somewhat like a horse's. And long before, Theodoret on Joel: Εἴ τις ἀκριβῶς κατίδοι τὴν κεφαλὴν τὴν ἀκρίδος, σφόδρα τῇ τοῦ ἵππου III, 308 ἐοικυῖαν εὑρήσει· [Ei tis akribōs katidoi tēn kephalēn tēn akridos, sphodra tē tou hippou eoikuian heurēsei;] If anyone considers the head of a locust attentively, he will find it very similar to the head of a horse. And Muhammiaddin Assarhuriensis, a poet of some renown among the Arabs: ¶

لها فخذ ابكر وساقا نعامة وقادمتا نسر وجوجو ضيغم ☼ ذنبها افاعي الارض وانعمت عليها col. 475 جياد الخيل بالراس والغم ☼

لها فخذ ابكر وساقا نعامة [lahā fakhdh abkur wa-sāqā naʿāma] وقادمتا نسر وجوجو ضيغم [wa-qādimatā nasr wa-jūjū ḍayghama] ☼ ذنبها افاعي الارض وانعمت عليها [dhanabuhā afāʿī al-arḍ wa-anʿamat ʿalayhā] col. 475 جياد الخيل بالراس والغم [jiyād al-khayl bi-l-rās wa-l-ghamm] ☼ ¶

Habent femur camelorum, crura struthionis, alas aquilae, pectus leonis. Cauda iis ut viperarum terrae: et decorans eas equorum species in capite et ore. Et alius Arabs anonymus apud Damirem, في جرادة خلق عشرة من جبابرة الحيوان مع ضعفها وجه فرس وعينا فيل وعنق ثور وقرنا ايل وصدر اسد وبطن عقرب وجناحا نسر وفخذا جمل ورجالا نعامة وذنب حية In locusta, quamlibet infirma, natura est decem magnorum animalium: 1. facies equi, 2. oculi elephantis, 3. collum tauri, 4. cornua cervi, 5. pectus leonis, 6. venter scorpionis, 7. alae aquilae, 8. femora cameli, 9. struthionis pedes, 10. cauda serpentis. In quo censu prima omnium ponitur equi facies. Pro بطن عقرب ventre scorpionis, lege بطن عقاب ventrem melanaeeti. Quia scorpio, qui locusta minor est, in magnis illis animalibus non potest habere locum. Sequitur quidem, وجناحا نسر et alae aquilae. Sed عقاب et نسر sunt aquilarum diversae species. Abarbanel anxie disquirit, quorsum Assyrii, de quibus Ioelem vult hic agere, cum equis aut equitibus conferantur. Et modo equorum nomine Centauros intelligit, quibus similes fuerint Chaldaeorum equites: modo illorum pedites veris equis, proceritate, viribus et celeritate similes fuisse contendit. Quae quam absurda sint, nemo non videt. Centaurorum hic neque vola, neque manus apparet: nec quisquam sanus crediderit, Chaldaeos a Propheta dici similes animali, quod nusquam est. Pedites autem cum equitibus conferri, novum est et inauditum. Denique quis Assyrios dixerit ut equites fuisse, cum re vera equites fuerint, iique magno numero? Quod Scriptura diserte docet Ezech. XXIII, 6. 12. 23. et XXVI, 7. 10. 11. Nahum. III, 2. Hab. I, 8. etc.

They have the thigh of camels, the legs of an ostrich, the wings of an eagle, the breast of a lion. Their tail is like that of the vipers of the earth; and the noble horses have graced them with their form in head and mouth. And another anonymous Arab in Damiri: في جرادة خلق عشرة من جبابرة الحيوان مع ضعفها وجه فرس وعينا فيل وعنق ثور وقرنا ايل وصدر اسد وبطن عقرب وجناحا نسر وفخذا جمل ورجالا نعامة وذنب حية [fī jarāda khalq ʿashara min jabābirat al-ḥayawān maʿa ḍaʿfihā wajh faras wa-ʿaynā fīl wa-ʿunuq thawr wa-qarnā ayyil wa-ṣadr asad wa-baṭn ʿaqrab wa-janāḥā nasr wa-fakhdhā jamal wa-rijlā naʿāma wa-dhanab ḥayya] In the locust, weak as it is, is the nature of ten great animals: 1. the face of a horse, 2. the eyes of an elephant, 3. the neck of a bull, 4. the horns of a stag, 5. the breast of a lion, 6. the belly of a scorpion, 7. the wings of an eagle, 8. the thighs of a camel, 9. the feet of an ostrich, 10. the tail of a serpent. In this list the face of a horse is put first of all. For بطن عقرب [baṭn ʿaqrab] the belly of a scorpion, read بطن عقاب [baṭn ʿuqāb] the belly of a black eagle. For the scorpion, which is smaller than the locust, can have no place among those great animals. True, there follows وجناحا نسر [wa-janāḥā nasr] and the wings of an eagle. But عقاب [ʿuqāb] and نسر [nasr] are different species of eagles. Abarbanel [=Isaac Abarbanel (1437–1508), commentary on the Bible] anxiously inquires why the Assyrians, whom he holds Joel to be treating here, are compared with horses or horsemen. At one moment he understands by horses the Centaurs, whom the horsemen of the Chaldeans resembled; at another he contends that their foot soldiers were like real horses in stature, strength and speed. Everyone sees how absurd this is. Of Centaurs there is here neither trace nor track; nor would any sane person believe that the Chaldeans are said by the Prophet to be like an animal that exists nowhere. And for foot soldiers to be compared with horsemen is novel and unheard of. Finally, who would say that the Assyrians were like horsemen, when they really were horsemen, and in great numbers? Scripture teaches this expressly in Ezek 23:6 12. 23. and XXVI, 7. 10. 11. Nahum. III, 2. Hab 1:8 etc. ¶

Verse 5. Like the noise of chariots they will leap upon the tops of the mountains, like the noise of a flame of fire devouring stubble, like a strong people set in array for battle] This too fits locusts exactly, which fly with a great noise, III, 309 fall upon the fields, and grind down the crops with their teeth. Hence John says of the locusts, Rev 9:9. The sound of their wings was as the sound of chariots, of many horses running to battle. Its face toward the eastern sea, that is, col. 481 the part turned toward the face of the world (or toward the east), into the Dead Sea, situated to the east of the city; and its rear toward the hinder sea, that is, the western part of the army into the western, or Mediterranean, sea. And its stench shall go up, its foulness (I say) shall go up. Its stench and its foulness are in Hebrew באשו and צחנהו; of these the latter occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew text, but is used by the Chaldee paraphrast at Ezek 23:20. For the Syrians too ܨܚܢܐ [צחנא] is the very same thing, with the guttural elided. Why then do the Greek translators here have Ἀναβήσεται ὁ βρόμος αὐτοῦ [Anabēsetai ho bromos autou], His noise shall go up? Hence Cyril: Ἐκεῖ τὸν βροῦχον καὶ τὴν ἀκρίδα διολέσαι φησι, δυσαχθῆ δὲ γενέσθαι τὸν βρόμον αὐτῶν [Ekei ton brouchon kai tēn akrida diolesai phēsi, dysachthē de genesthai ton bromon autōn]; There (in the Mediterranean sea) he says the bruchus and the locust perished, but their noise was grievous. I say that in both places there is a corruption, and that instead of βρόμον [bromon] one should read βρῶμον [brōmon], as Theodoret quite rightly has R5. For βρῶμος [brōmos] is stench, whence βρωμώδης [brōmōdēs], for fetid and rank, occurs frequently in Dioscorides and Athenaeus. But nothing is more frequent than this corruption. So also in Hesychius βρόμος [bromos] is rendered ὀσμὴ [osmē]. And in Dioscorides, book 5, sea water kept for a long time at length becomes ἄβρομος [abromos], that is, loses its rankness. And in Athenaeus, book 3, chapter 33: Τάριχος τέλειος, καὶ μὴ βρομώδης [Tarichos teleios, kai mē bromōdēs]; Salted fish perfectly cured, and not rank. That this is the very same as the Hebrew צחנה you may gather also from the fact that the Talmudists call small salted fish צחנתא, and the Arabs صحناة [ṣaḥnāh], namely from their rank smell. This, then, is what Joel says: that the locusts, plunged into the sea, would give off a foul and rank odour. Moreover, that they are often carried off by the wind from the lands they devastate and plunged into the seas, we have shown f) from Pliny, Jerome and Orosius, on the words of Moses Exod 10:19: God sent a west wind which, bringing the locusts, cast them into the Red Sea. As to what is added about the foul odour, it is proved by these words of Jerome about the locusts whose swarms, he writes, covered Judaea in his own time: When the wind rose, they were cast headlong into the first and the last sea, etc. And when the shores of both seas were filled with heaps of dead locusts which the waters had vomited up, III, 318 their putrefaction and stench were so noxious that it even corrupted the air, and a pestilence arose among beasts as well as men. And so too what Augustine g), Orosius h), Freculphus i), etc. relate, after Julius Obsequens and other older writers: that in the consulship of M. Plautius and M. Fulvius Flaccus, when the winds had cast an immense multitude of locusts into the African sea and the waves had driven them onto the shores, their wasting and putrefied mass gave off so pestilential a vapour that in Numidia alone eight hundred thousand people are reported to have perished, and on the coast around Carthage and Utica more than two hundred thousand; and at Utica itself thirty thousand soldiers, who had been stationed there to garrison all Africa, were destroyed and wiped out. Sigebert too, in the place cited above, relates that a multitude of locusts which had devastated Gaul, carried off by the blast of the winds and drowned in the British sea, was thrown back onto the shore by the backwash of the Ocean, and from its stench produced a corruption of the air, from which arose a great famine and a pestilence that carried off a third of mankind. More of this kind could be adduced, were these not more than sufficient R6. ¶

Versu vigesimo quinto, Et reddam vobis annos, quos comederunt arbe, jelek, chasil et gazam, exercitus meus magnus, quem in vos immiseram.] Versibus vigesimo secundo, vigesimo tertio et vigesimo quarto, promittit Propheta, Dei nomine, contraria omnia minis, quae praecesserant: fore nimirum, ut herbescant deserti pascua, et arbores fructum suum proferant, ficusque et vitis vim suam edant, pluvia tam matura, quam sera, primo quoque tempore, a Deo abunde demissa: et impleantur frumento areae, mustoque et oleo redundent lacus torcularium; ut sic miseris Iudaeis resti- col. 482 tuantur anni, id est, annui proventus, quos locustarum species quatuor absumpserant: non singulae singulis annis, ut monuimus, sed omnes eodem tempore sibi mutuo succedentes. Quae pestis in quot annos Chananaeam vexaverit, plane nescimus. Tantum Iudaeos scimus, et graviter, et in plures annos his locustis fuisse afflictos, Deo sic animadvertente in contumaces et nominis sui contemptores. Unde est, quod locustas vocat exercitum suum magnum. Quomodo etiam Arabes. Sic in Damire Lex citatur, tanquam a Mahumete sancita et promulgata, لا تقتلوا الجراد فانها جند III, 319 الله الاعظم Locustas non occidetis; quia sunt exercitus Dei maximi. Ut taceam, quod fabulatur Ibn-Omar, inter ejusdem Pseudo-Prophetae manus cecidisse locustas cum hac inscriptione in alis, نحن جند الله الاكبر Nos sumus exercitus Dei maximi.

Verse 25: And I will restore to you the years that the arbe, jelek, chasil and gazam have eaten, my great army, which I sent among you.] In verses 22, 23 and 24 the Prophet, in God's name, promises the exact opposite of all the threats that had gone before: namely, that the pastures of the wilderness will grow green, the trees will bear their fruit, and the fig tree and the vine will yield their strength, since God will send down abundant rain, both early and late, at the proper time; and the threshing floors will be filled with grain, and the vats of the winepresses will overflow with new wine and oil. Thus the years will be restored col. 482 to the wretched Jews, that is, the yearly produce which the four kinds of locusts had consumed: not one kind in each year, as we have pointed out, but all at the same time, succeeding one another. For how many years this plague afflicted Canaan we simply do not know. We know only that the Jews were afflicted by these locusts both severely and for several years, God thus punishing the stubborn and those who despised his name. That is why he calls the locusts his great army. So do the Arabs as well. Thus in Damir a law is cited as enacted and promulgated by Muhammad: لا تقتلوا الجراد فانها جند III, 319 الله الاعظم [lā taqtulū al-jarād fa-innahā jund Allāh al-aʿẓam] You shall not kill locusts, for they are the army of the most great God. Not to mention the tale told by Ibn-Omar, that locusts fell into the hands of that same pseudo-Prophet with this inscription on their wings: نحن جند الله الاكبر [naḥnu jund Allāh al-akbar] We are the army of the most great God. ¶

Those who twist into a mystical sense what has so far been adduced from Joel about the locusts make a great show of objecting that nothing is found in the histories of Kings and Chronicles about so severe an invasion of locusts into Judaea, or about the famine that followed it. Jerome: For neither the history of Kings nor of Chronicles relates it; and certainly, had it happened, Scripture would never have been silent, just as we read of the famine of three years and six months under Elias. Abarbanel on Joel, Question 4: If the famine and the locusts were so severe, whether in the days of Joram, son of Ahab, and in the days of Elisha, or in the time of Manasseh, when, according to the Seder Olam, Joel, Nahum and Habakkuk are said to have prophesied, the question is why no mention of this is made in the books of Kings. Or, if that very point is disputed, why did Jeremiah not mention it, who reports many things of no great importance? But neither in the books of Chronicles, where things are narrated more fully, does a single word about this occur, which is very remarkable. I reply that the sacred writers did not set themselves the task of reporting how often God afflicted Judaea with famine. Indeed, if the whole of Scripture is gone through from beginning to end, it will be found that the history of a famine is nowhere recounted except in passing, as an appendix, so to speak, to some other narrative. Thus in Gen 12:10 and XXVI, 1. a severe famine in the land of Canaan is reported under Abraham and Isaac, so that we might know why the former went to Egypt and the latter to Palestine [Philistia, Gen 26:1]. And in the forty-first chapter of Genesis Moses describes the seven-year famine that Joseph had foretold in order to pave the way for the marvellous histories of Joseph and his brothers, and of the harsh servitude of the Israelites in Egypt, from which God delivered them with a strong hand and an outstretched arm. In the whole book of Judges there is no history of a famine. Yet at the beginning of the book of Ruth it is told that Elimelech, a Bethlehemite, withdrew to the land of Moab on account of a famine that raged in Judaea under the judges. Likewise, because it concerned us to know how, once the family of Saul had been cut off, the kingdom of David was established, the second book of Samuel, chapter 21, deals with a three-year famine that could not be stopped except by crucifying seven men of Saul's line. Again, the histories of famine under the prophets Elias and Elisha, 1 Reg. XVIII, 2. and 2 Reg. IV, 38. and VIII, 1., are reported because of the miracles they performed on that account. Finally, Luke touches on the famine that occurred under Claudius in a single brief word, only to show that it had been foretold by Agabus, and that it gave the Christians of Antioch occasion to send alms III, 320 to their brethren in Jerusalem. Apart from those cited, no other history of a famine is to be found in either Testament. Therefore, since in a history of four thousand years so little is reported on this subject, and that only in passing and for the sake of something else, why should col. 483 we be surprised that the history of the famine which Joel had foretold has been passed over? ¶
