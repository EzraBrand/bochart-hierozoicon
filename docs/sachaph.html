III, 1 Chapter XVIII. col. 263 ¶

Probatur, שחף sachaph esse Larum, aut Cepphum, unde κεπφωθεὶς Prov. VII, 22.

It is proved that שחף sachaph is the gull or the cepphus, whence κεπφωθεὶς [kepphōtheis] Prov. VII, 22. ¶

Ut struthio vasta est avis, ita nos diu detinuit. Iam in iis, quae sequentur, rem peragemus paucioribus. Et primo se offert שחף sachaph, a Syro prorsus omissus; a Chaldaeis שחפא sachpha redditus; ab Arabibus سَاف saph; a Samaritano ࠔࠅࠐࠀ sopha: vocibus aeque ignotis. Itaque hinc nullum subsidium. Graeci, Hieronymus, Munsterus, Castalio explicant larum: Iunius aesalonem, tanquam ex R. Iuda: Deodatius la folica, id est, fulicam, in Italica versione; sed idem in Gallica, la movette, id est, larum: Genevenses et Angli, cuculum; Arias phthiticam avem, tanquam a phthisi sumto nomine. Nempe Hebraeorum plerique observant, hujus avis nomen שחף sachaph alludere ad שחפת sachepheth, morbi speciem, Levit. XXVI, 16. et Deut. XXVIII, 22. Sive ipsa avis huic morbo sit obnoxia, aut sua carne vescentibus illum ingeneret. Sed neque id satis notum, quod morbi genus sit שחפת sachepheth. Rabbi Selomo definit חולי שמשחפת את הבשר אנפו״ליש בלע״ז דומה לנפוח שהוקלה כפיחהו ומראית פניו זעיפה, morbum, qui carnem inflat. Gallice ampullas dixeris, quo qui laborat, similis est viro, qui buccam inflat leviter, et speciem habeat irati. Et David de Pomis, הוא חולי אשר יוליד אבעבועות כמו תפוחים לפי רש״י, Morbus est, pustulas gignens ut poma, ut ait Rabbi Selomo Iarchi. Quamvis de pomis R. Selomo nihil habeat. Sed pro דומה לנפוח, Pomarius videtur in Selomone legisse דומה לתפוח, pomo similis: quam lectionem sequentia palam refellunt. Utut sit, Rabbi Ionas hunc eundem morbum multo aliter definit, ut docent haec Kimchii in Lexico: פירשו רבי יונה בל׳שון ערבי סחא״ף והוא החולי שהגוף מתנונה בו, R. Ionas explicat morbum Arabice dictum sochaph; quo sit, ut corpus fit מתנונה mathnuna, id est, מתחלשת ומתכחשת ומתדלדלת, debilitatum, et emaciatum, et attenuatum. Ita enim in Aruch redditur vox מתנונה mathnuna, sumta ex Talmudico tractatu Sota cap. 4. fol. 26 a.

As the ostrich is a huge bird, so it has detained us long. Now in what follows we shall settle matters more briefly. And first presents itself שחף sachaph, entirely omitted by the Syrian; rendered by the Chaldeans שחפא sachpha; by the Arabs سَاف [sāf] saph; by the Samaritan ࠔࠅࠐࠀ [שופא] sopha: words equally unknown. So no help comes from here. The Greeks, Jerome, Munster and Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] explain it as the gull; Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] as the aesalon [merlin], as if on the authority of R. Judah; Diodati as la folica, that is, the fulica [coot], in the Italian version, but the same man in the French as la movette, that is, the gull; the Genevans and the English as the cuckoo; Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] as the phthisic bird, as if the name were taken from phthisis. For most of the Hebrews observe that this bird's name שחף sachaph alludes to שחפת sachepheth, a kind of disease, Lev 26:16 and Deut. XXVIII, 22, whether because the bird itself is subject to this disease, or because it produces it in those who eat its flesh. But it is not well enough known either what kind of disease שחפת sachepheth is. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] defines it: חולי שמשחפת את הבשר אנפו״ליש בלע״ז דומה לנפוח שהוקלה כפיחהו ומראית פניו זעיפה, a disease that inflates the flesh; in French you would call them blisters; one who suffers from it is like a man who puffs his cheek slightly, and has the look of an angry man. And David de Pomis [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary]: הוא חולי אשר יוליד אבעבועות כמו תפוחים לפי רש״י, It is a disease producing pustules like apples, as R. Selomo Jarchi [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac), called Jarchi by the Christian Hebraists] says. Yet R. Selomo says nothing about apples. But instead of דומה לנפוח, Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] seems to have read in R. Selomo דומה לתפוח, like an apple: a reading which what follows plainly refutes. However that may be, R. Jonah [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim] defines this same disease very differently, as these words of Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon show: פירשו רבי יונה בל׳שון ערבי סחא״ף והוא החולי שהגוף מתנונה בו, R. Jonah explains it as the disease called in Arabic sochaph, through which the body becomes מתנונה mathnuna, that is, מתחלשת ומתכחשת ומתדלדלת, weakened, and emaciated, and wasted. For so the word מתנונה mathnuna is rendered in the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], taken from the Talmudic tractate Sota, ch. 4, fol. 26a ¶

III, 2 Hinc idem Pomarius, qui supra אבל לפי קמחי הוא חולי שיוליד דקות הגוף ואבודו, Sed juxta Kimchium morbus est attenuationem corporis gignens, et perditionem ejus. Et Aquinas, חולי מה שגורם כיחוש ודלות לגוף עד שמאבדו והוא מן קדחא, Morbus est, qui causatur maciem, et corporis attenuationem, donec illud plane consumat, idque fit ex febri. Quibus verbis phthisim, seu tabem describi nemo non videt. Et vero سحاف sochaph Arabum in Giauhari col. 264 est idem, quod سل sil. Qua voce phthisim significari docet praeter alios Avicenna in tractatu De Phthisi lib. 3. Fen. 10. tract. 4. cap. 18. Porro Hebraeum שחפת sachepheth, et Arabicum سحاف sochaph easdem habent Radicales, nisi quod Arabes de more Scin Hebraeum in Sin mutant. Omnino igitur utraque voce idem morbus significatur. Proinde in Mose, pro שחפת, habent Arabes السل phthisim, vel حمي السل, febrim phthisicam. Graecos quoque suspicor ἀτροφίαν reddidisse, quae est inter phthisis species. Quamvis ἀπορίαν exhibeant nostra exemplaria jam inde a multis saeculis. Ita enim legit Hieronymus, ac proinde שחפת egestatem interpretatur. Utut sit, שחפת non esse ἀπορίαν, egestatem, ex eo patet, quod in utroque loco Legis censetur inter morborum species. Itaque שחפת est morbus, isque adeo, quem diximus; nempe phthisis, seu tabes. Id confirmatur ex Talmudicis, apud quos שחוף tabidum sonat, ut probat Cl. Buxtorfius ex Sota fol. 26. b. Sed et Hebraeorum aliis שחף, in Kal est tabere, in Niphal, tabidum fieri; in Piel, tabefacere: ut ab eodem affirmatur. Verisimile est igitur, שחף sachaph avem esse, a tabe dictam. Et Hieronymum, post Graecos, larum recte reddidisse (id est movette Gall.) de qua ave Bellonius libri tertii capite decimo tertio, Nulla est avis molis ratione tam levis. Cum enim sit gallina crassior, non habet tantum carnis, quantum coturnix. Id in ceppho maxime deprehenditur, lari specie levissima, quaeque plumae plurimum, sed carnis habet minimum. Aristophanis Scholiastes in Pace, Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος, etc. Ἐκ δὲ τῆς θαλάττης ζῆν αὐτὸν λέγουσιν, ἐσθίοντα τὸν ἀφρόν· εἶναι δὲ αὐτὸν πολὺν μὲν ἐν τοῖς πτεροῖς, ὀλίγον δὲ ἐν τοῖς κρέασι. Cepphus est animal stolidum, etc. Eum autem ajunt ex mari vivere, spumam edentem, et plumarum quidem multum habere, sed parum carnium. Hinc Hesychius, Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου κουφοτάτου, περὶ τὴν θάλασσαν διατρίβον-τος, ὃ εὐχερῶς ὑπὸ ἀνέμου μετάγεται. Cepphus, species avis levissimae, circa mare versari solitae, quae a vento facile abducitur. Et Suidas, Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου ὁ λεγόμενος λάρος· ἔστι δὲ κοῦφον, καὶ ἐπιπλέον τοῖς κύμασιν· Cepphus, avis species, quae et larus vocatur: est autem levis, et fluctibus supernatat. Quod alio nomine larum vocari dicit, habet ex Scholiaste Aristophanis in Plutum, cujus de ceppho verba sunt: Καλεῖται δὲ κοινῶς λάρος. Vulgo autem vocatur larus. Itaque junguntur ut synonyma in Proverbio Κέπφος λάρος apud Erasmum et alios. Et in Hesychio Κὴξ Apioni est ὁ λάρος, at alii κέπφον reddunt, III, 3 alii etiam ἐφυϊάν· lege αἰθυϊὰν, id est, mergum. Tamen larum et cepphum differre colligas ex his Aristotelis Historiae libri octavi capite tertio, Περὶ δὲ τὴν θάλατταν καὶ ἀλκύων, καὶ κήρυλος, etc. Ἔτι δὲ ὁ λάρος ὁ λευ-κὸς, καὶ κέπφος, καὶ χαράδριος· At circa mare sunt et alcyon, et cerylus, etc. Larus albus etiam, et cepphus, et charadrius. Cepphus igitur et larus non plane idem sunt, sed aves valde similes. Unde Nicandri Scholiastes in Alexipharmaca, Ὁ δὲ κέπφος θαλάσσιόν ἐστι ὄρνεον παραπλήσιον λάρῳ. Gesnerus putat, esse aves congeneres, aut cepphum lari speciem. Quoquo modo res se ha-beat, nomen שחף tam laro, quam ceppho apprime convenit, cum avis utraque describatur fere carnis expers, ac si sit tabe consumta (1).

III, 2 Hence the same Pomarius, who above wrote אבל לפי קמחי הוא חולי שיוליד דקות הגוף ואבודו, But according to Kimchi it is a disease producing wasting of the body and its destruction. And Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: חולי מה שגורם כיחוש ודלות לגוף עד שמאבדו והוא מן קדחא, It is a disease which causes leanness and wasting of the body, until it consumes it entirely; and this comes from fever. Everyone can see that these words describe phthisis, or consumption. And indeed the Arabic سحاف [suḥāf] sochaph in Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] col. 264 is the same as سل [sill] sil. That this word signifies phthisis is taught, among others, by Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] in the treatise On Phthisis, book 3, Fen 10, tract. 4, ch. 18. Moreover, the Hebrew שחפת sachepheth and the Arabic سحاف [suḥāf] sochaph have the same radicals, except that the Arabs, as is their custom, change the Hebrew Shin into Sin. So the two words certainly signify the same disease. Accordingly, in Moses, for שחפת the Arabs have السل [al-sill] phthisis, or حمي السل [ḥummā al-sill], phthisical fever. I also suspect that the Greeks rendered it ἀτροφίαν [atrophian], which is among the species of phthisis, although our copies have shown ἀπορίαν [aporian] for many centuries now. For so Jerome read, and accordingly he interprets שחפת as poverty. However that may be, that שחפת is not ἀπορίαν [aporian], poverty, is clear from the fact that in both passages of the Law it is counted among the kinds of diseases. Therefore שחפת is a disease, and indeed the one we have named: namely phthisis, or consumption. This is confirmed from the Talmudists, among whom שחוף means consumptive, as the distinguished Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] proves from Sota fol. 26b Moreover, among other Hebrew writers שחף in Kal is to waste away, in Niphal to become consumptive, in Piel to cause to waste away, as the same author affirms. It is therefore probable that שחף sachaph is a bird named from wasting, and that Jerome, following the Greeks, rightly rendered it larus [gull] (that is, French movette). Of this bird Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], book 3, chapter 13, says: No bird is so light in proportion to its bulk. For though it is bigger than a hen, it has not as much flesh as a quail. This is found above all in the cepphus, a very light species of gull, which has a great deal of plumage but very little flesh. The Scholiast on Aristophanes, on the Peace: Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος, etc. Ἐκ δὲ τῆς θαλάττης ζῆν αὐτὸν λέγουσιν, ἐσθίοντα τὸν ἀφρόν· εἶναι δὲ αὐτὸν πολὺν μὲν ἐν τοῖς πτεροῖς, ὀλίγον δὲ ἐν τοῖς κρέασι [Euēthes zōon ho kepphos, etc. Ek de tēs thalattēs zēn auton legousin, esthionta ton aphron; einai de auton polyn men en tois pterois, oligon de en tois kreasi]. The cepphus is a stupid animal, etc. They say that it lives from the sea, eating the foam, and that it has much plumage but little flesh. Hence Hesychius: Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου κουφοτάτου, περὶ τὴν θάλασσαν διατρίβοντος, ὃ εὐχερῶς ὑπὸ ἀνέμου μετάγεται [Kepphos; eidos orneou kouphotatou, peri tēn thalassan diatribontos, ho eucherōs hypo anemou metagetai]. Cepphus: a species of very light bird, accustomed to spend its time about the sea, which is easily carried off by the wind. And Suidas: Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου ὁ λεγόμενος λάρος· ἔστι δὲ κοῦφον, καὶ ἐπιπλέον τοῖς κύμασιν [Kepphos; eidos orneou ho legomenos laros; esti de kouphon, kai epipleon tois kymasin]; Cepphus: a species of bird, which is also called larus; it is light, and floats on the waves. His statement that it is called by another name larus comes from the Scholiast on Aristophanes, on the Plutus, whose words on the cepphus are: Καλεῖται δὲ κοινῶς λάρος [Kaleitai de koinōs laros]. It is commonly called larus. Accordingly they are joined as synonyms in the proverb Κέπφος λάρος [Kepphos laros] in Erasmus [=Desiderius Erasmus, Adagia] and others. And in Hesychius, Κὴξ [Kēx] for Apion is ὁ λάρος [ho laros], but others render it κέπφον [kephon], III, 3 and others again ἐφυϊάν [ephyian]; read αἰθυϊὰν [aithyian], that is, diver. Yet you may gather that the gull and the cepphus differ from these words of Aristotle, History, book 8, chapter 3: Περὶ δὲ τὴν θάλατταν καὶ ἀλκύων, καὶ κήρυλος, etc. Ἔτι δὲ ὁ λάρος ὁ λευκὸς, καὶ κέπφος, καὶ χαράδριος [Peri de tēn thalattan kai alkyōn, kai kērylos, etc. Eti de ho laros ho leukos, kai kepphos, kai charadrios]; But about the sea there are also the alcyon and the cerylus, etc. Also the white gull, and the cepphus, and the charadrius. The cepphus and the gull, then, are not entirely the same, but very similar birds. Hence the Scholiast on Nicander, on the Alexipharmaca: Ὁ δὲ κέπφος θαλάσσιόν ἐστι ὄρνεον παραπλήσιον λάρῳ [Ho de kepphos thalassion esti orneon paraplēsion larō]. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] thinks they are birds of the same genus, or that the cepphus is a species of gull. However the matter stands, the name שחף suits both the gull and the cepphus exceedingly well, since both birds are described as almost devoid of flesh, as if consumed by wasting R1. ¶

A ceppho factum verbum κεπφοῦσθαι non solum efferri significat, et le-viter circumagi cepphi instar ad omnem ventum, sed et facile decipi et inescari. Sic Prov. VII, 22. de incauto juvene, quem lenociniis suis demulsit adultera: Ὁ δὲ ἐπηκολούθησεν αὐτῇ κεπφωθείς· Eam autem ille sequitur, cepphi instar, facile actus, aut facile deceptus. Hesychius, Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρθεὶς, ἀπατη- col. 265 θείς· Elatus, deceptus. Et rursus post Κέφαλον· Κεπφωθεὶς, καταγελα-σθείς· εἴρηται δὲ ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων· Derisus: Dicitur autem de iis, qui, bruti instar, stulte ducuntur. Suidas, Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρ-θεὶς, ἐρεθισθεὶς, ὀξέως ἐλαυνόμενος· Elatus, concitatus, celeriter actus. Quae sunt ejusdem loci varia interpretamenta. Sed ἀπατηθεὶς maxime placet. 1. Quia quo loco diximus κεπφωθεὶς est pro Hebraeo פתאים, pro quo Graeci legerunt פתאים, id est, simplices, εὐαπάτητοι. Fit enim a verbo פתה, quod plerumque reddunt ἀπατᾷν. 2. Quia cepphus est avis deceptu facilis, quamque, spuma oblata, pueri nullo negotio ad se alliciunt, atque injecta manu capiunt. Unde est, quod κέπφος a Graecis pro stulto passim usurpatur. Ita proverbium, Κέπφος λάρος, vecordem ferit, qui cujusvis rei cupiditate deceptus capitur. Et apud Aristophanem in Pace,

The verb κεπφοῦσθαι [kepphousthai], formed from cepphus, means not only to be carried away and to be lightly driven about by every wind like a cepphus, but also to be easily deceived and lured with bait. So in Prov. VII, 22, of the heedless young man whom the adulteress has charmed with her enticements: Ὁ δὲ ἐπηκολούθησεν αὐτῇ κεπφωθείς [Ho de epēkolouthēsen autē kepphōtheis]; But he follows her, like a cepphus, easily driven, or easily deceived. Hesychius: Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρθεὶς, ἀπατηθείς [Kepphōtheis; epartheis, apatētheis]; col. 265 Carried away, deceived. And again, after Κέφαλον [Kephalon]: Κεπφωθεὶς, καταγελασθείς· εἴρηται δὲ ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων [Kepphōtheis, katagelastheis; eirētai de epi tōn alogou dikēn anoētōs synepagomenōn]; Mocked: it is said of those who, like a brute beast, are foolishly led along. Suidas: Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρθεὶς, ἐρεθισθεὶς, ὀξέως ἐλαυνόμενος [Kepphōtheis; epartheis, erethistheis, oxeōs elaunomenos]; Carried away, stirred up, swiftly driven. These are various interpretations of the same passage. But ἀπατηθεὶς [apatētheis] pleases me most. 1. Because in the passage we have cited κεπφωθεὶς [kepphōtheis] stands for the Hebrew פתאים, for which the Greeks read פתאים, that is, simple ones, εὐαπάτητοι [euapatētoi]. For it comes from the verb פתה, which they usually render ἀπατᾷν [apatan]. 2. Because the cepphus is a bird easily deceived, which boys, by offering it foam, draw to themselves without any trouble and catch by laying hands on it. Hence κέπφος [kepphos] is commonly used by the Greeks for a fool. Thus the proverb Κέπφος λάρος [Kepphos laros] strikes at the senseless man who is deceived by desire for anything and is caught. And in Aristophanes, in the Peace, ¶

Καὶ κέπφοι τρήρωνες ἀλωπεκίδεσσι πέπεισθε, III, 4 Et cepphi, et columbae (id est, stulti et simplices) vulpeculis fiditis. Et rursus in Pluto, Ὦ κέπφε, O cepphe, id est, O faute. Proinde ad priorem locum Scholiastes, Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος, Fatuum est animal cepphus. Et ad posteriorem, Ὄρνεον γὰρ ἄφρον, ὅπερ φιλεῖ ἀφρὸν θαλάττιον ἐσθίειν. Τοῦτο βουλό-μενοι οἱ τῶν ἁλιέων παῖδες χειρώσασθαι, ῥίπτουσι τὸ πρῶτον πόῤῥωθεν ἀφρὸν, εἶτα ἐγγύτατα, εἶτα φέρουσιν τοῦτο ἐν ταῖς χερσὶν, καὶ οὕτω κατὰ μικρὸν ἀπατῶντες, εὐχερῶς ἀγρεύουσι τοῦτο ἐμπεσὸν εἰς τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῶν· εἴλη-πται οὖν εἰς παροιμίαν ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλογίστων ἀνδρῶν, καὶ ἀνοήτων. Stulta enim est avis, quae marinae spumae esu delectatur. Cum igitur hanc capere volunt piscatorum filii, primo spumam eminus jaciunt, deinde propius propiusque, postea manibus ferunt, atque ita paullatim inescantes, in manus suas incidentem facile capiunt. Cessit itaque in proverbium in vecordes homines, atque dementes. Quod ipsum de hac ave tradiderant antiquiores. Sic Philosophus Historiae animalium libri noni capite trigesimo quinto, Οἱ δὲ κέπφοι ἁλίσκονται τῷ ἀφρῷ, κόπτου-σι γὰρ αὐτόν· διὸ προσραίνοντες θηρεύουσιν. Cepphi autem spuma capiuntur; eam enim appetunt: proinde spuma injecta eos venantur. Et Nicander Alexipharmacωn versu 165.

Καὶ κέπφοι τρήρωνες ἀλωπεκίδεσσι πέπεισθε [Kai kepphoi trērōnes alōpekidessi pepeisthe], III, 4 And you cepphi and doves (that is, fools and simpletons) trust little foxes. And again in the Plutus, Ὦ κέπφε [Ō kepphe], O cepphus, that is, O fool. Accordingly, on the former passage the Scholiast says: Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος [Euēthes zōon ho kepphos], The cepphus is a foolish animal. And on the latter: Ὄρνεον γὰρ ἄφρον, ὅπερ φιλεῖ ἀφρὸν θαλάττιον ἐσθίειν. Τοῦτο βουλόμενοι οἱ τῶν ἁλιέων παῖδες χειρώσασθαι, ῥίπτουσι τὸ πρῶτον πόῤῥωθεν ἀφρὸν, εἶτα ἐγγύτατα, εἶτα φέρουσιν τοῦτο ἐν ταῖς χερσὶν, καὶ οὕτω κατὰ μικρὸν ἀπατῶντες, εὐχερῶς ἀγρεύουσι τοῦτο ἐμπεσὸν εἰς τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῶν· εἴληπται οὖν εἰς παροιμίαν ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλογίστων ἀνδρῶν, καὶ ἀνοήτων [Orneon gar aphron, hoper philei aphron thalattion esthiein. Touto boulomenoi hoi tōn halieōn paides cheirōsasthai, rhiptousi to prōton porrhōthen aphron, eita engytata, eita pherousin touto en tais chersin, kai houtō kata mikron apatōntes, eucherōs agreuousi touto empeson eis tas cheiras autōn; eilēptai oun eis paroimian epi tōn alogistōn andrōn, kai anoētōn]. For it is a foolish bird, which delights in eating sea foam. So when the fishermen's sons wish to catch it, they first throw foam from a distance, then nearer and nearer, afterwards they carry it in their hands, and so, luring it little by little, they easily catch it as it falls into their hands. Hence it has become a proverb for senseless and witless men. The older writers had reported this very thing about this bird. So the Philosopher, History of Animals, book 9, chapter 35: Οἱ δὲ κέπφοι ἁλίσκονται τῷ ἀφρῷ, κόπτουσι γὰρ αὐτόν· διὸ προσραίνοντες θηρεύουσιν [Hoi de kepphoi haliskontai tō aphrō, koptousi gar auton; dio prosrainontes thēreuousin]. The cepphi are caught with foam, for they go after it; therefore they hunt them by throwing foam at them. And Nicander, Alexipharmaca, verse 165: ¶

Ἀφρὸν ἐπεγκεράσοιο θοοῦ δορπήϊα κέπφου.
Τῷ γὰρ δὴ ζωήν τε σαοῖ, καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖ.
Εὖτε δόλοις νήχοντα κακοφθόρα τέκν᾽ ἁλιήων
Οἰωνὸν χραίνωσιν. ὁ δ᾽ ἐς χέρας ἔμπεσε παίδων,
Θηρεύων ἀφροῖο νέην κλύδα λευκαίνουσαν.

Ἀφρὸν ἐπεγκεράσοιο θοοῦ δορπήϊα κέπφου. Τῷ γὰρ δὴ ζωήν τε σαοῖ, καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖ. Εὖτε δόλοις νήχοντα κακοφθόρα τέκν᾽ ἁλιήων Οἰωνὸν χραίνωσιν. ὁ δ᾽ ἐς χέρας ἔμπεσε παίδων, Θηρεύων ἀφροῖο νέην κλύδα λευκαίνουσαν. [Aphron epenkerasoio thoou dorpēia kepphou. Tō gar dē zōēn te saoi, kai potmon epispei. Eute dolois nēchonta kakophthora tekn᾽ haliēōn Oiōnon chrainōsin. ho d᾽ es cheras empese paidōn, Thēreuōn aphroio neēn klyda leukainousan.] ¶

Spumam admisce citi cepphi pabulum. Illo enim et vitam servat, et fatum accersit: cum natantem avem ea dolose inquinant perniciosi piscatorum filii. Is autem in puerorum manus incidit, persequens spumae recentem adspersionem albicantem. Plura vide apud Nicandri Scholiastem, Hesychium, Suidam, Etymologum (2) et alios, quos exscribere jam non vacat. Neque vero est opus, cum, quae jam dicta sunt, nude repetant. Tantum monebo, in his Hesychii et Suidae Κεπφωθεὶς· ἐπαρθεὶς, pro ἐπαρθεὶς legendum esse ἐπαχθεὶς; Ipso enim Hesychio auctore Κεπφωθεὶς dicitur ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων de iis, qui bruti ad instar stulte ducuntur.

Mix in foam, the food of the swift cepphus. For by it the bird both preserves its life and brings on its doom, when the destructive sons of fishermen craftily spatter the swimming bird with it. And it falls into the boys' hands, chasing the fresh white spray of foam. See more in the Scholiast on Nicander, Hesychius, Suidas, the Etymologicum R2 and others, whom I have no time to copy out now. Nor is there need, since they merely repeat what has already been said. I will only note that in these words of Hesychius and Suidas, Κεπφωθεὶς· ἐπαρθεὶς [Kepphōtheis; epartheis], one should read ἐπαχθεὶς [epachtheis] instead of ἐπαρθεὶς [epartheis]. For on Hesychius's own authority Κεπφωθεὶς [Kepphōtheis] is said ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων [epi tōn alogou dikēn anoētōs synepagomenōn], of those who are foolishly led along like a brute beast. ¶
