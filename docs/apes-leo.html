III, 352 Chapter X. col. 502 ¶

Apum nomen et origo. Quomodo repertae legantur in Leonis cadavere. Easdem audire, aeris tinnitu terreri, fumo pelli, et fortitudine esse insignes in varia Scripturae loca.

The name and origin of bees. How they are read to have been found in the lion's carcass. That they hear, are frightened by the ringing of brass, are driven out by smoke, and are remarkable for their bravery, on various passages of Scripture. ¶

Hebraeum דבר non solum loqui significat, sed et ordinare, regere, ducere, deducere, ex Chaldaeorum usu. Exempla vide 1 Sam. VIII, 21. Psal. XLVII, 4. et XVIII, 48. Inde apis Hebraice דברה debora; Chaldaice דברא dabbara, dicitur a mirabili ductu et ordine: ut Arabice دبر dabron est examen apum (1). Politicum enim est hoc animalculum, reges habens, et populos, et III, 353 urbes, et praetoria. De quibus e Graecis potissimum consulendi, Aristoteles, Aelianus, et scriptores Geoponicῶν; ut, e Romanis, Varro, Virgilius, Colu- mella, Plinius; et ex Arabibus, Damir et Alkazuinius: quorum scrinia in argumento tam trito compilare, mihi non vacat.

The Hebrew דבר means not only to speak but also to order, to rule, to lead, to lead down, following the usage of the Chaldeans. For examples see 1 Sam 8:21. Ps 47:4 and XVIII, 48. Hence the bee is called in Hebrew דברה debora, in Chaldee דברא dabbara, from its wonderful guidance and order; just as in Arabic دبر [dbr] dabron is a swarm of bees R1. For this little creature is a political one, having kings, peoples, III, 353 cities and palaces. On these, among the Greeks, the chief authorities to consult are Aristotle, Aelian and the writers of the Geoponica; among the Romans, Varro, Virgil, Columella, Pliny; and among the Arabs, Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]. I have no leisure to plunder their book-chests on so well-worn a subject. ¶

That bees are generated not only from bees but also from an ox is a constant report among writers. III, 355 Since the ancients report these things about the origin of bees with such agreement, it may seem surprising that we read, in Judges, chapter 14, verse 8, that Samson found עדת דברים, a swarm of bees, in the carcass of the lion. All the more so, since bees are said to abstain from flesh and to flee strong smells altogether. III, 356 His, quae in sacrum textum possunt objici, duo habeo, quae respondeam: Primo, apes in leonis corpore fuisse repertas, non tamen ibi natas. Neque enim id auctor asserit. Proinde falluntur, qui hinc adstruunt apum genus λεοντογενῶν, cum, apes a leone nasci, in veterum scriptis nusquam legatur. Nec audiendus Muffetus, Anglus, qui in Insectorum Theatro alias apes scribit esse λεοντογενεῖς, alias ταυρογενεῖς· Optimas nimirum, et vere nobiles, ex leone habere originem, harumque principes et reges ex ejus cerebro stemma deducere. Unde fieri, quod, tantulae cum sint, ingentissima nonnunquam animalia aggrediantur, et leonina ferocia praeditae nihil timeant. Quae si vera essent, in locis, quae leonibus scatent, apes essent acerbissimae. At rem plane secus se habere, nullo negotio probavero. In Aethiopia siquidem, ubi terribiles illos leones ex αὐτοψίᾳ describit Oppianus libro tertio Cynegeticῶn, apes sunt ita cicures, ut inter hominum cubilia nidificare soleant. III, 356 To these objections that can be raised against the sacred text I have two answers. First, the bees were found in the lion's body, but were not born there; for the author does not assert this. Those therefore are mistaken who on this basis posit a race of bees λεοντογενῶν [leontogenōn], since nowhere in the writings of the ancients do we read that bees are born from a lion. Nor should we listen to Moffett, the Englishman, who in the Insectorum Theatrum writes that some bees are λεοντογενεῖς [leontogeneis], others ταυρογενεῖς [taurogeneis]: namely, that the best and truly noble ones have their origin from the lion, and that their princes and kings derive their lineage from its brain. Hence, he says, it comes about that, small as they are, they sometimes attack the very largest animals and, endowed with leonine ferocity, fear nothing. If this were true, bees would be fiercest in places that teem with lions. But I shall prove without any trouble that the matter is quite otherwise. For in Ethiopia, where Oppian, in book 3 of the Cynegetica, describes those terrible lions from personal observation, bees are so tame that they usually build their nests among the dwellings of men. So Alvarez testifies in his Ethiopia. On the other hand, in Crete a), where there are neither lions, nor wolves, nor bears, nor any harmful animal, bees were so fierce that they sometimes drove the inhabitants from their homes, as will be proved below. And in Scythia b), which is very far from lions, certain places beyond the Ister were once inaccessible, because bees so occupied them that one could not advance further. Herodotus, book 5, chapter 10, reports that he received this from the Thracians, who live nearest the Ister. He himself, however, rejects it as hardly credible, on the ground that bees seem to be ζῶα δύσριγα [zōa dysriga], animals that cannot endure cold. But many writers refute Herodotus: thus Maximus of Tyre, Oration 2 On Love: Σκυθῶν δὲ οἱ μὲν πολλοὶ γάλακτι βιοτεύουσιν, ὡς οἱ ἄλλοι ἐπὶ οἴνῳ, τοῖς δὲ αἱ μέλιτται καθηδύνουσι τὸ πόμα, ἐπὶ πετρῶν καὶ δρυῶν διαπλάττουσαι τοὺς σίμβλους. Second, lest it be asked how Samson's bees could have touched the flesh of a carcass or endured its foul smell, since bees shrink from both, I say that what is here called a carcass was rather a σκελετὸν σῶμα [skeleton sōma], a dried and withered body, in which nothing remained but the framework of the bones. So the Syriac translator takes it, who rendered ܫܠܕܐ ܓܪܡܢܝܐ [שלדא גרמניא], that is, a bony carcass, or, what is the same, a σκελετόν [skeleton]. This interpretation is favoured by the fact that the lion's carcass had lain on the ground for a long time. For Samson, who had killed it, left it lying in the field, וישב מימים, and he returned after days (so it reads literally) to take Delilah, and he turned aside to see the carcass of that lion. After days: that is, after a year. So the Hebrews take it in this passage, and so it is used throughout Scripture. Thus Gen 4:3. And it came to pass מקץ ימים, at the end of days: that is, at the end of a year. Genes. XXIV, 55. Let the girl remain with us ימים או עשר, days, or ten: that is, a year, or ten months. Judg 11:4. And it came to pass after days (that is, after a year) that the Ammonites made war against the Israelites. Therefore, when Samson is said to have returned after days, etc., it is the same as if it were said that only after a year did he return and turn aside to see the carcass of the lion killed a year before R3. From III, 358 this it is therefore probable that nothing but the bones remained, since time had consumed the flesh, or wild beasts had devoured it. And when it, hanging there, had already become hollow, a swarm of bees entered it and filled it with honeycombs. Now that we have dealt fully with smoke, it remains for us to speak of the bravery of bees, with reference to those passages of Scripture in which the most hostile enemies are compared with bees: as above, the Amorites, of whom Moses says, And they pursued you, as bees do. And in Ps 118:12 David says of his enemies, They surrounded me like bees. And in Isa 7:18 The Lord will hiss for the bee that is in the land of Assur. There the Chaldee has: The Lord will call to the mighty forces, דאנון תקיפין כדבריתא, which are strong like bees, and he will bring them from the borders of the land of Assyria. And in the same place R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], III, 364 לדבורה חיל גבורים דעוקצים כדבורים, To the bee, that is, to an army of mighty men who sting like bees. Lest these comparisons should seem to fall below the dignity of the subject, it must be understood that the bee has a great spirit in so slight a little body; and that, to protect their honey, bees do not fear to attack with their hostile stings not only men but even all the largest animals, and sometimes they kill them. Aristotle, History, book 9, ch. 40: Κτείνουσι δὲ βάλλουσαι καὶ τὰ μεγάλα τῶν ζώων. καὶ ἵππος ἤδη ἀπέθανεν ὑπὸ μελιττῶν [Kteinousi de ballousai kai ta megala tōn zōōn. kai hippos ēdē apethanen hypo melittōn]. By driving in their sting they kill all the largest animals. Indeed, a horse once died from being stung by bees. Hence Pliny, book 11, ch. 18: There are instances of horses killed by them. Moreover, Aelian, book 17, ch. 35, reports from Antenor that the Rhaucians, a people of Crete, were driven out of their city by the stings of χαλκοειδῶν [chalkoeidōn] bees and migrated elsewhere. It is no wonder, then, if the same creatures proved fatal to Hermonax, a boy, of whom Antipater writes in book 1 of the Anthology, ch. 37: ¶

Τὸ βρέφος Ἑρμάνακτα διεχρήσασθε μέλισσαι,

Τὸ βρέφος Ἑρμάνακτα διεχρήσασθε μέλισσαι, [To brephos Hermanakta diechrēsasthe melissai,] ¶

Infantem Hermonactem interfecistis, apes. Et Bianor,

Κοῦρον ἀποπλανίην, ἐπιμάζιον, Ἑρμώνακτα Φεῦ! βρέφος ὡς ἀδίκως εἵλετε βουγενέες; [Kouron apoplaniēn, epimazion, Hermōnakta / Pheu! brephos hōs adikōs heilete bougenees?] ¶

Puerum errantem et lactentem Hermonactem, infantem, heu mihi! quam inique perdidistis, apes? Proinde in Theocrito Idyllio 19. apud matrem conqueritur Cupido κηριοκλέπτης,

The wandering, suckling boy Hermonax, an infant, alas! how unjustly you destroyed him, bees! Accordingly, in Theocritus, Idyll 19, Cupid the κηριοκλέπτης [kēriokleptēs] complains to his mother ¶

Θηρίον ἐντὶ μέλισσα, καὶ ἁλίκα τραύματα ποιεῖ·

— — — — ὅττι γε τυτθὸν col. 510 Θηρίον ἐντὶ μέλισσα, καὶ ἁλίκα τραύματα ποιεῖ· [— — — — hotti ge tytthon / Thērion enti melissa, kai halika traumata poiei;] ¶

Quod, tam exile animalculum cum sit apis, tanta vulnera faciat. Et apum examina saepe in bello profuere. Sic in Appiani Mithridatico, cum Luculliani per cuniculos Themiscyram aggrederentur, Themiscyrii ursos ferasque alias, et σμή-νη μελισσῶν, apum examina, in operarios emittebant. Et, Amurathe Albam Graecam obsidente, oppidani, apum examinibus in Turcas immissis, se tuebantur, ut scribit Bonfinius Decadis 3. libro 4. Nec absimile est, quod refert Wittichindus, lib. 1. De gestis Saxonum, unum ex ducibus Henrici, imperatoris, a Giselberto, Lotharingiae rege, obsessum, invasores, objectis apum alvearibus, inhibuisse, quorum aculeis equi, in furorem acti, sessores vel excusserint, vel ad pugnandum inutiles reddiderint. Et anno 1513. cum Barriga, dux Lusitanus, Tanli, Maurorum oppidum, in ditione Xiatimensi, pene ad incitas redegisset, oppidani, cum se in extremum discrimen adductos cernerent, alvearia apum innumerabilia, quibus abundabant, incensa e moenibus demisere. Nostri, et alvearium flammis ambusti, et apum aculeis stimulati, oppugnationem deserere coacti sunt. Osorii verba sunt lib. 8. De rebus Emmanuelis. Itaque, si minus verum, III, 365 saltem vero simile est, quod apud Photium scribit Iamblichus de copiis, quae Rhodanem et Sinonidem obsidebant: Φεύγουσιν ὁ στρατὸς, τῷ τῶν μελισσῶν πο-λέμῳ πονούμενοι. Copiae diffugiunt, apum pugna afflictae (5). Neque prorsus est incredibile, quod ex Herodoto supra attulimus, loca quaedam trans Istrum, propter apum multitudinem hominibus fuisse inaccessa. Hodie inter poenas, quas adultero imponit Iudaica gens, haec una est, ut nudus aestate per densam apum turbam transeat, et illarum aculeis corpus suum objiciat, donec totum intumuerit: et postquam convaluerit, iterum, imo saepius, pro peccati gravitate poenam eandem subeat. Ita habetur cap. 34. Synagogae Buxtorfii.

That though the bee is so tiny a little creature, it inflicts such great wounds. Swarms of bees have also often been of use in war. Thus in Appian's Mithridatic Wars, when the troops of Lucullus were attacking Themiscyra through tunnels, the Themiscyrans sent bears and other wild beasts, and σμήνη μελισσῶν [smēnē melissōn], swarms of bees, against the workmen. And when Amurath was besieging Alba Graeca, the townspeople defended themselves by letting loose swarms of bees on the Turks, as Bonfinius writes in Decade 3, book 4. Not unlike this is what Widukind reports in book 1 of On the Deeds of the Saxons: one of the commanders of the Emperor Henry, besieged by Giselbert, king of Lotharingia, held back the attackers by throwing beehives at them; the horses, driven to frenzy by the stings, either threw their riders or made them useless for fighting. And in the year 1513, when Barriga, a Portuguese commander, had reduced Tanli, a town of the Moors in the territory of Xiatima, almost to the last extremity, the townspeople, seeing themselves brought into the utmost danger, set on fire countless beehives, of which they had great plenty, and let them down from the walls. Our men, both scorched by the flames of the hives and goaded by the stings of the bees, were forced to abandon the assault. These are the words of Osorius, book 8, On the Deeds of Emmanuel. Therefore, if not true, III, 365 it is at least plausible, what Iamblichus writes in Photius about the forces besieging Rhodanes and Sinonis: Φεύγουσιν ὁ στρατὸς, τῷ τῶν μελισσῶν πολέμῳ πονούμενοι [Pheugousin ho stratos, tō tōn melissōn polemō ponoumenoi]. The forces flee, distressed by the attack of the bees R5. Nor is it altogether incredible, what we cited above from Herodotus, that certain regions beyond the Ister were inaccessible to men because of the multitude of bees. Today, among the penalties that the Jewish nation imposes on an adulterer, one is that he must pass naked in summer through a dense crowd of bees and expose his body to their stings until he is swollen all over; and after he has recovered, he must undergo the same penalty again, or even more often, according to the gravity of the sin. So it is stated in ch. 34 of Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum]'s Synagoga. ¶
