II, 590 Chapter VIII. col. 51 ¶

Avem דרור deror non esse Hirundinem, ut volunt, sed Columbae ferae speciem, puta Turturem, aut Palumbum.

That the bird דרור deror is not the swallow, as is held, but a kind of wild dove, namely the turtledove or the wood pigeon. ¶

Ex avium genere una est, quam Hebraei דרור deror, id est, libertatem, aut libe- ram appellant; מפני שדרה בבית כבשדה, quia tam libere versatur in domo, quam in agro. Ita statuit R. Ismael in tractatu Talmudico Betsa cap. 3. pag. 24. a. Hinc Hebraei recentiores, quique illos sequuti sunt nuperi Lexicographi et Bi- bliorum interpretes, summo consensu statuunt, דרור deror esse hirundinem: quia duae leguntur esse species hirundinum,

Among the birds there is one which the Hebrews call דרור deror, that is, freedom or free; מפני שדרה בבית כבשדה, because it dwells as freely in the house as in the field. So R. Ishmael determined in the Talmudic tractate Betsa, ch. 3, p. 24a. Hence the later Hebrews, and the recent lexicographers and interpreters of the Bible who have followed them, hold with the greatest unanimity that דרור deror is the swallow, because there are said to be two species of swallows, ¶

quarum petit altera sylvas, Altera tecta subit. — — — — a)

of which one seeks the woods, the other enters under roofs. — — — — a) ¶

Sed haec ratio non sufficit, cum et aliae sint aves partim domesticae, partim agrestes. In his principem locum columbae tenent, quarum unum genus κατοικί-διον, domesticum, alterum νομαδικὸν, vagum, et in agris pascens, ut in Galeno habetur libro decimo De Simplic. Quomodo etiam distinguit Varro Rustic. lib. 3. cap. 7. Ita tamen, ut utrumque genus in tectis habitare dicat; sed agreste in summa parte turrium, unde in agros evolet ac remeet: cum clementius alterum, cibo domestico contentum, intra limina januae soleat pasci. Ego nomine דרור deror et agreste hoc columbae genus, et quascumque columbarum species in feris Graeci recensent, significari puto: sed maxime φάτταν, palumbum, et τρυ-γόνα, turturem. Huc me movet primo veterum interpretum auctoritas, qui דרור, vel turturem, vel palumbum explicant. Sic Ps. LXXXIV, 4. דרור tur- tur exponitur in Graeca versione, et Vulgata, Chaldaea, Syra, et utraque Arabe, et in Apollinaris paraphrasi. Sed Aethiops habet መዐንቅ፡ id est, tor- quatum: quo epitheto palumbum insigniri nemo nescit, propter torquem, quem habet circa collum. Martialis in Xeniis,

But this reason is not sufficient, since there are also other birds that are partly domestic, partly wild. Among these the first place belongs to the doves, of which one kind is κατοικίδιον [katoikidion], domestic, the other νομαδικὸν [nomadikon], roaming, and feeding in the fields, as is found in Galen, book 10 On Simples. Varro also makes this distinction in On Agriculture, book 3, ch. 7, yet in such a way that he says both kinds dwell under roofs: but the wild kind in the highest part of towers, from which it flies out into the fields and returns, whereas the other, tamer kind, content with domestic food, usually feeds within the threshold of the door. For my part, I think that by the name דרור deror are meant both this wild kind of dove and whatever species of doves the Greeks list among the wild ones, but above all φάτταν [phattan], the wood pigeon, and τρυγόνα [trygona], the turtledove. I am moved to this, first, by the authority of the ancient interpreters, who explain דרור as either the turtledove or the wood pigeon. Thus in Ps. LXXXIV, 4 דרור is rendered turtledove in the Greek version, and in the Vulgate, the Chaldee, the Syriac, and both Arabic versions, and in the paraphrase of Apollinaris. But the Ethiopic has መዐንቅ፡ [maʿanəq], that is, the collared one: everyone knows that the wood pigeon is distinguished by this epithet, on account of the collar it has around its neck. Martial in the Xenia: ¶

Inguina torquati tardant hebetantque palumbi.

The collared wood pigeons slow and dull the loins. ¶

Abdorrachman, Abubecri filius, in carmine De repudio Aticae, uxoris olim charissimae,

Abdorrachman, son of Abubecr, in his poem On the Divorce of Atica, once his dearest wife, ¶

ما ناح قمري الحمام المطوق col. 52 II, 591 Quam luget palumbus columba torquata. Alkazuinius, ان بيض القماري يجعل تحت الفواخت وبيض الفواخت يجعل تحت القماري كلاهما ينغقان قماري كافورية مطوقة Sive palumbi ova sub phabe ponantur, aut ova phabis sub palumbo, inde nihil edetur praeter palumbos camphoratos, torquatos. Altera ducitur ex Arabica lingua, qua الحر alhorro est pullus columbae, aut turturis, et ساق حر sako horrin palumbus. Alcamus, الحر فرخ الحمامة Alhorro pullus columbae, Giggejus addit, vel turturis. ساق حر ذكر القماري Sako horrin, mas palumborum. Et alibi, الورشان طاير وهو ساق حر لحمه اخف من الحمام Alvvarsen avis, nempe sako horrin, cujus caro levior est, quam columbae. Ajunt Arabes, a voce sic appellari. Ibnosaida, سمي القماري ساق حر لصوته فانه يقول ساق حر ساق حر Vocatur palumbus sako horrin propter vocem suam, quia dicit Sako horrin, Sako horrin. Sic alia palumbi species قطا kata, vel katta, etiam a voce dici putatur. Damir, سميت بحكاية صوته Nomen habet a similitudine vocis. Nempe, cum ad pullos venit, inquiunt, aquam in ingluvie afferens, صاحت قطا قطا clamat Katta, Katta. Sed merum hoc commentum est. Nam قطا katta, deflexum ex palumbi Graeco nomine φάττα. Graecum φ et π Arabes in Kaph saepe mutant, quia ف Phe et ق Kaph apud illos sunt ejusdem figurae, nec nisi puncto differunt, quod Phe simplex, Kaph duplex habet. Proinde in Avicenna قوقي kauke sunt phocae. In Arabico Dioscoride قوطوما φύτευμα, قوقي keuke, πεύκη· قرقسيون karkesion, καρπήσιον· قوقس البحر kukos albahri, φύκος θαλάσσιον· Neque scribarum id esse mendum, sed auctorum, inde apparet, quod hae voces digestae sunt per ordinem Alphabeticum, et referuntur ad literam Kaph. Ita igitur قطا Kata, vel katta, est φάττα, seu palumbus: neque a voce dicitur, ut sibi fingunt Arabes. Idem judicium de ساق حر sako horrin. Nam ساق sako per se est columbae mas. Et ساق حر sako horrin, mas columbae libertatis, seu liberae, id est, palumbi, vel etiam turturis, ut quidam volunt. Nam ut Hebraeum דרור deror, ita Arabicum حر horro, liberum sonat. Et Radix حر harra, Syris et Chaldaeis חרר harar, liberum esse, et libertate donare. Itaque quoties דרור in Sacro textu pro libertate sumitur, toties col. 53 II, 592 pro eo Chaldaei interpretes habent חירות, et Arabes حرية. Neque vero est obscurum, cur apud Orientales columbae ferae et agrestes a libertate nomen habeant. Cum etiam Basilius Epistola 175. ad Iulittam talium columbarum turmam appellet αὐτόνομον ἀγέλην, liberum gregem, et suis legibus utentem. Graece enim αὐτόνομος idem, quod ἐλεύθερος. Hinc eosdem populos Thucydides libro secundo promiscue vocat αὐτονόμους et ἐλευθέρους. Et apud Plutarchum in Dialogo De Daemonio Socratis, Epaminondas profitetur se civem ἐλευ-θέρας καὶ αὐτονόμου πόλεως. Et Demosthenes in prima Philippica de populis, qui Philippi partium erant, αὐτονομούμενα καὶ ἐλεύθερα (ἔθνη) ὑπῆρχε. Itaque Isocrates servituti opponit αὐτονομίαν, Graecos habuisse dicens sub Evagora ἀντὶ δουλείας αὐτονομίαν. Quocirca Hebraeum דרור deror ex Basilio recte verteris αὐτόνομον περιστεράν.

ما ناح قمري الحمام المطوق [mā nāḥa qumrī al-ḥamām al-muṭawwaq] col. 52 II, 591 How the wood pigeon, the ring-necked dove, laments. Alkazuinius [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: ان بيض القماري يجعل تحت الفواخت وبيض الفواخت يجعل تحت القماري كلاهما ينغقان قماري كافورية مطوقة [inna bayḍ al-qamārī yujʿal taḥt al-fawākhit wa-bayḍ al-fawākhit yujʿal taḥt al-qamārī kilāhumā yanghaqān qamārī kāfūriyya muṭawwaqa] Whether the eggs of the wood pigeon are placed under the phabe, or the eggs of the phabe under the wood pigeon, nothing will be hatched from them but camphor-coloured, ring-necked wood pigeons. The other derivation is taken from the Arabic language, in which الحر [al-ḥurr] alhorro is the young of a dove or of a turtledove, and ساق حر [sāq ḥurr] sako horrin is the wood pigeon. Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: الحر فرخ الحمامة [al-ḥurr farkh al-ḥamāma] Alhorro, the young of a dove; Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)] adds, or of a turtledove. ساق حر ذكر القماري [sāq ḥurr dhakar al-qamārī] Sako horrin, the male of the wood pigeons. And elsewhere: الورشان طاير وهو ساق حر لحمه اخف من الحمام [al-warashān ṭāyir wa-huwa sāq ḥurr laḥmuhu akhaff min al-ḥamām] Alvvarsen, a bird, namely sako horrin, whose flesh is lighter than that of the dove. The Arabs say it is so called from its call. Ibn Sida: سمي القماري ساق حر لصوته فانه يقول ساق حر ساق حر [summiya al-qamārī sāq ḥurr li-ṣawtihi fa-innahu yaqūl sāq ḥurr sāq ḥurr] The wood pigeon is called sako horrin on account of its call, because it says Sako horrin, Sako horrin. In the same way another species of wood pigeon, قطا [qaṭā] kata or katta, is also thought to be named from its call. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: سميت بحكاية صوته [summiyat bi-ḥikāyat ṣawtihi] It has its name from the likeness of its call. For, they say, when it comes to its young, bringing water in its crop, صاحت قطا قطا [ṣāḥat qaṭā qaṭā] it cries Katta, Katta. But this is pure invention. For قطا [qaṭā] katta is bent from the Greek name of the wood pigeon, φάττα [phatta]. The Arabs often change Greek φ and π into Kaph, because among them ف Phe and ق Kaph have the same shape and differ only in the dots, Phe having a single one and Kaph a double. Accordingly in Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] قوقي [qūqī] kauke are seals. In the Arabic Dioscorides قوطوما [qūṭūmā] is φύτευμα [phyteuma], قوقي [qūqī] keuke is πεύκη [peukē], قرقسيون [qarqasiyūn] karkesion is καρπήσιον [karpēsion], قوقس البحر [qūqas al-baḥr] kukos albahri is φύκος θαλάσσιον [phykos thalassion]. That this is not a slip of the copyists but of the authors is clear from the fact that these words are arranged in alphabetical order and are listed under the letter Kaph. Thus, then, قطا [qaṭā] Kata or katta is φάττα [phatta], that is, the wood pigeon, and it is not named from its call, as the Arabs imagine. The same judgement applies to ساق حر [sāq ḥurr] sako horrin. For ساق [sāq] sako by itself is the male of the dove. And ساق حر [sāq ḥurr] sako horrin is the male of the dove of liberty, or of the free dove, that is, of the wood pigeon, or also of the turtledove, as some hold. For like the Hebrew דרור deror, so the Arabic حر [ḥurr] horro means free. And the root حر [ḥarra], in Syriac and Chaldee חרר harar, means to be free and to grant freedom. Therefore, whenever דרור is taken in the sacred text for liberty, the Chaldee col. 53 II, 592 translators have חירות in its place, and the Arabic ones حرية [ḥurriyya]. Nor indeed is it obscure why among the Orientals wild and field doves are named from liberty. For Basil too, in Epistle 175 to Julitta, calls a flock of such doves αὐτόνομον ἀγέλην [autonomon agelēn], a free flock, living by its own laws. For in Greek αὐτόνομος [autonomos] is the same as ἐλεύθερος [eleutheros]. Hence Thucydides in book 2 calls the same peoples indiscriminately αὐτονόμους [autonomous] and ἐλευθέρους [eleutherous]. And in Plutarch, in the dialogue On the Daemon of Socrates, Epaminondas declares himself a citizen ἐλευθέρας καὶ αὐτονόμου πόλεως [eleutheras kai autonomou poleōs]. And Demosthenes, in the First Philippic, says of the peoples who were of Philip's party: αὐτονομούμενα καὶ ἐλεύθερα (ἔθνη) ὑπῆρχε [autonomoumena kai eleuthera (ethnē) hypērche]. Accordingly Isocrates opposes αὐτονομίαν [autonomian] to servitude, saying that under Evagoras the Greeks had ἀντὶ δουλείας αὐτονομίαν [anti douleias autonomian]. Therefore, following Basil, you would rightly render the Hebrew דרור deror as αὐτόνομον περιστεράν [autonomon peristeran]. ¶

In Scripturae duobus locis, quibus pro ave occurrit דרור deror, nihil est, quod vel minimum huic interpretationi repugnet. Prov. XXVI, 2. דרור commendari videtur a pernicitate volatus, qua vel cum quavis alite decertare posse columbam, suo loco docuimus (1). Et Psal. LXXXIV, 4. ingemiscit Psaltes, sortem suam conferens cum vilissimis avibus, passere puta, et דרור deror, id est, agresti columba, quibus ad altare Dei non modo liber accessus, sed et ibi nidificare, et pullos excludere concessum: dum ipse vagus erat, et extorris, et ab aede sacra remotus. In altaris nomine figuram esse oportet. Neque enim in tanta hominum et sacrificiorum frequentia tuta fuisset avibus in ipso altari commoratio. Itaque altare pro tabernaculo ponitur, vel pro templo. Pro tabernaculo, si David loquitur in hoc Psalmo; pro templo, si quis alius post Davidem (2). Dubitant enim Hebraei, ut ex Kimchio discere est. Utut sit, tam II, 593 tabernaculum, quam templum b) nitebatur magnis trabibus cedrinis, et ἀρκευ-θίναις, in quo cedri genere palumbum libenter nidificare, docent haec Theocriti Idyll. 5.

In the two passages of Scripture in which דרור deror occurs for a bird, there is nothing that conflicts in the least with this interpretation. In Prov 26:2 the דרור seems to be commended for the swiftness of its flight, in which, as we have shown in its place R1, the dove can compete with any bird whatever. And in Ps 84:4 the Psalmist groans, comparing his lot with the meanest birds, namely the sparrow and the דרור deror, that is, the field dove, to which not only free access to the altar of God was granted, but also to nest and hatch their young there, while he himself was a wanderer, an exile, and far from the sacred house. In the name of the altar there must be a figure of speech. For amid so great a throng of men and sacrifices, staying on the altar itself would not have been safe for birds. Therefore altar is put for the tabernacle, or for the temple: for the tabernacle if David speaks in this Psalm; for the temple if someone else after David R2. For the Hebrews are in doubt, as one may learn from Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]. However that may be, both the tabernacle II, 593 and the temple b) rested on great beams of cedar and of ἀρκευθίναις [arkeuthinais], in which kind of cedar the wood pigeon likes to nest, as these lines of Theocritus, Idyll 5, teach. ¶

Κἠγὼ μὲν δώσω τᾷ παρθένῳ αὐτίκα φάσσαν, Ἐκ τᾶς ἀρκεύθῳ καθελὼν, τηνεὶ γὰρ ἐφείσδει. At mea, tu felix, donabere virgo palumbo, Cui suus arceuthi celsa latet in trabe nidus.

Sed libro primo Regum, ubi Solomonis templum accurate describitur, nihil tale reperias: ut neque in Iosepho, quamvis idem scribat de templo, ab Herode exstructo, De bello Iudaico lib. 6. cap. 6. Κατὰ κορυφὴν δὲ χρυσέους ὀβελοὺς ἀνεῖχε τεθηγμένους, ὡς μή τινι προσκαθεζομένῳ μολύνοιτο τῶν ὀρνέων, In summo autem aureis verubus horrebat acutissimis, ne ab insidentibus avibus pollueretur. Quae descriptioni τοῦ כלה עורב, a Talmudicis traditae, sunt consona satis. Pone tamen, etiam Solomo- nis templum in tecti vertice hujusmodi verubus inhorruisse: hoc quidem aves a summo, non tamen a toto tecto, aut ab aliis templi partibus arcuisset. Si ἐπὶ τὸ πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ, super pinnaculum templi stare potuisset Christus illaesus Matth. IV, 5. quanto magis aviculae (4)?

But Albertus and Turner [=William Turner, Avium praecipuarum historia (1544)] also assert that the livia dove nests in temples. And it is known from Herodotus c) what happened to Aristodicus when ἐξαῖρε τοὺς στρουθοὺς, καὶ ἄλλα ὅσα ἦν νενοσσευμένα ὀρνίθων γένεα ἐν τῷ νηῷ [exaire tous strouthous, kai alla hosa ēn nenosseumena ornithōn genea en tō nēō], he took out the sparrows and the other kinds of birds that were nesting in the temple R3. The Hebrews, it is true, deny that any birds settled on the temple at Jerusalem, because they were driven off by what they called כולה עורב II, 594, placed on the very top of the roof. Kimchi on Psal. LXXXIV.: בגג הבית עשו דבר שם כדי שלא ינוח שם עוף והיו קורין אותו כולה עורב, They made something on the roof of the temple so that birds should not rest there, and it was called chole oreb, that is, keeping off ravens. This is frequently mentioned in the Talmud. Thus in the tractate Middoth, ch. 4, fol. 71a, in the Gloss: וכולה עורב מאבד העורבים לפי שהיה מקיף ההיכל למעלה מן המעקה מארבע רוחותיו בחשק של ברזל גובה אמה חד כמו הסיף כדי שלו ישב עליו שום עוף על ההיכל מפני שנחתכים רגליו באותו הסיף, And the chole oreb destroyed ravens, because it surrounded the temple above the parapet on all four sides with an iron band a cubit high, sharp like a sword, so that no bird should settle on the temple, since its feet would be cut off by that sword. And in the tractate On the Sabbath, ch. 9, fol. 90a, on the word לכליא עורב (for so it reads there), the Glossator writes: טבלאת של אמה היו עושין והן חדין כסכין סביבותיה ומסמרין חדין בכלן ומחפין בהן גגו של היכל למנוע את העורבי׳ מלישב, They made plates a cubit in size, sharp all round like a knife and all furnished with sharp nails, with which they covered the roof of the temple to keep off the ravens, so that they would not settle on it. And in the tractate col. 54 Menachoth, ch. 13, fol. 107a, the Gloss defines כולה העורב as מסמרות חדין תחובין בראש גג ההיכל לכלות העורבי׳ שלא ישבו עליו, sharp nails fixed in the top of the temple roof to prevent the ravens from sitting on it. And another Gloss in the same place: כמין טבלה חדה וקיפה על צידה וחדודה לחתוך כמו סכין, A kind of sharp plate set up on its edge, with an edge like a knife for cutting. And the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome]: עשה צורה להבריח בה העופות מגג ההיכל כדרך שעושין עכשיו שומרי זרעים, They made a μορμολύκειον [mormolykeion], with which to chase birds from the temple roof, as those who guard crops do now. And again: במקדש ראשון ששכינה שרוייה בו לא היו צורכין כלה עורב שלא היו עופות פורחין עליו אבל במקדש שני שלו היתה קדושתו כקדושת ראשון הוצרכו לכלה עורב, Because the divine majesty dwelt in the first Sanctuary, there was no need for a προβασκανίῳ [probaskaniō]. For birds did not fly over it. But it was needed in the second, because the holiness of the second was not like that of the first. Yet something of the kind is described even in Solomon's temple by Eupolemus, when he says d) that he made δακτυλίους δύο χαλκοῦς ἀλυσιδωτοὺς, καὶ στῆσαι αὐτοὺς [daktylious dyo chalkous halysidōtous, kai stēsai autous] (read δικτύας δύο χαλκᾶς ἀλυσιδωτὰς, καὶ στῆσαι αὐτὰς [diktyas dyo chalkas halysidōtas, kai stēsai autas]) ἐπὶ μηχανημάτων ὑπερεχόντων τῷ ὕψει τὸν ναὸν πήχεις κ΄, καὶ σκιάζειν ἐπάνω παντὸς τοῦ ἱεροῦ, καὶ προσκρεμάσαι ἑκάστῃ δικτύϊ κώδωνας χαλκοῦς ταλαντιαίους τετρακοσίους, καὶ ποιῆσαι ὅλας τὰς δικτύας πρὸς τὸ ψοφεῖν τοὺς κώδωνας, καὶ ἀποσοβεῖν τὰ ὄρνεα, ὅπως μὴ καθίζῃ ἐπὶ τοῦ ἱεροῦ, μηδὲ νοσσεύῃ ἐπὶ τοῖς φατνώμασι τῶν πυλῶν, καὶ στοῶν, καὶ μολύνῃ τοῖς ἀποπατήμασι τὸ ἱερὸν [epi mēchanēmatōn hyperechontōn tō hypsei ton naon pēcheis k', kai skiazein epanō pantos tou hierou, kai proskremasai hekastē diktyi kōdōnas chalkous talantiaious tetrakosious, kai poiēsai holas tas diktyas pros to psophein tous kōdōnas, kai aposobein ta ornea, hopōs mē kathizē epi tou hierou, mēde nosseuē epi tois phatnōmasi tōn pylōn, kai stoōn, kai molynē tois apopatēmasi to hieron], two bronze nets linked with rings, and that he set them on machines which rose twenty cubits above the temple and overshadowed the whole of it, and hung from each net II, 595 four hundred bells of great weight, and fitted all those nets so that the bells would ring and drive off the birds with their sound, so that they would not settle on the temple, nor nest in the coffered ceilings of the gates or porticoes, nor pollute the temple with their droppings. But in the first book of Kings, where Solomon's temple is accurately described, you will find nothing of the sort; nor in Josephus, although he writes the same of the temple built by Herod, On the Jewish War book 6, ch. 6: Κατὰ κορυφὴν δὲ χρυσέους ὀβελοὺς ἀνεῖχε τεθηγμένους, ὡς μή τινι προσκαθεζομένῳ μολύνοιτο τῶν ὀρνέων [Kata koryphēn de chryseous obelous aneiche tethēgmenous, hōs mē tini proskathezomenō molynoito tōn orneōn], On the top it bristled with very sharp golden spits, so that it should not be polluted by birds settling on it. This agrees well enough with the description of the כלה עורב handed down by the Talmudists. Yet suppose that Solomon's temple too bristled with such spits on the ridge of the roof: this would have kept the birds off the top, but not off the whole roof or the other parts of the temple. If Christ could stand unharmed ἐπὶ τὸ πτερύγιον τοῦ ἱεροῦ [epi to pterygion tou hierou], on the pinnacle of the temple, Matt 4:5, how much more little birds R4? ¶

R4 Rosenmüller, II, 595 In this whole discussion I would think Bochart more successful in refuting the fancies of others than in establishing his own opinion. For at the beginning of this chapter he rightly objected to those who render it swallow, as if it were named from freedom, that this reason is not sufficient, since there are also other birds, some domestic, some wild; yet soon he himself uses the same reason to show that דרור is a species of wild dove.
