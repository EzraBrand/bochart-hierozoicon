III, 20 col. 277 Chapter XXI. ¶

Probatur, שלך salach, a Graecis recte reddi καταῤ-ῥάκτην catarrhacten.

It is shown that שלך salach is rightly rendered by the Greeks καταῤῥάκτην [katarrhaktēn] catarrhactes. ¶

Hebraeum avis nomen שלך salach, a Graecis redditur καταῤῥάκτης· recte col. 278 omnino. Nam השליך Verbum, unde avis nomen deductum, est idem, quod καταῤῥίπτειν projicere, et καταβάλλειν dejicere. Porro id ipsum est καταῤῥάττειν, ex quo factum καταῤῥάκτης. Hesychius, Καταῤῥάσσειν· κα-ταῤῥίπτειν. Item, Καταῤῥάξαι· καταβαλεῖν. Proinde Graecae versionis locis plus centum Hebraeo השליך respondent Graeca ῥίπτειν, ἀποῤῥίπτειν, ἐπιῤῥίπτειν, καταῤῥίπτειν, et βάλλειν, ἐκβάλλειν, ἐμβάλλειν, ἀποβάλλειν. Quin Psal. CI. (Hebr. CII.) pro Hebraeo השליכני, Graece habetur κατέῤῥα-ξάς με, dejecisti me. Itaque ut Verbo השליך καταῤῥάξαι, ita Nomini שלך respondet καταῤῥάκτης. Et quandoque est epithetum avium praedatricum, quae cum impetu in praedam ruunt, et quodammodo se praecipites conjiciunt. Ita Sophocles in Laocoonte aquilam καταῤῥάκτην, et in Phineo Harpyias καταῤ-ῥάκτας appellaverat. Hesychius, Καταῤῥάκτης· ὀχετὸς, ῥύαξ, καὶ ἀετός· Σοφοκλῆς Λαοκόοντι, καὶ τὰς Ἁρπυίας ἐν Φινεῖ. Et in Cassandra Lycophronis καταῤῥακτὴρ, quod idem est, accipitris est epithetum. Verba sunt versu 168.

The Hebrew bird name שלך salach is rendered by the Greeks καταῤῥάκτης [katarrhaktēs], and quite rightly. col. 278 For the verb השליך, from which the bird's name is derived, is the same as καταῤῥίπτειν [katarrhiptein], to throw forth, and καταβάλλειν [kataballein], to throw down. Moreover, this is the very meaning of καταῤῥάττειν [katarrhattein], from which καταῤῥάκτης [katarrhaktēs] is formed. Hesychius: Καταῤῥάσσειν· καταῤῥίπτειν [Katarrhassein· katarrhiptein]. Likewise: Καταῤῥάξαι· καταβαλεῖν [Katarrhaxai· katabalein]. Accordingly, in more than a hundred places of the Greek version the Hebrew השליך is answered by the Greek ῥίπτειν [rhiptein], ἀποῤῥίπτειν [aporrhiptein], ἐπιῤῥίπτειν [epirrhiptein], καταῤῥίπτειν [katarrhiptein], and βάλλειν [ballein], ἐκβάλλειν [ekballein], ἐμβάλλειν [emballein], ἀποβάλλειν [apoballein]. Indeed, in Psal. CI. (Hebr. CII.), for the Hebrew השליכני the Greek has κατέῤῥαξάς με [katerrhaxas me], you have thrown me down. Thus, just as καταῤῥάξαι [katarrhaxai] corresponds to the verb השליך, so καταῤῥάκτης [katarrhaktēs] corresponds to the noun שלך. And it is sometimes an epithet of birds of prey, which rush upon their prey with force and in a manner hurl themselves headlong. So Sophocles in the Laocoon had called the eagle καταῤῥάκτην [katarrhaktēn], and in the Phineus the Harpies καταῤῥάκτας [katarrhaktas]. Hesychius: Καταῤῥάκτης· ὀχετὸς, ῥύαξ, καὶ ἀετός· Σοφοκλῆς Λαοκόοντι, καὶ τὰς Ἁρπυίας ἐν Φινεῖ [Katarrhaktēs· ochetos, rhyax, kai aetos· Sophoklēs Laokoonti, kai tas Harpyias en Phinei]. And in the Cassandra of Lycophron, καταῤῥακτὴρ [katarrhaktēr], which is the same thing, is an epithet of the hawk. The words are at line 168: ¶

Τὸν δ' αὖ τέταρτον αὐθόμαινον ὄψεται
Κίρκου καταῤῥακτῆρος· —

Τὸν δ' αὖ τέταρτον αὐθόμαινον ὄψεται Κίρκου καταῤῥακτῆρος· — [Ton d' au tetarton authomainon opsetai / Kirkou katarrhaktēros· —] ¶

Quartum porro consanguineum videbit accipitris catarrhacteris, seu catarrhactis. Diomedeas quoque aves (1) Iuba vocat catarrhactas, ut habetur apud Plinium li-bri decimi capite quadragesimo quarto; quia in Aristotelis Mirabilibus [cap. LXXX.] leguntur Graecis parcere, sed venientes barbaros καταῤῥάσσειν.

The fourth, moreover, a kinsman, he will see, of the catarrhacter hawk, or catarrhactes. Juba also calls the Diomedean birds R1 catarrhactae, as is found in Pliny, book 10, chapter 44; because in Aristotle's Mirabilia [ch. 80] we read that they spare the Greeks but καταῤῥάσσειν [katarrhassein] the barbarians who come. ¶

Tamen, ut Hebraeis שלך, ita Graecis καταῤῥάκτης avis est certa species, cui peculiare est hoc nomen (2). Suidas, Καταῤῥάκτης· εἶδος ὀρνέου θαλασ-σίου.

Nevertheless, just as שלך among the Hebrews, so καταῤῥάκτης [katarrhaktēs] among the Greeks is a definite species of bird, to which this name is peculiar R2. Suidas: Καταῤῥάκτης· εἶδος ὀρνέου θαλασσίου [Katarrhaktēs· eidos orneou thalassiou]. ¶

III, 21 σὶς· Catarractes, avis marinae species. Eustathius in Dionysium, Τὶ ὄρνεον ἀετῶδες καταῤῥάκτης καλεῖται· Avis aliqua aquilini generis catarrhactes vocatur. Aristophanes in Avibus, Καὶ ἐρωδιῷ, καὶ καταῤῥάκτῃ, καὶ μελαγκορύ-φῳ· Et ardeae, et catarrhactae, et melancorypho. Et ibi Scholiastes, Καταῤ-ῥάκται εἰσὶ παρὰ Καλλιμάχῳ ἀναγεγραμμέναι· Catarractae a Callimacho describuntur. Aristoteles Historiae libri secundi capite decimo septimo, Νῆττα δὲ, καὶ χὴν, καὶ λάρος, καὶ καταῤῥάκτης, καὶ ὠτὶς τὸν στόμαχον εὐρὺν καὶ πλατὺν ὅλον ἔχουσι· Anas autem, et anser, et larus, et catarrhactes, et otis totum stomachum amplum et latum habent. Et libri noni capite duodecimo, Ὁ δὲ καταῤῥάκτης ζῇ μὲν περὶ θάλατταν. ὅταν δὲ καθῇ ἑαυτὸν εἰς τὸ βαθὺ, μένει χρόνον οὐκ ἐλάττονα, ἢ ὅσον πλέθρον διέλθοι τις. ἔστι δ᾽ ἔλαττον ἱέρα-κος τὸ ὄρνεον. Catarrhactes quidem circa mare vivit. Cum autem in profundum se demisit, manet non minus temporis, quam quo jugerum quis transierit. Est autem avis accipitre minor. Oppianus in Ixeuticis accipitri palumbario προσό-μοιον, similem esse scribit. Apud Eustathium vocari dicitur catarrhactes, ὡς ἄνω-θεν καταῤῥάσσων ἐν τῷ θηρεύειν τὰ ὄρνεα; tanquam superne irruens cum venatur aves. Perperam, cum catarrhactes nonnisi pisces venetur. Praestat igitur nominis rationem aliunde addiscere. Et commodum se offerunt Gesneri Excerpta ex Oppiani Ixeuticis, quae quidem hodie non extant. Sed illorum Graecam paraphrasim legerat, manu exaratam; in qua haec maxime ad rem [lib. II. c. 2.]. 1. Catarractes scopulis, et littoribus, et petris, quas χοιράδας vocant, supra aquam eminentibus, insidet, etc. Et ubi pisces aliquot natantes viderit (perspicacissimus enim est vel ad fundum usque maris) altius volat, et pennis omnibus contractis, in mare tanquam delapsus, per medium aëris intervallum quovis telo celerius fertur, et ad ulnae spatium immergitur, donec comprehensum piscem extraxerit, quem mox evolans palpitantem adhuc devorat (3). Quae Graece ex MSS. suo sic citat vir doctissimus Vossius noster in eximio illo in Melam opere pagina β. Πρὸς ὕψος ἑαυτὸν αἴρει, καὶ τὰ πτερὰ πάντα συστείλας, εἰς τὸν πόντον, col. 279 οἷα πίπτων, οἴσεται, διατεμὼν τὸν ἀέρα, παντὸς ἂν εἴποις βέλους ὀξύτερον, καὶ καταδύεται μέχρι ὀργυιᾶς, ἢ καὶ πλέον, ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε συλλαβὼν τὸν ἰχθὺν ἀνασπᾷ, καὶ ἀνιπτάμενος ἔτι πάλλοντα κατεσθίει. 2. Mors catarrhactis gravior, quam ulli ambiguae vitae avium contingit. Acies enim oculorum, senio III, 22 confectis obtusa, in causa est, ut in mare se praecipites ferri ad piscium praedam arbitrantes, scopulis ac praecipitiis illidantur, et pereant (4). 3. Catarrhactas imaginibus piscium, super tabulas pictis, capiunt. Nam, dum impetu in illas, seu vivos pisces feruntur, illisi pereunt (5). 4. Mas ova, unde marem procrean- dum conjicit, foemina unde foeminam, in sublime ferunt unguibus comprehensa. Inde mittunt in pelagus, atque hoc subinde repetunt per dies aliquot. Hoc motu concalefactis ovis pulli excluduntur. 5. Pulli catarrhactae, mox ut lucem aspexe- rint, nulla in nidis mora, parentes volatu sequi contendunt. A parentibus autem suscepti, in mare demittuntur, et rursus illico extrahuntur, ut sic facilius auxilio parentum hoc genus exercitii ipsis reddatur. Vides, lector, quam merito He- braice שלך dicatur, et Graece καταῤῥάκτης. Cum modo describatur משליך נפשו, ἑαυτὸν καταῤῥάσσων, seipsum dejiciens, aut certo consilio in mare, unde victus illi suppetit; aut per errorem vel in scopulos, vel in tabulas pictas, unde illi certa pernicies: modo משליך ביציו או בניו, ova sua projiciens, aut pullos suos in mare demittens, ad quod ultimum fortasse τὸ καταῤῥάσσειν non ita pertinet, quia id cum impetu non fit: sed Hebraeum השליך tam significat leniter demittere, quam cum impetu projicere. De projectione in praeceps usurpatur Ion. II, 4. ubi Propheta, hoc verbo usus, queritur, se projectum fuisse in profundum et in cor maris. Et 2 Par. XXV, 12. ubi legitur de decem Idumae- orum millibus, וישליכום, Et projecerunt eos de petrae vertice, et illi omnes cre- puerunt. Proinde Graeci reddunt, Καὶ κατεκρήμνιζον αὐτούς· Et Hieronymus; Praecipitaveruntque eos. Sed pro demissione sumitur, cum de Ieremia scribitur Ierem. XXXVIII, 6. וישליכו אותו אל הבור, Et jecerunt eum in foveam, id est, demiserunt. Sequitur enim ἐπεξηγητικῶς, illum demissum eo fuisse funibus. Catarrhactes igitur, etsi pullos suos in mare, fortasse etiam ova non tam projiciat, quam demittat: tamen etiam eo nomine שלך dici potuit. Et hujus historiae putaverim aliquid Hebraeis suboluisse, cum tradunt, auctore Aben Ezra, שלך avem esse, quae soleat להשליך ילדיו, pullos suos projicere. Haec de Graeca versione; quae, quia vera est, reliquas quoque libet ad eam expendere.

III, 21 σὶς [sis]: Catarractes, a species of sea bird. Eustathius on Dionysius: Τὶ ὄρνεον ἀετῶδες καταῤῥάκτης καλεῖται [ti orneon aetōdes katarrhaktēs kaleitai]: A certain bird of the eagle kind is called catarrhactes. Aristophanes in the Birds: Καὶ ἐρωδιῷ, καὶ καταῤῥάκτῃ, καὶ μελαγκορύφῳ [kai erōdiō, kai katarrhaktē, kai melankoryphō]: And to the heron, and to the catarrhactes, and to the blackcap. And there the Scholiast: Καταῤῥάκται εἰσὶ παρὰ Καλλιμάχῳ ἀναγεγραμμέναι [Katarrhaktai eisi para Kallimachō anagegrammenai]: Catarractae are described by Callimachus. Aristotle, History, book 2, chapter 17: Νῆττα δὲ, καὶ χὴν, καὶ λάρος, καὶ καταῤῥάκτης, καὶ ὠτὶς τὸν στόμαχον εὐρὺν καὶ πλατὺν ὅλον ἔχουσι [Nētta de, kai chēn, kai laros, kai katarrhaktēs, kai ōtis ton stomachon euryn kai platyn holon echousi]: The duck, the goose, the gull, the catarrhactes and the bustard have the whole stomach wide and broad. And book 9, chapter 12: Ὁ δὲ καταῤῥάκτης ζῇ μὲν περὶ θάλατταν. ὅταν δὲ καθῇ ἑαυτὸν εἰς τὸ βαθὺ, μένει χρόνον οὐκ ἐλάττονα, ἢ ὅσον πλέθρον διέλθοι τις. ἔστι δ᾽ ἔλαττον ἱέρακος τὸ ὄρνεον [Ho de katarrhaktēs zē men peri thalattan. hotan de kathē heauton eis to bathy, menei chronon ouk elattona, ē hoson plethron dielthoi tis. esti d' elatton hierakos to orneon]: The catarrhactes lives by the sea. When it has plunged into the deep, it stays there no less time than it takes a man to walk across a plethrum. The bird is smaller than a hawk. Oppian in the Ixeutica writes that it is προσόμοιον [prosomoion], similar, to the goshawk. In Eustathius it is said to be called catarrhactes ὡς ἄνωθεν καταῤῥάσσων ἐν τῷ θηρεύειν τὰ ὄρνεα [hōs anōthen katarrhassōn en tō thēreuein ta ornea], as rushing down from above when it hunts birds. Wrongly, since the catarrhactes hunts nothing but fish. It is better, therefore, to learn the reason for the name from elsewhere. And Gesner's [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] Excerpts from Oppian's Ixeutica, which indeed no longer survive, offer themselves conveniently. He had read a manuscript Greek paraphrase of them, in which the following is most to the point [book II, ch. 2]. 1. The catarractes sits on crags, shores and rocks that stand out above the water, which they call χοιράδας [choiradas], etc. And when it has seen some fish swimming (for it is very sharp-sighted, even down to the bottom of the sea), it flies higher, and with all its feathers drawn in, as if falling into the sea, it is borne through the intervening space of air faster than any dart, and plunges to the depth of an ell, until it has seized a fish and drawn it out; then, flying up, it devours it while it is still struggling R3. Our most learned Vossius, in that excellent work of his on Mela, page β, quotes this in Greek from his manuscripts thus: Πρὸς ὕψος ἑαυτὸν αἴρει, καὶ τὰ πτερὰ πάντα συστείλας, εἰς τὸν πόντον, col. 279 οἷα πίπτων, οἴσεται, διατεμὼν τὸν ἀέρα, παντὸς ἂν εἴποις βέλους ὀξύτερον, καὶ καταδύεται μέχρι ὀργυιᾶς, ἢ καὶ πλέον, ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε συλλαβὼν τὸν ἰχθὺν ἀνασπᾷ, καὶ ἀνιπτάμενος ἔτι πάλλοντα κατεσθίει [Pros hypsos heauton airei, kai ta ptera panta systeilas, eis ton ponton, hoia piptōn, oisetai, diatemōn ton aera, pantos an eipois belous oxyteron, kai katadyetai mechri orgyias, ē kai pleon, esth' hote syllabōn ton ichthyn anaspa, kai aniptamenos eti pallonta katesthiei]. 2. The death of the catarrhactes is harder than that which befalls any of the birds of uncertain life. For the keenness of their eyes, dulled when they are worn out by age, causes them, thinking they are hurling themselves headlong into the sea after fish as prey, to dash against crags and cliffs and perish III, 22 R4. 3. Catarrhactae are caught with images of fish painted on boards. For while they rush at these as if at live fish, they dash against them and perish R5. 4. The male carries the eggs from which he guesses a male will be produced, the female those from which a female will come, up into the air, grasped in their talons. Then they drop them into the sea, and repeat this again and again for some days. The eggs being warmed by this motion, the chicks are hatched. 5. The chicks of the catarrhacta, as soon as they have seen the light, make no delay in the nest but strive to follow their parents in flight. Taken up by the parents, they are let down into the sea and at once drawn out again, so that with the help of their parents this kind of exercise may become easier for them. You see, reader, how fittingly it is called שלך in Hebrew and καταῤῥάκτης [katarrhaktēs] in Greek. For at one point it is described as משליך נפשו, ἑαυτὸν καταῤῥάσσων [heauton katarrhassōn], casting itself down, either deliberately into the sea, from which its food is supplied, or by mistake onto crags or painted boards, whence certain destruction for it; at another as משליך ביציו או בניו, throwing its eggs or letting its chicks down into the sea. To this last, perhaps, τὸ καταῤῥάσσειν [to katarrhassein] does not so properly apply, since it is not done with violence; but the Hebrew השליך means both to let down gently and to throw with violence. It is used of throwing headlong in Jonah 2:4, where the Prophet, using this verb, complains that he had been cast into the deep, into the heart of the sea. And in 2 Chr 25:12, where we read of ten thousand Edomites: וישליכום, And they threw them from the top of the rock, and they were all dashed to pieces. Accordingly the Greeks render: Καὶ κατεκρήμνιζον αὐτούς [Kai katekrēmnizon autous]; and Jerome: And they cast them headlong. But it is taken for letting down when it is written of Jeremiah in Jer 38:6: וישליכו אותו אל הבור, And they cast him into the pit, that is, they let him down. For it follows by way of explanation that he was let down there with ropes. So the catarrhactes, although it does not so much throw as let down its chicks into the sea, and perhaps its eggs too, could nevertheless be called שלך for that reason as well. And I would suppose the Hebrews had caught some scent of this account when they relate, on the authority of Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra], that the שלך is a bird that is accustomed להשליך ילדיו, to throw out its young. So much for the Greek version; since it is true, it is fitting to weigh the others against it as well. ¶

Onkelos et Ionathan hic habent שלינונא salenuna: et Syrus, divisis vocibus, ܫܠܐ ܢܘܢܐ. Quomodo etiam in Ionathane quidam scribunt שלי נונא sale nuna, et reddunt praedonem piscium; quasi שלי esset a שלל, depraedari, cum sit a שלה, quod educere et extrahere significat, ut Arabicum سل et Syrum ܫܠܐ Psal. XVIII, 17. ושלני וקבלני מן מיא סגאה, Et extraxit me, et eduxit III, 23 me ex aquis multis. Itaque שליכון est extractor, vel eductor piscium. Ita exponit R. Iuda in Talmudico tractatu Cholin cap. 3. fol. 63. a. שלך זה השלה דגים מן הים, Salach est avis, quae pisces ex mari extrahit. Et Kimchius in Psalmum centesimum quadragesimum quintum, מצאתי בדברי רז״ל רבי יוחנן כד הוה חזי שלך ששולה דגים הים הוא אמר משפטיך תהום רבה, In magistrorum nostrorum beatae memoriae scriptis reperi, R. Ioannem, cum videbat avem salach, col. 280 pisces e mari extrahentem, dixisse: Iudicia tua abyssus magna. Locus est Psalmi trigesimi sexti versu septimo, quo significari voluit, Deum etiam in abysso magna, id est, in mari, judicia sua exercere. Quam recte, non jam quaerimus. Haec enim ideo solum proferimus, ut doceamus, quorsum שלך salach, Syri et Chaldaei שלי נונא, id est, piscium eductorem vocent. Nempe, quia haec est natura catarrhactis, ut non solum iis vescatur piscibus, qui mari innatant, sed et illos ad orgyiam usque e fundo maris extrahat. Unde supra Oppiani paraphrastes, Dionysius Characenus (6), Καταδύεται μέχρι ὀργυιᾶς, ἢ καὶ πλέον. ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε συλλαβὼν τὸν ἰχθὺν ἀνασπᾷ, etc. Et eundem Philosophus in fundo maris manere dicit χρόνον οὐκ ἐλάττονα ἢ ὅσον πλέθρον διέλθοι τις.

Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] and Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] here have שלינונא salenuna, and the Syriac, with the words divided, ܫܠܐ ܢܘܢܐ [שלא נונא]. In the same way some also write שלי נונא sale nuna in Jonathan, and render it plunderer of fish, as if שלי came from שלל, to plunder. But it comes from שלה, which means to draw out and to extract, like the Arabic سل [sall] and the Syriac ܫܠܐ [שלא]; Ps 18:17. ושלני וקבלני מן מיא סגאה, And he drew me out, and brought III, 23 me out of many waters. So שליכון is the extractor or drawer-out of fish. This is how R. Judah explains it in the Talmudic tractate Chullin, ch. 3, fol. 63a: שלך זה השלה דגים מן הים, Salach is the bird that draws fish out of the sea. And Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] on Psalm 145: מצאתי בדברי רז״ל רבי יוחנן כד הוה חזי שלך ששולה דגים הים הוא אמר משפטיך תהום רבה, I found in the writings of our masters of blessed memory that R. Johanan, when he saw the bird salach col. 280 drawing fish out of the sea, said: Thy judgments are a great deep. The passage is Psalm 36, verse 7, by which he meant that God exercises his judgments even in the great deep, that is, in the sea. How rightly, we do not ask now. We adduce this only to show why the Syrians and Chaldeans call the שלך salach שלי נונא, that is, drawer-out of fish. It is because it is the nature of the catarrhactes not only to feed on the fish that swim in the sea, but also to draw them out from the bottom of the sea, down to a fathom deep. Hence Oppian's paraphrast above, Dionysius Characenus R6: Καταδύεται μέχρι ὀργυιᾶς, ἢ καὶ πλέον. ἔσθ᾽ ὅτε συλλαβὼν τὸν ἰχθὺν ἀνασπᾷ [Katadyetai mechri orgyias, ē kai pleon. esth' hote syllabōn ton ichthyn anaspa], etc. And the Philosopher says that it stays at the bottom of the sea χρόνον οὐκ ἐλάττονα ἢ ὅσον πλέθρον διέλθοι τις [chronon ouk elattona ē hoson plethron dielthoi tis]. ¶

Eandem avem زمج zummag vocat uterque Arabs. Nomen est ab ira factum. زمج zamagia Verbum est irasci, et verbale nomen زمج zamgi ira. Itaque ex etymo colligitur, avem esse valde iracundam. Duae porro sunt species. Una, quae simpliciter dicitur زمج zummag, de qua dicetur infra. Altera زمج الماء zummag aquaticus: de quo Damir, هو طاير الذي يسمي بمصر النورس وهو ابيض في حد الحمام واكبر يعلو في الجو ثم يزج بنفسه في الما ويختلس منه السمك ولا يقع علي الجيف ولا ياكل غير السمك Avis est, quae in Aegypto vocatur nauras, alba, columbae statura, aut major etiam, in altum volans, deinde in aquam se immergens, ut inde pisces depraedetur; neque in cadavera involans, neque aliud quidquam comedens praeter pisces. Quae ad catarrhactae descriptionem pertinere palam est (7).

Both Arabic translators call the same bird زمج [zummaj] zummag. The name is derived from anger. زمج [zamija] zamagia is a verb meaning to be angry, and the verbal noun زمج [zamaj] zamgi means anger. So from the etymology one infers that the bird is very irascible. There are, moreover, two species. One is called simply زمج [zummaj] zummag, of which more below. The other is زمج الماء [zummaj al-māʾ] the water zummag, of which Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] says: هو طاير الذي يسمي بمصر النورس وهو ابيض في حد الحمام واكبر يعلو في الجو ثم يزج بنفسه في الما ويختلس منه السمك ولا يقع علي الجيف ولا ياكل غير السمك [huwa ṭāyir alladhī yusamī bi-Miṣr al-nawras wa-huwa abyaḍ fī ḥadd al-ḥamām wa-akbar yaʿlū fī al-jaww thumma yazujju bi-nafsihi fī al-mā wa-yakhtalisu minhu al-samak wa-lā yaqaʿu ʿalī al-jiyaf wa-lā yaʾkulu ghayr al-samak], It is the bird that in Egypt is called nauras; it is white, of the size of a dove or even larger; it flies up high, then plunges into the water to snatch fish from it; it neither descends on carcasses nor eats anything other than fish. It is evident that this fits the description of the catarrhactes R7. ¶

III, 24 Pro catarrhacte Hieronymus mergulum habet. Quem sequuntur Paginus, Arias, Castalio, Iunius, Buxtorfius, Itali, Genevenses, et multi alii recentiorum interpretum: quod minus probo; quia causa non apparet, cur mergus vocetur שלך, cum non soleat ex alto in aquas se dejicere, quomodo catarrhactes. Quod idem Munstero et Schindlero potest objici, qui ardeam; et Baptistae Ferrario, qui larum, gaviam, fulicam; et An-glis, qui cormorant exponunt, id est, corvum marinum, vel aquaticum, Graece φαλακροκόρακα. Quamvis etiam in Glossa Talmudis ad locum, quem ex tractatu Cholin supra citavimus, dicatur esse עורב המים, corvus aquarum. Sed Pomario nihil ineptius, qui Latine mergulum cum Hieronymo reddit, et Italice מר״לו, merlo; quasi mergulus seu mergus, sit idem cum merula.

III, 24 For catarrhactes Jerome has mergulus [little diver]. He is followed by Pagninus [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)], Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], the Italian and Geneva versions, and many other more recent translators. I do not approve of this, because there is no apparent reason why the diver should be called שלך, since it does not usually throw itself down from on high into the water, as the catarrhactes does. The same objection can be made to Münster and Schindler, who explain it as the heron; to Baptista Ferrarius, who has the gull, gavia, fulica; and to the English, who render it cormorant, that is, the sea raven or water raven, in Greek φαλακροκόρακα [phalakrokoraka]. And yet in the Gloss of the Talmud on the passage from the tractate Chullin cited above, it is also said to be עורב המים, the raven of the waters. But nothing is more inept than Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], who renders it in Latin as mergulus with Jerome, and in Italian as מר״לו, merlo [blackbird], as if mergulus or mergus were the same as merula [blackbird]. ¶
