In mensa Solomonis quid fuerint ברבורים אבוסים, barburi saginati, 1 Reg. IV, 23. [al. V, 3.].

What the ברבורים אבוסים, fattened barburim, at the table of Solomon were, 1 Reg. IV, 23. [var. V, 3.]. ¶

Ut in Romanis ancilibus, ita in variis interpretationibus vocis ברבורים barburim 1 Reg. IV, 23. [al. V, 3.] falsae sic effictae sunt ad verarum similitudinem, ut has ab illis internoscere non sit facile. Ex ipsa loci inspectione patet, recenseri ברבורי׳ barburim in iis animalibus, quae in mensa Solomonis absumebantur in dies. Sic enim habet sacer textus: Et erant cibaria Solomonis in dies decem boves, etc. praeter cervos, et capreas, et bubalos, ברבורים אבוסים, et barburos saginatos. Iunius et Mercerus explicant lectissima quaeque animalia. Apulejus pastus electiles dixisset: ut lib. 10. Metamorphoseωs, Neque asinum, qui solus interesset, talibus cibis affici posse, et tamen quotidie pastus electiles comparere nusquam. In eadem sententia fuere Graeci interpretes, quorum duplex extat versio. Una habet ἐκλεκτῶν σιτευτὰ, altera ὀρνίθων ἐκλεκτῶν νομάδων. Sed in utraque ἐκλεκτῶν est pro ברבר׳ barburim, et in secunda ὀρνίθων de suo supplent. Quasi significentur aves electae altiles, eaeque gallinacei generis. Nam id sunt eo loco ὄρνιθες, ut supra a) probavimus. Ita Talmudici in tractatu Bava Metsia cap. 7. fol. 86. b. electos boves, aut electas gallinas intelligunt, peculiari ratione saginatas. Locus sic habet: מאי ברבר׳ אבוס׳ אמר רב שאובסין אותן col. 128 בעל כרחן ושמואל אמר שאובסין ועומדין מאליהם ורבי יוחנן אמר מביאים תור ממרעיתו בדלא אניס ותרנגולת מאשפתו בדלא אסינא אמר רבי יוחנן מובחר שבבהמות שור מובחר שבעופות תרנגולת אמר אמימר זגתא אוכמתא בי בטניתא דמשתכחא ביני עצרי ולא מציא פסיא קניא, Quid sunt barburi saginati? Dixit Rab, Quos saginant per vim (id est cibo, ver vim in os ingesto). At Samuel dixit saginari prout per se stant (id est, sibi relictos, neque ulla vi adhibita). Et Rabbi Iochanan dixit, Bovem adducunt e pascuo suo, qui nondum laboravit (in cultu terrae), et gallinam e sterquilinio suo, quae nondum laboravit (in pullorum educatione). Rursus dixit Rabbi Iochanan, Pecudum electio est bos, et avium electio gallina. Dixit Amimar, Gallina nigra, ventricosa (al. domi saginata), quae reperitur inter torcularia (ubi acinis pinguescat), nec potest transgredi calamum, prae pinguedine. Inde R. Selomo Commentario in libros Regum, וברברים אבוסים יש מרבותינו אומרים תרנגולין פתומין, Barburos saginatos e Doctoribus nostris, qui dicant esse gallos pingues. Kimchius et Pomarius capones intelligunt; quia העוף המסורס הוא שיתפטם ביותר, avis castrata pinguescit II, 699 magis. De hac sententia plura vide, quae supra b) attulimus ad secundum locum cap. 16.

As with the Roman ancilia, so in the various interpretations of the word ברבורים barburim in 1 Reg. IV, 23. [var. V, 3.], the false ones have been fashioned so closely to resemble the true that it is not easy to tell the one from the other. From an inspection of the passage itself it is clear that the ברבורי׳ barburim are listed among the animals that were consumed daily at the table of Solomon. For the sacred text reads: And the provisions of Solomon for each day were ten oxen, etc., besides harts, and roebucks, and buffaloes, ברבורים אבוסים, and fattened barburim. Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] explain this as all the choicest animals. Apuleius would have said pastus electiles [choice fare], as in book 10 of the Metamorphoses: Nor could the ass, who alone was present, be affected by such food, and yet every day the choice fare was nowhere to be found. The Greek interpreters were of the same opinion; two versions of theirs survive. One has ἐκλεκτῶν σιτευτὰ [eklektōn siteuta], the other ὀρνίθων ἐκλεκτῶν νομάδων [ornithōn eklektōn nomadōn]. But in both ἐκλεκτῶν [eklektōn] stands for ברבר׳ barburim, and in the second they supply ὀρνίθων [ornithōn] on their own. As if choice fattened birds were meant, and these of the gallinaceous kind. For that is what ὄρνιθες [ornithes] are in that passage, as we proved above a). So too the Talmudists, in the tractate Bava Metsia, ch. 7, fol. 86b, understand choice oxen or choice hens, fattened in a particular way. The passage reads: מאי ברבר׳ אבוס׳ אמר רב שאובסין אותן col. 128 בעל כרחן ושמואל אמר שאובסין ועומדין מאליהם ורבי יוחנן אמר מביאים תור ממרעיתו בדלא אניס ותרנגולת מאשפתו בדלא אסינא אמר רבי יוחנן מובחר שבבהמות שור מובחר שבעופות תרנגולת אמר אמימר זגתא אוכמתא בי בטניתא דמשתכחא ביני עצרי ולא מציא פסיא קניא, What are fattened barburim? Rab said: Those which they fatten by force (that is, with food forced into the mouth). But Samuel said: they are fattened as they stand by themselves (that is, left to themselves, with no force applied). And Rabbi Iochanan said: They bring an ox from its pasture which has not yet worked (in tilling the land), and a hen from its dunghill which has not yet laboured (in rearing chicks). Again Rabbi Iochanan said: The choicest of cattle is the ox, and the choicest of birds is the hen. Amimar said: A black hen, pot-bellied (var. fattened at home), which is found among the winepresses (where it grows fat on grape-pips), and cannot step over a reed, because of its fatness. Hence R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in his Commentary on the books of Kings: וברברים אבוסים יש מרבותינו אומרים תרנגולין פתומין, Fattened barburim: there are some of our Doctors who say they are fat cocks. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] understand capons, because העוף המסורס הוא שיתפטם ביותר, a castrated bird grows II, 699 fatter. On this opinion see further what we adduced above b) on the second passage of Chapter 16. ¶

In Aruch Rabbi Iuda ברבורים barburim exponit מיני ביברים, species eorum animalium, quae aluntur in vivariis. Ambigue admodum. Incertum, an pisces innuat, aut volucres, aut feras: utut enim Hebraeum ביבר bibar, Graece βι-βάριον (Gall. vivier) apud Hebraeos c) et Graecos d) recentioris aevi pro piscina sumatur, tamen olim vivarii vox patuit multo latius. Ambitu suo scilicet amplectebatur septa quaevis, in quibus viva pascebantur animalia. Itaque vivariorum tres species facit Gellius libri secundi capite vigesimo, Ornithonas (les volieres), leporaria (les garennes) et piscinas (les estangs). Quamvis ex eodem colligatur, vivaria potius ad feras pertinuisse, ut Graecorum παραδείσους. Vivaria, inquit, quae nunc dicuntur, septa quaedam loca sunt, in quibus ferae vivae pascuntur. Item, Vivaria, quae nunc vulgus dicit, sunt, quos παραδείσους Graeci appellant. Ita plane Hebraei definiunt, quid sit ביבר bibar. Aruch, ביבר הוא פרדס שמאספין שם חיות וחפירה שמאספין שם דגין ומקום מקורה ששם עופות, Bibar est paradisus, in quo colliguntur ferae. Fossa item, in qua congregantur pisces. Locus denique contignatus, in quo sunt aves. Quod sumptum ex Talmudico tractatu De Sabbatho capite decimo tertio folio centesimo sexto b. ubi Gemara, ביברין של חיות ושל עופות ושל דגין אין צדין מהם ביום טוב, In bibarin (seu vivariis) ferarum, avium et piscium venari non licet die festo. Igitur vasta nimis est expositio R. Iudae, qua ברבורים barburim, definit species animalium, quae aluntur in vivariis. Addo, parum esse tutam; quia tota nititur allusione nominis vanissima. Putavit enim R. Iuda, ביבר bibar et ברבור barbur esse voces ejusdem originis, adeoque ביבר bibar dici pro ברבר birbar; litera Res post primam ablata, quomodo periit Lamed in Chaldaeis, aut Syris vocibus ܓܓܘܠܬܐ, et ܩܝܩܘܠܬܐ, et ܨܘܨܠܬܐ, et ܫܘܫܠܬܐ. Cum Hebraeum ביבר bibar, et Graecum βιβάριον, et Hispanicum bivar, et Gallicum vivier a vivarii Romana voce deduci sit planissimum. Tamen hoc sequutus Castalio locum, in quo versamur, ita reddidit: Ejus victus erat in dies singulos similae cori triginta, etc. praeter cervos, capreas, bubalos et vivaria. Quamvis Hebraeis hac in parte sit ineptior, quod vivaria posuit pro iis animalibus, quae aluntur in vivariis. Quod est absurdissimum. Neque enim in cibis col. 129 sunt ipsa vivaria; nec dicerentur אבוסים, saginata. Itaque hanc vocem Castalio praeteriit plane intactam. Obiter nota, in Aruch locum R. Iudae, quo Hebraeum ברברים explicat מיני ביברים, citari ex Vajekra Rabba capite וכי ימוך אחיך, II, 700 quod est trigesimum quartum. Ubi tamen nusquam comparet in editione Veneta anno 1545. quam solam videre licuit. Itaque vel fallitur Aruch, vel mutila est haec editio.

In the Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], Rabbi Judah explains ברבורים barburim as מיני ביברים, kinds of those animals that are kept in vivaria. This is very ambiguous. It is uncertain whether he means fish, birds or wild beasts. For although the Hebrew ביבר bibar, Greek βιβάριον [bibarion] (French vivier), is taken by the Hebrews c) and Greeks d) of a later age for a fishpond, the word vivarium once extended much more widely. Its scope, namely, embraced any enclosures in which living animals were fed. Thus Gellius, book 2, chapter 20, distinguishes three kinds of vivaria: aviaries (les volieres), hare-warrens (les garennes) and fishponds (les estangs). From the same author, however, it may be gathered that vivaria rather concerned wild beasts, like the παράδεισοι [paradeisoi] of the Greeks. “Vivaria,” he says, “as they are now called, are certain enclosed places in which wild beasts are fed alive.” Likewise: “The vivaria, as the common people now say, are what the Greeks call παραδείσους [paradeisous].” The Hebrews define exactly so what a ביבר bibar is. Aruch: ביבר הוא פרדס שמאספין שם חיות וחפירה שמאספין שם דגין ומקום מקורה ששם עופות, “A bibar is a park in which wild beasts are gathered; also a pit in which fish are collected; and finally a roofed place in which there are birds.” This is taken from the Talmudic tractate On the Sabbath, chapter 13, folio 106b, where the Gemara says: ביברין של חיות ושל עופות ושל דגין אין צדין מהם ביום טוב, “From bibarin (or vivaria) of wild beasts, birds and fish one may not hunt on a festival day.” Therefore the explanation of R. Judah, by which he defines ברבורים barburim as kinds of animals that are kept in vivaria, is far too broad. I add that it is hardly safe, because it rests entirely on a most empty play on the name. For R. Judah thought that ביבר bibar and ברבור barbur were words of the same origin, and hence that ביבר bibar was said for ברבר birbar, the letter Resh after the first being dropped, in the way Lamed has disappeared in the Chaldee or Syriac words ܓܓܘܠܬܐ [גגולתא], and ܩܝܩܘܠܬܐ [קיקולתא], and ܨܘܨܠܬܐ [צוצלתא], and ܫܘܫܠܬܐ [שושלתא]. Yet it is perfectly plain that the Hebrew ביבר bibar, the Greek βιβάριον [bibarion], the Spanish bivar and the French vivier derive from the Roman word vivarium. Nevertheless, following this view, Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] rendered the passage we are dealing with thus: “His provision for each day was thirty cors of fine flour,” etc., “besides harts, roes, wild oxen and vivaria.” In this respect, however, he is more inept than the Hebrews, since he put vivaria for the animals that are kept in vivaria. That is utterly absurd. For vivaria themselves are not col. 129 among foods; nor would they be called אבוסים, fattened. And so Castalio passed over this word entirely untouched. Note in passing that in the Aruch the passage of R. Judah in which he explains the Hebrew ברברים as מיני ביברים is cited from Vayikra Rabbah, the chapter וכי ימוך אחיך, II, 700 which is the thirty-fourth. Yet it appears nowhere there in the Venice edition of 1545, the only one I have been able to see. So either the Aruch is mistaken, or this edition is defective. ¶

Tertia interpretatio eorum est, quibus ברברים barburim sunt volucres in genere, quasi אברברים abarburim, ab אבר eber, id est, ab ala. Quod videntur sequi Iosephus, Chaldaeus, Syrus, Arabs, Vulgatus, R. Levi et Mardochaeus. Ergo ex illorum sententia ברבור barbur esset pro אברבר abarbur, formae אספסף asapsuph. Sed res est prope sine exemplo, ut prima radicalis excidat ex Nominibus, quae fiunt ex Quiescentibus Pe Aleph.

The third interpretation is that of those for whom ברברים barburim are birds in general, as if אברברים abarburim, from אבר eber, that is, from wing. This seems to be followed by Josephus, the Chaldee, the Syriac, the Arabic, the Vulgate, R. Levi and Mordecai. So in their opinion ברבור barbur would stand for אברבר abarbur, of the pattern of אספסף asapsuph. But it is almost without precedent for the first radical to drop out of nouns formed from quiescent Pe Aleph verbs. ¶

Quartam interpretationem ita profert Baal Aruch ex libro Vajekra Rabba jam citato: רבנן אמרו עוף טהור הוא ומעולא הוא והיה בא מברבריאה והיה עולה על שולחן שלמה בכל יום, Doctores nostri dixerunt, avem esse mundam et praestantem, quae ex Barbaria afferebatur, et mensae Solomonis quotidie apponebatur. Et Kimchius in libros Regum, ובדברי רז״ל עוף הבא מברבריא ונקראים ברבורים על שם מקומם, In verbis doctorum nostrorum beatae memoriae, avis erat ex Barbaria allata barbur (aut barbar) dicta, juxta nomen loci sui. Et in Le-xico citat illa ipsa verba, quae Baal Aruch. Elias quoque in Thisbi, אומרין שהוא עוף שמן מברבריאה, Ajunt avem esse pinguem e Barbaria, et reliqua, ut in aliis. Quae interpretatio non debuit a doctis tanto sibilo excipi; quasi Barbariae nomen pro certa regione sit hodie et heri natum. Putantur scilicet Arabes non ita pridem Barbariae nomen Numidiae indidisse, aut, ut nonnullis placet, toti Africae parti, quae mari mediterraneo objacet ab Aegypto ad Gades usque. Cujus appellationis causas, qui harum rerum curiosus est, reperiet apud Scaligerum in Cardanum Exercitatione quinquagesima prima, Leonem Africanum libri primi capite secundo, et Marmolium Africae suae libri primi capite sexto. Et vero Barbariae nomen pro ea parte Africae, quam diximus, nec in Strabone, nec in Mela, nec in Plinio, nec in Marciano, nec in Ptolemaeo reperire est, nec in ullo geographorum veteris aevi. Illud tamen ab Arabibus fuisse inditum, falsissimum est: cum Iustiniani aevo jam obtinuerit, diu antequam eo se conferrent Arabes. Hinc Iustiniano aequalis Anastasius Sinaita in Anagogis super sex dierum opus, Ἐν τοῖς πλησιοχώροις Αἰγύπτου τοῖς ἐν Πενταπόλει τῇ Βαρβαρικῇ λεγομένοις ἀδύτοις. Quibus verbis Cyrenaica Pentapolis in Africae Barbaria constituitur. Et Barbaricini vocabantur, qui a Vandalis circa illa tempora ex Mauritaniae monte Aurasio in Sardiniam translati sunt. Ita testantur Procopius libro secundo Vandalicorum, et Gregorius Epistola vigesima quinta, et vigesima septima libri tertii. Sed et aliam in veterum scriptis Barbariam reperio, cujus nomen multo est antiquius: nempe Barbariam Aethiopicam, quae rursus duplex. Una est Barbaria proprie dicta, altera Troglodytica. Utraque II, 701 mari praetenta est; haec Arabico, et Adulico, et Avaliti, illa Barbarico sinui. Barbariae nomine illam solam intelligunt Ptolemaeus et Marcianus Heracleota. Sed alii Barbaris etiam Arabici maris accolas et Troglodytas accensent. Sic Arrianus in Periplo locat Barbaros, et Barbariam circa Berenicen, et Orinen, et Diodori insulam, et Ptolemaida theron, et Avalite emporium, quae loca sunt Troglodyticae. Et Stephanus, in libro De urbibus, Barbariam scribit esse πα-ρὰ τὸν Ἀράβιον κόλπον, ubi Troglodytica. Arabs geographus itidem ارض col. 130 بربرة terram Barbaram, vel Barbariae, describit الي البحر اليماني ad mare Iemanense, id est, Arabicum, Clim. 1. part. 6. Et capite praecedenti describit مدن الحبشة الساحلية Abissinorum maritima oppida الي ما اتصل بها من عمارات البربرة donec perveniatur ad loca habitata Barbariae. Et Alcamus ad vocem بربر Barbar, بربر جيل البرابرة وهم بالغرب وامة اخري بين الحبوش والزنج يقطعون مذاكير الرجال ويجعلونها مهور نسايهم وكلهم ولد كيس غيلان او هم بطنان من حمير صنهاجة وكتامة صاروا الي البربر ايام فتح افريقيس الملك افريقية Barbar, gens Barbarorum, qui sunt ad occasum. Est et alia gens inter Abissinos et Zingitanos, quae virorum abscindit virilia, et ea pro dote uxoribus suis assignat (quomodo David ducentorum praeputiorum dote uxorem sibi acquisivit 1 Sam. XVIII, 25. 27. et 2 Sam. III, 14.). Omnes illi filii sunt Caisgailani, aut ex Humeiri stirpe (a quo Homeritae dicti). Sunhagia autem et Cutama in Barbariam profecti sunt, cum rex Africus Africam aperuit. Sed et geographi recentiores in Avalite sinu, Troglodyticae parte, urbem Barbaram, vel Barboram describunt, et ejusdem nominis insulam urbi proximam, ad quam Zeila solventem appulisse se dicit Vartomanus Navigat. lib. 2. cap. 16. Et de eadem multa habet Odoardus Barbosa in India sua capite De Barbora. Unde facilis conciliatio Galeni cum Dioscoride: quorum ille zingiber afferri tradit ἀπὸ τῆς Βαρβαρίας, ex Barbaria; hic ex Troglodytica. Nempe eadem est regio Barbaria et Troglodytis. Quod cum nesciret Plinius, perperam accepit, quod le-gerat apud Graecos, myrrham Troglodyticam etiam dici Barbaram, ac si eam myrrhae speciem vellent esse aspectu sordidam et barbaram. Ita enim interpretatur libri duodecimi capite decimo sexto. Cum contra in Dioscoride legatur esse διαυγὴς splendens, aut pellucida. Hinc Isidori lib. 17. cap. 8. a puritate commendatur. Myrrha, inquit, Troglodyta ab insula Arabiae, ubi melior colligitur et purior. Proinde laudatur his verbis in veteri Hymno De epiphania Domini: Thus Deo, myrrham Troglodytem humando. Ita enim esse legendum, non, II, 702 ut vulgo, trocletem, pridem Cassander recte monuit. Dicebatur ergo Barbara myrrha Troglodytica, non tanquam aspectu sordida, sed ratione soli natalis. Neque alia ratio, cur Gen. X, 3. Thogarma ברבריא Barbariam reddat Ierosolymitanus interpres, quam fallax allusio nominum inter Thogarma et τρώγλην, unde Troglitis, vel Troglodytis. Ex his constat plus satis, Barbariae nomine contineri totam oram maritimam Orientalis Aethiopiae, ab Aegypti finibus usque ad Rhaptam promontorium. Cui videtur id nominis obtigisse propter incultos mores. Arrianus, Ἀτακτότεροι δὲ οἱ κατοικοῦντες τὸν τόπον Βάρβα- col. 131 ροι. Vel propter sermonem barbarum et inconditum, si sermo dici queat, qui verius vespertilionum stridor est. Herodotus Musa quarta, Οἱ Τρωγλοδύται Αἰθίοπες γλῶσσαν οὐδεμίῃ ἄλλῃ παρομοίην νενομίκασι, ἀλλὰ τετρύγασι καθά-περ αἱ νυκτερίδες. Plinius lib. 5. cap. 8. Victus (illis) serpentium carnes, stridorque, non vox: adeo sermonis commercio carent. Martianus Capella libro sexto, Stridunt potius quam loquuntur. Ut Solinus, Ignari sermonis stridunt potius quam loquuntur. Merito igitur Barbari dicti. Sic enim proprie appellantur οἱ δυσεκφόρως, καὶ σκληρῶς, καὶ τραχέως λαλοῦντες, ut pluribus docet Strabo in eum Homeri versum e), in quo Cares nominat Βαρβαροφώνους. Quo ipso sensu Graeci, Chaldaei, Arabes, Latini interpretes Psal. CXIV, 1. populum barbarum eum vocant, quem Hebraei עם לועז. Is enim Hebraeis est לועז, qui lingua aliena loquitur, eaque absurda et incondita. Neque aliter بربار barbar explicant Arabum lexicographi. Giggejus, بربار (barbar) garrulus, strepitum edens, vociferans. بربرة (barbara) balatus caprinus, nugacitas, sermo irati, qui parum explanatis vocibus solet esse praeruptus, ut est apud Senecam. Golius, بربر (barbara Verbum) murmuravit ut iracundi solent: quod petitum ex Giauhari. Sed Giggejo praeivit Alcamus, cujus verba sunt: البربرة صوت المعز وكثرة الكلام والجلبة والصياح (Albarbara) vox caprae, multiloquium, murmur et vociferatio. Ergo Barbariae nomen tam Chaldaicum et Arabicum fuit, quam Graecum, adeoque Iudaeis et Syris notissimum, in quorum libris passim occurrit. Exempla ex Talmude et paraphrastis habentur in Baal Aruch: ex Ielammedenu in Buxtorfio: ex Semoth Rabba et Esther Rabba in Davide Cohen de Lara. Quibus addo ex Gorionide de Arabum rege Areta, in Herodem depugnaturo, lib. 5. cap. 18 et 19. Congregat omnem militiam suam, militiam scilicet Arabiae, וצבא ברבר, et militiam barbariae. Et paullo post Iudaei multa spolia referunt ex Arabibus, ומן ברבר, et ex Barbaris. Eo nimirum aevo Barbariae pars Arabum imperio suberat. Quin et Barbari remotiores II, 703 Rhaptam usque Felicis Arabiae reges pro dominis agnoscebant. Unde Arrianus de Rhapta, et regione circa Rhaptam: Νέμεται δὲ αὐτὴν κατά τι δίκαιον ἀρχαῖον, ὑποπίπτουσαν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τῆς πρώτης γινομένης Ἀραβίας, ὁ Μω-φαρίτης τύραννος· Possidet autem ipsam, antiquo quodam jure regno primae Arabiae subjectam, Mopharites tyrannus. Eosdem Barbaros a Talmudicis significari puto, cùm in tractatu Iebamoth fol. 63. b. stultum illum populum, de quo Moses Deut. XXXII, 21. dicunt esse אנשי ברבריא שמהלכין ערומין בשוק, viros Barbariae, qui nudi per vicos ambulant. Ii enim aut nudi erant, aut pelle semitecti. Dionysius de Erembis, seu Troglodytis, versu 965.

Baal Aruch [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh] gives the fourth interpretation from the book Vajekra Rabba, already cited, as follows: רבנן אמרו עוף טהור הוא ומעולא הוא והיה בא מברבריאה והיה עולה על שולחן שלמה בכל יום, Our teachers said that it is a clean and excellent bird, which was brought from Barbaria and was served daily at the table of Solomon. And Kimchi on the books of Kings: ובדברי רז״ל עוף הבא מברבריא ונקראים ברבורים על שם מקומם, In the words of our teachers of blessed memory, it was a bird brought from Barbaria, called barbur (or barbar) after the name of its place. And in the Lexicon he cites the very same words as Baal Aruch. Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] too, in the Thisbi: אומרין שהוא עוף שמן מברבריאה, They say it is a fat bird from Barbaria, and the rest, as in the others. This interpretation ought not to have been met with such hissing by the learned, as though the name Barbaria for a particular region had been born only today or yesterday. For it is supposed that the Arabs not long ago gave the name Barbaria to Numidia, or, as some hold, to the whole part of Africa that lies along the Mediterranean Sea from Egypt as far as Gades. Anyone curious about such matters will find the reasons for this name in Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)], Exercitation 51 against Cardano, in Leo Africanus, book 1, chapter 2, and in Marmol, Africa, book 1, chapter 6. And indeed the name Barbaria for the part of Africa we have mentioned is not to be found in Strabo, nor in Mela, nor in Pliny, nor in Marcianus, nor in Ptolemy, nor in any geographer of antiquity. Yet it is utterly false that it was given by the Arabs, since it was already current in the age of Justinian, long before the Arabs went there. Hence Anastasius Sinaita, a contemporary of Justinian, in his Anagogical Contemplations on the Hexaemeron: Ἐν τοῖς πλησιοχώροις Αἰγύπτου τοῖς ἐν Πενταπόλει τῇ Βαρβαρικῇ λεγομένοις ἀδύτοις [En tois plēsiochōrois Aigyptou tois en Pentapolei tē Barbarikē legomenois adytois]. In these words the Cyrenaic Pentapolis is placed in the Barbaria of Africa. And those whom the Vandals around that time transferred from Mount Aurasius in Mauretania to Sardinia were called Barbaricini. So testify Procopius in book 2 of the Vandal War, and Gregory, Epistles 25 and 27 of book 3. But I also find in the writings of the ancients another Barbaria, whose name is much older: namely the Ethiopian Barbaria, which is in turn twofold. One is Barbaria properly so called, the other the Troglodytic. Both II, 701 lie along the sea: the latter along the Arabian, Adulic and Avalite gulfs, the former along the Barbaric gulf. By the name Barbaria Ptolemy and Marcianus of Heraclea understand only the former. But others also count among the Barbarians the dwellers by the Arabian Sea and the Troglodytes. Thus Arrian in the Periplus places the Barbarians and Barbaria around Berenice, Orine, the island of Diodorus, Ptolemais Theron, and the emporium of Avalites, which are places of the Troglodytica. And Stephanus, in his book On Cities, writes that Barbaria is παρὰ τὸν Ἀράβιον κόλπον [para ton Arabion kolpon], where the Troglodytica is. The Arab geographer likewise describes ارض col. 130 بربرة [arḍ barbara], the Barbaran land, or land of Barbaria, الي البحر اليماني [ilā al-baḥr al-yamānī] towards the Yemenite sea, that is, the Arabian, Clim. [=climate (section) of al-Idrīsī's geography, in the Latin Geographia Nubiensis] 1, section 6. And in the preceding chapter he describes مدن الحبشة الساحلية [mudun al-ḥabasha al-sāḥiliyya] the coastal towns of the Abyssinians الي ما اتصل بها من عمارات البربرة [ilā mā ittaṣala bihā min ʿimārāt al-barbara] until one reaches the inhabited places of Barbaria. And Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] on the word بربر [barbar] Barbar: بربر جيل البرابرة وهم بالغرب وامة اخري بين الحبوش والزنج يقطعون مذاكير الرجال ويجعلونها مهور نسايهم وكلهم ولد كيس غيلان او هم بطنان من حمير صنهاجة وكتامة صاروا الي البربر ايام فتح افريقيس الملك افريقية [barbar jīl al-barābira wa-hum bi-l-gharb wa-umma ukhrā bayna al-ḥubūsh wa-l-zanj yaqṭaʿūna madhākīr al-rijāl wa-yajʿalūnahā muhūr nisāyihim wa-kulluhum walad kays ghaylān aw hum baṭnān min ḥimyar ṣanhāja wa-kutāma ṣārū ilā al-barbar ayyām fatḥ ifrīqīs al-malik ifrīqiya] Barbar, the nation of the Barbarians, who are in the west. There is also another nation between the Abyssinians and the Zinj, which cuts off men's genitals and assigns them as dowry to their wives (just as David acquired a wife for himself with a dowry of two hundred foreskins, 1 Sam 18:25 27. and 2 Sam 3:14). All of them are sons of Cais Gailan, or of the stock of Humeir (from whom the Homerites are named). But Sunhagia and Cutama went into Barbaria when the African king opened up Africa. And more recent geographers too describe, in the Avalite gulf, a part of the Troglodytica, a city Barbara, or Barbora, and an island of the same name next to the city, at which Varthema says he landed after setting sail from Zeila, Navigat. book 2, ch. 16. And Duarte Barbosa has much about the same in his India, in the chapter On Barbora. From this Galen is easily reconciled with Dioscorides: the former reports that ginger is brought ἀπὸ τῆς Βαρβαρίας [apo tēs Barbarias], from Barbaria; the latter, from the Troglodytica. For Barbaria and Troglodytis are the same region. Not knowing this, Pliny misunderstood what he had read in the Greeks, that Troglodytic myrrh is also called Barbaran, as if they meant that this kind of myrrh was squalid and barbarous in appearance. For so he interprets it in book 12, chapter 16. Whereas, on the contrary, in Dioscorides it is said to be διαυγὴς [diaugēs], shining or translucent. Hence in Isidore, book 17, ch. 8, it is commended for its purity: Myrrh, he says, Troglodyte, from an island of Arabia, where a better and purer kind is gathered. Accordingly it is praised in these words in the old Hymn On the Epiphany of the Lord: Incense to God, myrrh Troglodyte for burial. For that is how it should be read, not, II, 702 as commonly, trocletem, as Cassander rightly pointed out long ago. Troglodytic myrrh was therefore called Barbaran, not as squalid in appearance, but by reason of its native soil. Nor is there any other reason why at Gen 10:3 the Jerusalem translator renders Thogarma as ברבריא Barbaria than a deceptive play on the names Thogarma and τρώγλην [trōglēn], whence Troglitis or Troglodytis. From this it is more than sufficiently clear that the name Barbaria comprises the whole seacoast of Eastern Ethiopia, from the borders of Egypt as far as the promontory of Rhapta. It seems to have received this name because of its uncivilized customs. Arrian: Ἀτακτότεροι δὲ οἱ κατοικοῦντες τὸν τόπον Βάρβα- col. 131 ροι [Ataktoteroi de hoi katoikountes ton topon Barbaroi]. Or because of its barbarous and uncouth speech, if one can call speech what is more truly the squeaking of bats. Herodotus, Muse 4: Οἱ Τρωγλοδύται Αἰθίοπες γλῶσσαν οὐδεμίῃ ἄλλῃ παρομοίην νενομίκασι, ἀλλὰ τετρύγασι καθάπερ αἱ νυκτερίδες [Hoi Trōglodytai Aithiopes glōssan oudemiē allē paromoiēn nenomikasi, alla tetrygasi kathaper hai nykterides]. Pliny, book 5, ch. 8: Their (food) is the flesh of serpents, and they have a squeak, not a voice: so wholly do they lack the exchange of speech. Martianus Capella, book 6: They squeak rather than speak. As Solinus: Ignorant of speech, they squeak rather than speak. Rightly, then, were they called Barbarians. For properly so are called οἱ δυσεκφόρως, καὶ σκληρῶς, καὶ τραχέως λαλοῦντες [hoi dysekphorōs, kai sklērōs, kai tracheōs lalountes], as Strabo shows at length on that verse of Homer e) in which he calls the Carians Βαρβαροφώνους [Barbarophōnous]. In this very sense the Greek, Chaldee, Arabic and Latin translators at Ps 114:1 call barbarous the people which the Hebrews call עם לועז. For to the Hebrews לועז is one who speaks a foreign tongue, and that absurd and uncouth. Nor do the lexicographers of the Arabs explain بربار [barbār] barbar otherwise. Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)]: بربار [barbār] (barbar) garrulous, making a din, clamouring. بربرة [barbara] (barbara) the bleating of goats, triviality, the speech of an angry man, which is usually abrupt with ill-articulated words, as it is in Seneca. Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: بربر [barbara] (barbara, verb) he grumbled as angry men do: which is taken from Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ]. But Alcamus preceded Giggeius; his words are: البربرة صوت المعز وكثرة الكلام والجلبة والصياح [al-barbara ṣawt al-maʿz wa-kathrat al-kalām wa-l-jalaba wa-l-ṣiyāḥ] (Albarbara) the voice of the goat, much talking, clamour and shouting. Therefore the name Barbaria was as much Chaldee and Arabic as Greek, and so was very well known to the Jews and Syrians, in whose books it occurs everywhere. Examples from the Talmud and the paraphrasts are found in Baal Aruch; from the Ielammedenu in Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum]; from Semoth Rabba and Esther Rabba in David Cohen de Lara. To these I add from Gorionides, on Aretas, king of the Arabs, about to fight against Herod, book 5, ch. 18 and 19: He gathers all his army, the army, that is, of Arabia, וצבא ברבר, and the army of barbaria. And shortly after, the Jews bring back much spoil from the Arabs, ומן ברבר, and from the Barbarians. In that age, of course, part of Barbaria was subject to the rule of the Arabs. Indeed even the more remote Barbarians II, 703 as far as Rhapta acknowledged the kings of Arabia Felix as their lords. Hence Arrian on Rhapta and the region around Rhapta: Νέμεται δὲ αὐτὴν κατά τι δίκαιον ἀρχαῖον, ὑποπίπτουσαν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τῆς πρώτης γινομένης Ἀραβίας, ὁ Μωφαρίτης τύραννος [Nemetai de autēn kata ti dikaion archaion, hypopiptousan tē basileia tēs prōtēs ginomenēs Arabias, ho Mōpharitēs tyrannos]· The Mopharite tyrant possesses it, subject by some ancient right to the kingdom of the first Arabia. I think the same Barbarians are meant by the Talmudists when, in the tractate Iebamoth, fol. 63b, they say that the foolish people of whom Moses speaks in Deut 32:21 are אנשי ברבריא שמהלכין ערומין בשוק, the men of Barbaria, who walk naked through the streets. For they were either naked or half-covered with a hide. Dionysius on the Erembi, or Troglodytes, verse 965. ¶

Et Strabo libro decimo sexto, Γυμνῆται δὲ, καὶ δερματοφόροι, καὶ σκυταλη-φόροι διατελοῦσιν· Nudi degunt, aut pelliti et clavigeri. In Africa igitur duplex fuit Barbaria: una occidentalis usque ad Gades; altera orientalis ad Arabicum sinum, et orientalem Oceanum, Rhaptam usque protensa. De hac porro, non de illa Hebraeos egisse, certum est, cum ברברים barburim, vel barbarim, voluerunt esse a Barbaria denominatas, unde ad Selomonem allatae fuerint. Petita enim est haec interpretatio ex libro Vajekra Rabba, cujus auctor Rabba, col. 132 Nachmoni filius, floruit circa annum Christi trecentesimum. Quo aevo Barbariae in occidente nulla mentio. Neque tamen is de suo loquitur, sed citat majores suos, Iudam et Barachiam. Praeterea quocunque nomine vocentur illi Barbari, qui sunt ad occasum Africae, Solomon nullum habuit cum illis commercium. Neque enim in mediterraneo, sed in rubro mari classem instruebat Solomon Aelanis et Esion-gabere, unde in occidentales non fuit iter, sed in orientales Barbaros, ac inde afferri potuere meleagrides, et ברברים barburim, vel barbarim denominari, id est, aves Barbarae, vel Barbaricae. Quia quo tempore harum avium magna erat raritas et penuria, illis fuit summe fertilis rubri maris insula quaedam, ad Barbariam Troglodytarum pertinens. Hoc idonei auctores scribunt, praeter Agatharchidem Diodorus et Artemidorus apud Strabonem. Hujus verba sic habent libro decimo sexto: Φησὶ δ᾽ Ἀρτεμίδωρος ἀπὸ Ἡρώων πόλεως πλέουσι κατὰ τὴν Τρωγλοδυτικὴν πόλιν εἶναι Φιλοτέραν, etc. Εἶτα Μύος ὅρμον, καὶ Ἀφροδίτης ὅρμον καλεῖσθαι λιμένα μέγαν, τὸν εἴσπλουν ἔχοντα σκολιόν· προκεῖσθαι δὲ νήσους τρεῖς, δύο μὲν ἐλαίαις κατασκίους, μίαν δ᾽ ἧττον κατάσκιον, μελεαγρίδων μεστήν· Dicit Artemidorus, ab Heroum urbe navigantibus juxta Troglodyticam urbem Philoteram esse, etc. Deinde portum II, 704 magnum ingressu flexuoso, qui dicitur Muris statio, et Veneris statio. Huic objacere tres insulas, duas oleis obumbratas, tertiam minus opacam, meleagrīdum avium plenam. Et Diodorus libro 2. pag. 120. Ὑπέρκεινται δὲ τούτου νῆσοι τρεῖς, ὧν δύο μὲν πλήρεις εἰσὶν ἐλαιῶν, καὶ σύκων· μία δὲ λειπομένη τῷ πλήθει τῶν προειρημένων δένδρων· πλῆθος δ᾽ ἔχουσα τῶν ὀνομαζομένων μελεαγρίδων. Huic adjacent tres insulae, quarum duae oleis et ficubus scatent; una autem, his arboribus minus foecunda, iis avibus referta est, quae dicuntur meleagrides. Et utroque antiquior Agatharchides, De mari rubro libro quinto cap. 42. Ἐν οἷς καὶ νῆσοι τρεῖς εἰσι προσκειμέναι· ὧν αἱ μὲν ἐλαίαις πεπύκνωνται, μία δὲ ἧτ-τον μὲν δασεῖα, τῶν δὲ καλουμένων μελεαγρίδων ἐκτρέφουσα πλῆθος. Ubi tres insulae adjacent: quarum duae olivas crebras habent, una minus quidem sylvosa est, sed avium, quas meleagrides vocant, copiam nutrit. Ut appareat, hanc insulam illarum avium proventu fuisse celeberrimam.

And Strabo, in book 16: Γυμνῆται δὲ, καὶ δερματοφόροι, καὶ σκυταληφόροι διατελοῦσιν· [Gymnētai de, kai dermatophoroi, kai skytalēphoroi diatelousin] They live naked, or clad in skins and carrying clubs. In Africa, therefore, there was a twofold Barbaria: one western, extending as far as Gades; the other eastern, extending to the Arabian Gulf and the eastern Ocean, as far as Rhapta. It is certain, moreover, that the Hebrews were speaking of the latter, not the former, when they held that the ברברים barburim, or barbarim, were named after Barbaria, from which they had been brought to Solomon. For this interpretation is taken from the book Vajekra Rabba, whose author, Rabba, col. 132 son of Nachmoni, flourished around the three hundredth year of Christ. In that age there is no mention of a Barbaria in the west. Nor does he speak on his own authority; rather, he cites his predecessors, Judah and Berechiah. Besides, by whatever name those Barbarians who live in the west of Africa may be called, Solomon had no trade with them. For Solomon fitted out his fleet not on the Mediterranean but on the Red Sea, at Aelana and Ezion-geber. From there the route led not to the western but to the eastern Barbarians; and from there guinea fowl could have been brought and called ברברים barburim, or barbarim, that is, Barbarian or Barbaric birds. For at a time when these birds were very rare and scarce, a certain island of the Red Sea belonging to the Barbaria of the Troglodytes was extremely productive of them. This is recorded by reliable authors: besides Agatharchides, by Diodorus and by Artemidorus as cited in Strabo. Strabo's words in book 16 run thus: Φησὶ δ᾽ Ἀρτεμίδωρος ἀπὸ Ἡρώων πόλεως πλέουσι κατὰ τὴν Τρωγλοδυτικὴν πόλιν εἶναι Φιλοτέραν, etc. Εἶτα Μύος ὅρμον, καὶ Ἀφροδίτης ὅρμον καλεῖσθαι λιμένα μέγαν, τὸν εἴσπλουν ἔχοντα σκολιόν· προκεῖσθαι δὲ νήσους τρεῖς, δύο μὲν ἐλαίαις κατασκίους, μίαν δ᾽ ἧττον κατάσκιον, μελεαγρίδων μεστήν· [Phēsi d' Artemidōros apo Hērōōn poleōs pleousi kata tēn Trōglodytikēn polin einai Philoteran, etc. Eita Myos hormon, kai Aphroditēs hormon kaleisthai limena megan, ton eisploun echonta skolion; prokeisthai de nēsous treis, dyo men elaiais kataskious, mian d' hētton kataskion, meleagridōn mestēn] Artemidorus says that for those sailing from the City of the Heroes there is, near the Troglodytic city, Philotera, etc. Then a large harbour II, 704 with a winding entrance, which is called the Mouse's Anchorage, and the Anchorage of Venus. Before it lie three islands, two shaded with olive trees, the third less shaded, full of guinea fowl. And Diodorus, book 2, p. 120: Ὑπέρκεινται δὲ τούτου νῆσοι τρεῖς, ὧν δύο μὲν πλήρεις εἰσὶν ἐλαιῶν, καὶ σύκων· μία δὲ λειπομένη τῷ πλήθει τῶν προειρημένων δένδρων· πλῆθος δ᾽ ἔχουσα τῶν ὀνομαζομένων μελεαγρίδων. [Hyperkeintai de toutou nēsoi treis, hōn dyo men plēreis eisin elaiōn, kai sykōn; mia de leipomenē tō plēthei tōn proeirēmenōn dendrōn; plēthos d' echousa tōn onomazomenōn meleagridōn] Beside it lie three islands, two of which abound in olives and figs; but one, less fertile in these trees, is filled with the birds called guinea fowl. And, older than both, Agatharchides, On the Red Sea, book 5, ch. 42: Ἐν οἷς καὶ νῆσοι τρεῖς εἰσι προσκειμέναι· ὧν αἱ μὲν ἐλαίαις πεπύκνωνται, μία δὲ ἧττον μὲν δασεῖα, τῶν δὲ καλουμένων μελεαγρίδων ἐκτρέφουσα πλῆθος. [En hois kai nēsoi treis eisi proskeimenai; hōn hai men elaiais pepyknōntai, mia de hētton men daseia, tōn de kaloumenōn meleagridōn ektrephousa plēthos] Where three islands lie nearby: two of them are thick with olives; one is indeed less wooded, but it nourishes an abundance of the birds they call guinea fowl. So it appears that this island was most famous for its yield of these birds. ¶

Meleagrides quid proprie sint, non satis constat. Sunt, qui cum Africanis, seu Numidicis gallinis avem eandem esse putent. Varro Rei Rust. lib. 3. cap. 9. Gallinae Africanae sunt grandes, variae, gibberae, quas μελεαγρίδας appellant Graeci. Plinius lib. 10. cap. 26. postquam aves Memnonidas ad Memnonis sepulchrum confligere narravit: Simili modo, inquit, pugnant meleagrides in Boeotia (nempe ad Meleagri sepulchrum). Africae hoc est gallinarum genus gibberum, variis sparsum plumis, etc. Columella tamen ita distinguit libri noni capite secundo. II, 708 I shall now say why I think that ברברים אבוסים are neither fattened hens, nor guinea fowl, nor birds in general. 1. Although among the later Hebrews col. 135 אבס abas is said also of birds that are fattened, yet in Scripture אבוס ebus is the manger of cattle or beasts of burden, not a fattening coop for birds. And אבס abas, derived from it, is applied only to cattle. Hence, when those who had come with Cadmus occupied the island of Euboea, as is found in Strabo, their leader was called אבס Abas, that is, fattener of cattle. From this, with a Greek inflection, the inhabitants themselves were called Abantes R3. For the greatest Poet calls them by no other name. So too the island was called Εὔβοια [Euboia] from oxen, and Ἀργιβόειος [Argiboeios] in Aelian, book 12, chapter 36. For that this island was long ago famous for its ox pastures is shown by the oxen driven off from Euboea by Autolycus, of which Apollodorus speaks in book 2 of the Bibliotheca, and Tzetzes, Chil. 8. Accordingly, at the beginning of the Peloponnesian War the Athenians πρόβατα, καὶ ὑποζύγια εἰς τὴν Εὔβοιαν διεπέμψαντο [probata, kai hypozygia eis tēn Euboian diepempsanto], sent their sheep and beasts of burden to Euboea, as Thucydides has it in book 2; and on the coinage of the Euboeans an ox's head was engraved. So Antonius Augustinus testifies in Dialogue 3 On Coins. But on Euboea see more in our Chanaan. 2. If by ברברים אבוסים the Sacred Writer meant to signify birds, of which there had been no mention before, there appears no reason why, content with epithets alone, he did not expressly name צפורים, that is, birds, so that there would be no room for doubt. 3. The same objection can be raised regarding the gallinaceous kind. For no one would say that ברברים are cocks or hens. 4. If the ברבורים had their name from Barbary, they would not be called barburim, but barbarim. 5. And although the eastern coast of Africa long ago bore the name of Barbary, yet that this was already in use in the age of Solomon cannot be proved by any argument. 6. Finally, who would believe that guinea fowl, or birds of Barbary, whatever they may have been, came into the reckoning of Solomon's provisions rather than so many others native to Judaea and the neighbouring regions, which were set daily on the royal table? 7.
