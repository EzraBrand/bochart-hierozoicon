II, 520 col. 1090 Chapter VIII. ¶

Cur Idolorum arae dicantur esse ὡς χελῶναι, ut testudines, in Graeca versione Os. XII, 12. Et de chelonide Holofernis, Iudith. XIV, 13.

Why the altars of the idols are said to be ὡς χελῶναι [hōs chelōnai], like tortoises, in the Greek version of Hos 12:12. And on the chelonis of Holofernes, Jdt 14:13. ¶

Nulla vox est Hebraeis notior, quam גל gal, pro acervo lapidum. Inde nomen habuit Galaad, mons celeberrimus in terra Chanaan, Gen. XXXI, 48. Et Gallim, oppidum prope Accaronem, de quo 1 Sam. XXV, 44. Es. X, 30. col. 1091 Eusebius et Hieronymus in locis. Achane lapidibus obruto, quia de anathemate acceperat, in rei memoriam super eum congestus est lapidum magnus גל gal, (seu acervus) Ios. VII, 26. Ita etiam super rebellis Absalomi cadaver, 2 Sam. XVIII, 17. Domus quoque, et urbes, quae diruuntur, redigi leguntur לגל, in gal (id est, acervum), Es. XXV, 2. aut לגלים, in gallim (id est, acervos), Iob. XV, 28. et Ierem. IX, 11. et LI, 37. Et fieri גלים נצים gallim nitsim (id est, vasti acervi), 2 Reg. XIX, 25. Itaque nihil obscurum est in his Os. XII, 11. (al. 12.): Arae eorum כגלים, ut gallim (seu lapidum acervi) super porcas agri. Agit Propheta de aris, quas Galgalis idololatrae Diis suis non solum in urbe exstruxerant, sed et in vicinis agris: et confert cum acervis lapidum, ab iis congeri solitis, qui lapidosos agros expurgant. Graeci tamen interpretes, quibus גל gal alibi est βουνὸς, tumulus, χῶμα agger, σωρὸς acervus, συναγωγὴ λίθων collectio lapidum, in hoc Oseae loco Plurale גלים gallim plane aliter, nempe χε-λώνας testudines reddunt. Τὰ θυσιαστήρια αὐτῶν (inquiunt), ὡς χελῶναι ἐπὶ χέρσον ἀγροῦ, Altaria eorum ut testudines super desertum agri. Et ad illa verba II, 521 Theodoretus testudinem observat, in aquis natantem, piscatorum manus facilius evadere, cum eadem in terra gradiens sit πρόχειρος, καὶ εὐάλωτως· quomodo futurum erat, ut Galgalitarum arae facile caperentur, et ab hostibus subverterentur. Maluit Hieronymus, quae de altaribus dicuntur, transferre ad haereticos, qui, testudinum instar, sunt tardi gressibus, et in uno moventur loco, etc. Quibus fortasse prudentior videbitur Cyrillus, cui χελῶναι hoc loco non sunt animalia, sed αἱ τῶν χωμάτων ἐγέρσεις, aggerum exstructiones: ut Drusio etiam testudines significant γεωλόφους terrae tumulos. Et his libenter accederem, si quod daretur exemplum similis locutionis. Sed, cum nullum proferatur, duo habeo, quae conjiciam. Unum, pro ὡς χελῶναι, legendum esse ὡς κολῶναι, quae vox pro tumulis saepe usurpatur. Ut Iliad. β΄. versu 811.

No word is better known to the Hebrews than גל gal, for a heap of stones. From it Galaad, a very famous mountain in the land of Canaan, took its name, Gen 31:48. So too Gallim, a town near Accaron, on which see 1 Sam 25:44. Isa 10:30 col. 1091 and Eusebius and Jerome in the Places. When Achan had been stoned because he had taken from the devoted things, a great גל gal of stones (that is, a heap) was piled over him as a memorial of the deed, Josh 7:26. Likewise over the corpse of the rebel Absalom, 2 Sam 18:17. Houses, too, and cities that are demolished are said to be reduced לגל, to a gal (that is, a heap), Isa 25:2 or לגלים, to gallim (that is, heaps), Job 15:28 and Jer 9:11 and LI, 37. And to become גלים נצים gallim nitsim (that is, desolate heaps), 2 Reg. XIX, 25. There is therefore nothing obscure in these words of Hos 12:11 (var. 12.): Their altars are כגלים, like gallim (or heaps of stones) on the furrows of the field. The Prophet is speaking of the altars which the idolaters of Gilgal had built to their gods not only in the city but also in the neighbouring fields; and he compares them with the heaps of stones customarily piled up by those who clear stony fields. The Greek translators, however, for whom גל gal is elsewhere βουνὸς [bounos], a mound, χῶμα [chōma] an embankment, σωρὸς [sōros] a heap, συναγωγὴ λίθων [synagōgē lithōn] a collection of stones, render the plural גלים gallim in this passage of Hosea quite differently, namely as χελώνας [chelōnas], tortoises. Τὰ θυσιαστήρια αὐτῶν [Ta thysiastēria autōn] (they say), ὡς χελῶναι ἐπὶ χέρσον ἀγροῦ [hōs chelōnai epi cherson agrou], Their altars are like tortoises on the waste of the field. And on these words II, 521 Theodoret observes that the tortoise, when swimming in the water, escapes the hands of fishermen more easily, whereas when walking on land it is πρόχειρος, καὶ εὐάλωτως [procheiros, kai eualōtōs], ready to hand and easily caught; so it would come about that the altars of the Gilgalites would easily be taken and overthrown by their enemies. Jerome preferred to apply what is said of the altars to the heretics, who, like tortoises, are slow of step and move about in one place, etc. Wiser than these, perhaps, will seem Cyril, for whom χελῶναι [chelōnai] in this passage are not animals but αἱ τῶν χωμάτων ἐγέρσεις [hai tōn chōmatōn egerseis], the raising of embankments; just as for Drusius too tortoises signify γεωλόφους [geōlophous], mounds of earth. And I would gladly agree with them if any example of a similar expression were given. But since none is adduced, I have two conjectures. The first is that instead of ὡς χελῶναι [hōs chelōnai] we should read ὡς κολῶναι [hōs kolōnai], a word often used for mounds. As in the Iliad, β΄ [book 2], line 811. ¶

Ἔστι δέ τις προπάροιθε πόλεως αἰπεῖα κολώνη,

Ἔστι δέ τις προπάροιθε πόλεως αἰπεῖα κολώνη, [Esti de tis propároithe poleōs aipeia kolōnē,] ¶

Est autem prope urbem celsus quidam tumulus. Et Apollonii Argonauticῶn lib. 1. versu 1114.

Now there is near the city a certain lofty mound. And in Apollonius' Argonautica, book 1, line 1114. ¶

Φαίνετο δ᾽ ἠερόεν στόμα Βοσπόρου, ἠδὲ κολῶναι Μύσιαι. — — — — — —

Φαίνετο δ᾽ ἠερόεν στόμα Βοσπόρου, ἠδὲ κολῶναι Μύσιαι. — — — — — — [Phaineto d' ēeroen stoma Bosporou, ēde kolōnai Mysiai.] ¶

Apparebat autem nebulosum os Bosphori et juga Mysiae. Utuntur et prosaici scriptores: ut Polemo apud Athenaeum libro decimo tertio, et alius apud Suidam: et Pausanias in Laconicis, ubi de Colona, Spartae loco. Hesychio κολώ-νη est τόπος ὑψηλὸς, καὶ ἐπανάστημα γῆς βουνοειδὲς, locus celsus, et terrae eminentia in tumuli modum. Et Homeri Scholiastae, τόπος εἰς ὕψος ἀνήκων, καὶ γεώλεφος ἐξοχὴ, locus in altum elatus, et clivosa prominentia. Altera conjectura est גלים gallim, hic χελώνας, seu testudines, ex usu Syrae linguae, reddi, qua ܓܠܐ gala est testudo. Itaque Syrus hic habet ܐܝܟ ܓܠܐ, id est, quasi testudines, juxta Gabrielem Sionitam. Et in Syro Lexico Iohannis Baptistae Ferrarii pagina 107. ܓܠܐ gala testudo redditur. Tamen in Thesauro Thomae a Novaria pagina 241. Syrae voces ܫܠܘܦܝܬܐ seluphita, et ܥܓܠܐ gala, sic distinguuntur, ut illa testudinem in genere significet, haec nonnisi masculum testudinis. Quod puto esse verius; quia eundem Arabes non nisi allusione ad hoc verbum غيلم gailam vocant. Ita testantur Giauhari, Alcamus, Damir, alii. Et nomen a libidine factum. Quippe غلم galam libidinem sonat, et غلم galima Verbum libidine ardere. Unde marem testudinis merito غيلم col. 1092 gailam appellari, doceant haec Aeliani Historiae libri decimi quinti capite decimo nono, Χερσαία χελώνη ζῶον λαγνίστατον· ἀλλὰ ὅγε ἄῤῥην. ὁμιλεῖ δὲ ἡ θή-λεια ἄκουσα. Terrestris testudo animal est salacissimum; illius mas scilicet: foemina enim nonnisi invita coit. Et a coitu scribit Damostratus illam ideo abhorrere; quia, cum a mare resupinata propter nimium testae pondus non possit se II, 522 erigere, post coitum aquilae in praedam patet et aliis animantibus. Sed puto verius, quod alii scribunt, eam in ipso coitu gravi dolore affici; quia durum et aculeatum est maris veretrum. Taceo Damostrati fabulam, a Damire non praetermissam, de herba quadam ignota, quam mas in ore habens repugnantem ad se allicit. Interim licet non solis Graecis, sed Syro quoque et Arabi גלים gallim in Osea sint testudines, causam tamen non video, cur a recepta illius loci versione discedamus. Idolorum aras, in agris passim obvias, cum propter vilitatem, tum propter frequentiam, cum iis lapidum acervis, quos diximus, recte conferri, nemo non videt. Ut taceam, eminus intuentibus non absimilem esse speciem. Sed coactum et longe petitum est, quidquid affertur, ut inter easdem aras et testudines adstruatur similitudo. Possunt etiam Oseae verba sumi tanquam prophetice dicta, quasi vaticinetur, Galgalitarum aras brevi futuras כגלים, ut lapidum acervos, qui sunt in agris, id est, brevi diruendas. Neque negligenda est allusio vocis Galgal ad גלים gallim. Ita, ut Galgal et illius arae futurae dicantur ut gallim, seu lapidum acervi. Quomodo Sophon. II, 4. Aza (seu Gaza) erit עזובה (azuba) derelicta. Et Accaron תעקר (theaker) eradicabitur. Ut in Pseudo-Sibyllinis,

And the misty mouth of the Bosphorus and the ridges of Mysia appeared. Prose writers also use it: for example Polemo in Athenaeus, book 13, and another in the Suda; and Pausanias in the Laconica, where he speaks of Colona, a place in Sparta. In Hesychius κολώνη [kolōnē] is τόπος ὑψηλὸς, καὶ ἐπανάστημα γῆς βουνοειδὲς [topos hypsēlos, kai epanastēma gēs bounoeides], a lofty place, and an elevation of the ground in the manner of a mound. And in the scholiasts on Homer, τόπος εἰς ὕψος ἀνήκων, καὶ γεώλεφος ἐξοχὴ [topos eis hypsos anēkōn, kai geōlephos exochē], a place rising to a height, and a hilly prominence. The other conjecture is that גלים gallim is rendered here as χελώνας [chelōnas], or tortoises, following the usage of the Syriac language, in which ܓܠܐ [גלא] gala is tortoise. Accordingly the Syriac here has ܐܝܟ ܓܠܐ [איך גלא], that is, like tortoises, according to Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)]. And in the Syriac Lexicon of Giovanni Battista Ferrari, page 107, ܓܠܐ [גלא] gala is rendered tortoise. Yet in the Thesaurus of Thomas of Novara, page 241, the Syriac words ܫܠܘܦܝܬܐ [שלופיתא] seluphita and ܥܓܠܐ [עגלא] gala are distinguished in such a way that the former signifies the tortoise in general, the latter only the male tortoise. This I think is more correct, since the Arabs call the same animal غيلم [ghaylam] gailam, surely in allusion to this word. So testify Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ], Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author], Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and others. And the name is formed from lust. For غلم [ghalam] galam means lust, and the verb غلم [ghalima] galima means to burn with lust. That the male tortoise is rightly called غيلم [ghaylam] col. 1092 gailam may be shown by these words of Aelian, Historia, book 15, chapter 19: Χερσαία χελώνη ζῶον λαγνίστατον· ἀλλὰ ὅγε ἄῤῥην. ὁμιλεῖ δὲ ἡ θήλεια ἄκουσα [Chersaia chelōnē zōon lagnistaton; alla hoge arrhēn. homilei de hē thēleia akousa]. The land tortoise is a most lustful animal; that is, its male: for the female mates only unwillingly. And Damostratus writes that she shrinks from mating for this reason: because, when she has been turned on her back by the male, she cannot right herself on account of the excessive weight of her shell, II, 522 and after mating lies open as prey to the eagle and to other animals. But I think what others write is more correct, that in the very act of mating she suffers severe pain, because the male's member is hard and spiny. I pass over the fable of Damostratus, not omitted by Damir, about a certain unknown herb which the male holds in his mouth and so draws the resisting female to himself. Meanwhile, although not only for the Greeks but also for the Syriac and the Arabic translators גלים gallim in Hosea are tortoises, I nevertheless see no reason why we should depart from the received rendering of that passage. Everyone sees that the altars of the idols, met everywhere in the fields, are rightly compared with those heaps of stones we have mentioned, both on account of their cheapness and on account of their frequency. Not to mention that to those viewing them from afar their appearance is not dissimilar. But whatever is adduced to establish a likeness between these altars and tortoises is forced and far-fetched. Hosea's words can also be taken as spoken prophetically, as if he foretold that the altars of the people of Gilgal would shortly be כגלים, like heaps of stones that are in the fields, that is, would shortly be demolished. Nor should the allusion of the word Galgal to גלים gallim be neglected, so that Galgal and its altars are said to be destined to become like gallim, or heaps of stones. Just as in Zeph 2:4: Aza (or Gaza) shall be עזובה (azuba) forsaken. And Accaron תעקר (theaker) shall be uprooted. As in the Pseudo-Sibyllines, ¶

Καὶ Ῥώμη ῥύμη ἔσεται, καὶ Δῆλος ἄδηλος·

Καὶ Ῥώμη ῥύμη ἔσεται, καὶ Δῆλος ἄδηλος· [Kai Rhōmē rhymē esetai, kai Dēlos adēlos;] ¶

et in historia Susannae σχίσαι et πρίσαι alludunt ad arborum nomina σχί-νον et πρίνον.

and in the history of Susanna σχίσαι [schisai] and πρίσαι [prisai] allude to the names of the trees σχίνον [schinon] and πρίνον [prinon]. ¶

Cum truncum Holofernis projectum reperisse legitur Bagoas ἐπὶ τῆς χε-τλωνίδος, Iudith. XIV, 13. alii recte observant, pro χετλωνίδος, voce nihili, legendum χελωνίδος, quomodo legerunt Hesychius et Suidas: et χελωνίδα dici ἀπὸ τῆς χελώνης, a testudine. Sed quid tandem sit χελωνὶς, non satis constat. Vulgatus interpres cortinam reddit. Plerique recentiorum stratum, aut lectum; quos facile est refellere. Iudith enim Holoferni caput amputaverat, καὶ ἐπεκύ-λισε τὸ σῶμα αὐτοῦ ἀπὸ τῆς στρωμνῆς, et corpus ejus a strato evolverat, cap. XIII, v. 10. Χελωνὶς igitur, in qua hoc corpus ἐῤῥιμμένον projectum reperit Bagoas, a strato et lecto quid diversum est. Hesychius, in χελωνίδος, exponit οὐδὸς τῆς θύρας τῆς σκηνῆς, limen januae tabernaculi. Ita etiam Suidas ex eo corrigendus, cum scribit ὅδου pro οὐδοῦ. Quod parum est verissimile; cum quia nomen non quadrat, tum quia Holofernis corpus e strato devolutum est in chelonidem. Chelonis igitur non fuit prope limen januae: sed in intimo tabernaculo, in quo etiam ipse Imperatoris lectus. Ne lectorem pluribus detineam, mihi χελωνὶς est קבה kubba. Vox semel in scriptura legitur, nec satis intelligitur: nempe Num. XXV, 8. ubi, Zimri et Kozbi scortantibus, ingressus est Phinees אל הקבה, in kubba, et utrumque transfodit. Quod varii varie explicant. Sed Arabibus قبة kubba, vel kobba, et cum Articulo القبة alkobba, conclave est camerati operis, quo lectus circumdatur. Hispanis alcoba II, 523 dicitur, et alcova, quae vox apud nos quoque est vicinae significationis. Radix قب kabba in secunda Conjugatione concamerare significat, aut cameratum opus struere. Itaque ut musicum instrumentum χέλυς, seu testudo, Arabice vocatur العود alaud, unde Hispanis laud, vel laut, et nobis lut: ita القبة alkobba, col. 1093 seu conclave, quod descripsimus, Arabice alio nomine dicitur ام العود ommolaudi, id est, mater testudinis; quia est camerati et testudinei operis. Apud Arabes in tali القبة alkobba est sponsae thalamus. Et illam eo deducere بنا bana, et زف zappha dicitur. Holofernis igitur thalamus, cum Iuditha, ut putabat, concubituri, in hujusmodi alcobba fuit, quae cur Graece χελωνὶς appelletur, jam cuivis patet. Fortasse etiam χελωνὶς a materia dicta. Olim enim in magnatum lectis et spondis multus fuit testudinis usus. Qui luxus in Oriente primum usurpatus tandem ad Romanos devenit. Tempus, a Plinio notatum libri noni capite undecimo, Testudinum, inquit, putamina secare in laminas, lectosque, et repositoria his vestire, Carbilius Pollio instituit. Et libri decimi sexti col. 1094 capite quadragesimo tertio: Testudo in hoc secta. Nuperque portentosis ingeniis principatu Neronis inventum, ut pigmentis perderet se, plurisque veniret imitata lignum. Sic lectis pretia quaeruntur. Hinc Martialis libri duodecimi Epigrammate 67.

When we read that Bagoas found the trunk of Holofernes thrown down ἐπὶ τῆς χετλωνίδος [epi tēs chetlōnidos], Jdt 14:13, others rightly observe that in place of χετλωνίδος [chetlōnidos], a meaningless word, we should read χελωνίδος [chelōnidos], as Hesychius and Suidas read it, and that χελωνίς [chelōnis] is so called ἀπὸ τῆς χελώνης [apo tēs chelōnēs], from the tortoise. But what exactly a χελωνὶς [chelōnis] is, is not sufficiently clear. The Vulgate translator renders it cortina [curtain]. Most of the more recent interpreters render it stratum [couch] or lectus [bed]; they are easily refuted. For Judith had cut off Holofernes' head, καὶ ἐπεκύλισε τὸ σῶμα αὐτοῦ ἀπὸ τῆς στρωμνῆς [kai epekylise to sōma autou apo tēs strōmnēs], and had rolled his body off the couch, ch. 13, v. 10. Therefore the χελωνὶς [chelōnis], in which Bagoas found this body ἐῤῥιμμένον [erhrimmenon], thrown down, is something different from the couch and the bed. Hesychius, under χελωνίδος [chelōnidos], explains it as οὐδὸς τῆς θύρας τῆς σκηνῆς [oudos tēs thyras tēs skēnēs], the threshold of the door of the tent. So Suidas too must be corrected from him, since he writes ὅδου [hodou] for οὐδοῦ [oudou]. This is hardly likely, both because the name does not fit, and because Holofernes' body was rolled from the couch into the chelonis. The chelonis, then, was not near the threshold of the door, but in the innermost part of the tent, where the commander's bed itself also stood. Not to detain the reader any longer: to me the χελωνὶς [chelōnis] is קבה kubba. The word occurs once in Scripture and is not well understood, namely Num 25:8, where, while Zimri and Kozbi were fornicating, Phinehas went אל הקבה, into the kubba, and pierced them both through. Different people explain this differently. But among the Arabs قبة [qubba] kubba, or kobba, and with the article القبة [al-qubba] alkobba, is a chamber of vaulted work, by which a bed is enclosed. The Spaniards call it alcoba II, 523 and alcova, a word which among us too has a similar meaning. The root قب [qabba] kabba in the second conjugation means to vault, or to build vaulted work. So, just as the musical instrument χέλυς [chelys], or testudo [tortoise], is called in Arabic العود [al-ʿūd] alaud, whence the Spanish laud or laut, and our lut, so القبة [al-qubba] alkobba, col. 1093 or the chamber we have described, is called in Arabic by another name ام العود [umm al-ʿūd] ommolaudi, that is, mother of the tortoise, because it is of vaulted, tortoise-shaped work. Among the Arabs the bridal chamber is in such an القبة [al-qubba] alkobba. And leading the bride there is called بنا [banā] bana and زف [zaff] zappha. So Holofernes' bedchamber, when he was about to lie with Judith, as he thought, was in an alcobba of this kind; and why it is called χελωνὶς [chelōnis] in Greek is now clear to anyone. Perhaps the χελωνὶς [chelōnis] was also named from its material. For in former times tortoise shell was much used in the beds and bedsteads of the great. This luxury, first adopted in the East, finally reached the Romans. The date is noted by Pliny, book 9, chapter 11: To cut tortoise shells into plates, he says, and to veneer beds and sideboards with them, was introduced by Carbilius Pollio. And book 16, col. 1094 chapter 43: Tortoise shell is cut for this purpose. And recently, in the principate of Nero, it was devised by monstrous ingenuity that it should destroy itself with paints and sell for more by imitating wood. Thus high prices are sought for beds. Hence Martial, book 12, Epigram 67: ¶

Gemmantes prima fulgent testudine lecti.

Beds jewelled with the finest tortoise shell gleam. ¶

Et Iuvenalis Satyra undecima,

And Juvenal, Satire 11: ¶

Qualis in Oceani fluctu testudo nataret, Clarum Trojugenis factura et nobile fulcrum.

What kind of tortoise swam in the waves of Ocean, to make a splendid and noble headrest for the Trojan-born. ¶

Ubi Scholiastes, Ut lectum testudineum haberet. Lectos namque antiqui testudine Oceani ornabant. Apulejus quoque non procul a fine libri decimi, Lectus Indica testudine perlucidus, plumea congerie tumidus, etc. Sed haec plus satis de re non magni momenti. Itaque hic definimus, jam ad aves transituri.

Where the Scholiast says: That he might have a tortoise-shell bed. For the ancients adorned beds with the tortoise shell of Ocean. Apuleius too, not far from the end of book 10: A bed shining with Indian tortoise shell, swelling with a heap of feathers, etc. But this is more than enough on a matter of no great importance. So here we end, and now pass on to the birds. ¶
