III, 14 col. 272 CAP. XX.

III, 14 col. 272 CHAPTER 20. ¶

De ave כוס chos dubitatur, sitne nocturnae avis species puta Bubo, Noctua, Ulula, Nycticorax, vel ab his diversissima, Onocrotalus.

On the bird כוס chos it is doubted whether it is a species of night bird, namely the eagle owl, the little owl, the owl, the night raven, or, very different from these, the onocrotalus. ¶

Avis כוס chos, quae jam sequitur, solum in Lege nominatur inter aves prohibitas; et Psalm. CII, 7. ubi Psaltes הייתי ככוס חרבות, Fui ut chos solitudinum. Et Seniores, Ἐγενήθην ὡσεὶ νυκτικόραξ ἐν οἰκοπέδῳ, Fui ut nycticorax in parietinis. De quo loco consultus Hieronymus a Sunia et Fretella Epistola centesima trigesima quinta ita respondet: „Quaeritis, quid significet nycticorax „apud Latinos. In Hebraeo pro nycticorace verbum כוס chos scriptum est, „quod Aquila, et Septuaginta, et Theodotion, et Quinta editio nycticoracem „interpretati sunt: Symmachus vero upupam: Sexta editio noctuam; quod et „nos magis sequimur.“ Et paullo post, Plerique bubonem contentiose significare putant. Quibus tamen accedens etiam ipse bubonem reddit tam in Lege, col. 273 quam in Psalmis.

The bird כוס chos, which follows next, is named only in the Law among the forbidden birds, and in Psalm. CII, 7, where the Psalmist says הייתי ככוס חרבות, I was like the chos of the wastes. And the Elders: Ἐγενήθην ὡσεὶ νυκτικόραξ ἐν οἰκοπέδῳ [Egenēthēn hōsei nyktikorax en oikopedō], I was like the night raven among ruined walls. Consulted about this passage by Sunia and Fretela, Jerome replies thus in his 135th Epistle: "You ask what nycticorax means among the Latins. In the Hebrew, in place of nycticorax, the word כוס chos is written, which Aquila, and the Seventy, and Theodotion, and the Fifth edition rendered night raven; Symmachus, however, hoopoe; the Sixth edition, little owl, which we too rather follow." And a little later: Most contend that it means eagle owl. Yet he himself, siding with them, renders it eagle owl both in the Law col. 273 and in the Psalms. ¶

Arabes البوم album reddunt, id est, noctuam. Id patet ex descriptione τοῦ album in Damire, البوم طاير يقع علي الذكر والانثي حتي تقول صدي او فياد فيتخص بالذكر وكنية الانثي ام الخراب وام الصيبان ويقال لها غراب الليل ومن طبعها انها تدخل علي كل طاير في وكره ليلا وتخرجه منه وتاكل فراخه وبيضه وهي قوية السلطان بالليل لا يحملها شي من الطير واذا راها الطير بالنهار قتلوها ونتفوا ريشها للعداوة التي بينهم وبينها ومن اجل ذلك صار الصياد يجعلها تحت شبكته لتقع الطير عليها, Album, avis. Nomen est communis generis: sed, si dixeris tsada, aut phajad, id maris proprium erit. Foeminae cognomina sunt mater solitudinis, et mater lendium (quia iis scatet). Dicitur etiam corvus noctis (id est, nycticorax). E natura ejus est, ut noctu ingrediatur in omnium avium nidos, et eas inde ejiciat, ut vescatur pullis et ovis. Nempe noctu tantum valet, ut nulla avis eam ferat: sed illam aves vicissim impetunt, cum interdiu vident, et plumas ejus vellicant, propter odium, quo se mutuo prosequuntur. Propterea fit aucupium, quo sub reti ponitur, III, 15 ut aves in eam irruant. Et infra, والبوم اصناف وكلها يحب الخلوة بانفسها والتفرد وفي اصل طبعها عداوة الغربان Τοῦ album plures sunt species, quae omnes secessum et solitudinem amant: sed eorum naturae maxime convenit odium in corvos. Haec de noctua dici, nemo non videt. Noctua enim noctu vagatur. Philosophus Historiae libri primi capite primo, Τὰ μὲν ζῶα νυκτερόβια, οἷον γλαύξ. Atque inde nomen habet. Festus, Noctua a tempore noctis dicta, quo canit, vel volat. Et ejus contra aves solertem dimicationem describit Plinius libri decimi capite decimo septimo. Et usum in aucupiis Aristoteles libri noni capite primo, Τῆς δὲ ἡμέρας καὶ τὰ ἄλ-λα ὀρνίθια τὴν γλαῦκα περιπέτεται, ὃ καλεῖται θαυμάζειν, καὶ προσπετό-μενα τέλλουσι. διὸ ὀρνιθοθήραι θηρεύουσιν αὐτῇ παντοδαπὰ ὀρνίθια. Interdiu caeterae aviculae noctuam circumvolant, quod mirari vocatur, advolantesque vellicant. Quapropter omnis generis aviculas per eam capiunt aucupes. Noctuae porro dissidium cum cornice, quam Arabes corvi nomine designant, vide apud Philosophum, Plinium et Aelianum. Unde natum proverbium apud Suidam et col. 274 Apostolium; Ἄλλο γλαὺξ, ἄλλο κορώνη φθέγγεται ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλλήλοις μὴ συμ-φωνέντων· Aliud noctua sonat, aliud cornix, de iis, qui inter se non consentiunt.

The Arabs render it البوم [al-būm] album, that is, little owl. This is clear from the description of the album in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: البوم طاير يقع علي الذكر والانثي حتي تقول صدي او فياد فيتخص بالذكر وكنية الانثي ام الخراب وام الصيبان ويقال لها غراب الليل ومن طبعها انها تدخل علي كل طاير في وكره ليلا وتخرجه منه وتاكل فراخه وبيضه وهي قوية السلطان بالليل لا يحملها شي من الطير واذا راها الطير بالنهار قتلوها ونتفوا ريشها للعداوة التي بينهم وبينها ومن اجل ذلك صار الصياد يجعلها تحت شبكته لتقع الطير عليها [al-būm ṭāyir yaqaʿu ʿalā al-dhakar wa-al-unthā ḥattā taqūla ṣadā aw fayyād fa-yatakhaṣṣaṣu bi-al-dhakar wa-kunyat al-unthā umm al-kharāb wa-umm al-ṣībān wa-yuqālu lahā ghurāb al-layl wa-min ṭabʿihā annahā tadkhulu ʿalā kull ṭāyir fī wakrihi laylan wa-tukhrijuhu minhu wa-taʾkulu firākhahu wa-bayḍahu wa-hiya qawiyyat al-sulṭān bi-al-layl lā yaḥmiluhā shayʾ min al-ṭayr wa-idhā raʾāhā al-ṭayr bi-al-nahār qatalūhā wa-natafū rīshahā li-al-ʿadāwa allatī baynahum wa-baynahā wa-min ajl dhālika ṣāra al-ṣayyād yajʿaluhā taḥta shabakatihi li-taqaʿa al-ṭayr ʿalayhā]. Album is a bird. The name is of common gender; but if you say tsada or phajad, it will be proper to the male. The female's by-names are mother of the wasteland and mother of nits (because she swarms with them). It is also called raven of the night (that is, night raven). It is its nature to enter the nests of all birds by night and drive them out of them, so as to feed on their chicks and eggs. For by night it is so strong that no bird can withstand it; but the birds in turn attack it when they see it by day, and pluck its feathers, because of the hatred with which they pursue each other. Hence a kind of fowling arises in which it is placed under a net, III, 15 so that the birds rush upon it. And below: والبوم اصناف وكلها يحب الخلوة بانفسها والتفرد وفي اصل طبعها عداوة الغربان [wa-al-būm aṣnāf wa-kulluhā yuḥibbu al-khalwa bi-anfusihā wa-al-tafarrud wa-fī aṣl ṭabʿihā ʿadāwat al-ghirbān]: Of the album there are several species, all of which love retreat and solitude; but hatred of ravens belongs most of all to their nature. Everyone sees that this is said of the little owl. For the little owl roams by night. The Philosopher, History, book 1, chapter 1: Τὰ μὲν ζῶα νυκτερόβια, οἷον γλαύξ [Ta men zōa nykterobia, hoion glaux]. And from this it has its name. Festus: The noctua is named from the time of night [nox], in which it sings or flies. And Pliny, book 10, chapter 17, describes its skilful fighting against birds; and Aristotle, book 9, chapter 1, its use in fowling: Τῆς δὲ ἡμέρας καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ὀρνίθια τὴν γλαῦκα περιπέτεται, ὃ καλεῖται θαυμάζειν, καὶ προσπετόμενα τέλλουσι. διὸ ὀρνιθοθήραι θηρεύουσιν αὐτῇ παντοδαπὰ ὀρνίθια [Tēs de hēmeras kai ta alla ornithia tēn glauka peripetetai, ho kaleitai thaumazein, kai prospetomena tellousi. dio ornithothērai thēreuousin autē pantodapa ornithia]. By day the other small birds fly around the little owl, which is called admiring, and, flying at it, they pluck it. Therefore fowlers catch small birds of every kind by means of it. Further, on the enmity of the little owl with the crow, which the Arabs designate by the name of raven, see the Philosopher, Pliny and Aelian. Hence arose the proverb in Suidas and col. 274 Apostolius: Ἄλλο γλαὺξ, ἄλλο κορώνη φθέγγεται ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλλήλοις μὴ συμφωνέντων [Allo glaux, allo korōnē phthengetai epi tōn allēlois mē symphōnentōn]; The little owl sounds one thing, the crow another: said of those who do not agree with each other. ¶

Chaldaei quoque interpretes, et Syrus, et Samaritanus parum ab his dissident. Ionathan habet צירא tsajera; Onkelos קריא kadja; Ioseph Coecus קיפופא kiphupha; Syrus ܥܘܕܐ auda; et Samaritanus ࠑࠃࠉࠀ tsadja. Quae voces, utut diversae in speciem, tamen eodem fere recidunt. Sic Samaritanum ࠑࠃࠉࠀ tsadja est noctuae foemina, quam Arabice صدى tsada vocari iisdem Radicalibus, supra Arabes nos docuerunt. Et Ionathanis צירא vox est corrupta: pro qua scribendum צדיא, quomodo apud eundem Deut. XIV, 16. Neque aliud Onkelosi קריא, nec Syrum ܥܘܕܐ (1). Nam Tsade, Koph et Ajin literas esse permutabiles, docent Chaldaea ארעא, et ארקא, pro ארץ, terra. Et in Iosepho Coeco קיפופא kiphupha, si non sit idem, quod צדיא tsadja, vel קריא kadjah, saltem animal esse congener, probatur ex loco Talmudis in Nidda cap. 3. fol. 23. a. in quo קריא וקפופא, kadja et kiphupha (Sic legendum, non III, 16 קריא karja, ut perperam habent nostrae editiones (2)) censentur in animalibus, דעיניהן הולכות לפניהן כשל אדם, quorum oculi anterius prospiciunt ut humani; cum in reliquis avibus oculi sint ad latus. Unde ibi Glossa, קריא וקפופא עוף הצועק בלילה ופניו דומות לחתול ועיניו לפניו, Kadja (ita lego pro קריא karja) et Kiphupha, avis, quae nocte clamat, et faciem habet feli similem, et oculos an-terius (3). Quae noctuae aut bubonis est descriptio. Itaque, si Symmachum excipias, cui כוס chos est upupa, veteres omnes hoc nomine noctuam, nycticoracem, aut bubonem intelligunt, quae aves inter se non multum differunt. Et si in his Hieronymi: Symmachus vero upupam, pro upupam legas ululam, nullus erit inter veteres notabilis dissensus, cum nocturnae aves, noctua, bubo, ulula et nycticorax tam sint inter se similes, ut passim a scriptoribus promiscue sumantur.

The Chaldee translators too, and the Syriac and the Samaritan, differ little from these. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] has צירא tsajera; Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] קריא kadja; Joseph the Blind קיפופא kiphupha; the Syriac ܥܘܕܐ [עודא] auda; and the Samaritan ࠑࠃࠉࠀ [צדיא] tsadja. These words, however different in appearance, nevertheless come to almost the same thing. Thus the Samaritan ࠑࠃࠉࠀ [צדיא] tsadja is the female of the little owl, which, as the Arabs taught us above, is called in Arabic صدى [ṣadā] tsada, with the same radicals. And Jonathan's צירא is a corrupt word: in its place one should write צדיא, as the same author has at Deut 14:16. Nor is Onkelos's קריא anything else, nor the Syriac ܥܘܕܐ [עודא] R1. For that the letters Tsade, Koph and Ajin are interchangeable is shown by the Chaldee ארעא and ארקא for ארץ, earth. And as for קיפופא kiphupha in Joseph the Blind: if it is not the same as צדיא tsadja or קריא kadjah, that it is at least a kindred animal is proved from a passage of the Talmud in Nidda, ch. 3, fol. 23a, in which קריא וקפופא, kadja and kiphupha (so it should be read, not III, 16 קריא karja, as our editions wrongly have it R2), are counted among the animals דעיניהן הולכות לפניהן כשל אדם, whose eyes look forward like a human's, whereas in the other birds the eyes are at the side. Hence the Gloss there: קריא וקפופא עוף הצועק בלילה ופניו דומות לחתול ועיניו לפניו, Kadja (so I read for קריא karja) and Kiphupha, a bird which cries at night, and has a face like a cat's, and eyes in front R3. This is a description of the little owl or the eagle owl. Therefore, if you except Symmachus, for whom כוס chos is the hoopoe, all the ancients understand by this name the little owl, the night raven or the eagle owl, birds that do not differ much from one another. And if in these words of Jerome, but Symmachus the hoopoe, you read ululam [owl] for upupam [hoopoe], there will be no notable disagreement among the ancients, since the night birds, the little owl, eagle owl, owl and night raven, are so similar to one another that writers commonly use the names interchangeably. ¶

His accedunt Hebraei recentiores, qui כוס chos dici volunt, quasi כוסה chose, id est, se abscondentem, aut latentem; quia haec avis נכסה מן העין, visum fugit, aut נכסה מעין אדם, abscondit se ab oculis hominum. Ita Baal Hatturim, et Aben Ezra. Et R. Selomo in Leviticum, כוס וינשוף הם צוא״טיש הצועקי׳ בלילה ויש להם לסתות כאדם, Chos et jansuph, Gallis chovettes, aves, quae noctu ululant, et maxillas habent ut homo. Quae partim sumta sunt ex Talmudis loco, quem supra citavimus, ubi hoc ipsum asseritur de avibus קריא et קפופא. Haec porro Selomonis cum diserta sint, nullus capio, quo pertineant haec Kimchii in Lexico: כוס שם עוף טמא לא נודע אצלנו ורבינו שלמה ז״ל פי׳ פלקו״ן בלע״ז ואותו שקורין פלקו״ן הוא נמצא ביישוב וצרין בו בני האדם שאר העופות ואחר שאמר הכתוב ככוס חרבות ידמה שהוא מדברי, Chos, nomen avis ignotae. Magister noster R. Selomo beatae memoriae lingua vernacula falconem explicat. At avis, quam falconem vocant, in locis habitatis reperitur, et ea utuntur homines ad alias aves aucupandas: Scriptura autem cum dicat „Sicut chos solitudinis“, videtur esse avis, in deserto degens. Quibus verbis Selomoni diversissimam affingit sententiam ab ea, quam exhibent ipsius Commentaria. Tamen hoc sequuti David de Pomis, Munsterus, Arias, alii כוס chos, falconem interpretantur. Quin quasi liberum esset quidvis hic comminisci, quia ignotam esse col. 275 avem dicit Kimchius, alii cuculum reddunt, alii illicem, alii strigem, alii pelicanum, et quid non? Fatendum interim novorum etiam interpretum partem longe III, 17 maximam noctuam retinuisse. Et veram esse hanc vocis notionem, probare contendit Drusius ex Germanico kuts pro noctua, et Patavino chus pro scope, noctuae specie, quae videntur ad Hebraeum כוס chos accedere, tanquam ex eo deducta. Itaque tanto consensui se opponere videbitur periculosum (4). Dicam tamen, quid olim de hac voce conjecerim, non sine aliqua veri specie.

To these are added the more recent Hebrews, who hold that it is called כוס chos as if כוסה chose, that is, hiding itself or lying hidden, because this bird נכסה מן העין, shuns sight, or נכסה מעין אדם, hides itself from the eyes of men. So Baal Hatturim [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] and Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra]. And R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on Leviticus: כוס וינשוף הם צוא״טיש הצועקי׳ בלילה ויש להם לסתות כאדם, Chos and jansuph, in French chovettes, birds which hoot at night and have jaws like a man. This is partly taken from the passage of the Talmud cited above, where precisely this is asserted of the birds קריא and קפופא. Since these words of R. Selomo are explicit, I cannot at all understand what these words of Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon refer to: כוס שם עוף טמא לא נודע אצלנו ורבינו שלמה ז״ל פי׳ פלקו״ן בלע״ז ואותו שקורין פלקו״ן הוא נמצא ביישוב וצרין בו בני האדם שאר העופות ואחר שאמר הכתוב ככוס חרבות ידמה שהוא מדברי, Chos, the name of an unknown bird. Our master R. Selomo of blessed memory explains it in the vernacular as falcon. But the bird they call falcon is found in inhabited places, and men use it to hunt other birds; whereas Scripture, since it says “Like the chos of the wilderness”, seems to mean a bird living in the desert. In these words he ascribes to R. Selomo an opinion very different from the one his Commentaries present. Yet, following this, David de Pomis [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary], Münster, Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] and others render כוס chos as falcon. Indeed, as if one were free to invent anything here, because Kimchi says it is an unknown col. 275 bird, some render it cuckoo, others illex [decoy bird], others screech owl, others pelican, and what not? It must be admitted, meanwhile, that by far the greatest part III, 17 of the modern translators too have kept the little owl. And Drusius endeavours to prove that this is the true sense of the word from the German kuts for little owl and the Paduan chus for scops, a kind of little owl, words which seem to come close to the Hebrew כוס chos, as if derived from it. So it will seem dangerous to oppose such great agreement R4. I shall nevertheless say what I once conjectured about this word, not without some appearance of truth. ¶

Voce כוס chos suspicabar truonem significari, vel, quod idem est, onocrotalum. Nam, qui Graecis ὀνοκρόταλος, is Romanis TRUO dictus est. Nempe trua est vasis genus.

I suspected that the word כוס chos signifies the truo, or, what is the same, the onocrotalus. For the bird the Greeks call ὀνοκρόταλος [onokrotalos] the Romans called TRUO. Now trua is a kind of vessel. ¶

Unde diminutivum trulla. Varro De lingua Latina libro quarto, Trulla a similitudine truae, quae quod magna, et haec pusilla, ut truula. Nonnius, Truam veteres a terendo, quam nos Diminutivo trullam dicimus, appellari voluerunt. Inde igitur truo est onocrotalus. Festus, Truo, avis onocrotalus. Caecilius, irridens magnitudinem nasi, „Pro Dii immortales, unde prorepsit truo?“ Glossae Philoxeni, Ὀνοκρόταλος, Trica, lege Truo. Aliae Glossae optimae, sed librariorum incuria saepe corruptae, Triduo, tinuero. Legendum Trua vas, Truo vero, ὀνοκρόταλον, supple sonat. Truo scilicet vocatur a trua, quam habet in rostro. Proinde Sabaudi vocant gutturosam, id est, strumosam, ab ingluvie, quae in gutture, strumae instar, prominet. Et Arabes plerique الحوصل alhausal, id est, ingluviem, ac si avis tota sit ingluvies. Sic Aeliani libri decimi sexti capite quarto Indica avis κήλας, otide triplo major, vasto ore, magnis cruribus, habens πρηγορεῶνα, καὶ ἐκεῖνον μέγιστον, προσεμ-φερῆ κωρύκῳ, ingluviem, eamque amplissimam, sacco similem, omnino est onocrotalus, qui a Graecis κήλας dictus sit, propter κήλην, seu βρογχοκήλην, id est, tumorem in gutture. Neque immerito illa ingluvies asseritur esse amplissima, cum paene amphoram aut urnam capere nonnulli scribant. Alii Aethiopem infantulum in ea esse repertum. Alii pugnum cum brachio, alii pedem III, 18 cum crure usque ad genu in eam immisisse se prodant, ipso animali illaeso. Alii se pro cucullo capiti imposuisse, reliquo corpore a tergo dependente. Alii nautas Aegyptios onocrotali rostro sentinam exhaurire. Et Plinius (libri decimi capite quadragesimo septimo) faucibus ipsis inesse alterius uteri genus. Huc omnia inexplebile animal congerere, mira ut sit capacitas. Mox, perfecta rapina, sensim inde in os reddita in veram (alii, in inferam) alvum ruminantis modo referre. Unde est, quod Arabs Poeta de hac ave ملغز, id est, αἰνιγματι-ζόμενος,

Hence the diminutive trulla [ladle]. Varro, De lingua Latina, book 4: Trulla from its likeness to the trua; because that is large and this small, as it were truula. Nonius: The ancients wished the trua to be named from terere [to rub], which we call by the diminutive trulla. Hence, then, truo is the onocrotalus. Festus: Truo, the bird onocrotalus. Caecilius, mocking the size of a nose: "O immortal gods, whence has this truo crept forth?" The Glosses of Philoxenus: Ὀνοκρόταλος [Onokrotalos], Trica; read Truo. Other Glosses, excellent but often corrupted by the carelessness of copyists, have Triduo, tinuero. One should read Trua [is] a vessel; Truo, however, ὀνοκρόταλον [onokrotalon]; supply sonat [means]. The bird is evidently called truo from the trua [ladle] that it has in its beak. Accordingly the Savoyards call it gutturosa, that is, strumosa [the goitred one], from the crop which protrudes from the throat like a goitre. And most of the Arabs call it الحوصل [al-ḥawṣal] alhausal, that is, the crop, as if the whole bird were a crop. Thus the Indian bird κήλας [kēlas] in Aelian, book 16, ch. 4, three times larger than the bustard, with a huge mouth and great legs, having πρηγορεῶνα, καὶ ἐκεῖνον μέγιστον, προσεμφερῆ κωρύκῳ [prēgoreōna, kai ekeinon megiston, prosempherē kōrykō], a crop, and that a very large one, like a sack, is certainly the onocrotalus, which the Greeks would have called κήλας [kēlas] on account of its κήλη [kēlē], or βρογχοκήλη [bronchokēlē], that is, a swelling in the throat. Nor is that crop said without reason to be very large, since some write that it holds almost an amphora or an urn. Others report that a little Ethiopian child was found in it. Others relate that they put into it a fist together with the arm, others a foot III, 18 with the leg up to the knee, the animal itself remaining unharmed. Others that they put it on their head as a hood, the rest of the body hanging down behind. Others that Egyptian sailors bail out the bilge with the beak of the onocrotalus. And Pliny (book 10, chapter 47) says that in the throat itself there is a kind of second stomach. Into this the insatiable animal heaps everything, so that its capacity is astonishing. Then, when its plundering is complete, it gradually returns the food from there into the mouth and passes it into the true (others read the lower) belly, in the manner of a ruminant. Hence it is that an Arab poet, ملغز [mulghiz], that is, αἰνιγματιζόμενος [ainigmatizomenos] [speaking in riddles], says of this bird, ¶

ما طاير في قلبه * يلوح للناس عجب * منقاره كبطنه * والعين منه في الذنب *

ما طاير في قلبه * يلوح للناس عجب * منقاره كبطنه * والعين منه في الذنب * [mā ṭāyir fī qalbihi * yalūḥu li-l-nās ʿajab * minqāruhu ka-baṭnihi * wa-l-ʿayn minhu fī al-dhanab *] ¶

Quaenam avis est, quae ingenio suo apparet hominibus mirabilis? Rostrum illi pro ventre est, et oculus in cauda: id est, post ventrem illum, quem in rostro habet. Potuit igitur ab hoc, sive ventre, sive sacco, aut trua, quae ab inferiori rostri parte dependet, etiam ab Hebraeis dici avis הכוס, τοῦ chos, id est, vasis, seu poculi; quia כוס chos pro vase, aut poculo nomen est receptissimum. Cui conjecturae non parum favet Psalmi locus, quem citavimus, דמיתי לקאת מדבר הייתי ככוס חרבות, is enim optime sic redderetur, Similis factus sum pelicano deserti, et fui ut onocrotalus solitudinum. Et in eodem commate jungerentur hae duae aves, pelicanus et onocrotalus, quae sunt ita congeneres, ut etiam doctissimi eas promiscue sumant. In his Oppianus in Ixeuticis, ubi πελεκίνῳ, id est, pelicano tribuitur κόλπος, sinus quidam, ante pectus dependens, in quem cibum omnem aliquantisper recondit. Et Hieronymus, cui קאת in loco Psalmi est pelicanus; sed alibi onocrotalus, ut Levit. XI, 18. Es. XXXIV, 11. et Sophon. II, 14. Et Bellonius libri tertii capite secundo De Avibus, ubi pelicanus, platea et onocrotalus pro eadem ave sumuntur. Praeterea recte diceretur onocrotalus solitudinum. Docet enim Hieronymus in Es. XXXIV., hoc animal in desertis locis habitare consuevisse. Et in Sophon. II. Onocrotalorum esse duo genera, aliud aquatile, aliud solitudinis. Ita etiam Isidorus libri duodecimi capite septimo. Proinde Damir capite البجع albugia, seu De Onocrotalo, sectione De ejus esu, licitum quidem esse concludit, non tamen vi regulae generalis, quae Mahumetanis concedit omnium avium aquaticarum esum; ان col. 277 ذلك الوجة يجري في طاير لا يغارق الما وهذا يالغه ثم يغارقه Quia currit haec ratio (id est, valet haec regula) III, 19 in iis avibus, quae in aquis semper manent: at haec avis modo in aqua degit, modo ab ea divellitur; ut secedat scilicet in intimam solitudinem. Hoc quoque non omittendum, Psaltem huic avi se conferre propter assiduos questus et planctus. Nam praecesserat מקול אנחתי , propter vocem gemitus mei ossa mea carni adhaeserunt. Porro vox onocrotali moestum et horribile aliquid habet. Aelianus ubi supra, Φθέγμα δὲ ἔχει καὶ μάλα ἀπηχὲς, Vocem habet valde absonam. Proinde Graecis dictus est ὀνοκρό-ταλος, quasi rudat instar asini: et nonnullis Arabum جمل الما , camelus aquae, quasi blateret in cameli modum. Hinc Albertus, Quasi cornu sonans horribilem vocem emittit. Et Olaus, Magnum clamorem edit instar botauri avis, id est, ardeae stellaris, quae voce sua tauri mugitum imitatur. Denique non potuit haec avis ignota esse Iudaeis, cum idonei scriptores reperiri testentur circa Syriae Antiochiam et Palaestinae Gazam, et in Aegypto frequentem esse. Addi queat, inde factum esse, ut כוס chos a Graecis, quos reliqui sequuti sunt, pro onocrotalo redderetur νυκτικόραξ, quod pro כוס chos legerunt בום bum. Quae duo voces, quamvis sono differant immensum quantum, tamen apud Hebraeos scriptura sunt simillimae. Porro voce בום bum, noctuam, et غراب الليل , corvum noctis, id est, nycticoracem, significari, saltem apud Arabes, quorum lingua est Hebraicae dialectus, abunde supra a) probavimus.

What bird is there that by its nature appears wondrous to men? Its beak serves it for a belly, and its eye is in its tail: that is, behind that belly which it has in its beak. From this belly, then, or sack, or trough, which hangs from the lower part of the beak, the bird may also have been called הכוס by the Hebrews, from chos, that is, vessel or cup; for כוס chos is a thoroughly established word for a vessel or cup. This conjecture is considerably supported by the passage of the Psalm we have cited, דמיתי לקאת מדבר הייתי ככוס חרבות, for it would best be rendered thus: I am become like a pelican of the wilderness, and I was as an onocrotalus of the desolate places. And so in the same verse these two birds would be joined, the pelican and the onocrotalus, which are so closely akin that even the most learned take them indiscriminately. Among these is Oppian in the Ixeutica, where to the πελεκίνῳ [pelekinō], that is, the pelican, is attributed a κόλπος [kolpos], a kind of pouch hanging in front of the breast, in which it stores all its food for a while. Also Jerome, for whom קאת in the passage of the Psalm is the pelican, but elsewhere the onocrotalus, as in Lev 11:18. Isa 34:11 and Zeph 2:14. And Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], book 3, chapter 2 of De Avibus, where the pelican, the platea and the onocrotalus are taken for the same bird. Moreover, onocrotalus of the desolate places would be said correctly. For Jerome teaches on Es. XXXIV. that this animal is accustomed to live in desert places. And on Sophon. II., that there are two kinds of onocrotali, one aquatic, the other of the wilderness. So also Isidore, book 12, chapter 7. Accordingly Damir, in the chapter البجع [al-bajaʿ] albugia, or On the Onocrotalus, in the section On eating it, concludes that it is indeed lawful, yet not by force of the general rule which grants Muslims the eating of all water birds: ان col. 277 ذلك الوجة يجري في طاير لا يغارق الما وهذا يالغه ثم يغارقه [inna dhālika al-wajh yajrī fī ṭāyir lā yughāriq al-mā wa-hādhā yālighuh thumma yughāriquh] Because this reasoning runs (that is, this rule holds) III, 19 for those birds which always remain in the water; but this bird at one time lives in the water, at another is separated from it; namely, so as to withdraw into the remotest wilderness. This too must not be omitted: the Psalmist compares himself to this bird on account of his constant complaints and laments. For there had preceded מקול אנחתי, by reason of the voice of my groaning my bones cleave to my flesh. Now the voice of the onocrotalus has something mournful and dreadful about it. Aelian, in the place cited above: Φθέγμα δὲ ἔχει καὶ μάλα ἀπηχὲς [Phthegma de echei kai mala apēches], It has a very discordant voice. Hence by the Greeks it was called ὀνοκρόταλος [onokrotalos], as if it brayed like an ass; and by some of the Arabs جمل الما [jamal al-mā], camel of the water, as if it bellowed in the manner of a camel. Hence Albertus: It emits a dreadful voice, sounding as it were like a horn. And Olaus: It makes a great cry like the bird botaurus, that is, the bittern, which with its voice imitates the bellowing of a bull. Finally, this bird cannot have been unknown to the Jews, since reliable writers attest that it is found around Antioch in Syria and Gaza in Eretz Yisrael, and that it is common in Egypt. It might be added that this is how it came about that כוס chos was rendered by the Greeks, whom the rest followed, as νυκτικόραξ [nyktikorax] instead of onocrotalus: because for כוס chos they read בום bum. These two words, though they differ enormously in sound, are nevertheless very similar in Hebrew script. Further, that the word בום bum signifies the little owl, and غراب الليل [ghurāb al-layl], raven of the night, that is, the night raven, at least among the Arabs, whose language is a dialect of Hebrew, we have amply proved above a). ¶
