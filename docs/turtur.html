II, 596 col. 54 Chapter IX. ¶

De Turture.

On the Turtledove. ¶

Turtur Hebraice תור thor dicitur, et Graece τρυγών. Quae nomina, utut col. 55 similia, non tamen sunt ab invicem deducta, sed ὀνοματοποιητικά. Facta nimirum ad imitationem cantus turturis, qui Graece dicitur τρυσμὸς, a verbo τρύζειν, id est, turturis more gemere, Pollux lib. 5. cap. 14. sectione De avium vocibus, Εἴποις δ' ἂν τρυγόνας τρύζειν, περιστερὰς γογγύζειν. Sed et τρύζειν Suidae et Etymologo est ψιθυρίζειν, γογγύζειν, ἀσήμως λαλεῖν παρὰ καὶ ἡ τρυγὼν, ἐπεὶ καὶ ἀσήμως φθέγγεται καὶ γογγυστικῶς, susurrare, murmurare, obscure loqui, ut et turtur, quia obscure canit et cum murmure. Unde Clemens libro quinto Stromatων, Γογγύζουσα ἡ τρυγὼν, μέμψεως καταλαλιὰν ἀχάρι-στον ἐμφαίνουσα, εἰκότως ἐξοικίζεται. Turtur murmurans, quaerelae ingratam ostendens maledicentiam, merito domo expellitur. Aliis τρύζειν est πολυφωνεῖν, καὶ πολυλογεῖν, multa garrire. Quia τὴν τρυγόνα ἡ παροιμία ἐπὶ σκώμματι τῶν πολυλογούντων παραλαμβάνει, λαλιστέρους τῶν τρυγόνων τοιούτους καλοῦσα, turturem adhibet proverbium, in eorum suggillationem, qui multa garriunt, eos turturibus loquaciores appellans. Haec Eustathius, in illud Homeri Iliad. l. versu 31.

The turtledove is called תור thor in Hebrew and τρυγών [trygōn] in Greek. These names, however col. 55 similar, are not derived from one another but are onomatopoeic (ὀνοματοποιητικά [onomatopoiētika]). They were formed, that is, in imitation of the song of the turtledove, which in Greek is called τρυσμὸς [trysmos], from the verb τρύζειν [tryzein], that is, to moan in the manner of a turtledove. So Pollux, book 5, ch. 14, in the section On the calls of birds: Εἴποις δ' ἂν τρυγόνας τρύζειν, περιστερὰς γογγύζειν [Eipois d' an trygonas tryzein, peristeras gongyzein]. But for Suidas and the Etymologicum, too, τρύζειν [tryzein] is ψιθυρίζειν, γογγύζειν, ἀσήμως λαλεῖν παρὰ καὶ ἡ τρυγὼν, ἐπεὶ καὶ ἀσήμως φθέγγεται καὶ γογγυστικῶς [psithyrizein, gongyzein, asēmōs lalein para kai hē trygōn, epei kai asēmōs phthengetai kai gongystikōs], to whisper, to murmur, to speak indistinctly, like the turtledove too, because it sings indistinctly and with a murmur. Hence Clement, in the fifth book of the Stromata: Γογγύζουσα ἡ τρυγὼν, μέμψεως καταλαλιὰν ἀχάριστον ἐμφαίνουσα, εἰκότως ἐξοικίζεται [Gongyzousa hē trygōn, mempseōs katalalian acharistōn emphainousa, eikotōs exoikizetai]. The murmuring turtledove, displaying the ungrateful slander of complaint, is rightly driven from the house. For others τρύζειν [tryzein] is πολυφωνεῖν, καὶ πολυλογεῖν [polyphōnein, kai polylogein], to chatter a great deal. Because τὴν τρυγόνα ἡ παροιμία ἐπὶ σκώμματι τῶν πολυλογούντων παραλαμβάνει, λαλιστέρους τῶν τρυγόνων τοιούτους καλοῦσα [tēn trygona hē paroimia epi skōmmati tōn polylogountōn paralambanei, lalisterous tōn trygonōn toioutous kalousa], the proverb uses the turtledove to mock those who chatter a great deal, calling them more talkative than turtledoves. So Eustathius, on that line of Homer, Iliad 1, verse 31: ¶

Ὡς μή μοι τρύζητε παρήμενοι ἄλλοθεν ἄλλος,

Ὡς μή μοι τρύζητε παρήμενοι ἄλλοθεν ἄλλος, [Hōs mē moi tryzēte parēmenoi allothen allos,] ¶

Ut ne mihi garriatis assidentes aliunde alius. Proverbii meminere, praeter Paroemiographos, Alexis in Thrasone apud Athenaeum libro quarto, Menander in Plocio, Demetrius in Sicilia apud Aelianum De animalibus libri duodecimi capite duodecimo, et Clemens libro primo Stromatων. Quo pertinet, quod de garrulis mulierculis habetur in Theocriti Syracusiis,

So that you may not chatter at me, sitting by, one from here, another from there. Besides the paroemiographers, the proverb is mentioned by Alexis in the Thraso in Athenaeus, book 4; by Menander in the Plocium; by Demetrius in the Sicilia in Aelian, On Animals, book 12, chapter 12; and by Clement in the first book of the Stromata. To this belongs what is said of chattering women in Theocritus' Syracusan Women: ¶

Τρυγόνες ἐκκναισεῦντι πλατυάσδοισαι ἅπαντα,

Τρυγόνες ἐκκναισεῦντι πλατυάσδοισαι ἅπαντα, [Trygones ekknaiseunti platyasdoisai hapanta,] ¶

Ut turtures obtundunt ad omnia os diducentes. Nec placet, quod vir magnus pro ἐκκναισεῦντι legi vult ἐκ Ναύσοιο, id est, ex insula Sicilia. Ναῦσος, ait, sicut Νᾶσος, Dorum et Siculorum est, qui ita vocabant suam Siciliam. Poeta igitur urbanissime τοὺς Δωριζούσας, et ore lato omnia pronuntiantes, τρυγόνας ἐκ II, 597 Ναύσοιο vocat. Haec, inquam, conjectura non placet: quia nihil opus est quidquam mutare, cum ex recepta lectione sensus emergat commodissimus. Ἐκκναίειν est aures obtundere, ut ἀποκναίειν. Pollux, Ἀποκναίων ἀηδίᾳ, κό-πτων τὰ ὦτα, Insuavitate, seu ineptiis suis, ἀποκναίων, id est, aures obtundens. Quod scripsit Demosthenes contra Midiam pag. 362. Ἀποκναίει γὰρ ἀη-δίᾳ, δήπου καὶ ἀναιθησίᾳ καθ᾽ ἑκάστην τὴν ἐκκλησίαν ταῦτα λέγων· Obtundit enim ineptiis et stoliditate sua, in singulis concionibus eadem subinde inculcans. Ita igitur de loquaculis dictum, Τρυγόνες ἐκκναισεῦντι, Dorice pro ἐκκναίσουσι, Turtures (seu turturum instar) loquacitate sua aures obtundunt. Est in Proverbiis apud Suidam Τρυγόνα ψάλλειν Turturem canere, ἐπὶ τῶν φαύλως πρατ-των, de iis, qui rem aliquam perperam agunt, quia turturis vox est prorsus incondita. Atque eo fortasse pertinet, quod ab Hebraeis צלצל, et a Chaldaeis צרצילתא nominatur, et Arabice etiam صلصل, tanquam a cymbalo facto nomine, quia vocem habet tinnulam et obstreperam. Quin, si Gesnero credimus, ob id ipsum cum turture confertur cicadae species cercope in his Athenaei lib. 4. cap. 4. Ἔστι δ᾽ ἡ κερκώπη ζῶον ὅμοιον τέττιγι, καὶ τρυγονίῳ· Cercope autem est animal, cicadae et turturi simile. Ad quae verba sic ille habet, Non alia re cercope turturi similis est, quam obstrepera garrulitate. Sed conjectat Dale- col. 56 champius, gryllum ita vocari a soni tri proprietate. Et magnus Casaubonus secure praeteriit hunc locum. Quem tamen esse mendosum, tam certum est, quam quod est certissimum. Et τρυγονίῳ perperam legi pro τεττιγονίῳ, quae una est cicadae species. Aristoteles Hist. lib. 5. cap. 30. Καλοῦσι δέ τινες τοὺς μὲν μεγά-λους (τῶν τεττίγων) καὶ ᾄδοντας ἄχετας· τοὺς δὲ μικροὺς τεττιγόνια. Unde Plinius lib. 11. cap. 26. Quae canunt vocantur achetae, et quae minores ex iis, tettigonia. Itaque nihil habet hic locus, quod ad turturum garrulitatem possit referri. Quae, utut saepe molesta, habet tamen aliquid gratum, nempe ver et aestatem venisse significat, quia hyeme turtures non apparent. Unde dicitur Cant. II, 11. 12. Iam enim hyems transiit, pluvia praeteriit et recessit. Flores in terra conspiciuntur. Tempus cantus advenit, et vox turturis audita est in terra nostra. Et Ierem. VIII, 7. Turtur et hirundo, et grus observarunt tempus ad-ventus sui. Turtur enim est avis migratoria (1). Aristoteles Hist. lib. 8. cap. 3.

Like turtledoves they deafen us, broadening their mouths on every word. Nor do I approve the proposal of a great man to read ἐκ Ναύσοιο [ek Nausoio], that is, from the island of Sicily, instead of ἐκκναισεῦντι [ekknaiseunti]. Ναῦσος [Nausos], he says, like Νᾶσος [Nasos], is a word of the Dorians and the Sicilians, who so called their Sicily. The poet therefore very wittily calls the Dorian-speaking women (τοὺς Δωριζούσας [tous Dōrizousas]), who pronounce everything with a broad mouth, τρυγόνας ἐκ II, 597 Ναύσοιο [trygonas ek Nausoio]. This conjecture, I say, I do not approve, because there is no need to change anything, since the received reading yields a most fitting sense. Ἐκκναίειν [Ekknaiein] means to deafen the ears, like ἀποκναίειν [apoknaiein]. Pollux: Ἀποκναίων ἀηδίᾳ, κόπτων τὰ ὦτα [Apoknaiōn aēdia, koptōn ta ōta], ἀποκναίων [apoknaiōn] by his unpleasantness, or by his inanities, that is, deafening the ears. As Demosthenes wrote against Midias, p. 362: Ἀποκναίει γὰρ ἀηδίᾳ, δήπου καὶ ἀναιθησίᾳ καθ᾽ ἑκάστην τὴν ἐκκλησίαν ταῦτα λέγων· [Apoknaiei gar aēdia, dēpou kai anaithēsia kath' hekastēn tēn ekklēsian tauta legōn;] For he deafens us with his inanities and stupidity, hammering the same things in at every assembly. Thus, then, it is said of chatterboxes, Τρυγόνες ἐκκναισεῦντι [Trygones ekknaiseunti], Doric for ἐκκναίσουσι [ekknaisousi], Turtledoves (or like turtledoves) deafen the ears with their talkativeness. Among the Proverbs in Suidas there is Τρυγόνα ψάλλειν [Trygona psallein], To sing the turtledove, ἐπὶ τῶν φαύλως πραττων [epi tōn phaulōs prattōn], said of those who do something badly, because the voice of the turtledove is utterly unmusical. And perhaps this is why it is called צלצל by the Hebrews, צרצילתא by the Chaldeans, and in Arabic also صلصل [ṣalṣal], as if the name were formed from a cymbal, because it has a ringing and noisy voice. Indeed, if we believe Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], for this very reason a species of cicada, the cercope, is compared with the turtledove in these words of Athenaeus, book 4, ch. 4: Ἔστι δ᾽ ἡ κερκώπη ζῶον ὅμοιον τέττιγι, καὶ τρυγονίῳ· [Esti d' hē kerkōpē zōon homoion tettigi, kai trygoniō;] The cercope is an animal like a cicada and a turtledove. On these words he says: The cercope resembles the turtledove in nothing else than its noisy chatter. But Dale- col. 56 champius conjectures that a cricket is so called from the peculiarity of the sound tri. And the great Casaubon passed over this passage unconcerned. Yet that it is corrupt is as certain as whatever is most certain, and τρυγονίῳ [trygoniō] is wrongly read for τεττιγονίῳ [tettigoniō], which is a species of cicada. Aristotle, Hist. book 5, ch. 30: Καλοῦσι δέ τινες τοὺς μὲν μεγάλους (τῶν τεττίγων) καὶ ᾄδοντας ἄχετας· τοὺς δὲ μικροὺς τεττιγόνια [Kalousi de tines tous men megalous (tōn tettigōn) kai adontas achetas; tous de mikrous tettigonia]. Hence Pliny, book 11, ch. 26: Those that sing are called achetae, and the smaller of them tettigonia. So this passage contains nothing that can be referred to the chattering of turtledoves. That chattering, though often annoying, nevertheless has something pleasing: it signals that spring and summer have come, since turtledoves do not appear in winter. Hence it is said in Cant. II, 11. 12.: For now the winter is past, the rain is over and gone. Flowers are seen on the earth. The time of song has come, and the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land. And Ierem. VIII, 7.: The turtledove and the swallow and the crane have observed the time of their coming. For the turtledove is a migratory bird R1. Aristotle, Hist. book 8, ch. 3. ¶

II, 598 φάττα μὲν οὖν, καὶ περιστερὰ ἀεὶ φαίνονται· τρυγὼν δὲ τοῦ θέρους· τοῦ γὰρ χειμῶνος ἀφανίζεται· Palumbus et columba semper apparent: sed turtur aestate tantum. Nam hyeme non conspicitur. Et libri octavi capite duodecimo, de palumbo id ipsum negans, quod hic affirmaverat, solas columbas jugiter manere scribit. Verba sunt: Ἀπαίρουσι δὲ καὶ αἱ φάτται, καὶ αἱ πελειάδες, καὶ οὐ χει-μάζουσι, καὶ αἱ χελιδόνες, καὶ αἱ τρυγόνες, αἱ δὲ περιστεραὶ καταμένουσιν· Discedunt autem et palumbi, et liviae, et hirundines, et turtures, neque (hic) hyemant, sed columbae manent. Et capite decimo sexto docet, hoc turturis maxime esse proprium, ut hyeme lateat. Φωλεῖ γὰρ, inquit, καὶ πελαργὸς, καὶ κόττυφος, καὶ τρυγὼν, καὶ κόρυδος. καὶ ἥ γε τρυγὼν ὁμολογουμένως μάλιστα πάντων. οὐδεὶς γὰρ, ὡς εἰπεῖν, λέγεται τρυγόνα ἰδεῖν οὐδαμοῦ χειμῶνος· Nam et ciconia latet, et merula, et turtur, et alauda. Sed de turture maxime omnium constat: nemo enim, prope dixerim, turturem per hyemem uspiam vidisse memoratur. Varro etiam Rustic. lib. 3. cap. 5. Turdi quotannis in Italiam trans mare advolant circiter aequinoctium autumnale, et eodem revolant ad aequinoctium vernum. Et alio tempore turtures ac coturnices immani numero. Et Cicero, De Finib. libro secundo, Turtures cursu et peregrinatione laetantur. Et Gregorius Nyssenus in Cant. Homil. 5. Ἐν γὰρ τῇ χειμῶνι φεύγει ἡ ἀηδὼν, ναρκᾷ ἡ χε-λιδὼν, ἀποξενοῦται τῆς καλίας ἡ τρυγών· Hyeme enim fugit luscinia, torpet hirundo, a nido peregrinatur turtur. Sunt tamen, qui turturem putant non tam abire, quam occultari. Plinius libri decimi capite vigesimo quarto, Verius turtur occultatur, pennasque amittit. Alii vere perperam legunt. Neque enim turtur vere occultatur, sed hyeme. Quin ipse Aristoteles ubi supra capite duodecimo turtures nonnullos remanere ἐν εὐηλίοις χωρίοις non diffitetur.

II, 598 φάττα μὲν οὖν, καὶ περιστερὰ ἀεὶ φαίνονται· τρυγὼν δὲ τοῦ θέρους· τοῦ γὰρ χειμῶνος ἀφανίζεται· [phatta men oun, kai peristera aei phainontai; trygōn de tou therous; tou gar cheimōnos aphanizetai] The wood-pigeon and the dove are always to be seen, but the turtledove only in summer. For in winter it is not seen. And in book 8, chapter 12, denying of the wood-pigeon the very thing he had affirmed here, he writes that only doves remain all year. His words are: Ἀπαίρουσι δὲ καὶ αἱ φάτται, καὶ αἱ πελειάδες, καὶ οὐ χειμάζουσι, καὶ αἱ χελιδόνες, καὶ αἱ τρυγόνες, αἱ δὲ περιστεραὶ καταμένουσιν· [Apairousi de kai hai phattai, kai hai peleiades, kai ou cheimazousi, kai hai chelidones, kai hai trygones, hai de peristerai katamenousin] But the wood-pigeons, the rock-doves, the swallows and the turtledoves depart, and do not winter (here), but the doves remain. And in chapter 16 he teaches that it is above all characteristic of the turtledove to hide in winter. Φωλεῖ γὰρ [Phōlei gar], he says, καὶ πελαργὸς, καὶ κόττυφος, καὶ τρυγὼν, καὶ κόρυδος. καὶ ἥ γε τρυγὼν ὁμολογουμένως μάλιστα πάντων. οὐδεὶς γὰρ, ὡς εἰπεῖν, λέγεται τρυγόνα ἰδεῖν οὐδαμοῦ χειμῶνος· [kai pelargos, kai kottyphos, kai trygōn, kai korydos. kai hē ge trygōn homologoumenōs malista pantōn. oudeis gar, hōs eipein, legetai trygona idein oudamou cheimōnos] For the stork also hides, and the blackbird, and the turtledove, and the lark. But of the turtledove this is agreed most of all: for no one, I might almost say, is reported to have seen a turtledove anywhere in winter. Varro too, Rustic. book 3, ch. 5: Thrushes fly every year across the sea into Italy around the autumn equinox, and fly back to the same place at the spring equinox. And at another season turtledoves and quails in enormous numbers. And Cicero, De Finib. book 2: Turtledoves delight in running and in travel. And Gregory of Nyssa on the Song, Homily 5: Ἐν γὰρ τῇ χειμῶνι φεύγει ἡ ἀηδὼν, ναρκᾷ ἡ χελιδὼν, ἀποξενοῦται τῆς καλίας ἡ τρυγών· [En gar tē cheimōni pheugei hē aēdōn, narka hē chelidōn, apoxenoutai tēs kalias hē trygōn] For in winter the nightingale flees, the swallow grows torpid, the turtledove goes abroad from its nest. There are some, however, who think that the turtledove does not so much go away as hide. Pliny, book 10, chapter 24: More truly, the turtledove hides and loses its feathers. Others wrongly read vere [in spring]. For the turtledove does not hide in spring, but in winter. Indeed Aristotle himself, in chapter 12 cited above, does not deny that some turtledoves remain ἐν εὐηλίοις χωρίοις [en euēliois chōriois]. ¶

Cant. I, 10. sponsae dicitur in Graeca versione, Τί ὡραιώθησαν σιαγόνες σου ὡς τρυγόνος; et in Vulgata, Quam speciosae factae sunt genae tuae tanquam turturis? Ad quem locum multa habent de castitate turturis, et sede pudoris in genis Graeci doctores, Origenes, Gregorius Nyssenus, Philo Carpathius, Theodoretus et Romani, Iustus Orgelitanus, Gregorius Magnus, Beda, Aponius, Bernardus, atque alii magni nominis viri. Sed omnia praeter rem, quod illorum pace dixerim. Neque enim ibi agitur de turture, quae cum σιαγόνες, id est, genas, aut malas non habeat, quis credat, sponsae genas cum turturis genis conferri? Ἡ δὲ ὄρνις (inquit Philosophus De partib. animal. libro 2. cap. 16.)

In Song 1:10 it is said to the bride in the Greek version, Τί ὡραιώθησαν σιαγόνες σου ὡς τρυγόνος; [Ti hōraiōthēsan siagones sou hōs trygonos?] and in the Vulgate, How beautiful are your cheeks become, like the turtledove's! On this passage the Greek doctors, Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Philo of Carpasia, Theodoret, and the Latin ones, Justus of Urgel, Gregory the Great, Bede, Aponius, Bernard [=Bernard of Clairvaux], and other men of great name, have much to say about the chastity of the turtledove and about the seat of modesty in the cheeks. But all of it is beside the point, if I may say so with their leave. For the passage is not about the turtledove there: since it has no σιαγόνες [siagones], that is, cheeks or jaws, who would believe that the bride's cheeks are compared with the cheeks of a turtledove? Ἡ δὲ ὄρνις [Hē de ornis] (says the Philosopher, De partib. animal. book 2, ch. 16) ¶

II, 599 ἀντὶ σιαγόνων ἔχει τὸ καλούμενον ῥύγχος, pro malis id habet, quod rostrum vocant. Frustra Ovidium objeceris reliquas aves invitare ad genas lacerandas in morte psittaci sui Amorum libri secundi Elegia sexta,

II, 599 ἀντὶ σιαγόνων ἔχει τὸ καλούμενον ῥύγχος [anti siagonōn echei to kaloumenon rhynchos], in place of jaws it has what they call a beak. In vain would you object that Ovid, at the death of his parrot, invites the other birds to tear their cheeks, in Amores book 2, Elegy 6: ¶

Ite, piae volucres, et plangite pectora pennis,
Et rigido teneras ungue notate genas.

Go, pious birds, and beat your breasts with your wings, and mark your tender cheeks with rigid claw. ¶

Haec enim ille ποιητικῶς, et πρωσοποποιητικῶς ad aves accommodans, quae col. 57 hominum in luctu propria sunt. Proinde idem Tristium lib. 3. Elegia 3.

For he is applying to birds ποιητικῶς [poiētikōs], and πρωσοποποιητικῶς [prōsopopoiētikōs], things that are proper to human beings in mourning. col. 57 Accordingly the same poet, Tristia book 3, Elegy 3: ¶

Parce tamen lacerare genas, nec scinde capillos.

Yet forbear to tear your cheeks, and do not rend your hair. ¶

Et Tibullus lib. 1. Elegia 1.

And Tibullus book 1, Elegy 1: ¶

Tu manes ne laede meos, sed parce solutis
Crinibus, et teneris, Delia, parce genis,

Do not wound my shade, but spare your loosened hair, and spare, Delia, your tender cheeks, ¶

Et Artemidorus Onirocriticῶν lib. 1. cap. 30. Παρειὰς ἴσχνας ἔχειν, ἢ ἑλκομέ-νας, λύπας, ἢ πένθος σημαίνει. ἴσχνας μὲν λύπας, ἑλκομένας δὲ πένθος. καὶ γὰρ ἐν τοῖς πένθεσι λωβῶνται τὰς παρειὰς οἱ ἄνθρωποι· Genas (in somnis) habere graciles, aut exulceratas, tristitiam et luctum significat. Graciles quidem tristitiam, exulceratas autem luctum. Nam in luctu homines genas dilacerant. Fateor, in Horo legi, turturem habere dentes Hieroglyph. lib. 2. cap. 50. Lactantem, ait, mulierem, et quae bene nutriat, ut significent (Aegyptii) τρυ-γόνα ζωγραφοῦσιν· αὕτη γὰρ μόνη τῶν ἄλλων πτηνῶν ὀδόντας, καὶ μασθοὺς ἔχει, turturem pingunt: sola enim haec inter volucres dentes et mammas habet. Quae si vera sunt, malas etiam habet et genas. Sed a doctis jam observatum, haec ad vespertilionem pertinere, cui soli conveniunt. Itaque τρυγόνα in τρι-γόνα nonnulli mutant: quo nomine vespertilionem ibi appellari conjectant, quia illius est proprium τρίζειν. Alii melius in νυκτερίδα, quae cur permutata fuerint, caussa est obvia. Proxime antecesserat, Imbecillum hominem, et magna molientem significare volentes, νυκτερίδα ζωγραφοῦσιν, vespertilionem pingunt, qui sine pennis volat. Et proxime sequitur, Hominem, qui saltatione et tibia delinitur, designaturi, τρυγόνα ζωγραφοῦσιν, turturem pingunt, quae tibia et saltatione capitur. Turturem reddidi cum Mercero, Caussino et aliis. Quamvis, quod eos fugisse miror, τρυγὼν ibi non sit turtur, sed pastinaca, quam infra cap. 105. τρυγόνα simpliciter appellat hic idem scriptor, et alii θαλαττίαν τρυ-γόνα marinum turturem διακριτικῶς. Neque enim turtur avis, sed ἡ θαλατ-τία τρυγὼν, turtur marinus, id est, pastinaca, tibia et saltatione delinitus in nassam inducitur, ut pluribus narrat Aelianus De animal. lib. 17. cap. 18. Quamvis etiam Philo ad turturem avem id referat. Iam, ut eo redeat, unde digressa est oratio, Sacri Cantici verba Hebraice sic habent: נאוו לחייך בתורים צוארך בחרוזים, Ornatae sunt genae tuae (gemmarum) ordinibus, collum tuum torquibus. II, 600 Pro quibus Graeci, Τί ὡραιώθησαν σιαγόνες σου ὡς τρυγόνος, τραχηλός σου ὡς ὅρμισκοι; et Hieronymus, Quam speciosae factae sunt genae tuae tanquam turturis, collum tuum sicut monile. Quia ב et כ permutatis, pro בתורים, legerunt כתורים, et כחרוזים pro בחרוזים, et תורי ab iisdem sumptum pro turturibus, cum ibi ordines significet, puta gemmarum, aut clavorum aureorum. Quomodo esse reddendum multa docent: 1. Quod תורים et חרוזים hic fere sunt synonyma. Porro, quamvis vox חרוזים alibi in Scriptura non extet, tamen ex Arabica lingua est notissima, qua خرز Verbum est subula consuere, et الخرز monile ex gemmis consertis, item spina dorsi, cujus spondilia tanquam consuta et conserta sunt. Itaque חרוזים Graece ὁρμισκοὺς, et Hieronymus monilia explicant, recte omnino. 2. Statim sequitur, תורי זהב נעשה לך, ubi palam est, תורי non esse turtures, sed aurei ornamenti genus, nempe torques, aut quid simile. Proinde ibi Hieronymus, Muraenulas aureas faciemus tibi. Et Syrus ܕܕܗܒܐ ܥܢܩܐ, torquem aureum. Et quinta editio στρεπτὸν χρυσίου, ita le-gendum, non στρεπτοῦ. Nam στρεπτὸς, vel στρεπτὸν, pro torque ab Herodoto, Xenophonte, Plutarcho, et aliis passim usurpatur. In Symmacho, pro περίβλε-πτα χρυσίου, legerim περίπλεκτα. Nempe στρέφειν, unde στρεπτὸν, et πλέ-κειν, unde πλεκτὸν, sunt fere synonyma. Proinde in Hesychio πλόκιον est εἶ-δος περιδεραίου, monilis species. Atque eadem ratio Syri ܥܢܩܐ pro torque. Nam ܥܢܩ verbum plicare et torquere est. At Seniores quorsum reddunt ὁμοιώματα? An pro תורי legerunt ציורי, id est, imagines? vel תורי זהב dici putarunt turturum similitudines, ex auro factas: quae fuit Aben Ezrae sententia? 3. Quin תור per Thau hic idem est, quod טור per Teth, qua voce pro gemmarum ordine passim utitur Moses: ut Exod. XXVIII, 17. ארבעה טורים אבן, quatuor ordines gemmarum. Item, אדם פטדת וברקת הטור האחד, Sardius, topazius et smaragdus, ordo primus. 5. Neque obscurum est, quomodo gemmarum ordinibus ornentur genae. Nempe si ex auribus pendeant, aut si inspergantur mitrarum redimiculis, id est, latis fasciis, quae per malas productae mento subligabantur. Nam in Syria mitra muliebre fuit capitis integumentum. Virgilius in Catalectis, col. 58

And Artemidorus, Onirocritica book 1, ch. 30: Παρειὰς ἴσχνας ἔχειν, ἢ ἑλκομένας, λύπας, ἢ πένθος σημαίνει. ἴσχνας μὲν λύπας, ἑλκομένας δὲ πένθος. καὶ γὰρ ἐν τοῖς πένθεσι λωβῶνται τὰς παρειὰς οἱ ἄνθρωποι· [Pareias ischnas echein, ē helkomenas, lypas, ē penthos sēmainei. ischnas men lypas, helkomenas de penthos. kai gar en tois penthesi lōbōntai tas pareias hoi anthrōpoi] To have thin or ulcerated cheeks (in dreams) signifies sorrow and mourning: thin cheeks sorrow, ulcerated ones mourning. For in mourning people tear their cheeks. I admit that I have read in Horus, Hieroglyph. book 2, ch. 50, that the turtledove has teeth. To signify a nursing woman, and one who nourishes well, he says, (the Egyptians) τρυγόνα ζωγραφοῦσιν· αὕτη γὰρ μόνη τῶν ἄλλων πτηνῶν ὀδόντας, καὶ μασθοὺς ἔχει [trygona zōgraphousin; hautē gar monē tōn allōn ptēnōn odontas, kai masthous echei], paint a turtledove: for she alone among birds has teeth and breasts. If this were true, she would also have jaws and cheeks. But learned men have already observed that this belongs to the bat, to which alone it applies. So some change τρυγόνα [trygona] into τριγόνα [trigona], conjecturing that the bat is called by that name there, because τρίζειν [trizein, to squeak] is proper to it. Others more correctly change it into νυκτερίδα [nykterida]; and the reason why the words were interchanged is obvious. Immediately before it had said: Wishing to signify a weak man who attempts great things, νυκτερίδα ζωγραφοῦσιν [nykterida zōgraphousin], they paint a bat, which flies without feathers. And immediately after follows: To designate a man who is charmed by dancing and the pipe, τρυγόνα ζωγραφοῦσιν [trygona zōgraphousin], they paint a turtledove, which is caught by the pipe and dancing. I have rendered turtledove with Mercerus [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs], Caussinus and others. Yet — and I am surprised this escaped them — τρυγὼν [trygōn] there is not the turtledove but the stingray, which this same writer below, in ch. 105, calls simply τρυγόνα [trygona], and others, to distinguish it, θαλαττίαν τρυγόνα [thalattian trygona], the sea turtledove. For it is not the bird, the turtledove, but ἡ θαλαττία τρυγὼν [hē thalattia trygōn], the sea turtledove, that is, the stingray, that, charmed by the pipe and dancing, is lured into the fish-trap, as Aelian relates at length, On Animals book 17, ch. 18; although Philo too refers this to the turtledove, the bird. Now, to return to where the discussion digressed: the words of the Sacred Song in Hebrew read thus: נאוו לחייך בתורים צוארך בחרוזים, Your cheeks are adorned with rows (of gems), your neck with chains. II, 600 For this the Greeks have Τί ὡραιώθησαν σιαγόνες σου ὡς τρυγόνος, τραχηλός σου ὡς ὅρμισκοι; [Ti hōraiōthēsan siagones sou hōs trygonos, trachēlos sou hōs hormiskoi?] and Jerome: How beautiful have your cheeks become, like the turtledove's; your neck like a necklace. For, interchanging ב and כ, they read כתורים for בתורים, and כחרוזים for בחרוזים, and they took תורי for turtledoves, whereas there it means rows, namely of gems, or of golden studs. That it must be rendered so is shown by many things: 1. תורים and חרוזים are here nearly synonymous. Moreover, although the word חרוזים does not occur elsewhere in Scripture, it is very well known from the Arabic language, in which the verb خرز [kharaza] means to sew with an awl, and الخرز [al-kharaz] a necklace of gems strung together, and also the spine, whose vertebrae are as it were sewn and strung together. Accordingly the Greek renders חרוזים as ὁρμισκοὺς [hormiskous], and Jerome as necklaces, entirely correctly. 2. Immediately there follows תורי זהב נעשה לך, where it is plain that תורי are not turtledoves but a kind of golden ornament, namely chains or something similar. Hence there Jerome has: We will make you golden chains. And the Syriac ܕܕܗܒܐ ܥܢܩܐ [דדהבא ענקא], a golden chain. And the fifth edition στρεπτὸν χρυσίου [strepton chrysiou]; so it should be read, not στρεπτοῦ [streptou]. For στρεπτὸς [streptos] or στρεπτὸν [strepton] is used everywhere for a torque by Herodotus, Xenophon, Plutarch and others. In Symmachus, for περίβλεπτα χρυσίου [periblepta chrysiou] I would read περίπλεκτα [periplekta]. For στρέφειν [strephein], whence στρεπτὸν [strepton], and πλέκειν [plekein], whence πλεκτὸν [plekton], are nearly synonymous. Hence in Hesychius πλόκιον [plokion] is εἶδος περιδεραίου [eidos perideraiou], a kind of necklace. And the same reasoning holds for the Syriac ܥܢܩܐ [ענקא] for torque, for the verb ܥܢܩ [ענק] means to fold and to twist. But why do the Elders render ὁμοιώματα [homoiōmata]? Did they read ציורי, that is, images, for תורי? Or did they think that תורי זהב meant likenesses of turtledoves made of gold, which was the opinion of Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra]? 3. Indeed, תור with Thau here is the same as טור with Teth, a word Moses uses repeatedly for a row of gems, as in Exod. XXVIII, 17: ארבעה טורים אבן, four rows of stones. Likewise, אדם פטדת וברקת הטור האחד, sardius, topaz and emerald, the first row. 5. Nor is it obscure how cheeks are adorned with rows of gems: namely if these hang from the ears, or if they are scattered over the ribbons of the mitres, that is, the broad bands that were drawn down along the cheeks and tied under the chin. For in Syria the mitre was a woman's head-covering. Virgil in the Catalecta: col. 58 ¶

Copa Syrisca caput Graia redimita mitella.

The Syrian tavern-hostess, her head bound with a Greek little mitre. ¶

De turturum sacrificiis supra diximus. Actum etiam de castitate: et ex Aristotele, Aeliano et Clemente probatum, turtures nonnisi unum conjugem ad-mittere. Unde natum, quod magno consensu scripsere posteriores, mortuo conjuge turturem viduam permanere. Quo pertinere volunt haec verba Psaltis Psal. LXXIV, 19. Ne tradas catervae (hostium) animam turturis tuae, id est, inquiunt, Ecclesiae Israelis, quae, etiam a te deserta, alii Deo non adhaeret. Rabbi Selomo, התור הזה משמת הזכר בן זוגה אינה מזדווגת לאחר אף ישראל לא התחליפוך באל אחר אף על פי שרחקתה מהם והיו כאלמנה, Turtur foe- mina, ex quo obiit mas conjux ejus, alteri non copulatur. Ita nec Israelitae te cum alio Deo commutarunt, etiamsi ab iis recesseris, sed viduae instar manserunt. Sed nihil opus est eo usque descendere. Neque enim id satis certum est, turturem in viduitate alteri non copulari. Quamvis scriptores, qui id asserunt,

We have spoken above about the sacrifices of turtledoves. Their chastity has also been treated, and it was proved from Aristotle, Aelian and Clement that turtledoves admit only one mate. From this arose what later writers have stated with great unanimity: that when its mate has died, the turtledove remains a widow. To this they would refer these words of the Psalmist, Ps 74:19. Deliver not the soul of thy turtledove unto the multitude (of the enemies), that is, they say, the Church of Israel, which, even when forsaken by you, does not cleave to another God. R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)]: התור הזה משמת הזכר בן זוגה אינה מזדווגת לאחר אף ישראל לא התחליפוך באל אחר אף על פי שרחקתה מהם והיו כאלמנה, The female turtledove, once her male mate has died, does not pair with another. So too the Israelites have not exchanged you for another God, even though you have withdrawn from them, but have remained like a widow. But there is no need to go so far. For it is not sufficiently certain that the turtledove in widowhood does not pair with another, even though the writers who assert it ¶

Defendat numerus, junctaeque umbone phalanges.

may be defended by their number and by phalanxes joined shield to shield. ¶

At constat, saltem turturem, vivo conjuge, cum altero non commisceri. Ad quod exemplum ecclesia si se conformaverit, alios deos nunquam colet; quia Deus, quem colit, semper vivus est. Potuere etiam Israelitae cum turture conferri; לפי שהם חלושי הכח ונטרפים ואינם טורפים וכן ישראל, quia turtures viribus infirmae sunt, et diripiuntur, nec diripiunt, et sic Israelitae. Quae Kim- chii conjectura est. Nam et columbae genus est turtur, et in columbino genere omnium minima. Philosophus Hist. lib. 5. cap. 13. Ἐλάχιστον δὲ τῶν τοιούτων ἡ τρυγών· Minimum ex iis turtur est. Eodem quoque referri queant quaecunque de columbae simplicitate dicta sunt, cum turtur sit columbae genus. Mirabere, cur veterum interpretum, quotquot extant, nullus turturis meminerit in versione hujus loci. Nam, pro anima turturis tuae, Graeci habent ψυχὴν ἐξομολογουμένην σοι, animam, confitentem tibi. Quod idem sequuntur Syrus et Aethiops. Nempe תודך legerunt pro תורך, Daleth pro Res, quae litera sunt simillimae. Arabes editi habent نفسا عارفة بك animam, quae te no- vit. Et unus e manuscriptis, qui penes me sunt, انفس يايسيك animas, qui te sciunt, id est, qui te noverunt. Quasi legerint נפש תדעך, ani- col. 59 mam, quae novit te. At Hieronymus, Animam eruditam in Lege tua. Et Chal- daica versio Iosephi Coeci, נפשת מאלפי אוריתך, animas docentium Legem tuam. Quasi תורך alluderet ad vocem תורה, quae Legem sonat. Sed haec remota et aliena sunt. Et, si Graecos excipias, his omnibus vetustior Ionathan recte viderat, תור hic esse turturem. Atque is quidem non extat. Sed illum ita vertisse, II, 602 docent haec Selomonis: יונתן תירגם לשון תורים ובני יונה, Ionathan reddidit, ut in legis locis, quibus agitur de turturibus et filiis columbarum. Quod Hebraei merito sequuntur, et recentiores interpretes, quotquot habentur.

But it is certain at least that the turtledove does not mate with another while its mate is alive. If the Church conforms itself to this example, it will never worship other gods, because the God whom it worships is ever living. The Israelites could also be compared with the turtledove לפי שהם חלושי הכח ונטרפים ואינם טורפים וכן ישראל, because turtledoves are weak in strength, and are torn as prey but do not prey, and so are the Israelites. This is Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]'s conjecture. For the turtledove is a kind of dove, and the smallest of all in the dove kind. The Philosopher, Hist. book 5, ch. 13: Ἐλάχιστον δὲ τῶν τοιούτων ἡ τρυγών [Elachiston de tōn toioutōn hē trygōn]· The smallest of these is the turtledove. To the same purpose could also be referred whatever has been said of the simplicity of the dove, since the turtledove is a kind of dove. You will wonder why none of the ancient translators, as many as are extant, mentioned the turtledove in rendering this passage. For in place of the soul of thy turtledove the Greeks have ψυχὴν ἐξομολογουμένην σοι [psychēn exomologoumenēn soi], a soul confessing to thee. The Syriac and the Ethiopic follow the same. They evidently read תודך for תורך, Daleth for Resh, which are very similar letters. The printed Arabic versions have نفسا عارفة بك [nafsan ʿārifatan bika] a soul that knows thee. And one of the manuscripts in my possession has انفس يايسيك [anfus yāysīk] souls who know thee, that is, who have known thee. As if they had read נפש תדעך, a soul col. 59 that knows thee. But Jerome has A soul instructed in thy Law. And the Chaldee version of Joseph the Blind has נפשת מאלפי אוריתך, the souls of those teaching thy Law, as though תורך alluded to the word תורה, which means Law. But these renderings are far-fetched and beside the point. And, apart from the Greeks, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan], older than all of these, had rightly seen that תור here is the turtledove. His version, indeed, is not extant. But that he rendered it so is shown by these words of Selomo: II, 602 יונתן תירגם לשון תורים ובני יונה, Jonathan rendered it as in the passages of the Law dealing with turtledoves and young doves. The Hebrews rightly follow this, as do the more recent translators, as many as there are. ¶
