III, 374 Chapter 12. col. 517 ¶

De melle et cera.

On honey and wax. ¶

Sequitur, ut de melle dicamus, de quo Scriptura passim. Mel Hebraice voca- col. 518 tur דבש debas, aequivoco nomine, quo appellantur etiam dactyli. Kimchius in Lexico, ונקראו התמרים דבש, Etiam dactylos DEBAS (id est, mel) vocarunt. Quomodo sumi volunt R. Selomo et idem Kimchius 2 Paral. XXXI, 6. ubi Israelitae leguntur obtulisse primitias frumenti, musti et olei, ודבש, et debas, id est, inquiunt, dactylorum. Addit Kimchius, veteres ita sumere Deut. VIII, 8. in quo loco promittit Deus, se eum introducturum in terram זית שמן ודבש, oleae oliferae et debas. Huc facit, quod in Alcamus دبوس dubous sunt خلاص تمر يلقي في مسلاء السمن فيذوب فيه dactyli selectiores, qui in butyrum liquefactum conjiciuntur, ut in eo colliquescant. Et دبس dibs, vel dibis, غسل التمر وغسل النخل mel dactyli et mel palmae, id est, dactyli, vel palmae succus. Qui jure merito cum melle habet commune nomen, cum sit vicini saporis. Iosephus libri quinti De bello Iudaico capite tertio, ubi de valle Ierichuntina: Pulcherrimos paradisos ac densissimos educat, palmarumque irriguarum multa genera, tam sapore, quam nominibus varia. Τούτων οἱ πιότεροι πατούμενοι, καὶ μέλι δαψιλὲς ἀνίασιν, οὐ πολλῷ τοῦ λοιποῦ χεῖρον· Harum pinguissimae calcatae plurimum mellis emittunt, non multo deterius alio melle. Et Iosephus alius, propior quidem tempore, sed loco remotior, Indus nimirum Navigatione capite 138. Mense Augusto Indi palmam putant. Et quoniam tunc arbores potissimum luxuriant, gemunt incisae, lacrymasque instar sauciatae vitis emittunt. Has vasis excipiunt tribus solum diebus ab arbore sauciata, etc. Qui vero saccharum efficere voluerit, is aquam hujusmodi sumat emanantem intra triduum, et ahenis impositam tantisper coquat, donec vix tertia pars supersit. Tunc sic decocta mel efficitur suavissimum. Patet igitur, cur ab Hebraeis inditum fuerit mellis nomen vel dactylorum, vel palmae succo (1). Quin R. Selomo in Levit. II, 11. id extendit multo latius, quasi דבש debas significet כל מתיקת פרי, omnem dulcedinem fructus (2). Utut sit, hic agimus de melle proprie dicto.

Next we must speak of honey, which Scripture mentions in many places. In Hebrew honey is col. 518 called דבש debas, an ambiguous name, by which dates are also designated. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his Lexicon: ונקראו התמרים דבש, They also called dates DEBAS (that is, honey). R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] and the same Kimchi wish it to be taken in this way in 2 Chr 31:6, where the Israelites are said to have offered the first fruits of grain, of must and of oil, ודבש, and of debas, that is, they say, of dates. Kimchi adds that the ancients took it thus in Deut 8:8, in which passage God promises that He will bring him into a land זית שמן ודבש, of oil-bearing olives and of debas. To this point belongs the fact that in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] دبوس [dabūs] dubous are خلاص تمر يلقي في مسلاء السمن فيذوب فيه [khalāṣ tamr yulqā fī maslāʾ al-samn fa-yadhūbu fīhi] choicer dates, which are thrown into melted butter, so as to dissolve in it. And دبس [dibs] dibs, or dibis, is غسل التمر وغسل النخل [ghasl al-tamr wa-ghasl al-nakhl] the honey of the date and the honey of the palm, that is, the juice of the date or of the palm. It rightly shares its name with honey, since its taste is similar. Josephus, The Jewish War, book 5, chapter 3, speaking of the valley of Jericho: It produces most beautiful and very dense gardens, and many kinds of irrigated palms, varying both in taste and in names. Τούτων οἱ πιότεροι πατούμενοι, καὶ μέλι δαψιλὲς ἀνίασιν, οὐ πολλῷ τοῦ λοιποῦ χεῖρον· [Toutōn hoi pioteroi patoumenoi, kai meli dapsiles aniasin, ou pollō tou loipou cheiron] The richest of these, when trodden, give out a great deal of honey, not much inferior to other honey. And another Josephus, closer in time indeed but more remote in place, namely the Indian, in his Voyage, chapter 138: In the month of August the Indians prune the palm. And since the trees are then at their most luxuriant, when cut they groan, and emit tears like a wounded vine. These they catch in vessels for only three days after the tree has been wounded, etc. But whoever wishes to make sugar, let him take water of this kind flowing out within three days, and put it in copper pots and boil it, until scarcely a third part remains. Then, thus boiled down, it becomes a most sweet honey. It is therefore clear why the Hebrews gave the name of honey either to dates or to the juice of the palm R1. Indeed R. Selomo on Lev 2:11 extends it much more widely, as though דבש debas meant כל מתיקת פרי, all sweetness of fruit R2. However that may be, here we are dealing with honey properly so called. ¶

III, 375 Hujus duo sunt genera: domesticum unum, ex apibus, quae domi aluntur; et alterum sylvestre, quali legitur Iohannes in deserto vixisse, Matth. III, 4. Cibus ejus erant locustae et mel sylvestre. Et Marc. I, 6. Comedens locustas et mel sylvestre. Cum ibi μέλι ἄγριον, mel sylvestre diserte scribant Evangelistae duo, mirum, quosdam esse, qui μελαγρίαν intelligant herbae genus, si credimus, in deserto crescentis. Suidas, Μελαγρία· εἶδος βο-τάνης φυομένης ἐν ἐρήμῳ. Alii τὰ μέλαγρα scribunt, quasi herba sit hujus nominis, quam aliter ἀκρίδας vocent. Theophylactus, Τινὲς λέγουσι βοτάνας εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας, ἃς καὶ μέλαγρα καλοῦσι· Acridas quidam herbas esse ajunt, quas etiam melagra vocant. Quae utut concederemus, tamen extra rem essent. Neque enim legitur Ioannes comedisse μελαγρίαν, aut μέλαγρα, sed μέλι ἄγριον. Nihilo magis placet, quod afferunt Beda in libro De locis sanctis, et Thomas in Matthaeum ex Arnulpho, vel Archulpho, in deserto Iohannis arbores fuisse latis et rotundis foliis, lactei coloris, et mellei saporis, quae, natura fragi- col. 519 lia, manibus confricentur atque edantur, et hoc dici mel sylvestre. Nec Christiano Druthmaro assentior, cui mel sylvestre hoc loco est illud ipsum Manna, quo filii Israel pasti sunt per quadraginta annos. Mannae quidem sapor fuit כצפיחת בדבש, ut sapor lagani melliti, Exod. XVI, 31. Ac proinde a nonnullis Manna vocatur δροσόμελι ac ἀερόμελι (3). Tamen aliud Manna, aliud sylvestre III, 376 mel. Nec scripturae vocibus novae notiones sunt affingendae, cum receptae quadrare possunt. Hic igitur nihil quidquam intelligo, quam locustas et mel sylvestre. Et locustas jam probavimus a) in illis locis esse frequentes, adeoque in cibis receptas. Idem de sylvestri melle vel ex ipsa Scriptura probare est obvium. Notum est illud Solomonis Prov. XXV, 16. Mel reperisti? comede, quantum satis est. Et haud dubie pertinet ad sylvestre mel, quod in agris passim reperiebatur. Ita cum in agro Samson in leonem incidisset, eumque dilacerasset, III, 377 anno sequente mel reperit, quod in ejus cadavere apes elaboraverant, Iud. XIV, 8. Quale est, quod legitur 1 Sam. XIV, 25. 26. Populus venit in sylvam, in qua erat mel על פני השדה, in agri superficie. Et in eam sylvam populus ingressus est, et ecce הלך דבש, fluxum mellis. Id nempe fuit sylvestre mel, quod apes vel in nudo solo, vel in cavis arborum, vel in fissuris rupium confecerant. Qualis rupes Hebraice vocatur צור דבש, rupes mellis, Ps. LXXXI, 17. Et mel inde manans דבש מסלע, mel e petra, Deut. XXXII, 13. Sic a voce יער jaar, quae sylvam sonat, (ut jar Punice lignum apud Augustinum in Psalmum XXIII.), יער jaar, vel יערה jaara, est favus proprie in sylva repertus. Ut 1 Sam. XIV, 27. Extendit Ionathan summitatem virgae, quam habebat in manu, et immersit eam ביערת הדבש, in favum mellis. Et Cant. V, 1. Comedi יערי, favum meum cum melle meo (4). Neque est, quod quis hoc credat Iudaeae fuisse peculiare, quod ibi passim apes in agris aut sylvis mellificabant. Nam idem de Scythia et Moscovia, ex idoneis scriptoribus jam b) didicimus. Et Maldonatus in Matthaeum refert, se pauperes homines in Boetica vidisse, qui mel ex sylvis colligerent, eoque vendendo quaestum factitarent. Itaque apum, III, 378 quae vel in cavis arborum, vel in arundinetis, aut in antris nidificant, poetae passim meminere. Hesiodus Theogoniae versu 230.

III, 375 Of this there are two kinds: one domestic, from bees that are kept at home; the other wild, such as John is said to have lived on in the desert, Matt 3:4. His food was locusts and wild honey. And Mark 1:6. Eating locusts and wild honey. Since the two Evangelists expressly write μέλι ἄγριον [meli agrion], wild honey, it is surprising that there are some who understand μελαγρίαν [melagrian] as a kind of plant which, if we believe them, grows in the desert. Suidas: Μελαγρία· εἶδος βοτάνης φυομένης ἐν ἐρήμῳ [Melagria; eidos botanēs phyomenēs en erēmō]. Others write τὰ μέλαγρα [ta melagra], as though there were a plant of this name, which they otherwise call ἀκρίδας [akridas]. Theophylact: Τινὲς λέγουσι βοτάνας εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας, ἃς καὶ μέλαγρα καλοῦσι [Tines legousi botanas einai tas akridas, has kai melagra kalousi]; Some say that the acrides are plants, which they also call melagra. Even if we granted this, it would still be beside the point. For we do not read that John ate μελαγρίαν [melagrian] or μέλαγρα [melagra], but μέλι ἄγριον [meli agrion]. No more acceptable is what Bede in his book De locis sanctis, and Thomas on Matthew, report from Arnulphus or Archulphus: that in the desert of John there were trees with broad, round leaves, of milky colour and honeyed taste, which, being naturally brittle, are rubbed in the hands and eaten, and that this is called wild honey. col. 519 Nor do I agree with Christianus Druthmarus, for whom the wild honey in this passage is that very Manna on which the children of Israel were fed for forty years. The taste of Manna was indeed כצפיחת בדבש, like the taste of a honeyed wafer, Exod 16:31. And accordingly Manna is called by some δροσόμελι [drosomeli] and ἀερόμελι [aeromeli] R3. Yet Manna is one thing, wild III, 376 honey another. Nor should new meanings be foisted onto the words of Scripture when the received ones can fit. Here, therefore, I understand nothing other than locusts and wild honey. And we have already shown a) that locusts are frequent in those regions and therefore were accepted as food. The same about wild honey is easy to prove from Scripture itself. Well known is the saying of Solomon, Prov 25:16: Have you found honey? Eat as much as is sufficient. And this doubtless refers to wild honey, which was found here and there in the fields. Thus when Samson had come upon a lion in the field and torn it apart, III, 377 in the following year he found honey which bees had produced in its carcass, Iud. XIV, 8. Of the same kind is what we read in 1 Sam. XIV, 25. 26: The people came into the forest, in which there was honey על פני השדה, on the surface of the field. And the people entered that forest, and behold הלך דבש, a flow of honey. This, namely, was wild honey, which bees had made either on the bare ground, or in hollow trees, or in clefts of rocks. Such a rock is called in Hebrew צור דבש, rock of honey, Ps 81:17. And the honey flowing from it is דבש מסלע, honey from the rock, Deut 32:13. So from the word יער jaar, which means forest (as jar in Punic means wood according to Augustine on Psalm 23), יער jaar, or יערה jaara, is a honeycomb, properly one found in the forest. As in 1 Sam. XIV, 27: Jonathan stretched out the tip of the rod that he had in his hand, and dipped it ביערת הדבש, in the honeycomb. And Cant. V, 1: I have eaten יערי, my honeycomb with my honey R4. Nor is there reason for anyone to believe that it was peculiar to Judaea that bees made honey here and there in fields or forests. For we have already learned b) the same of Scythia and Muscovy from reliable writers. And Maldonatus on Matthew reports that he saw poor men in Baetica who gathered honey from the forests and made their living by selling it. Accordingly the poets frequently mention bees III, 378 that nest in hollow trees, in reed-beds, or in caves. Hesiod, Theogony, line 230: ¶

— — — οὔρεσι δὲ δρῦς
Ἄκρη μέν τε φέρει βαλάνους, μέσση δὲ μελίσσας,

Such is the mention in Iob. XX, 17 of נחלי דבש וחמאה, torrents of honey and butter. This expression is hyperbolic in such a way that it partly suits honey, because honey flows down from the comb when it is abundant. This flowing of honey is expressed in various ways among the Hebrews and Syrians. First by the word הלך halach, which properly means to go or to walk. But in 1 Sam 14:26, הלך דבש is a flow of honey. However that may be, since Samson had taken honey out of a lion's body, a thing otherwise without precedent, he drew from it the subject of a griphus or riddle. For it was the custom among the ancients to propose questions at banquets, in the solving of which their wits were exercised. III, 384 Clearchus calls such a question a griphus, and in Athenaeus defines it thus: Γρίφος πρόβλημά ἐστι παιστικὸν, προστακτικὸν τοῦ διὰ ζητήσεως εὑρεῖν τῇ διανοίᾳ τὸ προβληθὲν, τιμῆς ἢ ἐπιζημίου χάριν εἰρημένον [Griphos problēma esti paistikon, prostaktikon tou dia zētēseōs heurein tē dianoia to problēthen, timēs ē epizēmiou charin eirēmenon]. A griphus is a playful problem, by which one is bidden to find out by searching of the mind what has been proposed, put forward for the sake of a reward or a penalty. And that such were customarily proposed to guests at table, Athenaeus shows at length in book 10. Julius col. 523 Pollux also mentions them in book 6, chapter 19, where he distinguishes the riddles and griphi of drinking companions, holding that the former were always jocular, the latter sometimes serious. His words are: Τῶν μὲν συμποτικῶν αἴνιγμα καὶ γρῖφος· τὸ μὲν παιδιὰν εἶχεν, ὁ δὲ γρῖφος καὶ σπουδήν [Tōn men sympotikōn ainigma kai griphos; to men paidian eichen, ho de griphos kai spoudēn]. He goes on to say that the one who solved it was given a dish of meat, κρεῶν τινα περιφορὰν [kreōn tina periphoran], just as ἅλμης ποτήριον [halmēs potērion], a cup of brine, was given to be drunk by the one who could not solve it. Other rewards too, and likewise other penalties, customary among the Greeks, may be read in Athenaeus in the place cited. Gellius, book 18, ch. 2, reports that something of the sort was practised by Romans at Athens during the Saturnalia: Several of us Romans who had come to Greece and attended the same lectures and the same teachers used to meet at the same dinner. Then the one whose turn it was to provide the little dinner would set as the prize for solving a question a book by an old writer, either Greek or Latin, and a crown woven of laurel; and he would ask as many questions as there were men present. When he had set them all out, the lot assigned the question and the order of speaking. If the question was solved, the man was presented with the crown and the prize; if it was not solved, it passed to the one who came next by lot. And this was kept up in turn around the circle. If no one untied the knot of the question, the prize itself and the crown were dedicated to the god whose festival it was. But nothing establishes the antiquity of the custom so much as what we read of Samson: that he proposed to his thirty guests a riddle to be solved within the seven days of the feast, on the condition that, if they solved it, they would receive as a prize thirty linen garments and as many changes of clothing; if not, they themselves would give them. The riddle was this: Out of the eater came forth food, and out of the strong came forth sweetness. In these words, as food is set against the eater, so sweetness is set against the strong. Yet these do not seem to be opposites. For the opposite of strong is unwarlike or weak; the opposite of sweet is sharp or bitter. But sometimes these are confused, and bitter is taken for strong, and sharp for both. The word נכאת necoth, of very uncertain meaning, in Genes. XXXVII, 25. and XLIII, 11., is explained by Jonathan as שעוא and by Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] as شمعا [shamʿan], that is, wax, as also by the author of Beresith Rabba, section 91, near the end. And in Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] they think that שעך is the very same thing, because it comes close to שעוא. However that may be, other interpreters explain it very differently. The LXX have θυμίαμα [thymiama] or θυμιάματα [thymiamata]; Aquila, στύρακα [styraka]; Jerome in the first passage aromata [spices], in the second storacem [storax], like Aquila; the Syriac ܪ̈ܗܛܢܐ [רהטנא], resin; the Samaritan ࠒࠈࠐ [קטף], balsam; the Arabic version published at Paris, خرنوبا [kharnūban], just as some in Kimchi have חרוב, carob; two Arabic manuscripts in Sweden صمغا [ṣamghan], gum; Rabbi Moses the Priest and Kimchi, דבר נחמד, a desirable thing; R. Selomo, כנוסי בשמים הרבה, collections of many spices. In this doubtful matter the later writers follow one or another of the older ones, as seemed good to them. Junius alone, taking a path of his own, rendered it myxaria [sebesten plums], which, he says, are called in Arabic NUKETA, according to Aegineta, Aëtius, Psellus and Actuarius, a word very close to the Hebrew NEKOTH. But this man, otherwise pious and learned, has missed the point. For the Arabic name of the sebesten, مخيط [mukhayṭ] mucchait, as the Arabs consistently write it, has not a single letter in common with the Hebrew נכאת nechoth, as anyone who is not entirely ἀναλφάβητος [analphabētos] in these languages will at once recognize. Nor do the authors he cites mention the Arabic name; among the Greeks only Charito, as far as I know, does so, writing μουχαῖτας [mouchaitas], as the apothecaries write mukeitas and mokaitas. Most of the other more recent writers explain נכאת necoth as θυμιάματα [thymiamata], spices, with the Greeks, Jerome and some of the Hebrews. That this can hardly stand will be evident to anyone who reads the two passages of Scripture in which this word occurs. The first reads thus, Gen 37:25. Lifting up their eyes they saw; and behold, a company of Ishmaelite travellers coming from Gilead, whose camels were carrying נכאת nechoth, and resin, and stacte; and they were going to carry them down into Egypt. And the second, Gen 43:11. Take of the most praised fruits of this land in your vessels, to carry a gift to that man, a little resin, נכאת nechoth, and stacte, and pistachios, and almonds. From reading these the attentive reader will see that נכאת is not θυμιάματα [thymiamata], or spices, or a desirable thing III, 400 in general, but something particular, like honey, resin, stacte, etc., which are mentioned in the same place. And I have several reasons why Aquila, who rendered it storax, pleases me most. Let the first be this: that storax grows abundantly in Syria. Pliny, book 12, chapter 25: Syria, next to Judaea, above Phoenicia, produces storax around Gabala and Marathus, and Casius, the mountain of Seleucia. Dioscorides, book 2, after giving the marks of the best storax, says Τοιοῦτος δέ ἐστιν ὁ Καταβαλίτης, καὶ Πισσιδιακὸς, καὶ Κιλίκιος [Toioutos de estin ho Katabalitēs, kai Pissidiakos, kai Kilikios], where for ὁ Καταβαλίτης [ho Katabalitēs] one should read ὁ Γαβαλίτης [ho Gabalitēs], that is, from the very well-known city of Syria, Gabala, which Pliny mentions. Others assign it to Phoenicia, like Artemidorus in Stephanus under Κάρνη [Karnē]. And Josephus to Galilee, Antiquities book 15, chapter 23. Second, storax is rightly counted among the most praised fruits of the land, since physicians particularly commend its use among the spices. Third, as here the Ishmaelites, so in Pliny, book 12, chapter 17, the Arabs bring back storax from Syria. Fourth, it is not without reason that Moses joins storax with resin, col. 533 honey and stacte of myrrh, since it approaches their nature. Accordingly Dioscorides describes the best storax as ῥητινώδης [rhētinōdēs], resinous, καὶ ἐν τῷ μαλάσσεσθαι ἀνεὶς ὑγρασίαν τινὰ μελιτώδη [kai en tō malassesthai aneis hygrasian tina melitōdē], and when kneaded giving off a honey-like liquid. And to it is attributed δάκρυον κόμμι ἐοικὸς, διαυγὲς, σμηρνίζον [dakryon kommi eoikos, diauges, smērnizon], a tear like gum, translucent, like myrrh. So also Isidore, book 17, chapter 8: Storax calamites, fat, resinous, of a pleasant smell, moist, and as it were giving off a honeyed liquid. Hence in Pliny storax is adulterated with cedar resin, or gum, at other times with honey. Fifth, I think I can detect the origin of the Hebrew name, hitherto unknown. The Arabic verb نكت [nakata] nachatha means 1. to strike the ground with a spear so that a mark remains impressed; 2. to throw someone pierced by a spear on his head; and النكت [al-nakt] is such a piercing, and النكات [al-nakkāt] one who pierces thus. Why should the Hebrew name of storax, נכאת nechoth, not allude to this, since from the storax tree are made τὰ στυράκινα ἀκοντίσματα ἐοικότα τοῖς κρανεΐνοις [ta styrakina akontismata eoikota tois kraneinois], storax javelins similar to those made of cornel? So it is in Strabo, book 12. And accordingly in the Greek Hesychius σύραξ [syrax] means not only storax, the tree or the spice, but also a lance, and a part of the spear, either the lowest or the highest. For the ancients do not agree well on this. In Pollux, book 1, chapter 10: Τὸ μὲν τέλος τοῦ δόρατος σαυρωτὴρ, τὸ δὲ προῦχον αἰχμὴ, καὶ ἐπιδορατὶς, καὶ στύραξ [To men telos tou doratos saurōtēr, to de prouchon aichmē, kai epidoratis, kai styrax], the lowest part of the spear is the sauroter, and the top the point, and the styrax. Yet in the same author, book 10, chapter 31, σαυρωτὴρ [saurōtēr] and στύραξ [styrax] are the same. So also in Hesychius, Suidas and the Etymologicum. And in Athenaeus, book 12, Heraclides of Cyme describes the μηλοφόροι [mēlophoroi] of the Persians, the melophori, ἐπὶ τῶν συράκων μῆλα χρυσᾶ ἔχοντες [epi tōn syrakōn mēla chrysa echontes], having golden apples on their styraces (that is, on the tops of their spear-shafts). And this is precisely the στυράκιον [styrakion] in book 2 of Thucydides, where we read that a certain Plataean shut the gates of the city στυρακίῳ ἀντὶ [styrakiō anti] III, 401 βαλάνου χρησάμενος ἐς τὸν μοχλὸν [balanou chrēsamenos es ton mochlon], driving the butt of a spear-shaft into the bar instead of a bolt-pin. And the verb συρακίζειν [syrakizein] in Hesychius is κεντρίζειν [kentrizein], to prick, as with a spear or the point of a spear. Sixth, if we contend by authority, Aquila, for whom נכאת nechoth is σύραξ [syrax], is followed by Jerome at Gen 43:11, where he renders storax. And in the Book of Names you may read NECHOTA, storax, or spices. I suspect that the Syrians, who explained it as resin, and the Arabs, who explained it as gum, held the same view, but misused a generic name because the specific one was unknown to them. With נכאת nechoth, the spice, the Hebrews wrongly confuse נכותה necotha, which is plainly something else, Isa 39:2 and 2 Reg. XX, 13., as will be said elsewhere R12. ¶

Verum, ut taceam, exempla, quae Hillerus adfert, positae א pro ת repetenda, valde incerta esse, cum voces illae ad diversas potius radices sint referendae (veluti ימאס ad מאס, שגיאה ad שגא etc.), dubito etiam, נכאת, si vere tusa significet, absolute positum pro oleis, sive aromatibus tusis usurpatum fuisse. R12 Rosenmüller, III, 401 Yet everyone will himself feel that the derivation of the word נכאת which Bochart gave is too far-fetched and therefore hardly probable. Nor is the opinion of LOUIS DE DIEU more satisfactory, who in his Animadversiones on Genes. XXXVII, 25. compares the Arabic نكيث [nakīth], which, as Firuzabadi teaches, is one of the سواك [siwāk], the name by which are designated all the dentifrices in daily use among the Arabs, among which certain aromatics are most esteemed, by which not only are the teeth cleaned but above all a fragrance is imparted to the breath of the mouth. But whether نكيث [nakīth] is rightly compared with the Hebrew נכאת, I doubt. Much less, however, does the interpretation of HILLER seem acceptable, who in the Hierophyt. vol. I, p. 212, renders נכאת as things pounded, crushed, from כתת, he pounded, so that נכאת would be written for נכתות, with א put in place of a repeated ת, just as ימאס, it will melt, is said for ימסס Job 7:5; שגיאה, error, for שגיגה, Ps 19:13; רבאות for רבובות, myriads, Esr. II, 69., after the manner of the Syrians, who for רגג say ראג, desiring, Gal. V, 17., and for רותת, with Thau changed into Aleph, ܪܬܝܬ [רתית], trembling, Acts 16:29. By the name of pounded things here he thinks pounded oils are to be understood, since in Exod 27:20. XXIX, 4. Lev 14:2 mention is made of שמן זית כתית, pounded olive oil, used for the lamps in the temple and for the sacred offerings. But in the Institt. Linguae Sanctae, p. 409, he understands by pounded things crushed aromatics, aromatic powders. However, not to mention that the examples Hiller adduces of א put in place of a repeated ת are very uncertain, since those words should rather be referred to different roots (for instance ימאס to מאס, שגיאה to שגא etc.), I also doubt that נכאת, if it truly means pounded things, was used absolutely for oils or pounded aromatics. Whoever wishes to know the much less probable conjectures of others may consult CLODIUS, Lexic. Hebr. Select. p. 339. To me the most satisfactory interpretation is the one Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791) and Clavis über den Jesaias (1793)] offered in the Clavis in Iesaiam, Chapter XXXIX, 2, p. 275 (although he rightly considers the נכתה occurring there to be different from our נכאת). He compares the Hebrew נכאת with the Arabic نَكَأَةٌ [nakaʾatun], which in GOLIUS is said to be the same as نُكَعَةٌ [nukaʿatun], the top of the طُرْثُوث [ṭurthūth], conspicuous for its reddish bark, resembling the flower of the amaranth, or its reddish flower, with which one is wont to dye. Also gum tragacanth: the latter meaning would fit the passages Genes. XXXVII, 25. XLIII, 11. exceedingly well.
