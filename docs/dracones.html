De Draconibus.

On Dragons. ¶

Hebraice תנין thannin duo significat, nempe draconem et cetum; ac si cetus sit draco marinus. Quin aliquando תנין sumitur pro serpente quovis, plane ut נחש. Ut cum virga Mosis mutari legitur לתנין in thannin, Exod. VII, 9. id est, לנחש, in serpentem, ut explicat ipse Moses Exod. IV, 3. et VII, 15. Ita Nasrius, scriptor Arabs in Oxoniensi bibliotheca, cum refert in Ὀνειροκριτικοῖς (lib. 3. sect. 21. cap. 30.) de muliere quadam, quae somniavit se peperisse الحية, col. 429 serpentem, et peperit magnum et celebrem oratorem: quia تنين thannin est bilinguis. Patet, eum تنين thannin, et حية, id est, serpentem, pro eodem usurpasse. Tamen non solum Hebraeis, Syris, sed et Arabibus, proprie תנין thannin est draco. Atque, ut alia taceam, patet id ex paraphrastis, qui pro dracone תנין constanter habent in libro Revelationum Iohannis. Hinc تنين thannin in Alcamus definitur, حية عظيمة, magnus serpens (1). Et III, 223 apud Arabes التنين, draco vel est السماوي, coelestis, vel الارضي, terrestris, vel البحري, marinus. Et coelestis, inquiunt, qui Persice هستبر obscurior quaedam albedo est, quae sex occupat signa, et cum Planetis locum mutat. Draco marinus, Graece δράκων θαλάσσιος, (Gallice vive) est araneus Plinii, de quo Avicenna lib. 2. et 4. Canonis. Terrestrem idem describit his fere verbis lib. 4. Fen. 6. tractatu 3. cap. 53. Minimus in specie draconum, ut quidam referunt, est quinque cubitorum; maximus, triginta et supra. Oculi magni: in mandibula inferiore quid eminens instar barbae. Dentes plurimi. Ajunt reperiri multos in Nubia et India, sed Indos esse majores. Ionici in Asia quatuor cubitos non excedunt. Indis, praeterquam quod maximi sunt, et de caetero qua specie diximus, facies sunt flavae et nigrae, ora amplissima, supercilia oculos obtegentia, colla squamis obsita. In unaquaque maxilla tres dentes (id est, dentium ordines).

In Hebrew תנין thannin means two things, namely dragon and sea monster, as if the sea monster were a sea dragon. Indeed תנין is sometimes taken for any serpent whatever, just like נחש. So when the rod of Moses is said to be changed לתנין, into a thannin, Exod 7:9, that is, לנחש, into a serpent, as Moses himself explains, Exod 4:3 and VII, 15. Thus Nasrius, an Arab writer in the Oxford library, when in his Ὀνειροκριτικοῖς [Oneirokritikois] (book 3, sect. 21, ch. 30) he tells of a certain woman who dreamed that she had given birth to الحية [al-ḥayya], col. 429 a serpent, and she gave birth to a great and celebrated orator: because the تنين [tinnīn] thannin is double-tongued. It is plain that he used تنين [tinnīn] thannin and حية [ḥayya], that is, serpent, for the same thing. Yet not only for the Hebrews and Syrians but also for the Arabs, תנין thannin is properly a dragon. And, to say nothing of other evidence, this is plain from the paraphrasts, who consistently have תנין for dragon in the book of the Revelation of John. Hence تنين [tinnīn] thannin is defined in the Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] as حية عظيمة [ḥayya ʿaẓīma], a great serpent R1. And III, 223 among the Arabs التنين [al-tinnīn], the dragon, is either السماوي [al-samāwī], celestial, or الارضي [al-arḍī], terrestrial, or البحري [al-baḥrī], marine. The celestial one, they say, which in Persian is هستبر [hstbr], is a certain fainter whiteness which occupies six signs and changes place with the planets. The sea dragon, in Greek δράκων θαλάσσιος [drakōn thalassios] (in French vive), is the araneus of Pliny, on which see Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], books 2 and 4 of the Canon. The terrestrial one the same author describes in roughly these words, book 4, Fen 6, treatise 3, ch. 53: The smallest in the species of dragons, as some report, is five cubits; the largest, thirty and more. The eyes are large; on the lower jaw there is a projection like a beard. The teeth are very many. They say that many are found in Nubia and India, but that the Indian ones are larger. The Ionian ones in Asia do not exceed four cubits. The Indian ones, besides being the largest and otherwise of the form we have described, have yellow and black faces, very wide mouths, brows covering the eyes, necks covered with scales. In each jaw there are three teeth (that is, rows of teeth). ¶

Primo se offert Albedisimon Persin. Ita Latini interpretes. Sed textus Arabicus habet اغاذنيمون والسير agadanimon et alsir, vel assir, qui ab Avicenna censentur in genere draconum. Fallor, aut prius nomen factum ex Graeco ἀγαθοδαίμων, quod draconis fuit cognomen. Philo Bybliensis apud Eusebium De praeparatione Evangelii lib. 1. cap. 7. postquam Phoenices et Aegyptios, auctore Taauto, docuit, draconum et serpentum naturae divini aliquid tribuisse: Φοίνικες δὲ, inquit, αὐτὸ (τὸ ζῶον) ἀγαθὸν δαίμονα καλοῦσιν· ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ Αἰγύπτιοι Κνὴφ ἐπονομάζουσι· Hoc autem animal Phoenices agathodaemonem appellant, et Aegyptii similiter Kneph vocant. Lampridius de Heliogabalo, Aegyptios dracunculos Romae habuit, quos illi agathodaemonas vocant. Servius in haec Poetae de vipera,

It is worth explaining here in passing the barbarous names of dragons cited in what follows, which are everywhere very corrupt, but far more so in the Latin version than in the source, that is, in the Arabic text. First comes Albedisimon Persin. So the Latin translators. But the Arabic text has اغاذنيمون والسير [aghādhnīmūn wa-al-sīr] agadanimon and alsir, or assir, which Avicenna counts among the kinds of dragons. Unless I am mistaken, the first name is made from the Greek ἀγαθοδαίμων [agathodaimōn], which was a by-name of the dragon. Philo of Byblos in Eusebius, On the Preparation for the Gospel, book 1, ch. 7, after showing that the Phoenicians and Egyptians, on the authority of Taautus, attributed something divine to the nature of dragons and serpents, says: Φοίνικες δὲ αὐτὸ (τὸ ζῶον) ἀγαθὸν δαίμονα καλοῦσιν· ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ Αἰγύπτιοι Κνὴφ ἐπονομάζουσι· [Phoinikes de auto (to zōon) agathon daimona kalousin; homoiōs de kai Aigyptioi Knēph eponomazousi;] This animal the Phoenicians call agathodaemon, and the Egyptians likewise call it Kneph. Lampridius on Heliogabalus: he had Egyptian little dragons at Rome, which they call agathodaemons. Servius on these words of the Poet about the viper, ¶

— — — coelumque exterrita fugit,

— — — and terrified it shuns the sky, ¶

lib. 3. Georgicorum: id est, tectis gaudet, ut sunt ἀγαθοὶ δαίμονες, quos latine Genios vocant. Quo Lucanus alludens de draconibus dixit lib. 9.

Georgics book 3: that is, it delights in houses, as do the ἀγαθοὶ δαίμονες [agathoi daimones], whom in Latin they call Genii. Alluding to this, Lucan said of dragons, book 9: ¶

Vos quoque, qui cunctis innoxia numina terris.

You too, harmless deities to all lands. ¶

Et Sir, et Isidori Sirena, (imo Siren) Sirenae, inquit, sunt in Arabia serpentes cum alis. Et Eucherius, Sirenae in Esaia daemones, aut dracones magni, cristati pariter et volantes. Et multo ante Hieronymus in Esa. XIII, 22. Sirenae autem תנין vocantur; quas nos aut daemones, aut monstra quaedam, vel certe dracones magnos interpretati sumus, qui cristati sunt, et volantes. Melius Sirenes dixissent, quas poetae multo aliter describunt. Sed Patribus Siren est draconis species; col. 430 quia in Graeca versione aliquoties occurrit pro תנין: ut Iob. XXX, 29. Esa. XXXIV, 13. et XLIII, 20. Inde igitur factum Arabicum سير sir pro dracone.

And there are the Sir and Isidore's Sirena (or rather Siren): Sirens, he says, are winged serpents in Arabia. And Eucherius: Sirens in Isaiah are demons, or great dragons, crested and also flying. And much earlier Jerome on Esa. XIII, 22.: But the Sirens are called תנין; these we have rendered either demons, or certain monsters, or at any rate great dragons, which are crested and flying. They would better have said Sirenes, whom the poets describe very differently. But for the Fathers the Siren is a species of dragon, col. 430 because in the Greek version it occurs several times for תנין: as in Iob. XXX, 29. Esa. XXXIV, 13. and XLIII, 20. From this, then, the Arabic سير [sīr] sir for dragon was formed. ¶

There follows a chapter whose title in Latin is De haren carmen, but in Arabic في حيوانان بحريان [fī ḥayawānān baḥriyyān], On sea serpents. And what is meant is σμύραινα [smyraina], muraenae [morays], which the same author calls سوريا [sūriyā]. But the Latin translator here has semurion, and thinks that a doctor was so named, although σμύραινα [smyraina] is a fish. That my conjecture is correct will be plain to those who compare Aëtius with Avicenna. For there Avicenna reproduces the very words of Aëtius about the moray fish. ¶

III, 225 The title of the following chapter is De Alhatraf et Hauden, which again they hold to be among the kinds of dragons. But in the Arabic there is طروغورون [ṭrūghūrūn] truguron, for which read طروغون [ṭrūghūn], τρύγων [trygōn], that is, the sea stingray; that this is the subject is apparent from a comparison of the same Aëtius with Avicenna. So much for the benefit of the lovers of Arabic. Now from the digression back to the road. ¶

Magna quaedam sunt animantia dracones: majora non sunt super terram. Sed nemo facile dixerit, quousque crescant, cum hic veteres multa fabulentur, neque major sit uspiam mentiendi licentia, quod gradatim jam ostendemus. Dragons differ from serpents chiefly in bulk. Accordingly, as we said, the dragon is defined in Alcamus as حية عظيمة [ḥayya ʿaẓīma], a great serpent. And well known is the proverb of the Greeks, Ὄφις, εἰ μὴ φάγῃ ὄφιν, δράκων οὐ γενήσεται [Ophis, ei mē phagē ophin, drakōn ou genēsetai]· A serpent, unless it has eaten a serpent, will not become a dragon. Nor without reason does Augustine say on Psalm. CXLVIII.: Dragons are certain great animals; there are none greater upon the earth. But no one could easily say how large they grow, since here the ancients tell many fables, and nowhere is there greater licence for lying, as we shall now show step by step. Pliny, book 8, ch. 13, reports from Juba that among the Asachaean Ethiopians crested dragons of twenty cubits are bred. Aëtius and Avicenna write that they grow from five cubits up to thirty. This is confirmed from elsewhere too. For in Aelian, History book 2, chapter 21, dragons in Phrygia grow to ten orgyiae; and that each of these is three cubits, Hesychius and Eustathius teach. And Pausanias in the Corinthiaca relates that τοὺς ὄφεις τοὺς Μεγαλαύνας [tous opheis tous Megalaunas], the Megalaunian serpents around Epidaurus grow beyond a size of thirty cubits; such as are said to be bred in India and in Africa. So Philostratus, book 3, ch. 2, where he treats of the species of dragons in India: Οἱ μὲν δὴ ἕλειοι νωθροί τέ εἰσι, καὶ τριακοντάπηχυ μῆκος ἔχουσι [Hoi men dē heleioi nōthroi te eisi, kai triakontapēchy mēkos echousi]· The marsh ones are sluggish, and thirty cubits long. The same was the measure of the Egyptian dragon that was brought alive to Alexandria to Ptolemy Philadelphus. For Tzetzes is mistaken, who makes it τριακονταπεντάπηχυν [triakontapentapēchyn], of thirty-five cubits, Chiliad 3, History 113, since the author he follows, Diodorus, book 3, asserts in express words that it was only τριάκοντα πηχῶν [triakonta pēchōn], of thirty cubits. There are those who think that the serpent at its creation was four-footed, or at least two-footed like dragons, and that its now creeping on its belly belongs to the punishment. But it does not seem likely to me that God changed anything in the nature of the serpent on account of man's sin. Only what belonged to its nature was turned into a punishment, as nakedness in man and childbirth in woman. Nor indeed is it sufficiently certain that any dragon is two-footed, or, as Pliny says b), that serpents have been seen with goose's feet. Augustine, book 3 De Gen. ad lit., expressly asserts that dragons are without feet. And the Philosopher, in the book On the Progression of Animals, proves at length that neither two nor four feet could suffice a serpent because of the enormous length of its body, and that it could not have more, because τῶν ἐναίμων μηδὲν οἷον τ᾽ εἶναι πλείοσι σημείοις κινεῖσθαι, ἢ τέτταρσιν [tōn enaimōn mēden hoion t᾽ einai pleiosi sēmeiois kineisthai, ē tettarsin]. Eustathius in Hexaëmero, Ἐὰν δὲ γυμνὸν θεάσηται ἄνθρωπον οὐ πρόσ-εισιν· εἰ δὲ ἐνδεδυμένον, ἐφάλλεται· Hominem nudum si aspexerit, non adit; sed, si vestitum viderit, aggreditur. Hieronymus, vel quisquis auctor est Epistolae ad Praesidium sub Hieronymi nomine; Coluber nudum hominem timet, vestitum prosequitur. Isidorus lib. 12. cap. 4. Et id se non raro expertum scribit Olaus. Favorabile, inquit, verbum Isidorus de natura serpentum profert, quod hominem nudum et dormientem III, 248 nunquam laedant: idque mihi, in pueritia et adolescentia, serpentibus sine laesione saepissime congredienti, contigit. Tertium serpentis prudentiae argumentum idem profert, quod ab homine appetitus solum caput servat illaesum, et reliquum corpus objicit. De quo cum jam plura dixerim, hoc solum addam ex Caesario, Dial. 4. Quaest. 190. Κατευναζόμενος δὲ, καὶ καθεύδων (ὁ ὄφις) τρο-χίζων ἑαυτὸν, μέσην ὅλου τοῦ σώματος κεφαλὴν ἀσφαλίζεται, οἱονεὶ τειχίῳ τῷ ἐφεπομένῳ ὁλκῷ περιοχυρῶν αὐτήν· Cum quieti se tradit ut dormiat serpens, sese contorquens, medium intra totum corpus caput communit, consequente tractu, ut muro, illud circumvallans. Quartum, quod, cum ad fontem venit, ut bibat, οὐ φέρει τὸν ἰὸν, ἀλλά γε ἀφεὶς αὐτὸν ἐν τῇ νεοττίᾳ, οὕτω καθαρὸς πορεύεται, virus non defert, sed illud in nido dimittens, ita purus accedit. Et Eustathius in Hexaëmero, Ὅταν δὲ διψήσῃ τὸν ἰὸν πρότερον ἀποτίθεται, καὶ οὕτως ἐκπίνει τὸ ὕδωρ ἐκ τῆς πηγῆς· Cum sitit, virus ante deponit, atque ita aquam ex fonte bibit: nempe ne eum venenum, aqua concretum, occidat, ut addit auctor Epistolae ad Praesidium. Non absimile est, quod multi scribunt, viperam, cum muraena coituram, virus suum prius evomere, tanquam αἰδουμένην τὸν γάμον, nuptias reveritam, ut ait Basilius Hom. 7. in Hexaëm. Sed ut fabulosum id rejicit Andreas b) in libro Περὶ τῶν ψευδῶς πεπιστευμένων. Quamvis, praeter Basilium c) et Ambrosium, auctores habeat Nicandrum d), Aelianum e), Oppianum f), Licinium Macrum g), Achillem Tatium h), Philen in Iambis i), et Isidorum k), aliosque non parvo numero.

The Physiologus of Epiphanius offers four proofs of the serpent's prudence. First, when it senses old age coming on, it seeks a rock and ῥαγαδαστεύει [ragadasteuei], προκύψας δὲ διὰ τῆς ὀπῆς ὠθεῖται περάσαι, καὶ ἐκβάλλει τὸ γῆρας [prokypsas de dia tēs opēs ōtheitai perasai, kai ekballei to gēras], it slips into a crevice, and, bending forward, strives to pass through the hole, and puts off its old age. And this all assert with one voice. Second, although it flees from a clothed man, when it sees the same man naked, καταθαῤῥεῖ αὐτοῦ [katatharrei autou], it attacks him confidently. This too might seem consistent with reason; yet others expressly teach the opposite. Eustathius in the Hexaemeron: Ἐὰν δὲ γυμνὸν θεάσηται ἄνθρωπον οὐ πρόσεισιν· εἰ δὲ ἐνδεδυμένον, ἐφάλλεται [Ean de gymnon theasētai anthrōpon ou proseisin; ei de endedymenon, ephalletai]; If it has seen a naked man, it does not approach; but if it has seen a clothed one, it attacks. Jerome, or whoever is the author of the Letter to Praesidius under Jerome's name: The snake fears a naked man, and pursues a clothed one. Isidore, book 12, ch. 4: It is said, moreover, that the serpent does not dare to touch a naked man. Damir: ومن عجيب امره انها تهرب من الرجل العريان [wa-min ʿajīb amrihi annahā tahrub min al-rajul al-ʿuryān] Among the marvels of its nature is that it flees from a naked man. And Olaus writes that he has experienced this not rarely. Isidore, he says, offers a favourable word about the nature of serpents, that they never harm a naked and sleeping man III, 248: and this happened to me in boyhood and youth, when I very often encountered serpents without harm. The same author offers a third proof of the serpent's prudence: that when attacked by a man it keeps only its head unharmed and exposes the rest of its body. Since I have already said much about this, I shall add only this from Caesarius, Dial. 4, Quaest. 190: Κατευναζόμενος δὲ, καὶ καθεύδων (ὁ ὄφις) τροχίζων ἑαυτὸν, μέσην ὅλου τοῦ σώματος κεφαλὴν ἀσφαλίζεται, οἱονεὶ τειχίῳ τῷ ἐφεπομένῳ ὁλκῷ περιοχυρῶν αὐτήν [Kateunazomenos de, kai katheudōn (ho ophis) trochizōn heauton, mesēn holou tou sōmatos kephalēn asphalizetai, hoionei teichiō tō epheponemō holkō periochyrōn autēn]; When the serpent gives itself to rest in order to sleep, coiling itself up, it secures its head in the middle of its whole body, encircling it with the following length of its body as with a wall. Fourth, when it comes to a spring to drink, οὐ φέρει τὸν ἰὸν, ἀλλά γε ἀφεὶς αὐτὸν ἐν τῇ νεοττίᾳ, οὕτω καθαρὸς πορεύεται [ou pherei ton ion, alla ge apheis auton en tē neottia, houtō katharos poreuetai], it does not carry its poison along, but leaving it in its nest, it approaches thus pure. The same author sets this out as follows in Haer. 37: Οὐ συμπαραλαμβάνει τὸν ἰὸν μεθ᾽ ἑαυτοῦ, ἀλλὰ ἐν τῷ φωλεῷ καταλιμπάνει, καὶ οὕτως ἐλθὼν λαμβάνει τὸ πόμα τῶν ὑδάτων [Ou symparalambanei ton ion meth' heautou, alla en tō phōleō katalimpanei, kai houtōs elthōn lambanei to poma tōn hydatōn]; It does not bring its poison out with it, but leaves it in its lair, and approaching thus takes its drink of the waters. And Eustathius in the Hexaemeron: Ὅταν δὲ διψήσῃ τὸν ἰὸν πρότερον ἀποτίθεται, καὶ οὕτως ἐκπίνει τὸ ὕδωρ ἐκ τῆς πηγῆς [Hotan de dipsēsē ton ion proteron apotithetai, kai houtōs ekpinei to hydōr ek tēs pēgēs]; When it is thirsty, it first lays down its poison, and so drinks water from the spring: namely, lest the poison, congealed by the water, should kill it, as the author of the Letter to Praesidius adds. Not unlike this is what many write, that the viper, when about to mate with the moray, first vomits out its poison, as if αἰδουμένην τὸν γάμον [aidoumenēn ton gamon], revering the marriage, as Basil says, Hom. 7 on the Hexaem. But Andreas b) rejects this as fabulous in his book Περὶ τῶν ψευδῶς πεπιστευμένων [Peri tōn pseudōs pepisteumenōn]. Although, besides Basil c) and Ambrose, it has as authorities Nicander d), Aelian e), Oppian f), Licinius Macer g), Achilles Tatius h), Philes in the Iambics i), and Isidore k), and others in no small number. ¶

His ex Plinio addi queat, serpentem, hyberna latebra visu obscurato, oculos marathro inungere ac refovere. Si vero squamae obtorpuerunt, spinis juniperi scabere, et draconem vernam nauseam lactucae succo restinguere. Quae naturali solertia fieri verisimilius, quam qui quadam θεηλάτῳ, prout Arabes sibi fingunt. In his الزمخشري Azzammachsari, scriptor, prae caeteris fabulosus, cujus verba sunt: حكي ان الافعي اذا اتت عليها الف سنة عميت وقد الهمها الله ان تمسح عينيها بورق الرازيانج الرطب فير اليها بصرها وربما كان III, 249 في برية بينها وبين الريف مسيرة ثلثة ايام فتطوي تلك المسافة علي طولها وعلي عماها حتي تهاجم في بعض البساتين علي شجر الرازيانج لاتحظيه فتحك به عينها فترجع باصرة باذن الله تعالي Vipe- ram ajunt, cum vixit mille annis, excaecari, et inspirare illi Deum, ut ungat oculos suos recentis foeniculi folio, et recuperare visum. Quin nonnunquam est in deserto; et inter eam et locum cultum trium dierum iter intercedit. Quod spatium, quantum quantum est, illa emetitur, donec in aliquem hortum ingressa in foeniculi plantam incidat, a qua haudquaquam aberrat, et ea confricat oculos, et cum bono Deo redit illi visus. Non dissimilem fabulam narrat Abugiani Thaugidaeus الشمسية, id est, Solarem dici serpentis genus, qui, cum oculi debilitari incipiunt, septem diebus continuis eos per horae spatium ad solem obvertit, at-que ea ratione visum illius acui et intendi.

To these may be added from Pliny that the serpent, when its sight has been dimmed in its winter hiding place, anoints and refreshes its eyes with fennel; and if its scales have grown stiff, it scrapes itself on the thorns of the juniper; and that the dragon relieves its spring nausea with the juice of lettuce. It is more probable that these things happen by natural skill than by some θεηλάτῳ [theēlatō] impulse, as the Arabs imagine. Among them is الزمخشري [al-Zamakhsharī] Azzammachsari, a writer fabulous above others, whose words are: حكي ان الافعي اذا اتت عليها الف سنة عميت وقد الهمها الله ان تمسح عينيها بورق الرازيانج الرطب فير اليها بصرها وربما كان III, 249 في برية بينها وبين الريف مسيرة ثلثة ايام فتطوي تلك المسافة علي طولها وعلي عماها حتي تهاجم في بعض البساتين علي شجر الرازيانج لاتحظيه فتحك به عينها فترجع باصرة باذن الله تعالي [ḥukiya anna al-afʿā idhā atat ʿalayhā alf sana ʿamiyat wa-qad alhamahā Allāh an tamsaḥ ʿaynayhā bi-waraq al-rāziyānaj al-raṭb fa-yr ilayhā baṣaruhā wa-rubbamā kāna fī barriyya baynahā wa-bayna al-rīf masīrat thalathat ayyām fa-taṭwī tilka al-masāfa ʿalā ṭūlihā wa-ʿalā ʿamāhā ḥattā tuhājim fī baʿḍ al-basātīn ʿalā shajar al-rāziyānaj lā tukhṭīhi fa-taḥukk bihi ʿaynahā fa-tarjiʿ bāṣira bi-idhn Allāh taʿālā] They say that the viper, when it has lived a thousand years, is blinded, and that God inspires it to anoint its eyes with a leaf of fresh fennel, and it recovers its sight. Indeed sometimes it is in the desert, and between it and cultivated land there lies a journey of three days. This distance, however great it is, it traverses, until, having entered some garden, it comes upon a fennel plant, from which it by no means strays, and it rubs its eyes on it, and with God's favour its sight returns to it. Abugiani Thaugidaeus tells a not dissimilar fable: that a kind of serpent is called الشمسية [al-shamsiyya], that is, the Solar, which, when its eyes begin to weaken, turns them toward the sun for the space of an hour on seven successive days, and that by this means its sight is sharpened and strengthened. ¶

Etiam huc referunt, quod aspidem dicit Psaltes aures obturare ad cantum, Psal. LVIII, 7. Quasi mira quadam sagacitate, cum primum aspis audit incantatorem, aurium unam terrae applicet, alteram cauda obturet, ne incantationi via pateat. Sed suo loco Arabes nos docebunt, haec multo aliter accipi.

They also refer to this the Psalmist's statement that the asp stops its ears against the chant, Ps 58:7: as though, with a kind of wonderful sagacity, as soon as the asp hears the charmer, it presses one ear to the ground and stops the other with its tail, so that no way lies open to the incantation. But in their place the Arabs will teach us that these words are to be understood quite otherwise. ¶

Consalus Pontius de Leon in Notis ad Epiphanii Physiologum serpentis solertiam sitam esse vult in agilitate corporis, quam in dracone potissimum commendat antiquissimus ille Taautus, cum apud Eusebium lib. 1. Praeparat. draconem tradit esse πνευματικώτατον τὸ ζῶον πάντων τῶν ἑρπετῶν καὶ πυρῶδες. παρ᾽ ᾧ καὶ τάχος ἀνυπέρβλητον διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος παρίστησι, χωρὶς ποδῶν τε, καὶ χειρῶν, ἢ ἄλλου τινὸς τῶν ἔξωθεν, ἐξ ὧν τὰ λοιπὰ ζῶα τὰς κινήσεις ποιεῖται, καὶ ποικίλων σχημάτων τύπους ἀποτελεῖ, καὶ κατὰ τὴν πορείαν ἑλικοειδεῖς ἔχει τὰς ὁρμὰς, ἐφ᾽ ὃ βούλεται τάχος· Omnium reptilium maxime spiritualis, et igneae naturae, unde est, quod per solos spiritus eximiam celerita- tem consequitur, absque manuum, aut pedum, aut exterioris ullius organi adju- mento, per quae reliqua moventur animalia; et in varias figuras se conformat, adeoque inter gradiendum flexuoso fertur impetu quamlibet citissime. A qua sententia parum recedit Laurentius Ramirez Pentecontarchi sui capite primo, ubi serpentem contendit non alia de causa dici φρόνιμον, Gen. III. et Matth. X. quam quia nullum est animal, quod se insinuet magis. Unde ortum vult habere, quod angues in Mercurii caduceo impliciti finguntur, quia summi oratoris, qualis credebatur Mercurius, munus vel praecipuum est insinuatio. Addit, Evam lusitare solitam cum serpente, eoque sinus ornare, et collum, ac brachia: ideoque monilia hodieque fieri ad serpentum similitudinem, quae Graeci III, 250 ὄφεις appellant. Quod, praeter Nicostratum et Athenaeum, testatur etiam Clemens lib. 2. Paedagogi. Sed hae illius conjecturae longius petitae sunt, quam ut me in assensum trahant. Maxime cum ערמה aut φρόνησιν, vel pro corporis agilitate, vel pro insinuatione sumi nusquam legerim: et, ut demus, alibi sic usurpari, tamen in Christi verbis, Este φρόνιμοι, prudentes sicut serpentes, et simplices sicut columbae, ratio oppositionis docet, vocem φρόνιμοι intelligendam esse, ut sonat. Neque enim corporis agilitas, aut insinuatio, sed prudentia simplicitati opponitur. Unde Martialis lib. 10. Epigr. 47.

Consalus Pontius de Leon, in his Notes on the Physiologus of Epiphanius, holds that the serpent's shrewdness consists in the agility of its body. This is what that most ancient Taautus especially praises in the dragon, when, in Eusebius, book 1 of the Praeparatio, he relates that the dragon is πνευματικώτατον τὸ ζῶον πάντων τῶν ἑρπετῶν καὶ πυρῶδες. παρ᾽ ᾧ καὶ τάχος ἀνυπέρβλητον διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος παρίστησι, χωρὶς ποδῶν τε, καὶ χειρῶν, ἢ ἄλλου τινὸς τῶν ἔξωθεν, ἐξ ὧν τὰ λοιπὰ ζῶα τὰς κινήσεις ποιεῖται, καὶ ποικίλων σχημάτων τύπους ἀποτελεῖ, καὶ κατὰ τὴν πορείαν ἑλικοειδεῖς ἔχει τὰς ὁρμὰς, ἐφ᾽ ὃ βούλεται τάχος [pneumatikōtaton to zōon pantōn tōn herpetōn kai pyrōdes. par᾽ hō kai tachos anyperblēton dia tou pneumatos paristēsi, chōris podōn te, kai cheirōn, ē allou tinos tōn exōthen, ex hōn ta loipa zōa tas kinēseis poieitai, kai poikilōn schēmatōn typous apotelei, kai kata tēn poreian helikoeideis echei tas hormas, eph᾽ ho bouletai tachos]: of all reptiles the most spiritual and of a fiery nature; hence it attains extraordinary speed by its breath alone, without the aid of hands or feet or any external organ by which the other animals move; and it shapes itself into various figures, and so, as it goes, it is carried with a winding impetus as swiftly as it pleases. Not far from this opinion is Laurentius Ramirez, in the first chapter of his Pentecontarchus, where he contends that the serpent is called φρόνιμον [phronimon], Gen. III. and Matth. X., for no other reason than that no animal insinuates itself more. From this, he holds, arose the fact that snakes are represented entwined on the caduceus of Mercury, because insinuation is the chief, or nearly the chief, task of the supreme orator, such as Mercury was believed to be. He adds that Eve used to play with the serpent and adorn her bosom, neck and arms with it; and that for this reason necklaces are made even today in the likeness of serpents, which the Greeks III, 250 call ὄφεις [opheis]. This, besides Nicostratus and Athenaeus, Clement also attests in book 2 of the Paedagogus. But these conjectures of his are too far-fetched to draw me into assent. Especially since I have nowhere read ערמה or φρόνησιν [phronēsin] taken either for agility of body or for insinuation; and, granting that they are so used elsewhere, yet in Christ's words, Be φρόνιμοι [phronimoi], prudent as serpents, and simple as doves, the logic of the antithesis shows that the word φρόνιμοι [phronimoi] must be understood as it sounds. For it is not agility of body or insinuation, but prudence, that is opposed to simplicity. Hence Martial, book 10, Epigr. 47: ¶

— — Prudens simplicitas, pares amici.

— — Prudent simplicity, friends who are equals. ¶

Et Paulus Rom. XVI, 9. Volo vos sapientes, id est, prudentes, esse in bono, et simplices in malo. Itaque serpens vere ערום, id est, φρόνιμος, prudens, vel πανοῦργος, callidus est, quantum in brutum cadere possunt calliditas, aut prudentia.

And Paul, Rom 16:9: I would have you wise, that is, prudent, in what is good, and simple in what is evil. Therefore the serpent is truly ערום, that is, φρόνιμος [phronimos], prudent, or πανοῦργος [panourgos], cunning, so far as cunning or prudence can belong to a brute. ¶

Quod ipsum in causa fuit, cur hoc organo diabolus uteretur ad seducendum hominem. Quasi fuerit vero similius, a tam vafro animali tam vafra consilia proficisci, ne fraudem suboleret Eva. Id vero cum Satanae successisset ex voto, Deus non illi solum, sed et serpenti maledixit, tanquam instrumento seductionis. Ita judex falsarium una cum calamo damnat, et sicarium cum suo telo. Et si quis cum jumento rem habuerit, divina lex jumentum etiam morte mulctari jubet, Lev. XX, 15. Et bovem cornupetam Deus una cum domino occidi voluit, si quem feriisset per domini negligentiam, Exod. XXI, 28. 29.

This very thing was the reason why the devil used this instrument to seduce man: as though it were more plausible that such crafty counsels should proceed from so crafty an animal, so that Eve might not scent the fraud. But when this had succeeded for Satan as he wished, God cursed not him alone but also the serpent, as the instrument of the seduction. So a judge condemns the forger together with his pen, and the assassin with his weapon. And if anyone has lain with a beast, the divine law orders the beast also to be punished with death, Lev 20:15. And God willed a goring ox to be killed together with its owner if it had struck someone through the owner's negligence, Exod 21:28–29. ¶

In parte maledictionis est mutuum inter serpentem et hominem odium. Ponam inimicitias inter te et mulierem, Gen. III, 15. Cujus odii ratione scribebat Hyperides 1) in Oratione contra Demadem, τοὺς ὄφεις μισητοὺς εἶναι πάντας, odio dignos esse serpentes omnes; et Theocritus, Idyll. 15.

Part of the curse is the mutual hatred between the serpent and man: I will put enmity between you and the woman, Gen 3:15. On account of this hatred Hyperides 1) wrote in his Oration against Demades, τοὺς ὄφεις μισητοὺς εἶναι πάντας [tous opheis misētous einai pantas], that all serpents are worthy of hatred; and Theocritus, Idyll 15: ¶

Ἐκ παιδὸς τὸν ψυχρὸν ὄφιν τὰ μάλιστα δέδοικα, A teneris fuit horrori mihi frigidus anguis.

Ἐκ παιδὸς τὸν ψυχρὸν ὄφιν τὰ μάλιστα δέδοικα [Ek paidos ton psychron ophin ta malista dedoika], From childhood the cold snake has been my horror. ¶

Plautus etiam in Mercatore, Act. 4. Nempe ruri est uxor tua, quam dudum dixeras te odisse aeque atque angues. Hinc Artemidorus, Onirocrit. lib. 2. Ὄφις ἔχθραν ἐπάγει· ὅπως δ᾽ ἂν διαθῇ τινα, οὕτως καὶ ὁ ἐχθρὸς τὸν ἰδόντα διαθή-σει. Serpens, in somnis visus, inimicitias conciliat: quomodocunque igitur aliquem (in somnis) divexaverit, eodem modo, quisquis viderit, ab hoste investabitur.

Plautus too, in the Mercator, Act 4: Your wife, of course, is in the country, whom you said a while ago you hated as much as snakes. Hence Artemidorus, Onirocritica book 2: Ὄφις ἔχθραν ἐπάγει· ὅπως δ᾽ ἂν διαθῇ τινα, οὕτως καὶ ὁ ἐχθρὸς τὸν ἰδόντα διαθήσει [Ophis echthran epagei; hopōs d᾽ an diathē tina, houtōs kai ho echthros ton idonta diathēsei]. A serpent, seen in dreams, brings about enmities: in whatever way, then, it has harassed someone (in dreams), in the same way whoever has seen it will be assailed by an enemy. ¶
