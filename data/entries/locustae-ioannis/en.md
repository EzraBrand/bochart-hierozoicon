# Part 2, Book 4, chapter 7: That John the Baptist ate real locusts (vol. 3, pp. 326–334)

### ‎[III, 326] CHAPTER 7.

### ‎<i>That John the Baptist had real locusts for food.</i>

‎It has been shown so far that there is scarcely any animal more destructive to the human race than locusts. [col. 488] Yet these too have their use, which others explain at length. It will be enough for us to show that locusts served as food for not a few peoples long ago, and still serve so today, with regard to the much-discussed (πολυθρύλλητα [polythryllēta]) words about <i>John the Baptist</i> in <i>Matth. III,</i> 4: Ἡ δὲ τροφὴ αὐτοῦ ἦν ἀκρίδες, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον [Hē de trophē autou ēn akrides, kai meli agrion]. And <i>Mark 1:6</i>: Ἐσθίων ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἄγριον [Esthiōn akridas, kai meli agrion]. Although these words teach clearly and openly that locusts and wild honey were his food, nevertheless, as though it were intolerable to believe that the holy man sustained his life on locusts, many ancients and moderns have twisted themselves into every shape in order to bend these words to another sense. The <i>Ebionites</i> lead the column; in their Gospel the following was read about <i>John</i>: Τὸ βρῶμα αὐτοῦ μέλι ἄγριον, οὗ ἡ γεῦσις ἦν τοῦ Μάννα, ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν ἐλαίῳ [To brōma autou meli agrion, hou hē geusis ēn tou Manna, hōs enkris en elaiō]· <i>His food was wild honey, with the taste of Manna, like a cake fried in oil.</i> On this passage <i>Epiphanius</i> asserts that they set themselves this aim, <i>to change the word of truth into a lie, and</i> ἀντὶ ἀκρίδων ποιήσωσιν ἐγκρίδας ἐν μέλιτι [anti akridōn poiēsōsin enkridas en meliti], <i>to make out of locusts cakes fried in honey.</i> The occasion for this fabrication was taken from the fact that ἀκρίδες [akrides] and ἐγκρίδες [enkrides] are very similar words. And just as in the <i>Gospel</i> ἀκρίδες καὶ μέλι [akrides kai meli] are joined, so it is said of the Manna in <i>Exod 16:31</i>: <i>And its taste was</i> ὡς ἐγκρὶς ἐν μέλιτι [hōs enkris en meliti], <i>like a cake fried in honey</i> (or <i>with honey</i>). If <i>John</i>'s food in the desert had tasted like that, it would not have been much proof of an austere life. Others think otherwise, namely that ἀκρίδες [akrides] in this passage are ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya], or τῶν φυτῶν ἀκρέμονας [tōn phytōn akremonas], <i>the tips of trees and plants</i>, on which <i>John</i> lived in that desert. Thus <i>Isidore of Pelusium</i>, in the fifth <i>Letter</i> of book 1, wants the monk, taught by <i>John</i>, to be content with ἀκρέμοσι βοτανῶν καὶ φυλλῶν πρὸς ὀλίγην τροφήν [akremosi botanōn kai phyllōn pros oligēn trophēn], <i>the tips of plants and leaves for slender nourishment.</i> And in <i>Letter</i> 132 he clings so tenaciously to this interpretation that he brings a charge against those who understand <i>locusts</i> by the name ἀκρίδες [akrides], in these words: Αἱ ἀκρίδες, αἷς Ἰωάννης ἐτρέφετο, οὐ ζῶά εἰσιν, ὥς τινες οἴονται ἀμαθῶς, κανθάροις ἀπεοικότα· μὴ γένοιτο. ἀλλ᾽ ἀκρέμονες βοτανῶν, ἢ φυτῶν [Hai akrides, hais Iōannēs etrepheto, ou zōa eisin, hōs tines oiontai amathōs, kantharois apeoikota; mē genoito. all᾽ akremones botanōn, ē phytōn]. <i>Those acrides on which</i> John <i>fed are not animals resembling beetles, as some ignorantly suppose; far from it: but the tips of plants or trees.</i> So also <i>Paulinus</i>, in his poem on <i>John the Baptist</i>:

‎<i>Wild honey furnished easy sustenance, and fruits, and herbs sprung from untilled rocks.</i>

‎And <i>Pantaleon the deacon</i>, in his sermon <i>On the Holy Lights</i>: Ὁ τοῖς τῶν μελισσῶν κατὰ τὴν ἔρημον πόνοις, καὶ τοῖς τῶν βοτανῶν συντρεφόμενος ἐκ βρέφους ἀκρίσμασι· [Ho tois tōn melissōn kata tēn erēmon ponois, kai tois tōn botanōn syntrephomenos ek brephous akrismasi] [III, 327] <i>He who from infancy was nourished on the labours of the bees in the desert and on the tips of herbs.</i> And <i>Theophylact</i>: Τινὲς δὲ (λέγουσι) τὰ ἀκρόδρυα, ἤτοι ὀπώρας ἀγρίας (εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας·) [Tines de (legousi) ta akrodrya, ētoi opōras agrias (einai tas akridas)] <i>Some say that the acridas are the tips of trees, or wild fruits.</i> And <i>Euthymius</i>: <i>But some have said that the locusts were shoots of herbs.</i> And <i>Nicephorus Callistus</i>, <i>History</i> book 1, chapter 14: <i>There he used for food the tenderer tops of trees</i>, ἄκροις δρυῶν [akrois dryōn]. And in the last century <i>Cajetan</i>: <i>I suspect that the name is equivocal, applying both to those animals, locusts, and to roots, or the tender tops of shrubs or twigs, or something of this kind that we eat raw.</i> Swept away by this torrent, <i>Baronius</i>, under the year 31, says: <i>What we should think of these things is not for us to decide; we leave the whole matter to the reader's judgement. For it is agreed</i> that the Greek <i>word</i> ἀκρίδας [akridas] <i>signifies both</i> the locust, <i>a kind of insect</i> [col. 489] <i>having weak wings for flying</i>, <i>and</i> the tops of trees. On the contrary, it is agreed that ἀκρίδας [akridas] and ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] differ greatly, and that these two words are nowhere confused by truly <i>Greek</i> writers. Yet those of the ancients who shrank from eating locusts did this all the more readily because they knew that ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] had served some people as food. Thus in the <i>Paedagogus</i> of <i>Clement</i>, book 2, ch. 1, the apostle <i>Matthew</i> σπερμάτων, καὶ ἀκροδρύων, καὶ λαχάνων, ἄνευ κρέων, μετελάμβανεν [spermatōn, kai akrodryōn, kai lachanōn, aneu kreōn, metelambanen], <i>ate seeds, and tops of trees, and vegetables, without meat.</i> But in the same place <i>John</i> is distinguished from <i>Matthew</i> precisely by the fact that ὑπερτείνας τὴν ἐγκράτειαν, ἀκρίδας, καὶ μέλι ἤσθιεν ἄγριον [hyperteinas tēn enkrateian, akridas, kai meli ēsthien agrion], <i>carrying abstinence further, he ate locusts and wild honey.</i> So you may know that for him ἀκρίδας [akridas] were one thing and ἀκρόδρυα [akrodrya] another. Thus in the age of <i>Epiphanius</i> some were so rigid in their fasts that they abstained even from ἀκροδρύων, καὶ ἑψημάτων [akrodryōn, kai hepsēmatōn], <i>from the tops of trees and from vegetable broths</i>, as though this were too much. So he says in the <i>Exposition of the Faith</i>, ch. 23. The third opinion is that of those for whom αἱ ἀκρίδες [hai akrides] are a kind of herb. Euthymius: <i>But some have said that the locusts were shoots of herbs, and others a herb called locust.</i> Theophylact: Τινὲς λέγουσι βοτάνας εἶναι τὰς ἀκρίδας, ἃς καὶ μέλαγρα καλοῦσι· [Tines legousi botanas einai tas akridas, has kai melagra kalousi] <i>Some say that the acridas are herbs, which they also call melagra</i>, as an allusion to μέλι ἄγριον [meli agrion]. So, on this opinion, the honey and the locusts will be the same thing: namely a kind of herb otherwise entirely unknown, which nevertheless alone sufficed <i>John</i> for food. Nothing more absurd could be imagined. Yet even this opinion had its followers, among them <i>Surianus</i>, a monk, from whom <i>Jacques de Vitry</i>, ch. 53 of the <i>History of Jerusalem</i>, professes to have learned that the ἀκρίδας [akridas] of <i>John</i> were not <i>locusts</i> but a certain <i>herb</i> that used to be served to monks in <i>Syria</i>. Perhaps also <i>Dionysius the Carthusian</i>, who writes that some understand by <i>locusts</i> <i>herbs of the desert</i>, <i>since it does not seem probable that</i> John, <i>who did not even eat bread, ate the flesh of animals or anything cooked.</i> And <i>Hermolaus</i>, on <i>Dioscorides</i>, Corollary 256, says that the herb meant is the one called in <i>Arabic</i> رجل الجراد [rijl al-jarād] <i>locust's foot</i>; that is, <i>blitum</i> [blite], as some hold, or, as [III, 328] others, <i>rapunculum</i> [rampion]. Let the fourth opinion be the one that holds the ἀκρίδας [akridas] of <i>John</i> to be <i>little birds</i>. <i>Christian Druthmar</i>, who wrote on <i>Matthew</i> in the ninth century after <i>Christ</i>: <i>About the locusts I have heard someone say that these locusts were small birds with red legs.</i> This would be tolerable if he had said only <i>winged creatures</i>, for among flying locusts some have red feet. But since he said a <i>little bird</i> <i>with red feet</i>, what else are we to understand than the ἐρυθρόποδα [erythropoda] of the <i>Greeks</i>? They take it to be a species of <i>finch</i>. That <i>Druthmar</i> understood a real bird is certainly clear, since he refutes the author of this opinion thus: <i>Why would</i> (John) <i>be praised for the rigour of his abstinence, if he ate the flying creatures that the</i> Jews <i>longed for?</i> The fifth: that the <i>locusts</i> were <i>sea fish</i>, which <i>John</i> obtained from the <i>Jordan</i>, as the same <i>Druthmar</i> reports, which is quite ridiculous. For these locusts are found not in rivers but in the sea itself. Not to mention that <i>sea locusts</i> [lobsters] are called by the <i>Greeks</i> not ἀκρίδας [akridas] but καράβους [karabous]. Finally, since sea locusts are counted a delicacy, here too <i>Druthmar</i> rightly objects that there would be no reason to praise <i>John</i> for abstinence if he ate marine delicacies. The same objection can be raised against those who change ἀκρίδας [akridas] into καρίδας [karidas], that is, <i>squillas</i> [shrimps], than which nothing in the whole crab family is more excellent. To this opinion, which is the sixth, I add a seventh, that of a very great and most pious man, who conjectures that the <i>Evangelists</i> here wrote ἀχράδες [achrades], that is, <i>wild pears</i>, and that by a slight change this word was turned into ἀκρίδες [akrides] <sup>R1</sup>.

‎But there is no need to change anything, since all the books agree on the received reading, and it contains nothing at which a learned reader could rightly [col. 490] take offence. For it is certain that the eating of locusts was very widely accepted in the regions that lie either to the east or to the south. These are <i>Jerome</i>'s words in <i>Against Jovinian</i> book 2, ch. 6: <i>Among the peoples of the East and of</i> Libya, <i>because clouds of locusts are found throughout the desert and the vastness of the wilderness, it is the custom to feed on locusts</i>. Thus, from the age of <i>Alexander</i>, <i>Greece</i> described among the peoples of <i>Ethiopia</i> the <i>Acridophagi</i>, so called from <i>eating locusts</i>. On these <i>Agatharchides</i>, book 5, ch. 27: Ὑπὸ δὲ τὴν ἐαριτὴν ἰσημερίαν, ὅτε Λίβυες (lege Λίβες) παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς καὶ Ζέφυροι πνέουσι, παμμεγεθῶν ἀκρίδων πλῆθος ἀμύθητον ἐκ τῆς ἀνιςορήτου μετὰ τῶν ἀνέμων παραγίνεται χώρας. τῇ μὲν δυνάμει τῆς πλήσεως τῶν ὀρνίθων μικρὸν παραλλάττον, [III, 329] μακρὸν δὲ τῷ σώματι. ἀπὸ τούτου δὲ τοῦ ζώου τρέφονται πάντα τὸν χρόνον, ἄλλως τε τούτοις καὶ τεταριχευμένοις χρώμενοι. [Hypo de tēn earitēn isēmerian, hote Libyes (lege Libes) par᾽ autois kai Zephyroi pneousi, pammegethōn akridōn plēthos amythēton ek tēs anistorētou meta tōn anemōn paraginetai chōras. tē men dynamei tēs plēseōs tōn ornithōn mikron parallatton, makron de tō sōmati. apo toutou de tou zōou trephontai panta ton chronon, allōs te toutois kai tetaricheumenois chrōmenoi.] <i>Around the spring equinox, when the</i> south-west winds <i>and</i> west winds <i>blow among them, an indescribable swarm of very large locusts flies in together with the winds from unknown lands; in the force of their flight they differ little from birds, but in body very greatly. On this little creature they feed at every season, either salted or otherwise preserved.</i> So also <i>Diodorus</i>, book 3 of <i>Stephanus</i>'s edition, p. 113: Ἐκ τούτου δαψιλεῖς τροφὰς ἔχουσιν ἅπαντα τὸν βίον [Ek toutou dapsileis trophas echousin hapanta ton bion]: <i>From it they have abundant sustenance throughout their whole life.</i> And <i>Artemidorus</i> in <i>Strabo</i>, book 16: Ζῶσι δ' ἀπὸ ἀκρίδων, ἃς οἱ ἐαρινοὶ Λίβες καὶ Ζέφυροι, πνέοντες μεγάλοι, συνελαύνουσιν εἰς τοὺς τόπους τούτους· [Zōsi d' apo akridōn, has hoi earinoi Libes kai Zephyroi, pneontes megaloi, synelaunousin eis tous topous toutous] <i>They live on locusts, which the spring</i> south-west winds <i>and</i> west winds, <i>blowing strongly, drive into those places.</i> And Solinus: <i>The neighbours of</i> Mauretania <i>at a certain season gather land locusts and, having hardened them in brine, keep them as their only means of subsistence.</i> And <i>Pliny</i>, book 6, ch. 30: <i>A certain part of the</i> Ethiopians <i>lives on locusts alone, hardened with smoke and salt as their yearly food.</i> And book 11, ch. 29: <i>among the</i> Parthians <i>these too are welcome as food.</i> And on the <i>Nasamones</i>, <i>Eustathius</i> on <i>Dionysius</i>: Ἐσθίουσι δὲ ἀττελάβους πρὸς ἥλιον ξηράναντες· εἶδος δὲ ἀκρίδος ὁ ἀττέλαβος· [Esthiousi de attelabous pros hēlion xēranantes; eidos de akridos ho attelabos] <i>They eat attelabi, a species of locust, dried in the sun.</i> And <i>Dioscorides</i>, where he treats of the asiracus, a species of locust: Χρῶνται δ' αὐτῇ κατακόρως οἱ κατὰ Λέπτιν Λίβυες· [Chrōntai d' autē katakorōs hoi kata Leptin Libyes] <i>The</i> Libyans <i>who live near</i> Leptis <i>use it as food to the point of satiety.</i> <i>Porphyry</i> also, in book 1 of <i>On Abstinence from Meat</i>, writes that clouds of locusts presented themselves to an army suffering from hunger in <i>Libya</i>.

‎What of the fact that in <i>Greece</i> itself the same food was in use, which is easy to prove? In <i>Aristophanes</i>' <i>Acharnians</i>, Act 4, Scene 1, a <i>Boeotian</i> peasant, among the foods for sale that he brings to the market at <i>Athens</i>, says that he has something

‎Τῶν ὀρταλίχων, ἢ τῶν τετραπτερυλλίδων, [Tōn ortalichōn, ē tōn tetrapterullidōn,]

‎<i>of hens, or of locusts</i>, which, as we have observed <sup>a)</sup>, are called τετραπτερυλλίδας [tetrapterullidas] <i>from the four wings</i> with which they are furnished. And Scene 7:

‎Δ. Βούλει περιδόσθαι κἀπιτρέψαι Λαμάχῳ, Πότερον ἀκρίδες ἥδιόν ἐστιν, ἢ κίχλαι; Λ. Οἴμ' ὡς ὑβρίζεις. Δ. Τὰς ἀκρίδας κρίνει πολύ. [D. Boulei peridosthai kapitrepsai Lamachō, Poteron akrides hēdion estin, ē kichlai? L. Oim' hōs hybrizeis. D. Tas akridas krinei poly.]

‎D. <i>Do you want to make a wager and leave it to</i> Lamachus (to say) <i>which taste better, locusts or thrushes?</i> L. <i>Alas! how unjust you are.</i> D. <i>He prefers the locusts.</i> And [III, 330] there the <i>Scholiast</i> says: Διασύρει τὸν Λάμαχον, ὡς ἀκρίδας ἐσθίοντα, ἢ ὅτι πρότερον εὐτελεῖ ἐχρῆτο τῇ διαίτῃ [Diasyrei ton Lamachon, hōs akridas esthionta, ē hoti proteron euteleī echrēto tē diaitē]; <i>he mocks</i> Lamachus <i>as one who feeds on locusts, or because he had formerly used cheap food.</i> <i>Plutarch</i>, in the <i>Banquet</i>, says that a tyrant who would rather rule over slaves than over men is like a farmer who would want ἀκρίδας, καὶ ὀρνίθας, ἀντὶ πυρῶν, καὶ κριθῶν συγκομίζειν [akridas, kai ornithas, anti pyrōn, kai krithōn synkomizein], <i>to gather locusts and birds instead of wheat and barley</i> (to eat). In <i>Theophylact</i>'s <i>Letters</i> a certain rustic, boasting that he had been lavishly entertained, says: Ἰσχάδες, ἦν μοι, καὶ ἀκρίδες, τὰ ὄψα· ὁ δὲ θεσπέσιος οὑτοσὶ νεανίας τῶν ἰσχάδων τὰ πλεῖστα ἐκβέβρωκε καὶ τὰς ἀκρίδας, οὐκ οἶδ᾽ ὅπως, ἐξερροφήσει [Ischades, ēn moi, kai akrides, ta opsa; ho de thespesios houtosi neanias tōn ischadōn ta pleista ekbebrōke kai tas akridas, ouk oid᾽ hopōs, exerrophēsei]. <i>I had,</i> he says, <i>dried figs and locusts as a relish; but that divine young man devoured most of the figs, and the locusts too, I know not how, he gulped down.</i> [col. 491] So too <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 4, testifies that the ancients used to eat little creatures very like locusts, namely cicadas and cercopes, which are smaller cicadas, and indeed to whet the appetite. His words are: Ἤσθιον δὲ καὶ τέττιγας, καὶ κέρκωπας, ἀναστομώσεως χάριν [Ēsthion de kai tettigas, kai kerkōpas, anastomōseōs charin]. In the same place <i>Anaxandrides</i>, describing the preparations for the wedding of <i>Iphicrates</i>, when he took to wife the daughter of <i>Cotys</i>, king of <i>Thrace</i>, lists among other things τέττιγας ὀπτοὺς [tettigas optous], <i>cooked cicadas.</i> And from the <i>Anagyrus</i> of <i>Aristophanes</i> this is quoted: Πρὸς Θεῶν ἐρᾷς τέττιγα φαγεῖν, καὶ κερκώπην θηρευσαμένη καλάμῳ λεπτῷ [Pros Theōn eras tettiga phagein, kai kerkōpēn thēreusamenē kalamō leptō]. <i>By the gods, you gladly eat the cicada and the cercope, which you hunt with a thin reed.</i> And in book 9, <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Horae</i> praises Athens because even in winter the huckster sells there κίκλας, ἀπίους, σχαδόνας, ἐλάας, πύον, χόρια, χελιδόνα, τέττιγας [kiklas, apious, schadonas, elaas, pyon, choria, chelidona, tettigas], <i>thrushes, pears, honeycombs, olives, beestings, choria</i> (that is, <i>dishes made of milk and honey</i>), <i>swallows, cicadas.</i> Likewise in book 2 <i>Alexis</i> brings on an old woman whose usual food, as she herself says, is κύαμος, θέρμος, λάχανον, γογγυλὶς, ὦχρος, λάθυρος, φηγὸς, βόλβος, τέττιξ [kyamos, thermos, lachanon, gongylis, ōchros, lathyros, phēgos, bolbos, tettix], <i>the bean, the lupine, greens, the turnip, the bitter vetch, the chickling, the acorn of the oak, the bulb, the cicada.</i> Indeed the <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>History</i> book 5, ch. 30, and after him <i>Pliny</i>, book 11, ch. 26, teach at what season above all they taste best. And Pliny testifies that peoples in the <i>East</i> eat them, among them the <i>Parthians</i>, although <i>abounding in wealth.</i> Hence <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 10, ch. 6, is greatly indignant at the men of his age, some of whom sell the cicadas, an animal sacred to the Muses, strung in a row, for dinner, while others buy them and eat them greedily.

‎But, and this is the heart of the matter, the locust was edible among the <i>Jews</i> too. This is attested by the <i>Mosaic</i> law, <i>Lev 11:21–22.</i>, where four species of locust are permitted for eating: <i>arbe, solam, chargol</i> and <i>chagab.</i> So too the <i>Talmudic</i> doctors, who in the tractate <i>Cholin</i>, ch. 3, fol. 65 and 66, argue at length about the marks by which clean locusts are distinguished from unclean, that is, those which may be eaten from those whose eating is forbidden by the Law. Accordingly, at <i>Exod 10:19</i>, where we read that a wind sent by God [III, 331] carried off whatever locusts were in <i>Egypt</i> and cast them into the <i>Red</i> Sea, so that not even one remained in all the borders of the <i>Egyptians</i>, <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] in his paraphrase makes an exception: מה דמלחין במנייא לצרוך מיכלהון, <i>what they had preserved with salt in their vessels for use as food</i> <sup>R2</sup>.

‎The <i>Arabs</i> teach the same. <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter <i>On locusts</i>, under the heading الحكم [al-ḥukm], that is, <i>On use</i>: اجمع المسلمون علي اباحة اكل الجراد [ajmaʿa al-muslimūn ʿalā ibāḥat akl al-jarād], "The Muslims <i>agree that the eating of locusts is lawful.</i>" They rely, of course, on the authority of <i>Abdalla</i>, whose words are: غزونا مع رسول الله صلي الله عليه وسلم سبع غزوات ناكل الجراد فيها [ghazawnā maʿa rasūl Allāh ṣallā Allāh ʿalayhi wa-sallam sabʿ ghazawāt naʾkul al-jarād fīhā], <i>"We undertook seven military expeditions with the prophet of God, on whom God be gracious, eating the flesh of locusts."</i> Hence they keep saying that God sends them, sometimes to be رزقا القوم [rizqan li-l-qawm] <i>food for the peoples</i>, sometimes to be بلا علي القوم [balāʾ ʿalā al-qawm] <i>a plague upon the peoples.</i> Let these few examples, taken from <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], suffice: الصراح طاير كالجندب يوكل [al-ṣurāḥ ṭāʾir ka-l-jundub yuʾkal], Sorrach, <i>a small winged animal like the bruchus, which is eaten.</i> جرد شري جلده عن اكله [jurida shariya jilduhu ʿan aklihi], Giarida, <i>pustules broke out on his flesh because of eating it;</i> that is, because of eating the locust mentioned before. جرد شكا بطنه من [col. 492] اكله [jurida shakā baṭnuhu min aklihi], Giurida, <i>his belly ached from eating the same.</i> المرتجل من يقع برجل من جراد فيشتوي منها [al-murtajil man yaqaʿ bi-rijl min jarād fa-yashtawī minhā], Almurtegilo, <i>one who, coming upon a swarm of locusts, roasts them.</i> الحنتف الجراد المنتف المنقي للطبخ [al-ḥantaf al-jarād al-muntaf al-munaqqā li-l-ṭabkh], Alchantapho, <i>a locust that has been plucked and cleaned for cooking</i>; الوهيسة ان يطبخ الجراد وتجفف ويدق ويخلط بدسم [al-wahīsa an yuṭbakh al-jarād wa-tujaffaf wa-yudaqq wa-yukhlaṭ bi-dasam], Alvvahisatho, <i>when a locust is cooked, and after drying is pounded and mixed with fat:</i> that is, to serve as food. Add to these the testimonies of more recent writers. Thus <i>Nicolaus Clenardus</i> in his Letters: <i>"They bring wagons full of locusts</i> into Fez; <i>for here people commonly eat these enemies."</i> And <i>Leo Africanus</i> [III, 332] book 9: "<i>The desert of</i> Arabia <i>and the peoples of</i> Libya <i>regard their arrival as a happy omen: for they pound them into meal, either boiled or dried in the sun, and eat them.</i>" And <i>Hermolaus Barbarus</i>, Corollary 256 on <i>Dioscorides</i>: "<i>Today too</i> the Egyptians <i>eat locusts</i>" <sup>R3</sup>.

‎But many write that the same is done in <i>Judaea</i> as well. Thus <i>Bede</i>, <i>On the Holy Places</i> ch. 14, reports that he learned from a holy bishop, <i>Arculf</i>, who had made a pilgrimage to those places, that <i>"in</i> Palestine <i>there are very well-known locusts, which, with little bodies slight and short like a finger of the hand, are easily caught in the grass and, cooked in oil, provide a poor man's fare."</i> And <i>Euthymius</i> on <i>Matthew</i>: <i>"They say that to this very day in those parts many eat it</i> (that kind of insect) <i>dried in smoke, but that it has a strong smell and a bad taste."</i> <i>Jacques de Vitry</i> too, bishop of <i>Ptolemais</i>, in the place cited above <sup>b)</sup>, testifies that in <i>Judaea</i> locusts were customarily eaten both fresh and preserved.

‎The sum of what we have said is this: besides the <i>Acridophagi</i>, known to all, the <i>Nasamones</i> too, and the people of <i>Leptis</i>, and the <i>Parthians</i>, and the <i>Greeks</i>, and the <i>Hebrews</i> once had locusts for food; as have the <i>Arabs</i> also, and the <i>Numidians</i>, and the <i>Mauretanians</i>, and the new inhabitants of <i>Palestine</i>, now for many centuries. Therefore that <i>John</i> ate locusts was nothing new or unheard of. What was singular in him was that in the desert he fed only on locusts and wild honey, and these perhaps as they chanced to come his way, not procured from elsewhere; so that the <i>Jews</i> might know that this man had an extraordinary office, and might be more attentive to his preaching. There was no reason, then, for <i>Isidore of Pelusium</i> to accuse of [III, 333] ignorance those who understand <i>locusts</i> here. Among their number were many far more celebrated than he: such as <i>Theophilus of Antioch</i>, <i>Origen</i>, <i>Hilary</i> and <i>Chrysostom</i> on <i>Matthew</i>; <i>Ambrose</i> on <i>Luke</i>; <i>Clement of Alexandria</i>, book 2 of the <i>Paedagogus</i>; <i>Jerome</i>, book 2 <i>Against Jovinian</i> and <i>Letter</i> 23 to <i>Marcella</i>; <i>Augustine</i>, book 10 of the <i>Confessions</i>, ch. 31. In short, all the writers older than <i>Isidore</i>, except only <i>Paulinus</i> in his sacred <i>poem</i> on <i>John the Baptist</i>; and against him it would be easy to set other <i>Poets</i> who agree with us. For thus <i>Juvencus</i> writes of him in book 1 of the <i>Gospel History</i>:

‎<i>He who was wont [to eat] locusts in the country fields.</i>

‎And <i>Prudentius</i>, <i>Cathemerinon</i>, Hymn 7:

‎<i>Accustomed to give his body a meagre fare</i> <i>of locusts and the liquid of wild honeycombs.</i>

‎And <i>Amoenus</i> in the <i>Enchiridion</i>:

‎<i>The</i> Baptist, <i>fed on locusts, bathes [them] in the river.</i>

‎And <i>Paul the Deacon</i> in the Hymn <i>On the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist</i>:

‎<i>The camel provided a shaggy covering,</i> [col. 493] <i>sheep a girdle for his sacred limbs;</i> <i>for him water was drink, and food</i> <i>was honey joined with locusts.</i>
