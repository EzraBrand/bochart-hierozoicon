# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 20: Whether chos is a night bird (eagle owl, little owl, owl, night raven) or the onocrotalus (vol. 3, pp. 14–20)

### ‎[III, 14] [col. 272] CHAPTER 20.

### ‎<i>On the bird</i> כוס chos <i>it is doubted whether it is a species of night bird, namely the eagle owl</i>, <i>the little owl</i>, <i>the owl</i>, <i>the night raven</i>, <i>or, very different from these</i>, <i>the onocrotalus.</i>

‎The bird כוס <i>chos</i>, which follows next, is named only in the Law among the forbidden birds, and in <i>Psalm. CII</i>, 7, where the <i>Psalmist</i> says הייתי ככוס חרבות, <i>I was like the</i> chos <i>of the wastes.</i> And the <i>Elders</i>: Ἐγενήθην ὡσεὶ νυκτικόραξ ἐν οἰκοπέδῳ [Egenēthēn hōsei nyktikorax en oikopedō], <i>I was like the night raven among ruined walls.</i> Consulted about this passage by <i>Sunia</i> and <i>Fretela</i>, <i>Jerome</i> replies thus in his 135th Epistle: "<i>You ask what</i> nycticorax <i>means</i> <i>among</i> the Latins. <i>In</i> the Hebrew, <i>in place of</i> nycticorax, <i>the word</i> כוס chos <i>is written,</i> <i>which</i> Aquila, <i>and</i> the Seventy, <i>and</i> Theodotion, <i>and</i> the Fifth <i>edition</i> <i>rendered</i> night raven; Symmachus, <i>however</i>, hoopoe; the Sixth <i>edition</i>, little owl, <i>which</i> <i>we too rather follow.</i>" And a little later: <i>Most contend that it means</i> eagle owl. Yet he himself, siding with them, renders it <i>eagle owl</i> both in the Law [col. 273] and in the <i>Psalms.</i>

‎The <i>Arabs</i> render it البوم [al-būm] <i>album</i>, that is, <i>little owl.</i> This is clear from the description of the <i>album</i> in <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: البوم طاير يقع علي الذكر والانثي حتي تقول صدي او فياد فيتخص بالذكر وكنية الانثي ام الخراب وام الصيبان ويقال لها غراب الليل ومن طبعها انها تدخل علي كل طاير في وكره ليلا وتخرجه منه وتاكل فراخه وبيضه وهي قوية السلطان بالليل لا يحملها شي من الطير واذا راها الطير بالنهار قتلوها ونتفوا ريشها للعداوة التي بينهم وبينها ومن اجل ذلك صار الصياد يجعلها تحت شبكته لتقع الطير عليها [al-būm ṭāyir yaqaʿu ʿalā al-dhakar wa-al-unthā ḥattā taqūla ṣadā aw fayyād fa-yatakhaṣṣaṣu bi-al-dhakar wa-kunyat al-unthā umm al-kharāb wa-umm al-ṣībān wa-yuqālu lahā ghurāb al-layl wa-min ṭabʿihā annahā tadkhulu ʿalā kull ṭāyir fī wakrihi laylan wa-tukhrijuhu minhu wa-taʾkulu firākhahu wa-bayḍahu wa-hiya qawiyyat al-sulṭān bi-al-layl lā yaḥmiluhā shayʾ min al-ṭayr wa-idhā raʾāhā al-ṭayr bi-al-nahār qatalūhā wa-natafū rīshahā li-al-ʿadāwa allatī baynahum wa-baynahā wa-min ajl dhālika ṣāra al-ṣayyād yajʿaluhā taḥta shabakatihi li-taqaʿa al-ṭayr ʿalayhā]. <i>Album</i> <i>is a bird. The name is of common gender; but if you say</i> tsada <i>or</i> phajad, <i>it will be proper to the male. The female's by-names are mother of the wasteland</i> <i>and mother of nits</i> (because she swarms with them). <i>It is also called raven of the night</i> (that is, <i>night raven</i>). <i>It is its nature</i> <i>to enter the nests of all birds by night</i> <i>and drive them out of them</i>, <i>so as to feed on their chicks and eggs. For by night it is so strong that no bird can withstand it; but the birds in turn attack it</i> <i>when they see it by day</i>, <i>and pluck its feathers</i>, <i>because of the hatred</i> <i>with which they pursue each other. Hence a kind of fowling arises in which it is placed under a net,</i> [III, 15] <i>so that the birds rush upon it.</i> And below: والبوم اصناف وكلها يحب الخلوة بانفسها والتفرد وفي اصل طبعها عداوة الغربان [wa-al-būm aṣnāf wa-kulluhā yuḥibbu al-khalwa bi-anfusihā wa-al-tafarrud wa-fī aṣl ṭabʿihā ʿadāwat al-ghirbān]: <i>Of the</i> album <i>there are several species, all of which love retreat and solitude; but hatred of ravens belongs most of all to their nature.</i> Everyone sees that this is said of the little owl. For the little owl roams by night. The <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>History</i>, book 1, chapter 1: Τὰ μὲν ζῶα νυκτερόβια, οἷον γλαύξ [Ta men zōa nykterobia, hoion glaux]. And from this it has its name. Festus: <i>The noctua is named from the time of night</i> [nox]<i>, in which it sings or flies.</i> And <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 17, describes its skilful fighting against birds; and <i>Aristotle</i>, book 9, chapter 1, its use in fowling: Τῆς δὲ ἡμέρας καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ὀρνίθια τὴν γλαῦκα περιπέτεται, ὃ καλεῖται θαυμάζειν, καὶ προσπετόμενα τέλλουσι. διὸ ὀρνιθοθήραι θηρεύουσιν αὐτῇ παντοδαπὰ ὀρνίθια [Tēs de hēmeras kai ta alla ornithia tēn glauka peripetetai, ho kaleitai thaumazein, kai prospetomena tellousi. dio ornithothērai thēreuousin autē pantodapa ornithia]. <i>By day the other small birds fly around the little owl, which is called</i> admiring<i>, and, flying at it, they pluck it. Therefore fowlers catch small birds of every kind by means of it.</i> Further, on the enmity of the little owl with the crow, which the <i>Arabs</i> designate by the name of <i>raven</i>, see the <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>Pliny</i> and <i>Aelian.</i> Hence arose the proverb in <i>Suidas</i> and [col. 274] <i>Apostolius</i>: Ἄλλο γλαὺξ, ἄλλο κορώνη φθέγγεται ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλλήλοις μὴ συμφωνέντων [Allo glaux, allo korōnē phthengetai epi tōn allēlois mē symphōnentōn]; <i>The little owl sounds one thing, the crow another: said of those who do not agree with each other.</i>

‎The <i>Chaldee</i> translators too, and the <i>Syriac</i> and the <i>Samaritan</i>, differ little from these. Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] has צירא <i>tsajera;</i> Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos] קריא <i>kadja;</i> Joseph the Blind קיפופא <i>kiphupha;</i> the Syriac ܥܘܕܐ [עודא] <i>auda;</i> and the Samaritan ࠑࠃࠉࠀ [צדיא] <i>tsadja.</i> These words, however different in appearance, nevertheless come to almost the same thing. Thus the <i>Samaritan</i> ࠑࠃࠉࠀ [צדיא] <i>tsadja</i> is the <i>female of the little owl</i>, which, as the <i>Arabs</i> taught us above, is called in <i>Arabic</i> صدى [ṣadā] <i>tsada</i>, with the same radicals. And Jonathan's צירא is a corrupt word: in its place one should write צדיא, as the same author has at <i>Deut 14:16.</i> Nor is Onkelos's קריא anything else, nor the <i>Syriac</i> ܥܘܕܐ [עודא] <sup>R1</sup>. For that the letters <i>Tsade, Koph</i> and <i>Ajin</i> are interchangeable is shown by the <i>Chaldee</i> ארעא and ארקא for ארץ, <i>earth.</i> And as for קיפופא <i>kiphupha</i> in Joseph the Blind: if it is not the same as צדיא <i>tsadja</i> or קריא <i>kadjah</i>, that it is at least a kindred animal is proved from a passage of the <i>Talmud</i> in <i>Nidda</i>, ch. 3, fol. 23a, in which קריא וקפופא, <i>kadja and kiphupha</i> (so it should be read, not [III, 16] קריא <i>karja</i>, as our editions wrongly have it <sup>R2</sup>), are counted among the animals דעיניהן הולכות לפניהן כשל אדם, <i>whose eyes look forward like a human's,</i> whereas in the other birds the eyes are at the side. Hence the <i>Gloss</i> there: קריא וקפופא עוף הצועק בלילה ופניו דומות לחתול ועיניו לפניו, <i>Kadja</i> (so I read for קריא <i>karja</i>) <i>and Kiphupha, a bird which cries at night, and has a face like a cat's, and eyes in front</i> <sup>R3</sup>. This is a description of the little owl or the eagle owl. Therefore, if you except <i>Symmachus</i>, for whom כוס <i>chos</i> is the <i>hoopoe</i>, all the ancients understand by this name the <i>little owl</i>, the <i>night raven</i> or the <i>eagle owl</i>, birds that do not differ much from one another. And if in these words of Jerome, <i>but Symmachus the hoopoe</i>, you read <i>ululam</i> [owl] for <i>upupam</i> [hoopoe], there will be no notable disagreement among the ancients, since the night birds, the little owl, eagle owl, owl and night raven, are so similar to one another that writers commonly use the names interchangeably.

‎To these are added the more recent <i>Hebrews</i>, who hold that it is called כוס <i>chos</i> as if כוסה <i>chose</i>, that is, <i>hiding itself</i> or <i>lying hidden</i>, because this bird נכסה מן העין, <i>shuns sight</i>, or נכסה מעין אדם, <i>hides itself from the eyes of men.</i> So <i>Baal Hatturim</i> [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] and <i>Aben Ezra [=Abraham ibn Ezra].</i> And R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on <i>Leviticus</i>: כוס וינשוף הם צוא״טיש הצועקי׳ בלילה ויש להם לסתות כאדם, Chos <i>and</i> jansuph, <i>in French</i> chovettes, <i>birds which hoot at night and have jaws like a man.</i> This is partly taken from the passage of the <i>Talmud</i> cited above, where precisely this is asserted of the birds קריא and קפופא. Since these words of R. Selomo are explicit, I cannot at all understand what these words of Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his <i>Lexicon</i> refer to: כוס שם עוף טמא לא נודע אצלנו ורבינו שלמה ז״ל פי׳ פלקו״ן בלע״ז ואותו שקורין פלקו״ן הוא נמצא ביישוב וצרין בו בני האדם שאר העופות ואחר שאמר הכתוב ככוס חרבות ידמה שהוא מדברי, Chos, <i>the name of an unknown bird. Our master</i> R. Selomo <i>of blessed memory explains it in the vernacular as falcon. But the bird they call falcon is found in inhabited places, and men use it to hunt other birds; whereas Scripture, since it says “Like the</i> chos <i>of the wilderness”, seems to mean a bird living in the desert.</i> In these words he ascribes to R. Selomo an opinion very different from the one his Commentaries present. Yet, following this, David de Pomis [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary], Münster, Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] and others render כוס <i>chos</i> as <i>falcon</i>. Indeed, as if one were free to invent anything here, because Kimchi says it is an <i>unknown</i> [col. 275] <i>bird</i>, some render it <i>cuckoo</i>, others <i>illex</i> [decoy bird], others <i>screech owl</i>, others <i>pelican</i>, and what not? It must be admitted, meanwhile, that by far the greatest part [III, 17] of the modern translators too have kept the little owl. And <i>Drusius</i> endeavours to prove that this is the true sense of the word from the German <i>kuts</i> for <i>little owl</i> and the Paduan <i>chus</i> for <i>scops</i>, a kind of little owl, words which seem to come close to the <i>Hebrew</i> כוס <i>chos</i>, as if derived from it. So it will seem dangerous to oppose such great agreement <sup>R4</sup>. I shall nevertheless say what I once conjectured about this word, not without some appearance of truth.

‎I suspected that the word כוס <i>chos</i> signifies the <i>truo</i>, or, what is the same, the <i>onocrotalus</i>. For the bird the <i>Greeks</i> call ὀνοκρόταλος [onokrotalos] the <i>Romans</i> called TRUO. Now <i>trua</i> is a kind of vessel.

‎Pomponius in the <i>Pannuceates</i>: [col. 276]

‎<i>When the woman saw the ladle so wonderfully topped with a crest.</i>

‎Titinius in the <i>Setina</i>:

‎<i>When the great bronze pot boils, the cook stirs it a little with a ladle.</i>

‎Hence the diminutive <i>trulla</i> [ladle]. Varro, <i>De lingua Latina</i>, book 4: <i>Trulla from its likeness to the trua; because that is large and this small, as it were truula.</i> Nonius: <i>The ancients wished the</i> trua <i>to be named</i> from <i>terere</i> [to rub], <i>which we call by the diminutive</i> trulla. <i>Hence, then,</i> truo <i>is the onocrotalus.</i> Festus: <i>Truo, the bird onocrotalus.</i> Caecilius, <i>mocking the size of a nose: "O immortal gods, whence has this truo crept forth?"</i> The <i>Glosses</i> of Philoxenus: Ὀνοκρόταλος [Onokrotalos], <i>Trica</i>; read <i>Truo.</i> Other <i>Glosses</i>, excellent but often corrupted by the carelessness of copyists, have <i>Triduo, tinuero.</i> One should read <i>Trua</i> [is] a vessel; <i>Truo</i>, however, ὀνοκρόταλον [onokrotalon]; supply <i>sonat</i> [means]. The bird is evidently called <i>truo</i> from the <i>trua</i> [ladle] that it has in its beak. Accordingly the Savoyards call it <i>gutturosa</i>, that is, <i>strumosa</i> [the goitred one], from the crop which protrudes from the throat like a goitre. And most of the Arabs call it الحوصل [al-ḥawṣal] <i>alhausal</i>, that is, <i>the crop</i>, as if the whole bird were a crop. Thus the Indian bird κήλας [kēlas] in Aelian, book 16, ch. 4, three times larger than the bustard, with a huge mouth and great legs, having πρηγορεῶνα, καὶ ἐκεῖνον μέγιστον, προσεμφερῆ κωρύκῳ [prēgoreōna, kai ekeinon megiston, prosempherē kōrykō], <i>a crop, and that a very large one, like a sack</i>, is certainly the onocrotalus, which the Greeks would have called κήλας [kēlas] on account of its κήλη [kēlē], or βρογχοκήλη [bronchokēlē], that is, <i>a swelling in the throat.</i> Nor is that crop said without reason to be very large, since some write that it holds almost an amphora or an urn. Others report that a little Ethiopian child was found in it. Others relate that they put into it a fist together with the arm, others a foot [III, 18] with the leg up to the knee, the animal itself remaining unharmed. Others that they put it on their head as a hood, the rest of the body hanging down behind. Others that Egyptian sailors bail out the bilge with the beak of the onocrotalus. And Pliny (book 10, chapter 47) says that <i>in the throat itself there is a kind of second stomach. Into this the insatiable animal heaps everything, so that its capacity is astonishing. Then, when its plundering is complete, it gradually returns the food from there into the mouth and passes it into the true</i> (others read <i>the lower</i>) <i>belly, in the manner of a ruminant.</i> Hence it is that an Arab poet, ملغز [mulghiz], that is, αἰνιγματιζόμενος [ainigmatizomenos] [speaking in riddles], says of this bird,

‎ما طاير في قلبه * يلوح للناس عجب * منقاره كبطنه * والعين منه في الذنب * [mā ṭāyir fī qalbihi * yalūḥu li-l-nās ʿajab * minqāruhu ka-baṭnihi * wa-l-ʿayn minhu fī al-dhanab *]

‎<i>What bird is there that by its nature appears wondrous to men? Its beak serves it for a belly, and its eye is in its tail:</i> that is, behind that belly which it has in its beak. From this belly, then, or sack, or trough, which hangs from the lower part of the beak, the bird may also have been called הכוס by the <i>Hebrews</i>, from <i>chos</i>, that is, <i>vessel</i> or <i>cup</i>; for כוס <i>chos</i> is a thoroughly established word for a <i>vessel</i> or <i>cup</i>. This conjecture is considerably supported by the passage of the <i>Psalm</i> we have cited, דמיתי לקאת מדבר הייתי ככוס חרבות, for it would best be rendered thus: <i>I am become like a pelican of the wilderness, and I was as an onocrotalus of the desolate places.</i> And so in the same verse these two birds would be joined, the pelican and the onocrotalus, which are so closely akin that even the most learned take them indiscriminately. Among these is <i>Oppian</i> in the <i>Ixeutica</i>, where to the πελεκίνῳ [pelekinō], that is, the <i>pelican</i>, is attributed a κόλπος [kolpos], a kind of <i>pouch</i> hanging in front of the breast, in which it stores all its food for a while. Also <i>Jerome</i>, for whom קאת in the passage of the <i>Psalm</i> is the <i>pelican</i>, but elsewhere the <i>onocrotalus</i>, as in <i>Lev 11:18. Isa 34:11</i> and <i>Zeph 2:14.</i> And <i>Belon</i> [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], book 3, chapter 2 of <i>De Avibus</i>, where the pelican, the platea and the onocrotalus are taken for the same bird. Moreover, <i>onocrotalus of the desolate places</i> would be said correctly. For <i>Jerome</i> teaches on <i>Es. XXXIV.</i> that this animal <i>is accustomed to live in desert places.</i> And on <i>Sophon. II.</i>, that <i>there are two kinds of onocrotali, one aquatic, the other of the wilderness.</i> So also <i>Isidore</i>, book 12, chapter 7. Accordingly <i>Damir</i>, in the chapter البجع [al-bajaʿ] <i>albugia</i>, or <i>On the Onocrotalus</i>, in the section <i>On eating it</i>, concludes that it is indeed lawful, yet not by force of the general rule which grants <i>Muslims</i> the eating of all water birds: ان [col. 277] ذلك الوجة يجري في طاير لا يغارق الما وهذا يالغه ثم يغارقه [inna dhālika al-wajh yajrī fī ṭāyir lā yughāriq al-mā wa-hādhā yālighuh thumma yughāriquh] <i>Because this reasoning runs</i> (that is, this rule holds) [III, 19] <i>for those birds which always remain in the water; but this bird at one time lives in the water, at another is separated from it;</i> namely, so as to withdraw into the remotest wilderness. This too must not be omitted: the <i>Psalmist</i> compares himself to this bird on account of his constant complaints and laments. For there had preceded מקול אנחתי, <i>by reason of the voice of my groaning my bones cleave to my flesh.</i> Now the voice of the onocrotalus has something mournful and dreadful about it. <i>Aelian</i>, in the place cited above: Φθέγμα δὲ ἔχει καὶ μάλα ἀπηχὲς [Phthegma de echei kai mala apēches], <i>It has a very discordant voice.</i> Hence by the <i>Greeks</i> it was called ὀνοκρόταλος [onokrotalos], as if it <i>brayed like an ass</i>; and by some of the <i>Arabs</i> جمل الما [jamal al-mā], <i>camel of the water</i>, as if it <i>bellowed in the manner of a camel.</i> Hence Albertus: <i>It emits a dreadful voice, sounding as it were like a horn.</i> And Olaus: <i>It makes a great cry like the bird botaurus</i>, that is, the <i>bittern</i>, which with its voice imitates the bellowing of a bull. Finally, this bird cannot have been unknown to the <i>Jews</i>, since reliable writers attest that it is found around <i>Antioch in Syria</i> and <i>Gaza in Eretz Yisrael</i>, and that it is common in <i>Egypt</i>. It might be added that this is how it came about that כוס <i>chos</i> was rendered by the <i>Greeks</i>, whom the rest followed, as νυκτικόραξ [nyktikorax] instead of <i>onocrotalus</i>: because for כוס <i>chos</i> they read בום <i>bum.</i> These two words, though they differ enormously in sound, are nevertheless very similar in <i>Hebrew</i> script. Further, that the word בום <i>bum</i> signifies the <i>little owl</i>, and غراب الليل [ghurāb al-layl], <i>raven of the night</i>, that is, the <i>night raven</i>, at least among the <i>Arabs</i>, whose language is a dialect of <i>Hebrew</i>, we have amply proved above <sup>a)</sup>.
