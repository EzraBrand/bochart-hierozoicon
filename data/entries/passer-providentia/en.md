# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 23: The providence of God for sparrows and little birds, in Matt 10:29 and Luke 12:6 (vol. 2, pp. 735–738)

### ‎[II, 735] [col. 157] Chapter 23.

### ‎<i>On the providence of God concerning sparrows and little birds, in Matt 10:29 and Luke 12:6.</i>

‎<i>Christ, in Matt 10:29</i>, says that two sparrows are sold for an assarion, and yet not one of them falls to the ground without God. And in <i>Luke 12:6</i> he says that five sparrows are sold for two assaria, and yet not one of them is forgotten before God. Thus individual sparrows were sold for half an as, or a little less. For those are mistaken who hold that the assarion is half an as. Rather, the assarion is the same as the as. The <i>Latin-Greek</i> Glosses: <i>Asse</i>, Ἀσσάριον [assarion]; read Ἀσσαρίου [assariou]. And elsewhere: <i>Sesquas</i>, Ἓι ἥμισυ ἀσσάριον [hei hēmisy assarion]. Read Ἓν ἥμισυ καὶ ἀσσάριον [hen hēmisy kai assarion]. The <i>Greek-Latin Glosses of Philoxenus</i>: Ἀσσάριον [assarion], <i>Hic as.</i>; Ἀσσαρίου ἥμισυ [assariou hēmisy], <i>Sesquas</i>; read Ἓν ἀσσάριον καὶ ἥμισυ [hen assarion kai hēmisy]. The as, by <i>Budé</i>'s reckoning, was worth four of our little deniers and a trifle more. The <i>Syriac</i> translator has ܐܣܪ [אסר] <i>assar</i>, a word which the <i>Hebrews</i> also often use for the <i>assarion</i>. <i>David Cohen de Lara</i>, in his book <i>De convenientia Rabbinismi</i> [col. 158] <i>cum Graeco</i>, defines אסר <i>assar</i> in <i>Spanish</i> as <i>moneda de tres maravedis</i>, which comes very close to <i>Budé</i>'s λογισμὸν [logismon]. In Maimonides' [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] treatise <i>On Shekels</i>, ch. 1. 3.: הסלע ארבעה דינרין והדינר שש מעין ומעה היא היקראת בימי משה רבינו גרה ומאה היא שני פונדיונין ופונדיון שני אסרין ופרוטה אחת משמונה באסר נמצא משקל המעה והיא הגרה שש עשרה שעורות ומשקל האסר ארבע שעורות ומשקל פרוטה חצי שעורה, <i>The shekel is worth four denarii. The denarius six obols. The obol was called a gerah in the days of</i> Moses <i>our master. The obol is worth two</i> [II, 736] <i>pondia, and the pondion two assaria. And the perutah is the eighth part of an assarion. The weight of the obol, which is called a gerah, is sixteen barley grains. The weight of the assarion four grains, of the perutah half a grain.</i> <i>Drusius</i> cites a similar passage from the <i>Book of Commandments.</i> For <i>assarion</i> the <i>Arabic</i> translator has فلس [fals] <i>follis</i>, and for <i>two assaria</i> فلسين [falsayn] <i>two folles</i>; and <i>Jerome</i> has <i>dipondium.</i> But in <i>Matt 5:26</i> the same <i>Arabic</i> version has <i>follis</i> for <i>quadrans.</i> And in <i>Mark 14:42</i> فلسين [falsayn] <i>two folles</i> stands for <i>two mites</i>, that is, <i>two</i> פרוטות <i>perutoth</i>, which together make one quadrans. It is evident, therefore, that this translator, caring little about the true value of the coins, was content to put one cheap coin for another cheap coin, though of different value. And indeed, to be sold for an assarion or an as means nothing other than to be sold off at a low price. For whatever is sold or valued at an as must be cheap. Hence <i>Catullus</i> to his <i>Lesbia</i>:

‎<i>And the gossip of stern old men, let us value it all at a single as.</i>

‎And against the <i>Vibennii</i>:

‎<i>Son, you cannot sell it for an as.</i>

‎And against a certain man:

‎<i>You think it not worth an as? O filth, O brothel!</i>

‎And <i>Cicero</i>, for <i>Quinctius</i>: <i>He says he will not pay an as unless he has first settled all the affairs and accounts of the partnership.</i> And for <i>Roscius</i> the Comedian: <i>Say he gave</i> LLS. CCCIↃↃↃ, <i>he who gave not a single as.</i> And <i>Juvenal</i>, Satire 10:

‎<i>Whoever still worships Minerva, acquired for a single as.</i>

‎And the <i>Mishnah</i> at the end of the tractate <i>Chullin</i>, concerning the precept by which the <i>Israelites</i> are commanded to let the mother go when they take eggs and young from a nest, that it may be well with them and their days be prolonged, <i>Deut 22:6–7.</i>: אם מצוה קלה שהיא באסר אמרה תורה למען ייטב לך והארכת ימים קל וחומר על מצות חמורות שבתורה, <i>If concerning a lighter precept, about a thing that is scarcely worth an assarion, the Law says, “That it may be well with thee, and that thou mayest prolong thy days,” how much more</i> (is the same to be hoped) <i>for the weightier precepts that are in the Law?</i> For <i>assarion</i> the older <i>Greeks</i> would have said <i>obol</i>. Thus in the <i>Birds of Aristophanes</i> the birds complain bitterly of a certain <i>Philocrates</i>, who,

‎— συνείρων τοὺς σπίνους, πωλεῖ καθ᾽ ἑπτὰ ὀβολοῦ, [syneirōn tous spinous, pōlei kath᾽ hepta obolou,]

‎<i>Tying finches together, he sells seven for one obol. For an obol</i>, that is, <i>for two assaria</i>, or <i>asses.</i> This is shown by these words of Polybius, <i>Histories</i> book 2: Ὡς μὲν οὖν ἐπιτοπολὺ παρίενται τοὺς καταλύτας οἱ πανδοχεῖς ἡμιασσαρίου, τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐστὶ τέταρτον μέρος ὀβολοῦ [Hōs men oun epitopoly parieнтai tous katalytas hoi pandocheis hēmiassariou, touto d᾽ esti tetarton meros obolou]. <i>Innkeepers generally receive their guests for half an as, or half an assarion, which is a quarter of an obol.</i> So when <i>Christ</i> says that five sparrows are sold for two [II, 737] assaria, it is the same as if he had said they are sold for an obol. From this you may infer that at <i>Athens</i> in the time of <i>Aristophanes</i> provisions were somewhat cheaper than in <i>Judaea</i> in the time of <i>Christ</i>. For at the price for which the <i>Athenians</i> bought seven finches, the <i>Jews</i> bought only five sparrows. Further, the <i>Latin</i> translator of <i>Aristophanes</i> has <i>passeres</i> [sparrows] for <i>fringilli</i> [finches], because the sparrow and the finch are very similar birds. Hence the <i>Glosses</i> [col. 159] <i>of Philoxenus</i>: Σπίνος· ὁ στρουθὸς [Spinos; ho strouthos], <i>Frincillus, fringillus</i>, as if σπίνος [spinos] were the same as στρουθὸς [strouthos], or <i>passer</i> [sparrow]. <i>Fringillus</i> is in French <i>pinson</i>, formerly perhaps <i>pintion</i>, like σπίνθιον [spinthion] in Hesychius. So too <i>chanson</i> from <i>cantione</i>, <i>maison</i> from <i>mansione</i>, <i>raison</i> from <i>ratione</i>, etc. But whether five or seven such little birds are sold for an obol, and whether the author of the <i>Mishnah</i> valued the mother alone, or the mother with her young, at an as, they must be very cheap animals, since they are on sale for so slight a price. Yet here <i>Christ</i> teaches us that even these little creatures are such a concern to God that none of them falls to the ground without him, that is, perishes without his permission. With this agrees <i>Luke</i>: Ἓν ἐξ αὐτῶν οὐκ ἔστιν ἐπιλελησμένον ἐνώπιον τοῦ Θεοῦ [Hen ex autōn ouk estin epilelēsmenon enōpion tou Theou]; <i>Not one of them is forgotten before God.</i> The phrase is taken from <i>Ps 9:19</i>, where it is said of the poor man: Οὐκ εἰς τέλος ἐπιλησθήσεται [Ouk eis telos epilēsthēsetai], <i>He shall not be forgotten for ever</i>; in <i>Hebrew</i> לא לנצח ישכח. Hence the royal <i>Psalmist</i>, of small and great animals alike, <i>Ps 104:27</i>: <i>These all wait upon you, that you may give them their food in due season.</i> Yet, to say nothing for the present of the <i>Jews</i>, most of whom hold a different opinion, Jerome on <i>Habakkuk</i> ch. 1 says this: <i>It is absurd to lower the majesty of God to this, that he should know at every moment how many gnats are born and how many die; how great is the multitude of bugs, fleas and flies on the earth; how many fish swim in the water; and which of the smaller must fall prey to the larger. Let us not be such foolish flatterers of God that, while we drag his power down to the lowest things, we do injury to ourselves, saying that the providence over rational and irrational creatures is the same.</i> These words might seem rather harsh unless softened by a kindly interpretation, so that we understand them to be said comparatively (as he hints toward the end): lest God be thought to have the same regard for brutes as for men, whom he created in his own image, since he preserves the brutes chiefly for the sake of men, for whose use they were created. We therefore recognize different degrees of divine providence, and that God does not care for oxen in the same way as for men, as <i>Paul</i> teaches, <i>1 Cor 9:9.</i> Yet it is certain that oxen as well as men, and even the meanest [col. 160] creatures, are subject to God. Nor does he disdain to govern and preserve what he deigned to create. And we must beware of inventing limits to that providence, which, as the greatest things do not burden it, so the smallest do not escape it or go neglected by it. Who would believe that God did not know the number of the quails with which he fed the <i>Israelites</i> in the desert, or of the fish that swam in the lake of <i>Gennesaret</i> when <i>Peter</i> cast his nets at <i>Christ's</i> command? Or that he did not know how many frogs or lice he would use against the <i>Egyptians</i>; or that those locusts which are called <i>God's army</i>, Joel 2:11, were of no [II, 738] concern to him, and that this supreme commander did not know how many soldiers he had enlisted in his army? Finally, what could be plainer than <i>Christ's</i> words, that <i>none of the sparrows falls to the ground without God</i>, or <i>is forgotten before him?</i> On this passage note what the most learned Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] cites from the <i>Jerusalem Talmud</i> in his <i>Lexicon Talmudicum</i>, p. 553: Rabbi Simeon ben Yochai <i>was hiding in a cave. One day, sitting at the mouth of the cave and seeing a fowler catching birds and spreading his net, he heard a daughter of the voice</i> (an oracle); <i>when it said</i> דימוס <i>dimos</i> (that is, <i>release</i>), <i>the bird escaped. But when the daughter of the voice said</i> ספיקולא, <i>spicula</i> (that is, <i>sentence of death</i>), <i>the bird was caught. Then the oracle said,</i> צפור מבלברי שמיא לא יבדא כל שכן בר נשא, <i>A little bird does not perish without heaven</i> (that is, without God's will and providence): <i>how much less a man!</i> For לא יבדא read לא יאבד. The <i>pagans</i> too write things not unlike these. Thus Plutarch somewhere proves from Homer that the ancients held the opinion that by the divine power are cared for οὐ μόνον βόας, ἀλλὰ καὶ πάντα τὰ ἄλλα ζῶα [ou monon boas, alla kai panta ta alla zōa], <i>not only oxen, but all other animals too.</i> And Aelian, <i>Hist.</i> book 11, ch. 31: Ἴδιον δὲ ἄρα τῶν ζώων καὶ ἐκεῖνο ἀγαθὸν, πρόνοιαν αὐτῶν καὶ οἱ Θεοὶ ποιοῦνται, καὶ οὔτε αὐτῶν καταφρονοῦσιν, οὐδὲ μὴν ὀλιγώρως ἔχουσιν [Idion de ara tōn zōōn kai ekeino agathon, pronoian autōn kai hoi Theoi poiountai, kai oute autōn kataphronousin, oude mēn oligōrōs echousin]; <i>The brutes too have this good, that the gods provide for them, and neither despise them nor treat them with indifference.</i>
