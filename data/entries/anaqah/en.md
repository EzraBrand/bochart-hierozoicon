# Book 4, chapter 2: Anaka is a kind of stellio, named from its harsh cry (vol. 2, pp. 487–493)

### ‎[II, 487] [col. 1063] CHAPTER II.

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> אנקה anaka <i>of Lev 11:30 is a kind of stellio, so called from its harsh shrill cry.</i>

‎Next, in <i>Lev 11:30</i>, follow five species of creeping things whose names are not easy to ex- [col. 1064] plain, since they are found nowhere else. They are אנקה <i>anaka,</i> כח <i>coach,</i> לטאה <i>letaa,</i> חמט <i>chomet,</i> תנשמת <i>thinsemeth.</i> Of these, אנקה <i>anaka</i> is for the <i>Greeks</i> and for <i>Jerome</i> μυγάλη [mygalē] <i>mygale</i>, that is, the <i>mus araneus</i> [shrew]. They hold that it is called Μυγάλην [Mygalēn] because it is born ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς [ek myos kai galēs], <i>from a mouse and a weasel</i>. The <i>scholiast on Nicander</i> says: Ἀμύντας δή φησι τὴν μυγάλην ἐκ μύος καὶ γαλῆς γίνεσθαι [Amyntas dē phēsi tēn mygalēn ek myos kai galēs ginesthai]. This is utterly absurd, since the weasel is much larger than the mouse and as hostile to it as the cat, as we observed above <sup>a)</sup>. That a weasel should mate with a mouse and a shrew be born of it is therefore wholly improbable. Aetius puts it better in the <i>Tetrabiblos</i>, book 4, sermon 1, chapter 14: <i>The shrew is like the weasel in colour, but like the mouse in size; hence it also obtained the name</i> mygale. Yet the shrew has the appearance of a mouse rather than its size, for it is altogether smaller than a mouse. The <i>Romans</i> seem to call it <i>mus araneus</i> [spider mouse] from its venom, which it shares with the spider. And in <i>Hebrew</i> it has been thought to be called אנקה <i>anaka</i> by reason of its voice, which is extremely shrill, so that it can be taken for a groan and a wail; for otherwise אנקה <i>anaka</i> means <i>groaning</i> or <i>outcry</i> to the Hebrews. If <i>Onkelos</i> [=Targum Onkelos] understood the same, then in his <i>Paraphrase</i>, instead of ילא <i>jala</i> (the word by which אנקה <i>anaka</i> is rendered there), one must read פלא <i>pala</i>, or <i>phala</i>. For the <i>Arabs</i> call the <i>shrew</i> so. <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], book 4, <i>Fen</i> 6, tract. 3, last chapter, in the title: فصل في عضة موغالي وهو الغلا [faṣl fī ʿaḍḍat mūghālī wa-huwa al-ghalā] <i>Chapter on the bite of the mygale, which</i> (for the Arabs) <i>is</i> alphala. In the <i>Latin</i> version one reads <i>alcale,</i> which would be الغلا [al-ghalā]. I should think that half-learned men changed one or the other into ילא <i>jala.</i> Under this name two very different animals occur in the <i>Talmudic</i> books, namely the leech and the hedgehog. But neither of them has a place here. For neither is called אנקה <i>anaka</i> in <i>Hebrew</i>; rather the <i>hedgehog</i> is called קפוד <i>kippod,</i> and the <i>leech</i> עלוקה <i>aluka</i>, or rather עלקה <i>alka</i>. Moreover, the subject here is those creeping things that creep upon the earth, and the leech is not of that number. So much for the first and most ancient interpretation of the word אנקה <i>anaka.</i>

‎The second is that of <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] and of the <i>Jerusalem</i> Targumist, of whom one has here מינקת חויא, and the other פלית חויא, or פיילת חויא. So it should be read, not חיוא as in the <i>Lexicon Baal-Aruch [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome].</i> The <i>lexicographers</i> understand neither. For it is [II, 488] beside the point that they take פליית here for a locust, as in פליא ביארי in chapter 9 of the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>On the Sabbath.</i> For פליא ביארי is one thing, פליית חויא another. Namely, the very thing that the <i>Arabs</i> call فالية الافاعي [fāliyat al-afāʿī] <i>phaliath alaphai</i>, that is, <i>the searcher of serpents</i>, or <i>of vipers</i> (as they have taught us above <sup>b)</sup> is the name of a spotted kind of beetle which usually lives with scorpions and serpents in the lair of the land crocodile), that very thing, I say, is the <i>Chaldee</i> פליית חויא. For to them חויא is <i>serpent</i>, and פליית <i>paljath</i>, or <i>phaljath</i>, is plainly derived from the <i>Arabic</i> فالية [fāliya] <i>phaliath</i>, which we render <i>searcher</i>, because فلي [falā] <i>phala</i> means <i>to search</i>. Thus فلي الراس [falā al-raʾs] <i>he searched the head</i>, in order to look for lice in it; [col. 1065] فلي الشعر [falā al-shiʿr] <i>he searched the poem</i>, that is, he explored and examined it so as to penetrate the <i>poet's</i> mind. And this kind of beetle is rightly said to <i>search</i> serpents, since it lives continually with them in the same lair, and so constantly accompanies the serpents, whether coming or going, so that by its arrival or its departure it signals that they are near or absent. Hence the proverb current among the <i>Arabs</i>, اتكلم فالية الافاعي [a-takallama fāliyat al-afāʿī] <i>Have you spoken with</i> phaliath alaphai, <i>the searcher of serpents?</i> This is used لاول شر ينتظر بعده شر منه [li-awwal sharr yuntaẓar baʿdahu sharr minhu] <i>of a first evil after which another is expected to arise from it.</i> But since the verb فلي [falā] <i>phala</i> means not only <i>to search</i> but also <i>to nourish</i> and <i>to rear</i>, <i>Jonathan</i>, instead of פליית חויא, has מינקת חויא, i.e. <i>nurse of the serpent.</i> Further, why the two <i>Chaldee</i> translators wished this kind of beetle to be denoted by the word אנקה <i>anaka</i>, I see no reason except the very order of the words. Namely, as the word צב <i>tsab</i> signifies the <i>land crocodile</i>, as we have proved at length <sup>c)</sup>, so the following אנקה <i>anaka</i> seemed to signify פליית חויא, that is, <i>the searcher of the serpent</i>, which is the constant attendant of the land crocodile and usually lives in the same lair with it. But this reason, such as it is, carries little weight with me, as does the version itself, which seems to rest on it. Something I had nearly omitted: to some of the <i>Arabs</i> these little creatures are not beetles but بنات وردان [banāt wardān] <i>benath vvardan</i>, the name by which <i>Abenbitar</i> [=Ibn al-Bayṭār] calls the σίλφας [silphas] of <i>Dioscorides</i>, or <i>blattae</i> [cockroaches].

‎In the third place I count those for whom אנקה <i>anaka</i> is a kind of <i>lizard</i>, who are neither few nor to be despised. For in the <i>Syriac</i> we find ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] <i>amaktha</i>, or <i>amkatha</i>, which the most learned of the <i>Maronites</i>, the late <i>Gabriel Sionita</i> [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)] (ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs]), explained as <i>lizard</i>. In the <i>Samaritan</i> [?] <i>semamitha</i>, that is, <i>stellio</i>, as will become clear in its place. In <i>Erpenius' Arabic</i> الورل [al-waral] <i>alvvarlo</i>, that is, a species of lizard which the barbarians call <i>guaril.</i> In the <i>Arabic</i> edited at <i>Paris</i>, الورك [al-wark] <i>alvvarco</i>, which is the same.

‎[II, 489] is. For by what chance I do not know, in <i>Avicenna</i> and others it is much more often written الورك [al-wark] <i>alvvarco</i>, although the true spelling is الورل [al-waral] <i>alvvarlo</i>. This is taught by all the <i>Arabic lexicographers</i>, and the barbarous name <i>guaril</i> is derived from it. Not to mention the critical observation found in <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter ورل [waral] <i>Guaril</i>: لا تلتقي الراء مع اللام الا في اربع مواضع الورل وهو الحيوان المذكور وارل اسم جبل والغرلة وهو القلفة وحرل وهو ضرب من الحجارة [lā taltaqī al-rāʾ maʿa al-lām illā fī arbaʿ mawāḍiʿ al-waral wa-huwa al-ḥayawān al-madhkūr wa-Waral ism jabal wa-al-ghurla wa-hiya al-qulfa wa-ḥaral wa-huwa ḍarb min al-ḥijāra]. <i>Re does not occur together with</i> Lam (that is, R with L) <i>except in four places.</i> 1. <i>In</i> alvvarlo, <i>the name of the animal discussed above.</i> 2. <i>In</i> Aral, <i>the name of a mountain, discussed above.</i> 3. <i>In</i> alghorla, <i>which means</i> foreskin (like the <i>Hebrew</i> ערלה). 4. <i>In</i> charal, <i>which is a kind of stone.</i> These, then, are the four ancient versions, the <i>Syriac</i>, the <i>Samaritan</i> and the two <i>Arabic</i>, for which the <i>anaka</i> is a species of <i>lizard</i>. I have weighty reasons for adding my vote to theirs. 1. Because צב <i>tsab</i>, that is, the <i>land crocodile</i>, had preceded, and four other species of lizards are immediately added [col. 1066], as will be clear from what follows. Hence the <i>anaka</i> seems to belong to the same genus. For the <i>mygale</i>, or any other animal, would have been inserted in a less fitting place among so many species of lizards. 2. From the <i>Hebrew</i> אנקה <i>anaka</i> the <i>Syriac</i> ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] <i>amaktha</i>, or <i>amkatha</i>, has plainly arisen. For these two words (apart from the ending, which is <i>Hebrew</i> in the one and <i>Syriac</i> in the other) differ in no respect, except that the <i>Syrians</i> changed the <i>Hebrew</i> NUN into MEM, and nothing is more frequent in those languages than this change. Thus among the <i>Chaldeans</i> or <i>Syrians</i> "strong" is ܚܣܝܢ [חסין] <i>chasin</i> and ܚܣܝܡ [חסים] <i>chasim</i>; "reason" ܛܥܢܐ [טענא] <i>taana</i> and ܛܥܡܐ [טעמא] <i>taama</i>; "nymph" ܢܝܢܦܐ [נינפא] <i>ninphe</i> and ܢܝܡܦܐ [נימפא] <i>nimphe</i>; "club" ܩܘܪܢܣܐ [קורנסא] <i>kurnesa</i> and ܩܘܪܡܙܐ [קורמזא] <i>kurmeza</i>; the terebinth, or the tree that bears the pistachio, ܒܘܛܡܐ [בוטמא] <i>butem</i>, as in <i>Hebrew</i> בוטן <i>boten</i>. And in <i>Arabic</i> "thumb" is ابهام [ibhām] <i>ibham</i> for the <i>Hebrew</i> בהן <i>bohen</i>; as "to grow fat" is دسم [dasama] <i>dasama</i> for the <i>Hebrew</i> דשן <i>dasen</i>; and "to sing" is رنم [ranima] <i>ranima</i> for the <i>Hebrew</i> רנן <i>ranan</i>. In <i>Ethiopic</i> ዘመወ፡ zamavva, <i>he committed fornication</i>, for the <i>Hebrew</i> ונה <i>zana</i>; እድሜ፡ edem, <i>time</i>, for the <i>Chaldee</i> עדן <i>iddan</i>; አደመ፡ adam, <i>pleasant</i>, <i>sweet</i>, from the <i>Hebrew</i> עדן adan, <i>to delight oneself</i>. Such is the <i>Egyptian</i> ΜΕΤΩΠΟΝ, a kind of <i>ointment</i>, from the <i>Hebrew</i> נטף <i>nataph</i> or the <i>Chaldee</i> נטופא <i>netopha</i>; ΜΕΙΣΙ, <i>serpent</i>, from the <i>Hebrew</i> נחש <i>naas</i>. So too the <i>Egyptian</i> city of <i>Memphis</i> is called by the <i>Prophets</i> now נוף <i>Noph</i>, now מוף <i>Moph</i>. By the same pattern the <i>Syriac</i> ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] <i>amaktha</i> here is derived from the <i>Hebrew</i> אנקה <i>anaka</i>, with N and M interchanged. Therefore, whatever the <i>Syriac amaktha</i> may be, it is highly probable that the <i>Hebrew anaka</i> is the very same. Now it is certain that ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] is a kind of <i>lizard</i>, namely the <i>stellio</i>, <i>Prov. XXX</i>, 28, where it stands in the <i>Syriac</i> [II, 490] Paraphrase for the <i>Hebrew</i> שממית <i>semamith</i>. Likewise in the <i>Chaldee</i>, by a μετάθεσιν [metathesin] of letters, אקמתא <i>akmetha</i>, which is the same thing. Therefore, just as the <i>Syriac</i> ܐܡܩܬܐ [אמקתא] <i>amaktha</i>, or <i>amkatha</i>, and the <i>Chaldee</i> אקמתא <i>akmetha</i> is the <i>stellio</i>, so also is the <i>Hebrew</i> אנקה <i>anaka</i>. Hence it is that the <i>Samaritan</i> too renders אנקה <i>anaka</i> by * [?] <i>semamitha</i>, that is, <i>stellio</i>; and both <i>Arabic</i> versions by الورل [al-waral] <i>alvvarlo</i>, or الورك [al-wark] <i>alvvarco</i>, a kind of lizard close to the stellio, which many therefore take for the <i>stellio</i>, because it is similar in form. Accordingly <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] says of it: هو العظيم من شكل الوزغ وسوام ابرص [huwa al-ʿaẓīm min shakl al-wazagh wa-sawām abraṣ] <i>This is large, of the same form as the</i> guezga <i>and the</i> savvamabras, that is, <i>as the smaller and larger stellios.</i> This is taken from <i>Avicenna</i>. And <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: الورل دابة كالضب والعظيم من اشكال الوزغ [al-waral dābba ka-al-ḍabb wa-al-ʿaẓīm min ashkāl al-wazagh] <i>Alvvarlo</i> (commonly <i>guaril</i>), <i>an animal like the</i> dhab (or <i>land crocodile</i>). <i>And it is large among those which have the form of the</i> guezga, or <i>stellio.</i> 3. In Hebrew אנקה <i>anaka</i> means a <i>groan</i> and a <i>shrill cry</i>, such as is uttered in great mourning. Should anyone deny that the <i>stellio</i> can be so named, as if it were a mute animal, he will be refuted by these words of <i>Pliny</i> on the stellio of <i>Greece</i>, book 29, chapter 4: <i>This the</i> Greeks <i>call</i> colotes, <i>and</i> ascalabotes, <i>and</i> galeotes. <i>It does not occur in</i> Italy. <i>For it is full of freckles, has a harsh shrill cry</i>, <i>and * feeds. All of which are foreign to our stellios.</i> Note <i>has a harsh shrill cry.</i> In Greek you would say τρισμοῦ τραχέος [trismou tracheos], whence it is rightly called אנקה <i>anaka</i>. And just as among the <i>Hebrews</i> a masculine plural is often formed from a feminine singular, as [col. 1067] יעלים from יעלה, and יענים from יענה, so from אנקה <i>anaka</i> the plural could be אנקים. And in the second <i>Targum of Esther</i>, chapter 1, verse 2, where, among the various animals that each utter their own cry before <i>Solomon</i>, אנקין בכיין <i>anakin weeping</i>, or <i>wailing</i>, are described, this should perhaps be understood not (as it is commonly taken) of the screaming of eagles, but of the cry and harsh shrill noise of stellios, which the very word אנקה <i>anaka</i> attests comes close to wailing. And for this very reason the <i>Arabs</i>, knowing at least this much, that the <i>anaka</i> was a kind of lizard, rendered it الورل [al-waral] <i>alvvarlo</i>, which itself also has a voice, though perhaps less sonorous. Hence <i>Alcamus</i>: ضغث الورل صوت [ḍaghatha al-waral ṣawwata] <i>Dhagathsa alvvarlo</i>, that is, <i>it cried out.</i> Those of our scholars who have published <i>Arabic lexicons</i> did not understand this sufficiently. For one rendered it <i>The thigh gave a crack</i>, another <i>The hip sounded</i>, because they read الورك [al-wark] <i>alvvarco</i>, which means ירך, i.e. <i>hip</i>. But they should have remembered that this very الورك [al-wark] occurs everywhere for الورل [al-waral], as we noted above <sup>d)</sup>. Not to mention that in the copy of τοῦ [tou] <i>Alcamus</i> which I used, الورل [al-waral] <i>alvvarlo</i> is quite correctly written. Therefore, what has hitherto escaped our scholars, let this stand as [II, 491] established: in the genus of lizards some are vocal, and among them is the stellio, which shrieks most shrilly. But we shall say more about the stellio in explaining the word שממית <i>semamith</i>.

‎To the three interpretations of this word that we have adduced, three others may be added, in which not even a drop of good sense appears. The first is that of the <i>Arabic</i> paraphrasts, whom I have read in manuscript, where אנקה <i>anaka</i> is عنكبوت [ʿankabūt] <i>ancabuths</i>, that is, <i>aranea</i> [spider]. This <i>aranea</i> perhaps arose from the <i>mygale</i> of the <i>Greeks</i> and of Jerome, or <i>mus araneus</i> [shrew-mouse]: as though the shrew-mouse were the same as the spider, which would be the height of ignorance; or from the <i>Syriac</i> ܐܡܟܬܐ [אמכתא] <i>amkatha</i>, since most of the <i>Hebrews</i> take the <i>Chaldee</i> אקמתא <i>akmetha</i>, which is synonymous with it, for the <i>spider</i>. But for the <i>Hebrews</i> the <i>spider</i> is not אנקה <i>anaka</i> but עכביש <i>accabis</i>. Nor is it at all likely that the <i>Prophet</i> mentions the <i>spider</i> here among the species of <i>lizards</i>.

‎The second interpretation is that of <i>R. Mardochai</i> in the <i>Hebrew Concordance</i>, where he defines this animal as שרץ העוף, a <i>flying creeping thing</i>, although <i>Moses</i> expressly places it <i>among the creeping things that creep upon the earth</i>, Lev 11:29–30.

‎The third is that of <i>R. Solomon</i>, who rendered it with the <i>French</i> word בברא <i>bieure</i>, that is, <i>fiber</i> or <i>castor</i> [beaver], as it is in the <i>Pomarium</i>: an animal so amphibious that it is rather aquatic. For it has the tail of a fish, and feet fitted for swimming rather than for walking, and it lives on the banks of rivers, from which it seeks its food. <i>Dioscorides</i>: Ζῶόν ἐστιν ἀμφίβιον, τὸ πλεῖστον ἐν ὕδασι σὺν ἰχθύσι, καὶ καρκίνοις τρεφόμενον [Zōon estin amphibion, to pleiston en hydasi syn ichthysi, kai karkinois trephomenon]; <i>It is an amphibious animal, mostly feeding in the waters on fish and crabs.</i> Alkazuini: القندر بري بحري يكون في الانهار العظيمة من بلاد ايسو يتخذ في البر بيتا الي جانب النهر [al-qundur barrī baḥrī yakūn fī al-anhār al-ʿaẓīma min bilād Īsū yattakhidh fī al-barr baytan ilā jānib al-nahr] <i>Alkundur</i> (that is, the <i>beaver</i>) <i>is an amphibious animal. It is found in the great rivers of the region of</i> Aisu, <i>and chooses a home for itself on the river bank.</i> Accordingly <i>Varro</i> and <i>Festus</i> hold that it is called <i>fiber</i> from the outermost edges of rivers. For the ancients said <i>fibrum</i> for <i>extremum</i> [outermost]. Hence the <i>fibrae</i> [lobes] of the liver and the <i>fimbriae</i> [fringes] of garments were so named. Who then would believe that <i>Moses</i> listed such an animal among the creeping things of the [col. 1068] earth? Especially since the proportion is very unequal. For here <i>Moses</i> calls moles, mice and kinds of lizards <i>creeping things</i>. Moreover, there is no reason why the <i>beaver</i> should be called אנקה <i>anaka</i> from crying and wailing, since the voice of the beaver has nothing remarkable about it, so far as I know. But the first author of this opinion, whoever he was, seems to have confused <i>castores</i> and <i>castorides</i>: these are said to be marine animals of so horrible a wailing that whoever hears it soon dies. Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 9, chapter 50: Αἱ καστορίδες ζῶόν εἰσι θαλάττιον, καὶ ἐπὶ ταῖς ἀκταῖς, καὶ ταῖς πέτραις ταῖς προβεβλημέναις, ἀπόΦημόν [II, 492] τινα κωκυτὸν μεθιᾶσι, καὶ ὠρύονται βαρύτατα. Τούτου τοίνυν τοῦ ἤχου ὅστις ἂν ὑπακούσῃ, ἄφυκτα οἵ ἐστι, καὶ οὐ μετὰ μικρὸν ἀποθνήσκει [Hai kastorides zōon eisi thalattion, kai epi tais aktais, kai tais petrais tais probeblēmenais, apophēmon tina kōkyton methiasi, kai ōryontai barytata. Toutou toinyn tou ēchou hostis an hypakousē, aphykta hoi esti, kai ou meta mikron apothnēskei]; <i>The castorides, a marine animal, utter an ill-omened wail on the shores and the jutting rocks, and roar most heavily. So that whoever hears their cry cannot escape departing from life shortly afterwards.</i> Oppian, <i>Halieutica</i>, book 1, v. 398 ff.

‎Καστορίδες τ᾽ ὀλοαὶ δυσπενθέες, αἵ τ᾽ ἀλεγεινὴν Ὅσσαν ἐπὶ κροκαλοίσιον ἀπαίσιον ὠρύονται Ἀνδράσιν· ὃς δέ κε γῆρυν ἐν οὔασιν ἀλγινόεσσαν Δέξηται, στυγερῆς τ᾽ ἐνοπῆς κωκυτὸν ἀκούσῃ, Οὐ τηλοῦ θανάτοιο τάχ᾽ ἔσσεται· ἀλλὰ οἱ ἄτην Καὶ μόρον αἰνοτάτη κείνη μαντεύεται αὐδή. [Kastorides t᾽ oloai dyspenthees, hai t᾽ alegeinēn Ossan epi krokaloision apaision ōryontai Andrasin; hos de ke gēryn en ousin alginoessan Dexētai, stygerēs t᾽ enopēs kōkyton akousē, Ou tēlou thanatoio tach᾽ essetai; alla hoi atēn Kai moron ainotatē keinē manteuetai audē.]

‎<i>And the deadly castorides of grievous mourning, which on the shores utter a sad voice, ill-omened for men. Whoever receives the doleful cry in his ears and hears the wailing of the plaintive voice will not be far from death: that most grievous voice foretells ruin and death for him.</i> Tzetzes, <i>Chiliad</i> 6, History 47:

‎Ἡ καστορὶς δ᾽ ὠρύεται, ὃς δ᾽ ἂν ἀκούσῃ ταύτης Χρόνῳ βραχεῖ παρέρχεται τοῦ ταλαιπώρου βίου· [Hē kastoris d᾽ ōryetai, hos d᾽ an akousē tautēs Chronō brachei parerchetai tou talaipōrou biou;]

‎<i>And the castoris roars, and whoever hears it will shortly depart from this wretched life.</i> This is what I find about the wailing and roaring of the castorides. But those are mistaken who take the <i>castoris</i> for the <i>fiber</i> or <i>castor</i> [beaver], among them the most learned <i>Rittershusius</i> in his <i>Notes</i> on Oppian. For the <i>castor</i> is one thing, the <i>castoris</i> another. The former is an amphibious quadruped, the latter is a fish. <i>Tzetzes</i>, in the place cited above:

‎Πάντες δ᾽ ἰχθύες ἄφωνοι, πλὴν σκάρου, καὶ καστορίδος· [Pantes d᾽ ichthyes aphōnoi, plēn skarou, kai kastoridos;]

‎<i>But all fishes are mute, except the scarus and the castoris.</i> The former is described on the banks of rivers, the latter around the shores of the sea, and it is counted by Oppian among those sea monsters that sometimes come out of the sea onto dry land; whereas the beaver, on the contrary, goes from land into rivers. Finally, the beaver is a real animal; the castoris is fabulous, as I at least think, since I know of no animal in the sea to which this description fits.

‎Among the more recent writers, אנקה <i>anaka</i> is, for <i>Pagnino</i>, who followed the Greeks and Jerome, the <i>mygale</i>; for Deodati the <i>mus araneus</i> [shrew-mouse]; for <i>Münster</i> and the Genevans the <i>ericius</i> [hedgehog], as if on the authority of Onkelos, whose Paraphrase is corrupt here; for <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Buxtorf the <i>attelabus</i>, as if on the authority of the <i>Jerusalem</i> translator, whom they had not sufficiently understood; for <i>Castellio</i> the <i>fiber</i> [beaver], on the authority of <i>Rabbi Solomon</i>; for Arias the <i>viverra</i> [ferret], as if from the same source. But Solomon's ב״ברא should not have been read as <i>viverra</i>, since <i>viverra</i> is a <i>Roman</i> word, whereas ב״ברא is purely <i>French</i>. [II, 493] For <i>Rabbi Solomon</i> was a <i>Frenchman</i>, and he interprets <i>Hebrew</i> words only by <i>French</i> ones. Yet from the same source the <i>English</i> too have here <i>a ferret</i>, that is, <i>furo</i>, an animal similar to the viverra. But it is no wonder that so many translators went astray, since they had seen neither the <i>Syriac</i> nor the <i>Samaritan</i> paraphrase, nor those two <i>Arabic</i> ones, from which we have extracted the true meaning of the word אנקה <i>anaka</i>.
