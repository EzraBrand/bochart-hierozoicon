# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 20: The peacocks (vol. 2, pp. 709–720)

### ‎[II, 709] [col. 135] Chapter 20.

### ‎<i>On Peacocks.</i>

‎There is a notable passage on the peacock in <i>1 Reg. X, 22.</i> and <i>2 Chr 9:21</i>: <i>Once every three</i> [col. 136] <i>years the fleet</i> (of Solomon) <i>went to</i> Tarshish, <i>and brought gold and silver, elephants' teeth, apes and peacocks.</i> The Hebrew name of the <i>peacocks</i>, תכיים <i>thoucijim</i>, or תוכיים <i>thuccijim</i>, the <i>Greek</i> translators left ἀνεϱμήνευτον [anermēneuton] [untranslated], because they did not understand it. But the <i>Chaldee</i>, the <i>Syriac</i>, the <i>Arabic</i> and the <i>Latin</i> translators explain it as <i>peacocks</i>, and so do all the <i>Hebrews</i> of any repute: <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], <i>R. Levi</i>, <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], <i>Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)].</i> For those who, in <i>Kimchi</i>'s <i>Lexicon</i>, render it גאטו מימון <i>gato maimon</i>, that is, <i>long-tailed monkey</i>, are obscure and unknown. So too are those who render it <i>parrot</i>, as <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] notes in the margin <sup>R1</sup>. Therefore I cannot at all understand why the late <i>Diodati</i> preferred to follow them. It is hardly safe to depart from the received version unless there are weighty reasons, and I see none here. On the origin of the word, <i>Mercerus</i> [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] says in his <i>Thesaurus</i>: <i>The word seems foreign, for its etymology is unknown. Perhaps it is from striking, by onomatopoeia.</i> The French, to be sure, express onomatopoeically the sound of someone knocking at a door with the words <i>toc, toc</i>; hence <i>toquer</i> means <i>to strike</i>, as does <i>toccare</i> in Italian. But since such things are largely arbitrary: among the <i>Hebrews</i> תוך תוך <i>thoc thoc</i> is said of the sound of a boiling pot. Thus in <i>Berachoth</i> ch. 6, fol. 39a: דיתיב אבי תפי ועביד תוך תוך, <i>That which is placed in the place of setting</i> (of pots on the fire), <i>and makes thoc thoc.</i> But however you understand it, this has nothing to do with the peacock. Nor can any reason be devised why it should be named from striking. I therefore hold a different view: namely, that peacocks are called תוכיים <i>thuccijim</i> by an inversion of the word, for כותיים <i>cuthijim</i>, as if you were to say <i>Cuthaeans</i>, or <i>Persians</i>. For <i>Cuth</i> (var. <i>Chuth</i>) is in <i>Persia</i>. <i>Josephus</i>, <i>Antiquities</i>, book 9, near the end: Οἱ δὲ μετοικισθέντες εἰς Σαμάρειαν Χουθαῖοι (ταύτῃ γὰρ ἐχρῶντο μέχρι δεῦρο τῇ προσηγορίᾳ, διὰ τὸ ἐκ τῆς Χουθᾶς καλουμένης χώρας μεταχθῆναι, αὕτη δ᾽ ἐστὶν ἐν τῇ Περσίδι, καὶ ποταμὸς τοῦ τ᾽ ἔχων ὄνομα) [Hoi de metoikisthentes eis Samareian Chouthaioi (tautē gar echrōnto mechri deuro tē prosēgoria, dia to ek tēs Chouthas kaloumenēs chōras metachthēnai, hautē d᾽ estin en tē Persidi, kai potamos tou t᾽ echōn onoma)]. <i>The</i> Cuthaeans <i>deported into</i> Samaria (<i>for so they were then called, because they had been transferred from the region of</i> Chutha, <i>which is in</i> Persia, <i>where there is also a river of the same</i> [II, 710] <i>name</i>). Nor is it anything new for birds to be named after places. Thus the <i>pheasant</i> is named from the <i>Phasis</i>; <i>African</i> and <i>Numidian</i> birds from <i>Numidia</i> and <i>Africa</i>; <i>Seleucides</i> from <i>Seleucia</i>; <i>Garamantides</i> from the <i>Garamantes</i>. And, not to seek examples further afield, the very peacock we are discussing is often called the <i>Median</i> or <i>Persian</i> bird by the ancients. <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Birds</i>:

‎Ὁ μὲν ὄρτυγα δοὺς, ὁ δὲ πορφυρίων᾽, ὁ δὲ χῆν᾽, ὁ δὲ Περσικὸν ὄρνιν᾽ [Ho men ortyga dous, ho de porphyriōn᾽, ho de chēn᾽, ho de Persikon ornin᾽]

‎<i>One giving a quail, another a porphyrion, another a goose, another a</i> Persian <i>bird.</i> And there the <i>Scholiast</i>: Τὰ πολυτελῆ πάντα, οἷς μόνος βασιλεὺς ἐχρῆτο, ἐκαλεῖτο Περσικά· καὶ νῦν οὐκ ἰδίως τις ὄρνις Περσικός. τινὲς δὲ τὸν ἀλεκτρυόνα, οἱ δὲ τὸν ταῶ [Ta polytelē panta, hois monos basileus echrēto, ekaleito Persika; kai nyn ouk idiōs tis ornis Persikos. tines de ton alektryona, hoi de ton taō]. <i>All costly things, which the king alone used, were called</i> Persian. <i>Some, however, understand the cock, and others the peacock.</i> And in the <i>Acharnians</i>, of the <i>Persian</i> envoys:

‎Ἥκοντες ἀπὸ Περσίδος ταὼν ἔχοντες ἐληλύθασιν, [Hēkontes apo Persidos taōn echontes elēlythasin,]

‎<i>when they had set out from</i> Persia, <i>they had come bringing a peacock.</i> Suidas: Μηδικὸς ὄρνις· ὁ ταώς [Mēdikos ornis; ho taōs]· <i>The Median bird, the peacock.</i> Likewise: Ταὼς εὐπήληξ· ὁ Μηδικὸς καὶ χρυσόπτερος, καὶ ἀλαζονικὸς ὄρνις [Taōs eupēlēx; ho Mēdikos kai chrysopteros, kai alazonikos ornis]. <i>The well-helmeted peacock, the</i> Median <i>bird, furnished with golden wings, and proud.</i> Hence <i>Clement, Paedag.</i> book 2, ch. 1: Ὄρνεις ἐπὶ τούτοις συνωνοῦνται τοὺς ἀπὸ Φάσιδος, ἀτταγὰς Αἰγυπτίας, Μῆδον ταῶνα [Orneis epi toutois synōnountai tous apo Phasidos, attagas Aigyptias, Mēdon taōna] [col. 137]· <i>Besides, they buy up birds from</i> the Phasis, Egyptian <i>francolins</i>, the Median <i>peacock.</i> And book 3, ch. 4: Ὄρνεις Ἰνδικοὺς, καὶ ταῶνας Μηδικοὺς ἐκτρέφουσι [Orneis Indikous, kai taōnas Mēdikous ektrephousi]· <i>They rear</i> Indian <i>birds and</i> Median <i>peacocks.</i> And <i>Diodorus</i>, book 2: Ἡ μὲν γὰρ Βαβυλωνία ταώνων ἐκτρέφει πλῆθος παντοίαις χρόαις ἐπηνθισμένων [Hē men gar Babylōnia taōnōn ektrephei plēthos pantoiais chroais epēnthismenōn]. <i>For</i> Babylonia <i>rears a great many peacocks, variegated with colours of every kind.</i> For although the true homeland of peacocks was <i>India</i>, as we prove elsewhere, they were nevertheless called <i>Median</i> and <i>Persian</i> because they were first carried from <i>India</i> into <i>Persia</i> and <i>Media</i>, and from there gradually dispersed into <i>Judaea, Egypt, Greece, Latium</i> and other regions of the world. Indeed, the very peacocks which <i>Solomon</i>'s fleet brought into <i>Judaea</i> may have been obtained from <i>Persia</i>. For it is not likely that on that three-year voyage, by which they made their way to <i>Taprobane</i> (as we show in its place), they always kept a straight course. Rather, by various detours they sought out whatever served their purpose: also in the <i>Persian</i> Gulf, where lay the most celebrated islands of the <i>Phoenicians</i>, <i>Tyrus</i> or <i>Tylus</i>, and <i>Aradus</i>, not far distant from <i>Persia</i>. The name תוכיים <i>thuccijim</i> could also have been given to <i>peacocks</i> after <i>Solomon</i>'s time, once they began to be brought overland from <i>Persia</i> and <i>Babylon</i> into <i>Judaea</i>.

‎Now, that they were called תוכיים with the letters inverted, instead of כותיים, is not without precedent, whether this happened through the carelessness of copyists or, as I believe more likely, was done by deliberate [II, 711] design, so that the name of the bird might be distinguished from the name of the people. Thus you may see the same wood called אלמוגים <i>almugin</i>, 1 Reg. X, 11. and אלגומים <i>algumim</i>, 2 Chr 2:7. The same city in which <i>Joshua</i> was buried is called תמנת־סרח <i>Thimnath-Serach</i>, Josh 24:30 and תמנת־חרס <i>Thimnath-Cheres</i>, Judg 2:9. The same son of <i>Dan</i> is called חושים <i>Chusim</i>, <i>Gen 46:23</i> and שוחם <i>Sucham</i>, Num 26:42. The same man is רחום <i>Rechum</i>, Neh 12:3 and חרם <i>Charim</i>, in the same place, verse 15. Another is שמלי <i>Samlai</i>, Esd. II, 46. and שלמי <i>Salmai</i>, Neh 7:48. So the <i>sheep</i> is called now כבס <i>chebes</i>, now כשב <i>cheseb</i>. And the <i>Ethiopians</i> sometimes invert <i>Hebrew</i> words entirely. Thus רחם, <i>to have pity</i>, is for them ምሕረ፡ [məḥəra]; קלס, <i>to mock</i>, for them ሰለቀ፡ [salaqa]; זנק, <i>to leap</i>, for them ቀነጸ፡ [qanaṣa]. Therefore, on the same pattern, <i>peacocks</i> could have been called תוכיים instead of כותיים. However, I propose this, I do not decide it, and I leave everyone free to judge for himself.

‎If the peacock were unknown, we would ask what moved <i>Solomon</i> to fetch from so far away, along with treasures of gold and silver, birds of no great use. Or why the sacred writers thought it worth recording? But these doubts are resolved by the appearance of this exceptional bird, which seems made for the very purpose of feasting the eyes of beholders with a delightful spectacle. Accordingly <i>Varro</i> asserts that nature gave it the prize for beauty among birds. And after the <i>Greeks</i>, who vied with one another in describing it, <i>Tertullian</i> paints it thus, <i>De Pallio</i> ch. 3: <i>And yet the peacock too has a garment of feathers, and indeed one of the finest: deeper than any purple where the neck blooms, more gilded than any border where the back gleams, more flowing than any trailing robe where the tail lies. Many-coloured, and diverse-coloured, and changing-coloured. Never itself, always other; and yet always itself when other. In short, changed as often as it is moved.</i> The <i>Arabs</i> too have toiled in the same arena, among them <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: تري في وسط كل ريش دايرة من الذهب مخلطة بالزرقة والصفرة والخضرة وغيرها من الاوان التي [tarī fī wasaṭ kull rīsh dāyira min al-dhahab mukhallaṭa bi-l-zurqa wa-l-ṣufra wa-l-khuḍra wa-ghayrihā min al-awān allatī] [col. 138] تلايم بعضها بعضا لينشا من تركيبها [talāyam baʿḍuhā baʿḍan li-yansha min tarkībihā] <i>You will see in the middle of all its feathers a golden circle, mixed with blue, yellow and green, and other colours which harmonize with one another, so that from their combination the highest beauty arises.</i> He adds that gold, laid over any colours whatever, is nothing compared with <i>this</i> splendour, although it is scarcely dug out of the earth and only refined with much labour; whereas so beautiful a bird emerges from the egg of its own accord, without human effort. <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] has much more, which I have preferred to pass over, lest I weary the reader on a subject most hackneyed of all. So for the present it will suffice to add a few verses from an anonymous <i>Arabic poet</i>: [II, 712] سبحان من خلق الطاوس [subḥān man khalaqa al-ṭāwūs] * طير علي اشكاله رييس [ṭayr ʿalā ashkālihi rayīs] * كانه من نقشه عروس [ka-annahu min naqshihi ʿarūs] * في الريش منه ركبت فلوس [fī al-rīsh minhu rukkibat fulūs] * تشرق في داراته شموس [tushriqu fī dārātihi shumūs] * في راس منه شجرة مغروس [fī raʾs minhu shajara maghrūs] * كانه ببنفسج يميس [ka-annahu bi-banafsaj yamīs] *

‎<i>Glory be to him who created the peacock, a bird of exceptional beauty, which in the variety of its colours resembles a bride. Scales are set into its feathers. In its dwelling many suns rise. On its crown a little tree</i> <sup>a)</sup> <i>is planted. It struts in pomp as though in a violet garment.</i> In Arabic, <i>as though in a violet.</i> But there <i>violet</i> is taken for <i>a garment of violet</i>, or <i>purple colour</i>, which stands out most among the other colours in the neck and tail of the peacock. Hence <i>Tertullian</i> above: <i>Deeper than any purple dye, where the neck blooms.</i> And <i>Achilles Tatius</i>, book 1: Ὁ δὲ τοῦ ταῶ λειμὼν εὐανθέστερος· πεφύτευται γὰρ αὐτῷ καὶ χρυσὸς ἐν τοῖς πτεροῖς, κύκλῳ δὲ τὸ ἁλουργὲς τὸν χρυσὸν περιθέει τὸν ἴσον κύκλον [Ho de tou taō leimōn euanthesteros; pephyteutai gar autō kai chrysos en tois pterois, kyklō de to halourges ton chryson peritheei ton ison kyklon]. <i>The meadow of the peacock</i> (than any meadow) <i>is more flowery: for in its feathers gold also is set, and purple surrounds the gold in an equal circle.</i> To this belongs the description of the peacock by <i>Publilius Syrus</i>:

‎<i>Clad in a feathered golden</i> Babylonian <i>mantle.</i>

‎As though <i>Babylonian</i> were the same as <i>purple</i>, since <i>Arrian</i> in the <i>Periplus</i> mentions purple from <i>Apologus</i>, a city of <i>Babylonia</i>, at the mouth of the <i>Euphrates</i>. And <i>Philostratus</i>, Epistle 28, to a woman, praises τὸν Βαβυλώνιον κόκκον [ton Babylōnion kokkon]. Accordingly, אדרת שנער, <i>the mantle of Shinar</i>, or <i>Babylonian mantle</i>, Josh 7:21, is for <i>Rabbi Chanina</i> a <i>mantle</i> מן פרפרא בבלאה, <i>of Babylonian purple;</i> and for Jerome <i>scarlet.</i> But by <i>feathered Babylonian</i> we understand <i>variegated.</i> For, as we have proved elsewhere from <i>Pliny</i> and others, <i>to weave different colours into a picture</i> Babylon <i>most made famous and gave it its name.</i> We are led to this by these words of <i>Aelian</i> on the peacock, book 5, ch. 21: Δεικνὺς φιλοπόνως τὸ τῆς πτερώσεως πολύμορφον ὑπὲρ τὴν τῶν Μήδων ἐσθῆτα, καὶ τὰ τῶν Περσῶν ποικίλματα [Deiknys philoponōs to tēs pterōseōs polymorphon hyper tēn tōn Mēdōn esthēta, kai ta tōn Persōn poikilmata]; <i>Ostentatiously displaying the multiform appearance of its plumage, surpassing the garment of the</i> Medes <i>and the varied colours of the</i> Persians. Accordingly <i>Antiphon</i>, in his speech <i>On the Peacocks</i> against <i>Erasistratus</i>, cited by <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9, and by <i>Aelian</i> in the place above, nowhere calls peacocks by their proper name, that is, ταῶνας [taōnas], but only ὄρνεις ποικίλους [orneis poikilous], <i>variegated birds;</i> as if by this very feature they were most distinguished from others.

‎[II, 713] From this same speech, compared with the life of <i>Pericles</i> in <i>Plutarch</i>, it is gathered [col. 139] that around the time of <i>Pericles</i> peacocks began to be kept at <i>Athens</i> in the house of a certain <i>Demus</i>, son of <i>Pyrilampes</i>, who made great profit from it. For he exhibited them to be viewed for a fixed fee at every new moon. And to this spectacle the curious flocked from <i>Lacedaemon</i> and <i>Thessaly</i>, that is, from both extremities of <i>Greece</i>. He also sold a male and female of the peacock kind for a thousand drachmas. Indeed, in <i>Greece</i> peacocks were not yet common in the age of <i>Alexander</i>, who, on the testimony of <i>Aelian</i>, ἐν Ἰνδοῖς ἰδὼν τοὺς δὲ τοὺς ὄρνιθας ἐξεπλάγη, καὶ τοῦ κάλλους θαυμάσας, ἠπείλησε τῷ καταθύσαντι ταὼν ἀπειλὰς βαρυτάτας [en Indois idōn tous de tous ornithas exeplagē, kai tou kallous thaumasas, ēpeilēse tō katathysanti taōn apeilas barytatas], <i>seeing those birds among the</i> Indians <i>was astonished, and, admiring their beauty, fixed a heavy penalty for those who should kill them.</i> Likewise at <i>Rome Hortensius</i>, as the same author reports, καταθύσας ἐπὶ δείπνῳ ταὼν πρῶτος, ἐκρίθη [katathysas epi deipnō taōn prōtos, ekrithē], <i>having been the first to slaughter a peacock for a dinner, was summoned to court:</i> or, as others read, κατεκρίθη [katekrithē], <i>was condemned</i>, or <i>fined.</i> Yet, as luxury prevailed, many following <i>Hortensius</i> raised the prices of peacocks; so much so that in <i>Varro</i> Merula asserts that in his time their eggs sold for five denarii each, the birds themselves easily for fifty. And that <i>M. Aufidius Lurco</i> made an annual income of over sixty thousand sesterces from flocks of peacocks. For although the flesh of the peacock tastes no better than the flesh of a hen, the peacock was valued much more highly because of its tail and feathers: as if this concerned the palate, or as if they ate the feathers with the flesh. This folly of gluttons <i>Horace</i> wittily mocks in these words, book 2, <i>Satire</i> 2:

‎<i>Yet I shall hardly keep you, when a peacock is served, from wishing to wipe your palate with it rather than with a hen, corrupted as you are by empty things: because the rare bird is sold for gold and spreads a spectacle with its painted tail. As if that mattered at all. Do you eat that plumage you praise? Once it is cooked, does the same glory remain? Yet though in flesh there is no difference, it is plain that you are deceived by the unequal appearance into preferring this to that.</i> — —

‎For these dishes were valued not for their flavour but for their price; nor were they sought for any other reason than that they sold more dearly, like parrots brought from <i>India</i>. <i>Claudian</i>, <i>Against Eutropius</i>, book 2:

‎<i>Who whet the belly with price, and hand over to the palate the starry birds of</i> Juno, <i>and any green bird skilled in speech brought from the coloured</i> Indians.

‎What of the fact that in <i>Symeon Seth</i> [=Symeon Seth (11th c.), Syntagma de alimentorum facultatibus] the flesh of the peacock is ἰνώδης, καὶ περιττωματικὴ, καὶ δύσπεπτος, καὶ κακόχυμος [inōdēs, kai perittōmatikē, kai dyspeptos, kai kakochymos], <i>fibrous, productive of excrements, hard to digest, and of bad juice?</i> Just as in <i>Galen</i> it is σκληροτέρα, καὶ δυσπεπτοτέρα, καὶ ἰνωδεστέρα [sklērotera, kai dyspeptotera, kai inōdestera].

‎[II, 714] And in <i>Damir</i>: لحمه عسر الهضم ردي المزاج وهي يولد كيموسا غليظا [laḥmuhu ʿasir al-haḍm radī al-mizāj wa-hiya yuwallid kaymūsan ghalīẓan], <i>Its flesh is difficult to digest, of a bad temperament, and it produces a thick chyme.</i> In <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)]: لحوم الطواويس صلبة [luḥūm al-ṭawāwīs ṣulba], <i>The flesh of peacocks is hard.</i> In <i>Mesue</i>: ردي المزاج [radī al-mizāj], <i>of bad juice</i>; whence <i>Juvenal</i> in his first <i>Satire</i>:

‎— — <i>you carry the undigested peacock into the baths: hence sudden deaths, and an old age without a will.</i>

‎Therefore, apart from that exceptional beauty, there was no reason why the peacock was so highly valued among the ancients. Indeed, the <i>Arabs</i> imagine that the peacock, however beautiful, is nonetheless a bird of ill omen, because, as they tell the tale, كان سببا لدخول ابليس الجنة وخروج ادم منها [kāna sababan li-dukhūl Iblīs al-janna wa-khurūj Ādam minhā], <i>it was the reason why the devil entered Paradise, and why</i> Adam <i>went out of it</i>. But for what reason, I have not yet found. Of the same sort is what the anonymous author of the روضة العلما [Rawḍat al-ʿulamā], <i>Garden [col. 140] of the Learned</i>, reports: that the vine was sprinkled by the devil with the blood of four animals, namely of the peacock when it was first planted, of the ape when it began to put out leaves, of the lion when it pushed out its grapes, and finally of the pig when the fruit was already ripening. And that this is why whoever drinks wine 1. becomes more handsome, like the peacock; 2. plays, dances and gesticulates in the manner of an ape; 3. rages like a lion; 4. like a pig

‎— — <i>lies buried in sleep and wine.</i>

‎God's words in <i>Job 39:13</i>, כנף רננים נעלסה, are explained by most of the more recent writers, following <i>Kimchi</i> and <i>Pomarius</i>, as <i>The wing of the peacocks exults</i>, as though it dealt with peacocks, which take pride in the beauty of their feathers. Yet in that passage רננים are <i>ostriches</i>, not <i>peacocks</i>, as we prove at length in the treatise <i>On the Ostrich</i>; and those who interpret them as peacocks rest on no reasoning, although today no explanation of this word is more widely accepted.

‎No more acceptable is the view of the <i>Chaldee</i> translator, who held that in <i>Ezek 27:15</i> the <i>Hebrew</i> word הבנים <i>hobnim</i> means <i>peacocks</i>, although <i>R. Selomo</i> follows it, and the other <i>Hebrews</i> are in doubt, and among our own scholars <i>Pagninus</i> [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae] rendered the whole passage thus: <i>The sons</i> of Dedan <i>were thy merchants. Many isles were the merchandise of thy hand. They brought horns of ivory and peacocks as thy present.</i> The <i>Greeks</i> (whom the <i>Arab</i> follows) and the <i>Syrian</i> seem to have misread this same word. For the former render τοῖς εἰσαγομένοις [tois eisagomenois], <i>to those who are brought in</i>, that is, הובאים; the latter לבונתא, <i>frankincense</i>, which in <i>Hebrew</i> would be לבנה. Thus of the ancients only <i>Symmachus</i> and <i>Jerome</i> saw that הובנים <i>hobnim</i> is <i>ebony</i>. Many things confirm this interpretation. [II, 715] First, <i>hobnim</i> and <i>hebenus</i> are not dissimilar words. In the first syllable the vowels O and E have been interchanged, which everyone knows to be frequent. <i>Priscian</i>, book 1: <i>O passes into E, as tutor tutela, bonus bene,</i> γόνυ [gony] <i>genu</i>, ποῦς [pous] <i>pes. The ancients said</i> compes <i>for</i> compos. <i>In this we follow the</i> Aeolians, <i>for they say</i> ἐδόντα [edonta] <i>for</i> ὀδόντα [odonta]. <i>Quintilian</i>, book 1, ch. 7: <i>What shall I say of</i> vortices <i>and</i> vorsus, <i>and the rest of the same kind, which</i> Scipio Africanus <i>is said to have been the first to turn into the second letter, E.</i> Hence the same people are called Ὀσσηνοὶ [Ossēnoi] and Ἐσσηνοί [Essēnoi]. And <i>Ptolemy</i> speaks of the Ὁρκύνιος δρυμὸς [Horkynios drymos], which others call Ἑρκύνιος [Herkynios], that is, the <i>Hercynian forest</i>. Second, קרנות שן והבננים, <i>horns of ivory and ebony</i>, are rightly joined in the <i>Prophet</i> as wares in some way homogeneous, although the one is obtained from an animal, the other from a tree. For, as <i>Fuller</i> [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)] notes in his <i>Miscellanea</i>, book 6, ch. 14, each has an extreme colour in the same degree of excellence: ivory, namely, of the most beautiful whiteness, ebony of the most splendid blackness. Both are highly polished, very lustrous, and remarkable for an incomparable smoothness. Hence they are applied to almost the same uses; and from both are made bows, combs, gaming boards, knife handles, etc. Third, the words of <i>Ezekiel</i> could be rendered thus: <i>horns</i> of (elephant) <i>tooth and of ebony</i>, in the genitive, so that the word קרנות, <i>horns</i>, belongs to both. For as <i>ivory</i> is called <i>horn</i> because of its resemblance to true horn, ebony too could be called horn by the same καταχρήσει [katachrēsei], since <i>Dioscorides</i> describes it as ἐμφερὴς κέρατι εἰργασμένῳ τῇ λειότητι [empherēs kerati eirgasmenō tē leiotēti], <i>like polished horn in smoothness</i>; and <i>Isidore</i> as <i>a black wood, smooth and horn-like</i>; and <i>Avicenna</i> as يشبه في ملاسته قرنا محكوكا وقيل مخروطا [yushbihu fī malāsatihi qarnan maḥkūkan wa-qīla makhrūṭan] <i>resembling in its smoothness polished horn, or, as others say, horn worked smooth.</i> Ebony therefore could be called horn καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs]. I would not, however, call it a <i>tooth</i> with <i>Jerome</i>, who has here <i>teeth of ivory and of ebony</i>. Fourth, ivory and ebony originate in the same places. [col. 141] For just as <i>Polybius, Pliny, Philostratus</i>, and, in the <i>Scholiast on Pindar</i>, <i>Amyntianus</i> in his book <i>On Elephants</i> distinguish elephants into <i>Indian</i> and <i>Ethiopian</i>, so <i>Dioscorides, Pliny</i> and <i>Isidore</i> report that ebony grows in <i>India</i> and <i>Ethiopia</i>. And as <i>Theophrastus, Arrian</i> and <i>Ennodius</i> mention <i>Indian</i> ebony specifically, so <i>Herodotus, Diodorus, Mela, Avicenna</i> and the <i>Nubian Geographer</i> mention <i>Ethiopian</i>. Lest you object to them these words of the supreme poet in <i>Georgics</i> book 2,

‎— — — <i>India alone bears black ebony:</i> — — —

‎one must know that by the name <i>India</i> <i>Ethiopia</i> is also understood. Hence <i>Servius</i> on that passage: <i>By India we understand the whole region of</i> Ethiopia. That this is very true is shown by these words of the same <i>poet</i> on the <i>Source of the Nile</i> in <i>Georgics</i> book 4,

‎<i>The river flowing down all the way from the dark-coloured</i> Indians, [II, 716] that is, from innermost <i>Ethiopia</i>. There are some who add <i>Egyptian</i> ebony to the <i>Indian</i> and <i>Ethiopian</i>, as on the authority of <i>Lucan</i>, whose words in book 10 are:

‎— — — <i>Mareotic ebony</i> <i>does not cover the vast doorposts.</i> — — —

‎<i>Maereotic</i>, that is, from <i>Mareotis</i>, a part of <i>Egypt</i> near <i>Alexandria</i>, as they would have it. Consonant with them are these words of <i>Servius</i>: <i>And yet ebony also grows in</i> Egypt. But in both places there is an error. For in <i>Lucan</i> one must read <i>Meroitica</i>, as the distinguished <i>Salmasius</i> [=Claude Saumaise (1588–1653), Plinianae exercitationes] excellently proves in his work on <i>Solinus</i>; Meroë, which appears below in the same book of <i>Lucan</i>,

‎— — <i>Meroë, fertile with black settlers</i>, <i>rich in the foliage of ebony.</i> — — —

‎Accordingly <i>Diodorus</i>, book 1, where he speaks of the island of <i>Meroe</i>: Πρὸς δὲ τούτοις (λέγουσιν) ἔχειν πλῆθος ἐβένου [Pros de toutois (legousin) echein plēthos ebenou], <i>Moreover they say that it has an abundance of ebony.</i> And <i>Pliny</i> records that <i>the rare tree ebony</i> was brought to <i>Nero</i> (fetched) <i>from as far as</i> Meroe, <i>a distance of 996 miles from</i> Syene, <i>the boundary of the empire.</i> But in these words of <i>Servius</i> too, <i>And yet it grows also in</i> Egypt, there is an obvious error. For he had certainly written, <i>And yet it grows also in</i> Ethiopia, as what follows shows: <i>But by</i> India <i>we understand the whole region of</i> Ethiopia. This would be ἀνακόλουθα [anakoloutha] if we read <i>in Egypt.</i> For <i>Servius</i> says this on the passage of <i>Virgil</i> discussed above,

‎— — — <i>India alone bears</i> <i>black ebony.</i> — — —

‎which he maintains is true, even though ebony also grows in <i>Ethiopia</i>, because the name of <i>India</i> includes <i>Ethiopia</i>. But if you say that ebony grows in <i>Egypt</i>, this excuse will not hold. For <i>Egypt</i> is one thing, <i>Ethiopia</i> another; nor is there anyone who understands <i>Egypt</i> by the name of <i>India</i>. Fifth, ebony and ivory, together with gold, were the constant tribute of the <i>Ethiopians</i>. Hence in <i>Diodorus</i>, book 1, p. 35, when <i>Sesoosis</i> (var. <i>Sesostres</i>), the most ancient king of the <i>Egyptians</i>, had subdued the <i>Ethiopians</i>, ἠνάγκασε τὸ ἔθνος φόρους τελεῖν, ἔβενον, καὶ χρυσὸν, καὶ τῶν ἐλεφάντων τοὺς ὀδόντας [ēnankase to ethnos phorous telein, ebenon, kai chryson, kai tōn elephantōn tous odontas], <i>he imposed on the nation as tribute ebony, and gold, and elephants' teeth.</i> The <i>Persians</i> are read to have exacted the same things from the same <i>Ethiopians</i>. <i>Herodotus</i>, <i>Muse</i> 3, ch. 97: Ἀγινέουσι δὲ καὶ τὸ μέχρι ἐμεῦ δύο χοίνικας ἀπύρου χρυσίου, καὶ διηκοσίας φαλάγγας ἐβένου, καὶ ἐλέφαντος ὀδόντας μεγάλους εἴκοσι· [Agineousi de kai to mechri emeu dyo choinikas apyrou chrysiou, kai diēkosias phalangas ebenou, kai elephantos odontas megalous eikosi] <i>Down to my own time</i> (<i>the Ethiopians</i>, as a triennial tribute <i>to the Persians</i>) <i>bring two choenixes of apyrous gold</i> (that is, not yet tried by fire), <i>and two hundred logs of ebony, and twenty large elephant tusks.</i> For the region is rich in these three things, as the same author writes in ch. 114: Αὕτη δὲ χρυσόν τε φέρει πολλὸν, καὶ ἐλέφαντας ἀμφιλαφέας, καὶ δένδρεα πάντα ἄγρια, καὶ ἔβενον· [Hautē de chryson te pherei pollon, kai elephantas amphilapheas, kai dendrea panta agria, kai ebenon] <i>This land produces much gold, and very large elephants, and wild trees of every kind, and ebony.</i> <i>Pliny</i> too, book [II, 717] 12, ch. 4, where he states of ebony that <i>by way of tribute</i> the Ethiopians <i>paid to the kings</i> of Persia <i>every third year a hundred logs of its wood, together with gold and ivory.</i> And in the procession of <i>Ptolemy Philadelphus</i>, according to <i>Callixenus of Rhodes</i>, <i>Ethiopians</i> are reported to have carried these same things. [col. 142] Ἐχόμενοι τούτων ἦσαν (he says) Αἰθίοπες δωροφόροι, ὧν οἱ μὲν ἔφερον ὀδόντας ἑξακοσίους, ἕτεροι δὲ ἐβένου κορμοὺς δισχιλίους, ἄλλοι χρυσοῦ καὶ ἀργυρίου κρατῆρας ἑξήκοντα, καὶ ψήγματα χρυσίου [Echomenoi toutōn ēsan … Aithiopes dōrophoroi, hōn hoi men epheron odontas hexakosious, heteroi de ebenou kormous dischilious, alloi chrysou kai argyriou kratēras hexēkonta, kai psēgmata chrysiou]. <i>Next to these came</i> Ethiopians <i>bearing gifts, some of whom carried six hundred</i> (elephants') <i>tusks, others two thousand logs of ebony, others sixty bowls of gold and silver, and gold dust.</i> Accordingly, at the festival of <i>Adonis</i> celebrated with great pomp at <i>Alexandria</i> under the same king, the <i>Syracusan</i> women, on seeing the ebony and the gold and the abundant ivory, burst out in these words, <i>Idyll</i> 15, line 123:

‎Ὦ ἔβενος, ὢ χρυσὸς, ὢ ἐκ λευκῶ ἐλέφαντος Αἰετώ! [Ō ebenos, ō chrysos, ō ek leukō elephantos Aietō!]

‎<i>O ebony, O gold, O eagles of white ivory!</i> Moreover, <i>at Rome,</i> as <i>Pliny</i> teaches, <i>ebony held the third place in the esteem of tributes.</i> Sixthly, although the <i>Hebrews</i> defer greatly to the <i>Chaldee</i> paraphrast, for whom הבנים <i>hobnim</i> are <i>peacocks,</i> they nevertheless acknowledge that some interpret it as <i>ebony,</i> and they waver between the two opinions. Hence <i>Kimchi</i> in his <i>Commentaries:</i> הובנים פירשו בו העץ הנקרא בערבי אבנוס, <i>They interpret</i> hobnim <i>as the wood which is called ebony</i> in Arabic. This is better than in his <i>Lexicon,</i> where it is יבנים <i>jabnim,</i> or <i>jebenim.</i> And <i>Abarbanel [=Isaac Abarbanel (1437–1508), commentary on the Bible]:</i> פירשו בו שהוא העץ הנקרא בערבי אוינוס שמביאין גם כן מארצות המזרח, <i>They explain it as the wood which in</i> Arabic <i>is called evenus, which they also</i> (like ivory) <i>bring from the eastern lands.</i> <i>Mardochaeus</i> and <i>Aquinas:</i> עצם חיה מה או מין עץ, <i>The bone of some animal, or a kind of wood.</i> Their repeated claim that it is <i>the bone of an animal</i> is a third opinion, which <i>Kimchi</i> explains in his <i>Lexicon</i> thus: הבנים הם חיות שעושין מלאכה מהעצם שלהן או מהקרנות כמו שעושין מעצם הפיל שהוא השן, Hobnim <i>are animals from whose bones or horns certain works are made, as they are made from the bone of the elephant, which is ivory.</i> But he reduces this third opinion to the first, which explains <i>hobnim</i> as <i>peacocks,</i> adding that it may be שיעשו מלאכה מעצם שלהם, <i>that some work is made from their bones</i>, namely as from ivory, with which הבנים <i>hobnim</i> is joined here. This is a monstrous opinion. For who has ever heard tell of peacock bones being carved or engraved, or used for any work in the manner of ivory? Thus the very fact that those who would have הבנים <i>hobnim</i> be <i>peacocks</i> slide into such absurd fictions shows how much more soundly those judge who understand ebony here; in that rendering there is nothing that does not fit exactly.

‎Yet one objection can be raised, namely that the <i>Hebrew</i> הבנים <i>hobnim</i> is a plural, whereas the word for ebony is only singular. It is indeed inflected and spelled in various ways. For one says <i>haec ebenus</i>, or <i>evenus</i>, or <i>hebenus</i>, and <i>hoc ebenum</i>, or <i>evenum</i>, [II, 718] or <i>hebenum</i>. And in <i>Greek</i> ἡ ἔβενος [hē ebenos], or ἔβελος [ebelos], as in the <i>Suda</i>; or ἐβένη [ebenē], as in <i>Theophrastus</i>. But in the plural, ἐβένους [ebenous] in <i>Greek</i> was said only by <i>Symmachus</i>; and in <i>Latin</i> <i>ebenos</i> or <i>ebena</i> only, as far as I remember, by <i>Arias</i> [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] and <i>Mercerus</i> in their rendering of this passage. <i>Junius</i> answers that it is named in the plural by the <i>Hebrews</i> because there are two kinds of ebony. That is indeed what <i>Theophrastus</i> teaches, <i>Hist</i>. book 4, ch. 5, where he treats the trees of <i>India</i>: Ἴδιον δὲ καὶ ἡ ἐβένη τῆς χώρας ταύτης. Ταύτης δὲ δύο γένη τὸ μὲν εὔξυλον, καὶ καλόν· τὸ δὲ φαῦλον. σπάνιον δὲ τὸ καλὸν, θάτερον δὲ πολύ. Τὴν δὲ χρόαν οὐ θησαυριζομένη λαμβάνει τὴν εὔχρουν, ἀλλ᾽ εὐθὺς τῇ φύσει ἐστὶ δὲ τὸ δένδρον θαμνῶδες, ὥσπερ ὁ κύτισος [Idion de kai hē ebenē tēs chōras tautēs. Tautēs de dyo genē to men euxylon, kai kalon; to de phaulon. spanion de to kalon, thateron de poly. Tēn de chroan ou thēsaurizomenē lambanei tēn euchroun, all᾽ euthys tē physei estin de to dendron thamnōdes, hōsper ho kytisos]. <i>Ebony too is peculiar to this region. Of it there are two kinds: one of excellent wood, and good; the other bad. The good one is rare, but the other common. Nor does ebony acquire its fine colour by being stored away, but has it at once from nature. Moreover it is like a shrub, as the cytisus is</i>. That this is the true meaning of <i>Theophrastus</i> <i>Salmasius</i> proves at length in his <i>Exercitations on Solinus</i>, and not the one that <i>Pliny</i>, book 12, chapter 4, ascribes to him in these words: <i>There are two kinds of it. The rarer is the better one, tree-like, with a trunk</i> [col. 143] <i>free of knots, its wood of a black lustre, and pleasing at once even without craftsmanship. The other is shrubby, like the cytisus, and spread over all</i> India. Good ebony is not called εὔξυλος [euxylos] because it is tree-like, that is, large like a tree, but from the excellence of its wood. Nor is being θαμνώδης [thamnōdēs] peculiar to the bad kind; it is common to both. And there θαμνῶδες [thamnōdes] does not mean <i>fruticosum</i> [shrubby], that is, dense with many twigs, but behaving like a shrub, as all bushes do. <i>Dioscorides</i> also makes two kinds of ebony, but distinguishes them by place: Ἔβενος κρατίστη ἡ Αἰθιοπικὴ, καὶ μέλαινα, καὶ μὴ ἔχουσα κτηδόνας [Ebenos kratistē hē Aithiopikē, kai melaina, kai mē echousa ktēdonas]; <i>The best ebony is the</i> Ethiopian, <i>black, and without veins running through it like the teeth of a comb. The Arabic</i> translator calls them طبقات [ṭabaqāt]. And a little later: Ἔστι δέ τις καὶ Ἰνδικὴ, ἔχουσα διαφύσεις λευκὰς, καὶ κιῤῥὰς, καὶ σπίλους ὁμοίως πυκνούς· πλὴν βελτίων ἡ πρώτη [Esti de tis kai Indikē, echousa diaphyseis leukas, kai kirrhas, kai spilous homoiōs pyknous; plēn beltiōn hē prōtē]. <i>There is also another,</i> Indian, <i>with white and reddish lines running through it, and frequent spots as well. But the former is better</i>. So also <i>Isidore</i>: <i>Ebony grows in</i> India <i>and</i> Ethiopia; <i>when cut it hardens into stone; its wood is black, and its bark smooth like that of the laurel. But the</i> Indian <i>is spotted, with small white and tawny markings</i>. The Ethiopian, <i>however, which is held to be superior, is spotted nowhere, but black, smooth and horn-like</i>. And <i>Serapion</i> [=Serapion, Liber de simplicibus medicinis (the Arabic-Latin pharmacopoeia)]: <i>It has two species, namely</i> Absi <i>and</i> Inda. <i>And</i> Absi <i>is a black wood, without lines, etc. In the</i> Indian, <i>however, there are white lines, and of a reddish colour</i>, etc. This is taken from <i>Dioscorides</i>. <i>Absi</i> is حبشي [ḥabashī] <i>Habasi</i>, that is, <i>Ethiopian</i>. Common to all of these is that they recognize only two species of ebony. To which the <i>Hebrew</i> הבנים <i>hobnim</i> does not correspond, because it is a plural, not a dual noun. For the dual would be <i>hobnajim</i>. So something else must be sought. <i>Fuller</i> indeed refers this plural to the <i>palangae</i> of ebony, which were brought from <i>India</i> and <i>Ethiopia</i> in great [II, 719] numbers. <i>Herodotus</i> and <i>Arrian</i> in the <i>Periplus</i> call them φάλαγγας [phalangas]; Pliny <i>palangae</i> or <i>phalangae</i>, the spelling varying; that is, round poles, such as are placed under ships, or on which several men carry the same load. <i>Pollux</i>, book 7, ch. 33, §. 9: Τὰ δὲ τῶν νεῶν ξύλα, οἷς ὑποβληθεῖσιν ἐφέλκονται αἱ νῆες, φάλαγγες, καὶ φαλάγγια [Ta de tōn neōn xyla, hois hypoblētheisin ephelkontai hai nēes, phalanges, kai phalangia]. Hesychius: Φαλάγγια· στρόγγυλα ξύλα, καὶ σύμμετρα [Phalangia; strongyla xyla, kai symmetra]. Nonius: <i>Palangae is the name for round poles which are put under ships when they are drawn down to the sea, or when they are hauled up onto the shore.</i> The Glosses: <i>Phalangarii</i>, Οἱ τοῖς δοκοῖς ἀποκρεμῶντες βάρη, καὶ πλείονες ἅμα βαστάζοντες [Hoi tois dokois apokremōntes barē, kai pleiones hama bastazontes]. Again <i>Nonius</i>: <i>We call palangarii those who carry some load on poles.</i> The origin of the word seems to be <i>Hebrew</i>. For in <i>Hebrew</i> פלך <i>pelec</i>, or in archaic form <i>phelec</i>, is <i>baculus</i> [staff], 2 Sam 3:29. And in <i>Arabic</i> فلك [falaka] phalaca means <i>to be round</i>. Hence the round ebony staves which the <i>Tyrian</i> merchants called פלכין <i>pelakin</i> or <i>phelakin</i> could have been called φάλαγγες [phalanges], <i>phalanges</i>, or <i>palanges</i>. <i>Callixenus</i>, in book 5 of <i>Athenaeus</i>, names ἐβένου κορμοὺς [ebenou kormous], <i>logs of ebony</i>. <i>Apuleius</i>, in the first <i>Apology</i>, <i>staves of ebony</i>, though some prefer to read <i>loculos</i> [little boxes] there. These, then, whether staves or κορμοὺς [kormous], or these <i>palangae</i> of ebony, <i>Fuller</i> thinks are signified by the plural הבנים <i>hobnim</i>. But learned men seem to weary themselves here in vain, since the <i>Hebrews</i> scarcely name precious woods of this kind otherwise than with a plural word. Thus the tree from which the ark was made is called once in the singular שטה <i>sitta</i>, <i>Isa 41:19.</i> But in the plural שטים <i>sittim</i> more than twenty times in <i>Exodus</i>. And another tree, from which the beams and supports in the temple of <i>Solomon</i> were made, and the harps and lutes of the singers, is called only by a plural word, אלגומים <i>algumim</i> or אלמוגים <i>almugim</i>, <i>1 Reg. X, 11. 12.</i> and <i>2 Chr 9:10–11.</i> And in the <i>Chaldee</i> paraphrases of <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] and <i>Jerusalem</i>, Gen 50:1: <i>Joseph laid his father</i> בערס דסנדפין, <i>on a couch of sindaphin</i>, that is, as they hold, on a bed made of the most excellent kind of wood, so called with a plural word. [col. 144] And I once suspected that it was so named after two villages of <i>Egypt</i> called سندف [Sndf] <i>Sendapha</i>. Yet in the treatise <i>On the Elephant</i> above, a truer explanation of this word is given.

‎It should be noted in passing that the books of the <i>Arabs</i> mention certain woods so similar to ebony that some take them for ebony. Among these is the wood شيز [shīz] <i>Siz</i>, or شيزي [shīzī] <i>Siza</i>, so called from a place; and the wood ساسم [sāsam] <i>sasem</i>, or سمسم [simsim] <i>semsem</i>, in the plural سماسم [samāsim] <i>semasim</i>, called by the same name as sesame, although it is entirely different from sesame. To this belong these words in <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: الشيز والشيزي خشب اسود للقصاع وهو الابنوس والساسم او خشب الجوز وناحية بارض بادر بيجان [al-shīz wa-l-shīzī khashab aswad li-l-qiṣāʿ wa-huwa al-abanūs wa-l-sāsam aw khashab al-jawz wa-nāḥiya bi-arḍ bādr bījān] Siz, <i>or</i> Siza, <i>a black wood for making dishes.</i> <i>It is either ebony, or</i> sasim, <i>or the wood of the walnut; and also a certain region in the land of</i> Adarbigian (that is, the <i>Atropatia of Strabo</i> and [II, 720] the <i>Atropatena of Ptolemy</i>), from which, namely, this wood is brought and takes its name. The same author elsewhere: الساسم شجر اسود او الابنوس او الشيز او شجر يعمل منه القسي [al-sāsam shajar aswad aw al-abanūs aw al-shīz aw shajar yuʿmal minhu al-qisī] Sasim: <i>a black tree, or ebony, or</i> siz, <i>or a tree from which bows are made.</i> <i>Damir</i>, in the chapter <i>On</i> سمسمة [simsima] <i>simsima</i>, which is a kind of red ant, like שומשמנא among the <i>Chaldeans</i>, says that this same wood is called سمسم [simsim] <i>semsem</i>, in the plural سماسم [samāsim] <i>semasim</i>. He proves this from these words of the pseudo-prophet about الجهنميين [al-jahannamiyyīn], that is, about those who are tormented in Gehenna: تخرجون من النار بعد ان يكونوا فيها فيخرجون كانها عيدان السماسم [takhrujūn min al-nār baʿd an yakūnū fīhā fa-yakhrujūn ka-annahā ʿīdān al-samāsim] <i>They will come out of the fire after some stay in it; they will come out</i>, I say, <i>like the woods semasim</i>, that is, equally black from being scorched by the fire. On these words the commentators offer many quite absurd things. But the more learned recognize that the <i>semasim</i> woods are ebony woods, or woods resembling ebony in blackness, so that they seem burnt. This throws light on these words of <i>Dioscorides</i> in the chapter <i>On ebony</i>: Ἔνιοι δὲ τὰ σησάμινα, ἢ ἀκάνθινα ξύλα ἐμφερῆ ὄντα, ἀντὶ ἐβένου πωλοῦσι· [Enioi de ta sēsamina, ē akanthina xyla empherē onta, anti ebenou pōlousi]. <i>Marcellus Virgilius</i> rendered this as follows: <i>Some sell in place of ebony the timber of the</i> Indian <i>thorn, or of the mulberry, the resemblance deceiving.</i> He thinks, namely, that σησάμινα [sēsamina] should be read συκάμινα [sykamina], because sesame has nothing in common with ebony. But neither has the sycamine or sycomore, except that both are wood. Beware, therefore, of changing anything. For <i>sesamina</i> here means something other than in common usage: namely, the wood of that tree which is called <i>sasim</i> and <i>semsem</i> in <i>Arabic</i>, and in the plural <i>semasim</i>. Accordingly the <i>Arabic</i> translator of <i>Dioscorides</i> rightly has here: ومن الناس من ياخذ اعضان خشب بعض اصناف الشوك والخشب الذي يقال له سيساما فيبيعه بدل الابنوس لانه [col. 145] شبيه به [wa-min al-nās man yaʾkhudh aʿḍān khashab baʿḍ aṣnāf al-shawk wa-l-khashab alladhī yuqāl lahu sīsāmā fa-yabīʿuhu badal al-abanūs li-annahu shabīh bihi] <i>There are some who take the wood of a certain kind of thorn, and the wood which they call sesamum, and sell it in place of ebony, because it resembles it.</i> What is more, besides <i>Dioscorides</i>, <i>Arrian</i> too in the <i>Periplus</i> mentions φαλάγγων σησαμίνων, καὶ ἐβενίνων [phalangōn sēsaminōn, kai ebeninōn], <i>beams of sesamine and of ebony</i>, which are brought from the <i>Indian</i> city of <i>Barygaza</i> to <i>Persia</i>. This reading is therefore certain and beyond doubt.
