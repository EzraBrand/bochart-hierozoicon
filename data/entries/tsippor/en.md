# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 21: The word tsippor, which means any bird and specially the sparrow (vol. 2, pp. 721–727)

### ‎[II, 721] [col. 145] Chapter 21.

### ‎<i>On the word</i> צפור <i>tsippor, which denotes both birds of any kind and specifically the sparrow. Its kinds according to the Arabs; and on the house sparrow and its chirping.</i>

‎Among the infirmities of old age which <i>Solomon</i> vividly depicts, this too is listed, <i>Eccl 12:6</i>: that one is roused and rises לקול הצפור, <i>at the voice</i> of the <i>tsippor</i>, or <i>bird</i>, that is, of the <i>cock</i>, as many think. The most learned <i>Mercerus</i> [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] refutes them thus: <i>Those who understand the cock, at whose voice one is roused, do not understand the force of the</i> Hebrew <i>word</i> צפור (tsippor), <i>which means</i> a little bird, etc., <i>and is not said of larger birds.</i> And the same author in his <i>Thesaurus</i>: צפור (tsippor) <i>means smaller birds</i>, etc.; עוף (oph), <i>however, is a bird or winged creature in general.</i> And <i>Schindler</i>: צפור (tsippor) <i>is a little bird flying about, or one that circles in the air. It comprises the smaller birds, and among them the sparrow, the one most frequently found among men, a very common bird.</i> So too most other recent <i>lexicographers.</i> And in <i>Philo</i>'s book <i>Who is the Heir of Divine Things</i>, <i>Sepphora</i> is ὀρνίθιον [ornithion], <i>a little bird.</i> Yet among the <i>Hebrews</i> עוף <i>oph</i> and צפור <i>tsippor</i> are equally general, and both names embrace absolutely all winged creatures. Hence <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] in their <i>Lexicons</i>, under the word צפור <i>tsippor</i>: שם כלל לכל עוף, <i>it is a name common to every bird.</i> And <i>Aquinas</i> [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: שם כולל לכל מיני עוף שיעופף, <i>This name comprises every species of bird that flies.</i> That this is entirely true will be clear to anyone who compares the passages of Scripture. For in <i>Deut 4:17</i>, when God forbids the worship of תבנית כל צפור כנף אשר תעוף בשמים, <i>the likeness of any winged</i> tsippor <i>that flies through the heavens</i>, who would say that only the worship of little birds is forbidden, and not that of larger birds as well? And in <i>Deut 14:11</i>, when the same God permits the eating of כל צפור טהורה, <i>every clean</i> tsippor, will he be said to have permitted only little birds, and not hens, guinea fowl and pheasants? Or who ever restricted to little birds the law found in <i>Deut 22:6</i>: <i>If there come before you</i> קן צפור, <i>a nest of a</i> tsippor, <i>in any tree or on the ground, a nest of young or of eggs, where the mother is sitting on the young or on the eggs, do not take the mother with the young</i>? Again, who would deny that all birds are meant when we read that God subjected to man <i>the beasts of the field</i>, צפור שמים, <i>the</i> tsippor <i>of the heavens, and the fish of the sea</i>, Ps 8:9? Or when [col. 146] <i>wild beasts, and all cattle, creeping things</i>, וצפור כנף, <i>and the winged</i> tsippor are invited to praise God, <i>Ps 148:10</i>? Or when we read that in the branches of the mystical tree dwell כל צפור כל כנף, <i>every</i> tsippor, <i>everything winged</i>, Ezek 17:23? And in the plural, צפרי שמיא, <i>the</i> tsippare <i>of the heavens</i>, Dan 4:9 18.? Finally, it is clear that birds of prey, which are generally by far the largest, are designated by this name, when in <i>Ezek 39:4 17.</i> God hands over the hosts of <i>Gog</i> and <i>Magog</i> to be devoured לעיט צפור, [II, 722] <i>by the ravenous winged</i> tsippor. And in <i>Daniel IV</i>, <i>20</i>, where the nails of the <i>Babylonian</i> king are said to have grown כצפרין, <i>like tsipparin</i>, that is, like the talons of birds of prey, which from the curvature and size of their talons are called in <i>Greek</i> γαμψώνυχες [gampsōnyches]. It is therefore evident that the name צפור <i>tsippor</i> extends even to the largest birds. <i>Rabbi Moses</i>, son of <i>Gershom</i>, maintains that birds are so called שבעלות השחר מתחילות לרנן ולצפצוף, <i>because at the rising of the dawn they begin to sing and to warble</i>. I, for my part, hold that they are named not from singing, which is not common to all, but from צפר, that is, <i>morning</i>; because it is customary for birds to rise very early in the morning, whether they are songbirds or not, while wild beasts are then sound asleep, because they spend the night awake, <i>Psal. CIV</i>, <i>20</i>.

‎But although the name צפור <i>tsippor</i> is common to all birds, the <i>Hebrews</i> nevertheless assert that this same name sometimes signifies the <i>sparrow</i> specifically. <i>Kimchi</i> on <i>Psal. LXXXIV</i>, <i>4</i>: וצפור הוא עוף קטן וקורין לו בלעז פשרא <i>And tsippor</i> (in that place) <i>is a small bird, which in the vernacular</i> (that is, <i>Spanish</i>) <i>they call paxaro</i> (that is, <i>sparrow</i>). Hence <i>Pomarius</i>: קמחי אומר שלפעמים יוקח בעד עוף קטן שקורין לו בלעז פאסרו, Kimchi <i>said that it is sometimes taken for a little bird, which in the vernacular</i> (or <i>Italian</i>) <i>tongue they call passaro</i>. Aquinas: הוא שם מין עוף מה, <i>It is the name of a certain species of bird</i>. And a little later: והוא עוף קטן וידוע, <i>It is a well-known little bird</i>, namely the sparrow. Accordingly, just as <i>Jerome</i> renders it <i>sparrow</i> in <i>Lev. XIV</i>, <i>4</i>, so too does the old translator in <i>Psal. X</i>, <i>1</i>, and <i>LXXXIII</i>, <i>4</i>, and <i>CI</i>, <i>8</i>, and <i>CIII</i>, <i>16</i>, and <i>CXXIII</i>, <i>7</i>, following the <i>Greeks</i>, who render it στρουθίον [strouthion] in those same passages of the <i>Psalms</i>, and in <i>Eccles. XII</i>, <i>6</i>, and <i>Thren. III</i>, <i>52</i>. And conversely, the <i>Greek</i> στρουθία [strouthia], that is, <i>sparrows</i>, in <i>Matth. X</i>, <i>29</i>, and <i>Luc. XII</i>, <i>6</i>, the Syriac translator renders ܨܦܪܝܢ [צפרין] <i>tsipparin</i>, for which the <i>Arabic</i> has عصافير [ʿaṣāfīr] <i>atsaphiro</i>. For to them عصفور [ʿuṣfūr] <i>otsphur</i> is the <i>sparrow</i>, a word formed from צפור <i>tsippor</i> with a guttural prefixed. Not knowing this, <i>Chamza</i>, a not undistinguished <i>Arab grammarian</i>, thought that the <i>sparrow</i> is called عصفور by a compound word, because عصي وفر [ʿaṣá wa-farra] <i>it strikes and flees</i>. Nor is it peculiar to the <i>Hebrews</i> that they call the <i>sparrow</i> by the same word as they also call <i>larger birds</i>. For the <i>Greek</i> name of the <i>sparrow</i>, στρουθὸς [strouthos], is also used of the <i>hen</i>. <i>Nicander</i> in the <i>Alexipharmaca</i>, line 60:

‎Ἠέ τι μηελόεντα χαλικρότερον ποτὸν ἴσχοις Ὄρνιθος στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — — — [Ēe ti mēeloenta chalikroteron poton ischois / Ornithos strouthoio katoikados]

‎<i>Or may you have some marrowy, undiluted drinkable</i> remedy <i>from the domestic sparrow-bird</i>, that is, from the hen. And again, line 535:

‎Ἠὲ πάτον στρουθοῖο κατοικάδος, — — [Ēe paton strouthoio katoikados]

‎<i>Or the dung of the domestic sparrow</i>, that is, of the hen. Hence it came about that the great <i>Libyan</i> hen, by far the largest of birds, was called στρουθὸς [strouthos], in a word the <i>ostrich</i>. Imitating this, the [II, 723] <i>Roman</i> writers call the ostrich the <i>sea sparrow</i>. <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Persa</i>, Act 2:

‎<i>Fly at a run.</i> P. <i>That is what the sea sparrow usually does around the circus.</i>

‎<i>Ausonius</i>, Epistle 11:

‎<i>Cook you the egg of the sea sparrow.</i> [col. 147]

‎Festus: <i>The sea sparrow, which the common people call the struthiocamelus.</i> The <i>Roman</i> word <i>passer</i> is thought to allude to the <i>Hebrew</i> צפור <i>tsippor</i> and to have arisen from it. But a great man, commenting on <i>Solinus</i>, observes that the sparrow was also called <i>passa</i>, as the female sparrow is called <i>paisse</i> in <i>French</i>. Hence the Glosses: <i>Passa</i>, εἶδος ὀρνέου [eidos orneou]. And <i>passa</i> was formed from the <i>Aeolic</i> πίζα [piza], for σπίζα [spiza]. For σπίζα [spiza] is the <i>finch</i>, a bird similar to the sparrow. <i>Hesychius</i>: Σπίζα· ὀρνιθάριον στρουθῷ παρεμφερές [Spiza: ornitharion strouthō paremferes]. And sometimes it is taken more broadly for a <i>little bird</i> in general, as in <i>Timon</i>, quoted by <i>Laertius</i>, on <i>Arcesilaus</i>:

‎Οἱ δέ μιν ἠΰ τε γλαῦκα περὶ σπίζαι τερατοῦντο, [Hoi de min ēy te glauka peri spizai teratounto]

‎<i>But they will marvel at him like little birds that surround an owl.</i> Accordingly <i>Hesychius</i>: Σπίζια· ὄρνεα ἅπαντα [Spizia; ornea hapanta]. Rightly, provided that his ὄρνεα [ornea] are ὀρνίθια [ornithia] or ὀρνιθάρια [ornitharia], that is, <i>little birds</i>, not <i>birds</i>. Thus the <i>Arabic</i> عصفور [ʿuṣfūr], although properly it means <i>sparrow</i>, nevertheless embraces many species of little birds. <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: وهى انواع منها ما هو مطرب بصوته ومنها ما يعجب بصورته وحسنه والعصفور الصوار هو الذي يجيب اذا ادعي من الصرورة وعصفور الجنة وهو الخطاف والعصفور البيوت فان في طباعه اختلافا [wa-hiya anwāʿ minhā mā huwa muṭrib bi-ṣawtihi wa-minhā mā yuʿjib bi-ṣūratihi wa-ḥusnihi wa-l-ʿuṣfūr al-ṣawwār huwa alladhī yujību idhā duʿiya min al-ṣarūra wa-ʿuṣfūr al-janna wa-huwa al-khuṭṭāf wa-l-ʿuṣfūr al-buyūt fa-inna fī ṭibāʿihi ikhtilāfan] <i>There are various species of sparrows: some please by the modulation of their voice; others are admirable for their appearance and form. And the sparrow called</i> suar <i>is the one that, when called, answers by chirping. And the sparrow of paradise is the swallow. And the house sparrow is the one that is quarrelsome by nature,</i> etc. And further on: ومن انواعه عصفور الشوك واكثر ماواه السياج وزعم ارسطو ان بينه وبين الحمار عداوة [wa-min anwāʿihi ʿuṣfūr al-shawk wa-akthar maʾwāhu al-siyāj wa-zaʿama Arisṭū anna baynahu wa-bayna al-ḥimār ʿadāwa] <i>Among its species is the sparrow of thorns, which mostly lives in thickets. Aristotle reports that there is mutual hatred between it and the ass.</i> It is plain that the acanthis is meant, which some explain as the goldfinch, others as the siskin, others as the <i>ligurinus</i>. For of it the <i>Philosopher</i> writes in <i>Hist.</i> book 8, chapter 3: Τὰ δὲ τοιάδε ἀκανθοφάγα, ἀκανθὶς, θραυπίς· ἔτι ἡ καλουμένη χρυσομήτρις. Ταῦτα γὰρ πάντα ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκανθῶν νέμεται [Ta de toiade akanthophaga, akanthis, thraupis; eti hē kaloumenē chrysomētris. Tauta gar panta epi tōn akanthōn nemetai]. <i>Gaza</i> rendered this as follows: <i>These seek thorns: the siskin or ligurinus, the goldfinch, and also the one called aurivittis. For all these live on thorns.</i> And in book 9, chapter 1: Ὄνος δὲ, καὶ ἀκανθίδες πολέμιοι· αἱ μὲν γὰρ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀκανθῶν βοητεύουσιν [Onos de, kai akanthides polemioi; hai men gar apo tōn akanthōn boēteuousin] (read [II, 724] βιοτεύουσιν [bioteuousin]), ὁ δὲ ἁπαλὰς οὔσας ἐσθίει τὰς ἀκάνθας [ho de hapalas ousas esthiei tas akanthas]: <i>The ass and the acanthides are enemies: for they get their living from thorns, which the ass eats while they are still tender.</i> And the <i>Arabs</i> rightly assign this bird to the sparrow kind. For the acanthis is the same as the acanthyllis, of which <i>Hesychius</i> says: Ἀκανθυλλίς· στρουθοῦ γένος [Akanthyllis; strouthou genos], <i>Acanthyllis, a kind of sparrow.</i> In <i>French</i> it is called <i>serin</i>; whether from the song of the <i>Sirens</i>, as <i>Belon</i> [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] holds in the <i>Ornithology</i>, book 7, chapter 14. There follows in <i>Damir</i>: ومن انواعه القبرة والحسون والبلبل والصعوة والحمرة والعندليب والمكاكي والصافر والتنوط والوصع والابوبراقش [col. 148] والقبعة [wa-min anwāʿihi al-qubbara wa-l-ḥassūn wa-l-bulbul wa-l-ṣaʿwa wa-l-ḥumra wa-l-ʿandalīb wa-l-makākī wa-l-ṣāfir wa-l-tanawwuṭ wa-l-waṣaʿ wa-l-abū barāqish wa-l-qubaʿa] <i>Also among its species are the kubbara</i> (the crested lark), <i>and the hassun</i> (I do not find it elsewhere; it seems to be named from its beauty) <sup>R1</sup>, <i>and the bulbul</i> (the nightingale), <i>and the savva</i> (a little bird with a red crown), <i>and the hamra</i> (a red species of lark), <i>and the andalib</i> (a species of bird similar to the nightingale), <i>and the mechachi</i> (plural of the singular مكا [mukkā] <i>maccha</i>, a bird named from its whistling), <i>and the saphir</i> (which also whistles all night long with its head bent down and its feet fastened to the tree), <i>and the thenavvut</i> (a little bird not unlike this one, named from the nest that it hangs from a tree), <i>and the vvasa</i> (the wren), <i>and the abubarakis</i> (a songbird with long neck and legs, and red, yellow, green and blue feathers, which repeatedly change their colours), <i>and the kubaa</i> (a variegated little bird that draws in its neck when afraid, whence its name). These are the birds that the <i>Arabs</i> assign to the sparrow kind. So too among the <i>Hebrews</i>, according to <i>Kimchi</i> in his <i>Lexicon</i>: הוא רב המין לפיכך כלל כל עוף בזה השם, <i>The sparrow has many species: hence this name is applied to all birds.</i>

‎He describes the البيوتي [al-buyūtī] <i>domestic</i> sparrow as follows: في طباعه اختلاف وذلك فيه من الطباعه ما يشبه السباع وهو اكله اللحم ولا يزق فراخه ومن البهايم انه ليس بذي مخلب ولا منسر وياكل الحب والبقول وهو اذا سقط علي العود قدم اصابعه الثلاث واخر الدايرة وساير الطير تقدم اصبعين وتوخر اصبعين وتميز الذكر منها بلحيه سودا كما الرجل والتيس والديك وليس في الارض طاير ولا سبع ولا بهيمة احني من العصفور علي [II, 725] ولده ولا اشد له عشقا <sup>a)</sup> وذلك مشاهد عند اخذ فراخه ووكره في الغمران تحت السقوف خوف من الجوارح واذا حلت مدينة من اهلها ذهبت العصافير منها فاذا عاد واعادت العصافير والعصفور لا يعرف المشي وانما يثب وثبا وهو كثير السفاد فربما سفد في الساعة الواحدة ماية مرة ولذلك قصر عمره فانه لا يعيش في الغالب اكثر من سنة والفرخه تدرب علي [col. 149] الطيران حتي انه يدعي فيجيب قال الجاحظ انه رجع من فرسخ <i>It is of a pugnacious nature. For something wild has been implanted in it by nature. Accordingly it feeds on flesh, and does not feed its young by inserting its beak into their mouths. But it has this in common with non-raptorial birds, that it has no talons with which to tear, nor a hooked beak, and it eats grain and vegetables. When it alights on a tree, of its four toes it stretches three forward and one backward, whereas in other birds two toes are in front and as many behind. The male is distinguished from the female by a black beard, which it has like a man, a he-goat, or a cock. Nowhere is there a bird, wild beast, or beast of burden more devoted to its offspring or loving it more ardently. Hence it places its young and its nest in inhabited places and under the roofs of houses, for fear of birds of prey. When a city is deserted, the sparrows leave it, and return together with the inhabitants. The sparrow does not know how to walk, and moves only by hopping. It is a bird of such frequent coition that it sometimes mates a hundred times in an hour, and consequently it is short-lived, and generally does not live beyond a year. Its young are more teachable than the common run of birds, so that when called they answer. Algiahid testifies that a sparrow returns from a parasang away.</i> One could add more from <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt], but these things apply to the whole sparrow genus, not to the domestic sparrow alone.

‎In <i>Hebrew</i> צפצף <i>tsiphseph</i> means <i>to murmur</i> and <i>to mutter</i>, Isa 8:19 and XXIX, 4. This is taken from the twittering of birds. <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on <i>Es. XXIX.</i>: ציפצוף הוא לשון קול לחש כקול עופות דקין, <i>tsiphtsuph</i> <i>signifies a muttering voice, like the voice of little birds.</i> This is evident from <i>Isa 38:14</i>, where it is applied to the swallow. Yet more properly צפצוף is <i>the chirping of sparrows.</i> Pomarius: צפצף עשה קול צפורים, <i>tsiphseph</i>, <i>he chirped in the manner of sparrows.</i> That this is true is clear from the <i>Arabs</i>, for whom صفصف [ṣafṣaf] <i>tsophtsoph</i> is عصفور [ʿuṣfūr] <i>sparrow</i>, and صفصفة [ṣafṣafa] <i>tsaphtsapha</i> is صوته [ṣawtuhu] <i>its voice.</i> So it is given in the <i>Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author].</i> Hence in <i>Es.</i> [II, 726] <i>X</i>, 14. the <i>Hebrew</i> words ופצה פה ומצפצף, <i>And opening the mouth, and chirping</i>, are rendered in <i>Greek</i> thus: Καὶ ἀνοίγων στόμα, καὶ στρουθίζων [Kai anoigōn stoma, kai strouthizōn]. On which <i>Theodoret</i> says: Ἀπὸ τῆς κρατούσης συνηθείας παρὰ ἀνθρώποις εἴρηται τῇ γραφῇ. εἰώθαμεν γὰρ λέγειν περὶ τῶν λίαν ἀγωνιώντων, Οὐκ ἐτόλμησε γρύξαι· οἱ δὲ λοιποί φασιν, Οὐκ ἐτόλμησε τρύσαι. τὸ οὖν Ἀνοίγων στόμα, καὶ στρουθίζων, ἀντὶ τοῦ Οὐ μόνον οὐδεὶς ἀντιπαρατάξασθαί μοι τολμήσει, ἀλλ᾽ οὐδὲ γρύζειν στρουθοῖς παραπλησίως ἐπιχειρήσει [Apo tēs kratousēs synētheias para anthrōpois eirētai tē graphē. eiōthamen gar legein peri tōn lian agōniōntōn, Ouk etolmēse gryxai; hoi de loipoi phasin, Ouk etolmēse trysai. to oun Anoigōn stoma, kai strouthizōn, anti tou Ou monon oudeis antiparataxasthai moi tolmēsei, all' oude gryzein strouthois paraplēsiōs epicheirēsei]. <i>Scripture has taken this from human usage. For we are accustomed to say of those who are in great distress, He did not dare to mutter. Others, however, say, He did not dare to squeak. Thus "opening the mouth and chirping"</i> (there will be no one) <i>is the same as if he said, Not only will no one dare to oppose me, but no one will even attempt to chirp in the manner of sparrows.</i> From the same source is taken that gloss of <i>Suidas</i>: Στρουθίζων· τρίζων [Strouthizōn; trizōn]. For τρίζειν [trizein], <i>to squeak</i>, is also said of sparrows, as is clear from <i>Iliad</i> 2, line 314, where the serpent devoured eight little sparrows together with their mother

‎— — ἐλεεινὰ κατήσθιε τετριγῶτας, [eleeina katēsthie tetrigōtas,]

‎<i>devoured, miserably shrieking</i>: not τιτίζοντας [titizontas], as <i>Zenodotus</i> wanted it read. Nor should anyone be surprised that <i>Isaiah</i> describes men chirping like birds. For it is a continuation of the metaphor. There the <i>Prophet</i> speaks in the words of the king of <i>Assyria</i>, who boasts like a <i>Thraso</i> and compares the peoples he has conquered with a nest of birds which fear of the fowler so dismays that they do not even dare to chirp. So the whole verse reads thus: <i>And my hand has reached the wealth of the peoples as</i> (birds') <i>nest. And as one gathers abandoned eggs, I have gathered the whole earth; and there is none who moves a wing, or opens the mouth, or chirps.</i> The words Καὶ ἀνοίγων στόμα, καὶ στρουθίζων [Kai anoigōn stoma, kai strouthizōn] [col. 150] are missing in most editions, and were read neither by <i>Basil</i> nor by <i>Cyril</i>. Yet the <i>Complutensian</i> edition and the recent <i>Paris</i> edition present them so. <i>Theodoret</i> also read them, and so did the <i>Arabic</i> translator, who made his version from the <i>Greek</i>. And in <i>Procopius</i> they are found in the margin with an asterisk. The version of <i>the Elders</i> [=the translators of the Septuagint], namely, is often rather free: provided they express the sense in some way, they are often little concerned about the words. Thus the <i>Hebrew</i>, <i>And there is none who moves a wing, or opens the mouth, or chirps</i>, they had rendered thus: Καὶ οὐκ ἔστιν ὃς διαφεύξεταί με, ἢ ἀντείπῃ μοί [Kai ouk estin hos diapheuxetai me, ē anteipē moi]: <i>And there is none who escapes me, or contradicts me.</i> But someone supplied the rest from another translator. Was it <i>Aquila</i>, who was most skilled in the <i>Hebrew</i> language and accustomed to render word for word?

‎The <i>Arabic</i> مصع [maṣʿ] <i>matsaon</i> for the <i>male sparrow</i> echoes the <i>Dutch</i> name of the <i>sparrow</i>, <i>mosche</i>, and the <i>Norman moisson</i>. Alcamus: المصع العصفور الذكرة [al-maṣʿ al-ʿuṣfūr al-dhakara].
