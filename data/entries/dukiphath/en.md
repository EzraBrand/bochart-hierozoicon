# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 31: Dukiphath is the hoopoe (vol. 3, pp. 107–115)

### ‎[III, 107] CHAPTER 31. [col. 343]

### ‎<i>Proof that</i> דוכיפת dukiphath <i>is the Hoopoe.</i>

‎The masters of the <i>Jews</i> rightly assert that the name of the bird דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> is a compound. But the reason for the compounding escapes them. If we believe <i>Pagninus</i> [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in his <i>Commentary</i> explains it as the <i>wild cock</i>, δίλοφον [dilophon], that is, <i>having a double crest</i>; and he holds that it is called דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> <i>from its double crest</i>, as though having דוכיפות <i>dukephoth</i>, that is (he says), <i>two crests</i>. Compounds of this kind occur not infrequently in the writings of the <i>Rabbis</i>. Thus the cave of <i>Machpelah</i>, or the <i>double</i> cave, is called by them דיוטרון, as if <i>double cavern</i>, a word fused from δύο [dyo] and ἄντρον [antron]. The first man is דיופרצוף, διπρόσωπος [diprosōpos], as though God had created him two-faced. A <i>siphon</i> is דיופי, as though it had two mouths. A <i>tree bearing twice a year</i> is דיופרא, as if δίφορος [diphoros]. A <i>double bag</i> is דיוסקיא, as if δίσακκος [disakkos], like the French <i>bissac</i> and <i>besace</i>. The <i>two magistrates governing a city</i>, or <i>consuls</i>, are דיופולין, from δύο [dyo] and πόλις [polis]. A <i>two-edged sword</i> is דיוסטר, because of its two sides; for to them סטר is <i>side</i>. An <i>image copied from another</i> is דיוקון, from δύο [dyo] and εἰκών [eikōn]. <i>Two columns</i> are דיומדין, from דיו and עומדין. And דיוזגי, δίζυγος [dizygos], <i>bijugus</i> [two-yoked]. And דוכסוסטוס, as if δύξυστος [dyxystos], <i>scraped twice</i>: just as παλίμψηστος [palimpsēstos] is a certain kind of parchment, scraped and polished either twice or on both sides. Accordingly, as though the words of the <i>Prophets</i> admitted similar compounds, <i>Joseph Kimchi</i>, the father of <i>David</i>, on <i>Psalm</i> 50, verse 20, contends that the word דופי <i>dophi</i>, that is, <i>reproach, censure</i>, is compounded of דו <i>do</i>, <i>two</i>, and פי <i>pi</i> or <i>phi</i>, <i>mouth</i>; because a slanderer speaks one way in your presence and another in your absence, as though he [col. 344] had two mouths. It is quite absurd that a word used by <i>David</i> should be taken for half-<i>Latin</i>, or at least half-<i>Greek</i>. But it is far more absurd in the case of <i>Moses</i>, to whom belongs the word דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> that we are dealing with. In truth, to tell the matter as it is, <i>R. Selomo</i> does not write what <i>Pagninus</i> attributes to him, that the bird דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> takes its name from דוכפות <i>du-kephoth</i>, or <i>two crests</i>. For there is no bird that is δίλοφος [dilophos]. And a <i>crest</i> in Hebrew is not called כיפה <i>kepha</i> or כיפת <i>kephath</i>, but כרבלה <i>kirbela</i>, as the Bretons say <i>cribel</i>. Finally, the words of <i>Solomon</i> cited by <i>Pagninus</i> mean something quite different: ולמה נקרא שמו דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזו היא כרבלתו. They are to be rendered thus: <i>And why is its name called</i> dukiphath? <i>Because its eminence is bound, that is, its crest.</i> For הוד hod means <i>splendour, dignity, majesty, eminence</i>. And the verb כפת <i>kephath</i> in Chaldee is <i>to bind</i> and <i>to tie</i>. He means, then, that from the two words הודו כפות <i>hodo kaphuth</i>, that is, <i>its eminence</i> (or <i>crest</i>) <i>is bound</i> (supply <i>to its head</i>), <i>dukiphath</i> was formed by aphaeresis, because it is the name of a crested bird. Nor is this so much <i>R. Selomo</i>'s as the <i>Talmudists</i>', in <i>Chullin</i> ch. 3, fol. 63a, where <i>R. Judah</i> says that the דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> is so called שהודו כפות, <i>because it has its eminence bound</i>. And [III, 108] the <i>Gloss</i> in the margin: כרבלתו עבה ורומה כמו שכפולה לתוך הראש וכפותה שם, <i>Its crest is thick, and as it were tied in the middle of the head and bound there.</i> For in place of שכפולה, which means <i>doubled</i>, one must read שכבולה, that is, <i>tied</i>, as the sense shows, together with the synonymous word כפותה that follows. There is a similar error in the <i>Aruch</i> [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] in these words: דוכיפת זה שהודו כפוף. For one must read כפות, as in the <i>Talmud</i> and <i>Selomo</i>, not כפוף. Meanwhile this derivation is not much better than the former. For although הוד <i>hod</i> signifies the ὑπεροχὴν [hyperochēn] and eminence of <i>splendour</i>, <i>strength</i>, <i>glory</i>, <i>dignity</i> and <i>majesty</i>, it is nowhere taken for a <i>crest</i>. Nor would a crested bird rightly be said to have its crest <i>bound</i>, since in crested birds the crest is innate, not bound on. It is clear, therefore, that this ἔτυμον [etymon] is very forced and wrung out as if on the rack.

‎But something else comes to mind, on which I should like the learned to judge. Among the <i>Arabs</i> ديك [dīk] <i>dik</i> is a <i>cock</i>, and ديكة [dīka] <i>dika</i> is a <i>hen</i>. Hence there is a not insubstantial conjecture that among the <i>Hebrews</i> too, or the <i>Syrians</i>, דיך <i>dik</i>, or דוך <i>duk</i>, signified a <i>cock</i>, and דוך כפא <i>duk kepha</i> a <i>cock of the rock</i>. When these words coalesced into one κατὰ σύνθεσιν [kata synthesin], I suspect that from <i>duk-kepha</i>, by a slight change, דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> was formed, one of the two <i>Caph</i>s being dropped; its absence could not be supplied by the point <i>Daghes</i> because of the long vowel preceding. The teachers of the <i>Hebrews</i>, who could not have thought of such a composition, since they did not know that <i>duk</i> or <i>dik</i> is a <i>cock</i>, nevertheless strongly confirm our conjecture when they explain <i>dukiphath</i> as the <i>field cock</i>, or the <i>wild cock</i>, or the <i>mountain cock</i>. And almost all the more recent writers explain it so. Kimchi [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] render it תרנגולא ברא, <i>field cock</i>. R. Serira Gaon, גאלו דמונ״טייא, <i>cock of the mountains</i>. Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], תרנגול הבר, <i>cock of the field</i>, and in Italian <i>gallo, o gallina salvatica</i>. <i>Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] Levita</i> in German אוירהאן <i>awerhan</i>, which is a kind of wild cock. The <i>Syriac</i> too has here ܬܪܢܓܠܐ ܕܒܪܐ [תרנגלא דברא], <i>field cock</i>. And the three <i>Chaldees</i>, the <i>Jerusalem</i>, Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] and Onkelos [=Targum Onkelos], נגר טורא, <i>mountain cock</i>. The <i>field cock</i>, moreover, or <i>mountain cock</i>, is (I believe) the same as the <i>rock-dwelling cock</i>; especially since mountain and rock are so far synonymous that the Elders [=the translators of the Septuagint] use them interchangeably: hence they render the <i>Hebrew</i> צור in some places πέτραν [petran], <i>rock</i>, in others ὄρος [oros], <i>mountain</i>.

‎Although I think this is the true origin of the word, nevertheless another explanation of this composition can also be given [col. 345], and a fairly probable one. Namely, if the word דוכיפת is split into דוך and יפת, the former being <i>cock</i>, the latter <i>to portend</i>. For from it comes מופת, a <i>sign</i>, not of any kind, says Kimchi, but <i>one by which something future is portended</i>. Taken in this way as דוך־יפת, it will be the <i>portentous cock</i>, or even the <i>ominous cock</i>, or the <i>augural cock</i> <sup>R1</sup>.

‎[III, 109] Now we must see to which bird above all these names can be assigned. There are four interpretations. One is that of the <i>Sadducees</i>, who, understanding by the name דוכיפת the <i>common hen</i>, abstained from eating it as an unclean bird forbidden in the Law. To this belongs Aben Ezra's [=Abraham ibn Ezra] sarcasm against them: אמרו הצדוקים שהיא תרנגולת ואלה טפשי עולם כי מי הגיד להם, <i>The</i> Sadducees <i>said that</i> dukiphath <i>is the hen. But these are the fools of the world</i> (that is, the most foolish of mortals). <i>For who told them this?</i> The second is the interpretation of Elias in the <i>Methurgeman</i>, where he says that נגר טורא is called in <i>German</i> אוריארה״אן <i>awerhan</i>, or <i>orhan</i>. That is a kind of wild cock, twice as large as the common one. Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] thinks it is Pliny's <i>tetrao</i>. He himself, following <i>Albertus</i>, calls it <i>urogallus</i>, a <i>Latin</i> name coined on the model of the <i>German</i>. They think it is so called from the <i>uri</i> [aurochs], because it exceeds common cocks in size as much as the aurochs exceed common oxen. The third interpretation is that of <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], for whom דוכיפת is the <i>attagen</i>, a bird that was among the chief delicacies of the ancients. Horace, <i>Epode</i> 2:

‎<i>May no</i> African <i>bird descend into my belly,</i> <i>no</i> Ionian <i>attagen</i>.

‎Martial in the <i>Xenia</i>, Epigram 61:

‎<i>Among the flavours of birds the first is said to be</i> <i>the taste of</i> Ionian <i>attagens</i>.

‎The <i>Greeks</i> call them ἀτταγᾶς [attagas] and ἀτταγῆνας [attagēnas] <sup>a)</sup>. <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Storks</i>:

‎Ἀτταγᾶς, ἥδιστον ἑψεῖν ἐν ἐπινικίοις κρέας. [Attagas, hēdiston hepsein en epinikiois kreas.]

‎[III, 110] <i>Phoenicides</i> ἐν Μισουμένῃ [en Misoumenē], after a long list of dainties:

‎— — κ᾽ οὐδὲν ἦν τούτων ὅλως — [k᾽ ouden ēn toutōn holōs —] Πρὸς τὸν ἀταγῆνα συμβαλεῖν τῶν βρωμάτων. [Pros ton atagēna symbalein tōn brōmatōn.]

‎But the description of so delicate a bird has been so neglected that today the learned dispute whether the <i>attagen</i> is the <i>gallinago</i> [snipe], or the <i>rusticula</i> [woodcock], or the <i>coturnix</i> [quail], or the <i>francolinus</i> of the Italians. By <i>Junius</i> it seems to be taken for the <i>rusticula</i>, which is called <i>woodcock</i>, that is, <i>wild cock</i>, and in some places by the French <i>gelinotte de bois</i>, that is, <i>little wild hen</i>.

‎It remains to speak of the LXX <i>Elders</i> and the <i>Vulgate</i> translator, who render דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> as ἔποπα [epopa], <i>upupa</i> [hoopoe]. So also four <i>Arabic</i> translators, two printed and as many in manuscript, which I happened to compare with one another. For in all of them you will find الهدهد [al-hudhud] <i>alhudhud</i>, that is, the hoopoe. Many of the more recent scholars rightly follow this. For I hold the view that among many interpretations the most ancient is to be preferred, unless there are very weighty reasons for departing from it, and I see none such here. For why should Moses not have called the hoopoe the <i>rock cock</i>, the Chaldeans the <i>mountain cock</i>, the Rabbis the <i>wild cock</i>, when I see that the <i>Greeks</i> did the same? Thus <i>Hesychius</i> explains ἔποπα [epopa] as ἀλεκτρυόνα ἄγριον [alektryona agrion], and <i>Aeschylus</i> calls it πετραῖον ὄρνιν [petraion ornin], a rock <i>bird</i> (or even a rock <i>hen</i>). For we have shown in its place <sup>b)</sup> that ὄρνις [ornis] or ὄρνιθα [ornitha] is taken more specifically for the gallinaceous kind. The passage of <i>Aeschylus</i> to which we alluded is cited thus by the <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>History</i>, book 9, chapter 49:

‎Τοῦτον δ᾽ ἐπόπτην ἔποπα τῶν αὑτοῦ κακῶν Πεποικίλωκε, κ᾽ ἀποδηλώσας ἔχει Θρασὺν πετραῖον ὄρνιν ἐν παντευχίᾳ. [Touton d᾽ epoptēn epopa tōn hautou kakōn pepoikilōke, k᾽ apodēlōsas echei thrasyn petraion ornin en panteuchia.]

‎Which a great man renders thus:

‎<i>He made him a hoopoe, to be himself the witness of his own crimes, with variegated body, a winged dweller of the rocks, fierce in his array.</i> [col. 346]

‎The subject is <i>Tereus</i>, whom he calls the ἐπόπτην [epoptēn], or <i>overseer</i>, of his own evils, alluding to the name ἔποψ [epops], or hoopoe, into which <i>Tereus</i> was changed and became a πετραῖος ὄρνις [petraios ornis], a <i>rock bird</i>, or <i>hen</i> if you prefer. What of the fact that even today the common people of the <i>Greeks</i> call the hoopoe ἀγριοκόκορον [agriokokoron], <i>wild cock</i>? It is called a <i>cock</i> on account of its crest, although in the cock the crest consists of cartilage, in the hoopoe of feathers. Further, why it is called <i>mountain</i>, <i>wild</i> and <i>rock</i> bird is not obscure, since [III, 111] it usually dwells on mountain ridges, steep cliffs and the recesses of forests. The <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>History</i>, book 1, chapter 1: Τὰ δ᾽ ὄρεια (ζῶα), ὥσπερ ἔποψ [Ta d᾽ oreia (zōa), hōsper epops]: <i>Some animals are mountain-dwellers, like the hoopoe.</i> And book 9, chapter 11: Ἔνιοι δὲ τῶν ὀρνίθων ἐν τοῖς ὄρεσι καὶ τῇ ὕλῃ κατοικοῦσιν, οἷον ἔποψ [Enioi de tōn ornithōn en tois oresi kai tē hylē katoikousin, hoion epops]: <i>Among birds there are some that inhabit mountains and forests, like the hoopoe.</i> <i>Aeschylus</i>, in the passage cited above:

‎Ἀστῆ δὲ μισεῖ, τῶν δὲ Παλλήνης τόπων Δρυμοὺς ἐρήμους καὶ πάγους κατοικέει. [Astē de misei, tōn de Pallēnēs topōn drymous erēmous kai pagous katoikeei.] <i>But it hates cities, and in the land of</i> Pallene <i>it dwells in the wilds of the groves and the ridges of the rocks.</i>

‎<i>Aelian</i> writes the same, <i>History</i>, book 3, chapter 26: that hoopoes ὑποπλέκειν τὰς καλιὰς ἐν ταῖς ἐρήμοις, καὶ τοῖς πάγοις τοῖς ὑψηλοῖς [hypoplekein tas kalias en tais erēmois, kai tois pagois tois hypsēlois], <i>build their nests in deserts and on lofty rocks.</i> You see, therefore, reader, whoever you are, that this bird is rightly called דוכיפת, or <i>rock cock</i>.

‎But also, if one prefers to interpret דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> as the <i>portentous</i> or <i>ominous</i> and <i>augural cock</i>, the reason will be plain at once to anyone who looks at the hoopoe, whose appearance is so portentous that no one who sees this bird fails to be astonished. <i>Ovid</i>, <i>Metamorphoses</i>, book 6, Fable 7:

‎— — <i>on whose head a crest stands up,</i> <i>an immoderate beak juts out with a very long point;</i> <i>the bird's name is epops; its face seems armed.</i>

‎Accordingly in <i>Aristophanes' Birds</i>, <i>Euelpides</i>, struck by this sight, exclaims thus:

‎Ὦ Ἡράκλεις, τοῦτο τί ποτ᾽ ἐστὶ θηρίον; Τίς ἡ πτέρωσις; τίς ὁ τρόπος τῆς τριλοφίας; [Ō Hērakleis, touto ti pot᾽ esti thērion? Tis hē pterōsis? tis ho tropos tēs trilophias?] <i>By Hercules, what new kind of beast is this?</i> <i>Feathered with what manner of triple crest?</i>

‎Likewise:

‎Τὸ ῥάμφος ἡμῖν σου γέλοιον φαίνεται, [To rhamphos hēmin sou geloion phainetai,] <i>We see that your beak is ridiculous.</i>

‎I pass over the fact that some write that the hoopoe, in the ancient superstition of magicians and augurs, was among the foremost of the birds of omen. The <i>Arabic Pandectarius</i> tells the fable that the stone <i>Quirin</i> <i>betrays secrets and stirs up fantasies if it is placed on the breast of a sleeper, and that it is found in the nest of the hoopoe, which</i>, he says, <i>is wholly a bird of sorcery and augury, as magicians and augurs report.</i> According to <i>Isidore</i>, <i>Origins</i>, book 12, chapter 7: <i>If anyone going to sleep anoints himself with its blood,</i> [III, 112] <i>he will see demons choking him.</i> <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] has much of this sort in the chapter <i>On the properties of the hoopoe</i>: that by fumigation with this or that part of it harmful things are driven from a dovecote; demons are cast out of the body; a man who has been bound by a spell against his marriage and <i>devotatus</i> [bewitched], to use <i>Apuleius'</i> word, is released; that eating its roasted heart with rue sharpens the wit and stirs the memory; that by hanging up its feathers one wins a lawsuit. He also prescribes various love-potions from the same bird, and likewise drugs by which enemies may be removed, places made desolate, eyes knocked out, and other such superstitious, vain, impious and — ἄῤῥητ᾽ ἀμείνω [arrhēt᾽ ameinō] things. <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] treats this same subject in such a way that he scarcely yields to <i>Damir</i>. From this it is clear that the magicians held the hoopoe in the highest wonder as a prodigious animal.

‎The <i>Jews</i> also relate that the דוכיפת <i>dukiphat</i> brought <i>Solomon</i> the שמיר <i>samir</i>, that is, a little worm by whose power the hardest stones of the temple were split. So it is found in <i>Cholin</i>, in the place cited above: דוכיפת שהודו כפות וזהו שהביא שמיר לבית המקדש [col. 347] Dukiphat, <i>the bird whose crest is bound up; it is the one that brought</i> the little worm <i>Samir to the house of the sanctuary.</i> Whoever can may read a long tale about this in the tractate <i>Gittin</i> ch. 7, fol. 68a b. The gist is that <i>Solomon</i>, on the advice of <i>Asmodaeus</i>, king of the demons, sent to the nest of this bird, which it keeps in the mountains among the hardest rocks; and that the chicks found there were shut up by <i>Solomon</i>'s servants in transparent glass. Since the mother no longer had access to them, she brought the worm שמיר <i>samir</i>, at whose touch the glass at once burst apart and was shattered. For the <i>samir</i>, they say, has such power that it splits not only glass but even the hardest of all things. Therefore <i>Moses</i> used it to cut and engrave the gems of the <i>Ephod</i>, and <i>Solomon</i> to dress the stones of the temple without any iron tool. I have thought this worth noting because in <i>Greek</i> writers too the hoopoe is said to break through all obstacles, not indeed by the power of a worm, but by the force of a certain herb. Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 3, chapter 26: Ἔτυχε δὲ ἐν τῷ τοῦ τείχους ἐρημοτέρῳ ὅδε ὁ ὄρνις παιδοποιησάμενος, ἔν τινι ῥήγματι λίθου ὑπὸ τοῦ χρόνου διαστάντι, ἐκεῖν ὁ τοῦ τείχους μελεδωνὸς, ἰδὼν ἔνδον τὰ βρέφη, κατήλειψε τὸν χηραμὸν τῷ πηλῷ· καὶ ὑποστρέψας ὁ ἔποψ, ὡς εἶδεν αὐτὸν ἀποκλεισθέντα, πόαν ἐνόμιζε, καὶ προσήνεγκε τῷ πηλῷ. ὁ δὲ κατεῤῥύη, καὶ προσῆλθε πρὸς τὰ αὐτοῦ ἐκεῖνος τέκνα. εἶτα ἐπὶ νομὴν ἦξεν, αὖθις οὖν ὁ αὐτὸς ἐπήλειψεν ἄνθρωπος· καὶ ἡ ὄρνις τῇ αὐτῇ πόᾳ ἀνέῳξε τὸν χηραμὸν καὶ τὸ τρίτον ἐπράχθη τὰ αὐτά. [Etyche de en tō tou teichous erēmoterō hode ho ornis paidopoiēsamenos, en tini rhēgmati lithou hypo tou chronou diastanti, ekein ho tou teichous meledōnos, idōn endon ta brephē, katēleipse ton chēramon tō pēlō; kai hypostrepsas ho epops, hōs eiden auton apokleisthenta, poan enomize, kai prosēnenke tō pēlō. ho de katerrhyē, kai prosēlthe pros ta autou ekeinos tekna. eita epi nomēn ēxen, authis oun ho autos epēleipsen anthrōpos; kai hē ornis tē autē poa aneōxe ton chēramon kai to triton epratchthē ta auta.] <i>When this bird had hatched its young in the most deserted part of a wall, in a crack of a stone opened by age, and the keeper of the wall, having noticed the chicks inside, had plastered over the crack with mud, the hoopoe on its return, seeing the wall blocked, brought a herb and applied it to the mud. This at once dissolved, and she went in to her chicks, and then went back out to feed. And when the same man had plastered the wall over again, the bird opened it once more with this same herb; and this happened a third time.</i> You see in both accounts chicks shut in by human contrivance [III, 113], and the mother hoopoe opening these barriers by a hidden power in order to help them. So it is very likely that the one fable arose from the other.

‎To the same effect are <i>Solomon</i>'s conversations with the hoopoe in the <i>Sura of the Qur'an</i> entitled <i>The Ant</i>; and the embassy it undertakes for <i>Solomon</i> to the queen of the <i>Sabaeans</i>; and the springs hidden deep in the earth revealed by it to <i>Solomon</i>. Hence the pseudo-<i>Prophet</i> says: لا تقتلوا الهدهد فان كان دليل سليمان علي بعد الما وقربه [lā taqtulū al-hudhud fa-inna kāna dalīl Sulaymān ʿalī buʿd al-mā wa-qurbih] <i>Do not kill the hoopoe, for it used to show</i> Solomon <i>how far off or how near the waters were</i>, that is, how far they lay below the surface of the earth. For, as <i>Ibno Abas</i> relates, كانت له الارض كالزجاجة يري من باطنها [kānat lahu al-arḍ ka-al-zujāja yarī min bāṭinihā] <i>the earth was for it as transparent as glass, so that it saw to the bottom.</i> <i>Naphi</i> objects: كيف يبصر الما من تحت الارض ولا يري الفخ اذا غطي له بقدر اصبع من التراب [kayfa yubṣiru al-mā min taḥt al-arḍ wa-lā yarī al-fakhkh idhā ghuṭṭiya lahu bi-qadr iṣbaʿ min al-turāb] <i>How does it see the waters that are under the earth, and not see a snare when it is covered with dust a finger's breadth deep?</i> And <i>Ibno Abas</i> replies: اذا نزل القضا عمي البصر [idhā nazala al-qaḍā ʿamiya al-baṣar] <i>When the decree</i> (of God) <i>descends</i> (that is, when God so wills), <i>its sight is blinded.</i> Thus, just as the <i>Hebrews</i> do with the bird <i>dukiphath</i>, so the <i>Arabs</i> insert many trifles about the hoopoe into the history of <i>Solomon</i>.

‎I add that there is no apparent reason why the hen, or the attagen, or the urogallus [col. 348] or tetraon should be counted among the unclean birds. Indeed, everyone knows that the use of the hen was permitted to the <i>Jews</i>. And you may read the same about the attagen in <i>Aristeas</i>, a spurious author, to be sure, but nevertheless a <i>Jewish</i> one and very old, since he is cited by <i>Josephus</i> as a genuine writer. His words are: Τῶν γὰρ πτηνῶν οἷς χρώμεθα πάντα ἥμερα καθέστηκε. διαφέρει καθαρότητι, πυροῖς καὶ ὀσπρίοις χρώμενα πρὸς τὴν τροφήν· οἷον περιστεραὶ, τρυγόνες, ἀτταγαὶ, πέρδικες, ἔτι χῆνες, καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ὅσα τοιαῦτα. [Tōn gar ptēnōn hois chrōmetha panta hēmera kathestēke. diapherei katharotēti, pyrois kai ospriois chrōmena pros tēn trophēn; hoion peristerai, trygones, attagai, perdikes, eti chēnes, kai ta alla hosa toiauta.] <i>The birds that we use are all tame; they excel in cleanness and are fed on wheat and pulses, such as doves, turtledoves, attagens, partridges, geese too, and others of that kind.</i> (Instead of ἀτταγαὶ [attagai], <i>attagens</i>, ἀττακοὶ [attakoi] is wrongly read, which is a kind of locust, <i>Levit. XI,</i> 22.) The hen and the attagen, therefore, were permitted to the <i>Jews</i>, and there appears to be no reason why they should have been forbidden to them. But in the case of the hoopoe the matter is clear, since it is a most filthy bird, which builds its nest out of dung and feeds on the most fetid things. <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>History</i>, book 9, chapter 15: Ὁ δὲ ἔποψ τὴν νεοττίαν μάλιστα ποιεῖται ἐκ τῆς ἀνθρωπίνης κόπρου. [Ho de epops tēn neottian malista poieitai ek tēs anthrōpinēs koprou.] <i>The hoopoe makes its nest chiefly out of human excrement.</i> Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 3, [III, 114] chapter 26: Οἱ δὲ ἀντὶ τοῦ πηλοῦ χρίουσι τὴν καλιὰν, ἀποπάτημα ἀνθρώπου περιβαλόντες, τῇ δυσωδίᾳ τε καὶ κακοσμίᾳ ἀνείργοντες καὶ ἀναστέλλοντες τὸ ζῶον τὸ ἑαυτοῖς πολέμιον· [Hoi de anti tou pēlou chriousi tēn kalian, apopatēma anthrōpou peribalontes, tē dysōdia te kai kakosmia aneirgontes kai anastellontes to zōon to heautois polemion;] <i>Instead of mud, they plaster their nest with human excrement, warding off and repelling by the stench and foul smell any animal hostile to them.</i> Pliny, book 10, chapter 29: <i>A bird otherwise filthy in its feeding.</i> <i>Cyril</i>, book 3, <i>On Adoration</i>: Εἶδος δὲ τοῦτο στρουθίου τὰ τῶν τελμάτων ἀεὶ δυσωδέστατα περιθέοντος, καὶ τὰ ἐκ βορβόρου καὶ ἀκαθαρσίας συλλέγοντος εἰς τροφήν· [Eidos de touto strouthiou ta tōn telmatōn aei dysōdestata peritheontos, kai ta ek borborou kai akatharsias syllegontos eis trophēn;] <i>It is a kind of little bird that constantly flies about the most fetid parts of marshes and seeks its food from mire and filth.</i> And on <i>Zechariah</i>, volume 3: Φιλοβόρβορον (ἐστὶ) καὶ κοπροβόρον καὶ ταῖς σκωλήκων ἀγέλαις ἀγρίως ἐπιμαινόμενον, καὶ τροφὴν ἀεὶ ποιεῖσθαι κατειθισμένον τὴν ἐσχάτην ἀκαθαρσίαν· [Philoborboron (esti) kai koproboron kai tais skōlēkōn agelais agriōs epimainomenon, kai trophēn aei poieisthai kateithismenon tēn eschatēn akatharsian;] <i>It is a bird that delights in mire, feeds on dung, rages fiercely against swarms of worms, and is accustomed to take all the most unclean things as food.</i> And in <i>Homily</i> 10 <i>On the Paschal Feasts</i>: Ἀκάθαρτον γὰρ τὸ στρουθίον, φημὶ δὴ τὸν ἔποπα, σκωλήκων τε καὶ περισσευμάτων τῶν ἀπὸ γαστρὸς ὅτι μάλιστα βορόν· [Akatharton gar to strouthion, phēmi dē ton epopa, skōlēkōn te kai perisseumatōn tōn apo gastros hoti malista boron;] <i>The hoopoe, I say, is an unclean little bird, which delights above all in feeding on worms and the excrements of the belly.</i> Jerome <i>on</i> Zechariah <i>ch. 5</i>: <i>They call it</i> Ἔποπα [Epopa] <i>from the fact that it settles on human excrement. They say it is a most filthy bird, always dwelling in tombs, always in human dung. Finally, it is also said to make its nest from it, and to feed its young on the little worms of rotting dung.</i> Hence <i>Isidore</i>, <i>Etymologies</i>, book 12, chapter 7: <i>The Greeks call it</i> upupa, <i>because it settles on human excrement and feeds on stinking dung, a most filthy bird</i> <sup>R2</sup>. Yet I by no means approve this derivation of the <i>Greek</i> name. I prefer to agree with <i>Varro</i>, who derives the name <i>upupa</i> from its call. For it seems constantly to repeat <i>epop</i> or <i>upup</i>. Hence it is told in the myths that <i>Tereus</i>, changed into a hoopoe, cried ποῦ; ποῦ; [pou? pou?] <i>where? where?</i>, as pursuers do, when he was pursuing <i>Procne</i> and <i>Philomela</i>. And the <i>Arabic</i> name of the hoopoe, هدهد [hudhud] <i>hudhud</i>, seems to belong to the same origin. For the calls of birds, since they are not articulate, can be rendered in various ways.

‎Close to the <i>Hebrew</i> דוכיפת <i>dukiphath</i> is the <i>Egyptian</i> KUKUPHA, which means the hoopoe. Hence in the <i>Coptic Lexicon</i> ΚΟΥΚΟΥΦᾺ [koukoupha] is rendered <i>in Arabic</i> by الهدهد [al-hudhud] <i>alhudhud</i>, that is, the <i>hoopoe</i>. The <i>Syriac</i> ܩܝܩܘܦܐ [קיקופא] <i>kikupha</i> too [III, 115] is so explained in <i>Ferrarius's Syriac Lexicon</i>. And that the hoopoe is meant by the name <i>cucupha</i> is clear from these words of <i>Horapollo</i>, book 1, chapter 52: Εὐχαριστίαν γράφοντες (Αἰγύπτιοι) κουκουφὰν ζωγραφοῦσι, διότι τοῦτο μόνον τῶν ἀλόγων ζώων, ἐπειδὰν ὑπὸ τῶν γονέων ἐκτραφῇ, γηράσασιν αὐτοῖς, τὴν [col. 349] αὐτὴν ἀνταποδίδωσι χάριν. [Eucharistian graphontes (Aigyptioi) koukouphan zōgraphousi, dioti touto monon tōn alogōn zōōn, epeidan hypo tōn goneōn ektraphē, gērasasin autois, tēn autēn antapodidōsi charin.] <i>When they signify gratitude, they</i> (the Egyptians) <i>paint a cucupha, because this alone of the dumb animals, after it has been reared by its parents, repays them the same kindness when they are worn out with age.</i> For the <i>Egyptians</i> hold hoopoes in honour, as <i>Aelian</i> testifies, <i>History</i>, book 10, chapter 16, because they are πρὸς τοὺς γειναμένους εὐσεβεῖς [pros tous geinamenous eusebeis], <i>dutiful towards their parents.</i>

‎Now see, reader, a remarkable metamorphosis of the hoopoe into a lark, whose author is the <i>Pandectarius</i>: <i>Alauda</i> [lark], he says, <i>is in Arabic the bird hoopoe.</i> On the contrary, <i>alauda</i> in Arabic is a meaningless word. But he made <i>alauda</i> out of <i>alhudhud</i>. Thus Aelian observes, <i>History</i> book 16, chapter 5, that the <i>Athenians</i> tell of the lark the same story that the <i>Brahmins</i> tell of the hoopoe.

Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer] proposed another etymology in <i>Supplemm. ad Lexx. [=J. D. Michaelis, Supplementa ad lexica Hebraica] Hebrr.</i> vol. 2, p. 416. He thinks that the Hebrew name of the <i>hoopoe</i>, דוכיפת, is compounded from דו, Arabic ذو [dhū], <i>en-</i> [III, 109] <i>dowed with</i>, <i>having</i>, and Chaldee כִיפָה, <i>ambergris</i>, so that this bird would be called <i>endowed with ambergris</i> ironically, on account of its dungy smell. But Tychsen rightly says on the <i>Physiologus Syrus</i>, Chapter 22, §3, p. 119: "The etymology is indeed ingenious, if only it were true. For the report that the hoopoe feeds on human excrement and builds its nest from it is very unreliable, and arose from a deception of the senses. For it does not feed on excrement, but, like hens, searches through it diligently for seeds or insects, sometimes carrying away a strong smell from it, as I often noticed in a hoopoe which I had given as a companion to my hens at Bützow. Otherwise it was clean of smell, and fed on insects, berries of black elder, red currant, gooseberry, raspberry, vine, etc., and on seeds of garlic, leek, celery, etc." So Tychsen; and this is also confirmed by Buffon, <i>Hist. Nat. des oiseaux</i>, vol. 6, p. 439 f., and by Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde], part 5, ch. 7, p. 71. But the etymology proposed by Michaelis is also opposed by the fact that neither דו nor כִיפָה is a <i>Hebrew</i> word: one is Arabic, the other Chaldee. Nor is Oedmann's opinion any more acceptable, who thinks that דוכיפת is compounded from דוך, <i>cock</i>, and יפה, <i>beautiful</i>, since יְפַת is used only in the construct state. Preferable, therefore, seems the etymology that Bochart put in first place, that דוכיפת is properly the <i>mountain cock</i>, or <i>wild cock</i>.
