# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 22: Nine passages of the Prophets that some interpreters refer to sparrows, most of them not certain (vol. 2, pp. 727–735)

### ‎[II, 727] [col. 150] CHAPTER XXII.

### ‎<i>Nine passages of the Prophets which some interpreters refer to sparrows, for the most part rather uncertain. I. Levit. XIV. passim. II. Job 40:24. III. Ps 10:1. IV. Ps 84:4. V. Ps 102:8. VI. Ps 104:16. VII. Ps 124:7. VIII. Eccl 12:4. IX. Lam 3:52.</i>

‎Interpreters do not sufficiently agree in which passages of Scripture צפור <i>tsippor</i> is taken for the <i>sparrow</i>. For of the nine passages that are adduced, most are very doubtful.

‎I. The first is <i>Levit. XIV.</i>, at the beginning of the chapter and at the end, where it treats of two צפורים <i>tsipporim</i>, by which, one being killed and the other released, the leper was purified, and also a house infected with leprosy. Most hold that the <i>tsipporim</i> there are <i>sparrows</i>, as <i>Jerome</i>, <i>Pagninus</i> [=Santes Pagnino, Latin Bible (1528) and Thesaurus linguae sanctae], <i>Arias</i> [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot], the <i>Genevans</i> and others render it. But that <i>birds</i> in general are meant will be clear to anyone who reads attentively verse four: <i>And let him that is to be cleansed take</i> שתי צפורים חיות טהרות, <i>two living clean</i> tsipporim. This does not fit the <i>sparrow</i>, whether it is held to have been clean or unclean. For it could not be called clean if by the Law it was unclean. Conversely, if it was clean, the Law would have commanded in vain that clean sparrows be taken, since there were no unclean ones. Nowhere is it commanded that clean doves [col. 151] or turtledoves be offered, because all were clean. It appears, therefore, that צפורים <i>tsipporim</i> there are <i>birds</i> in general; and since some of these were clean and others unclean, God commands that clean ones be taken, for instance doves or turtledoves, which are called הצפור in <i>Gen 15:10</i>, or any others, provided they are clean and permitted as food according to the law of <i>Deut 14:11.</i> כל צפור טהרה תאכלו, <i>Every clean</i> tsippor <i>you shall eat.</i> Thus, as we showed above, some understand <i>hens</i> here too, and the <i>Greek</i> ὀρνίθια [ornithia] can be referred to this. Nor do the <i>Hebrews</i> specify a species, provided they are צפרים דרור טהורות, <i>free and clean birds.</i> On the rite of this purification <i>Maimonides</i> [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] has much that is notable in the treatise <i>On Leprosy</i>, chapter 11, which does not belong here.

‎II. The second passage is in the description of the <i>Leviathan</i>, <i>Job 40:24</i> (var. <i>29.</i>), and in <i>Hebrew</i> it reads thus: <i>Will you play with him</i> כצפור, <i>as with a</i> tsippor? <i>And will you bind him for your girls?</i> And in the <i>Greek</i> version: Παίξῃ δὲ ἐν αὐτῷ ὥσπερ ὀρνέῳ; ἢ δήσεις αὐτὸν ὥσπερ [col. 152] στρουθίον παιδίῳ; [Paixē de en autō hōsper orneō; ē dēseis auton hōsper strouthion paidiō?] <i>Will you play with him as with a bird? and will you bind him like a sparrow for a child?</i> And in the <i>Arabic</i>: هل تضحك عليه مثل العصفور [hal taḍḥaku ʿalayhi mithla al-ʿuṣfūr] <i>Will you play with him as with a sparrow</i>, etc. Those who pursue mysteries refer this to the devil, as though through baptism and conversion he were handed over to us bound, [II, 728] like a little sparrow to a child. <i>Origen</i>, of the faithful man: Ὅ γε δράκων αὐτῷ ὥσπερ στρουθίον παραδέδοται παιδίῳ· [Ho ge drakōn autō hōsper strouthion paradedotai paidiō] <i>The dragon has been handed over to him as a sparrow to a child.</i> And shortly after: Τῇ χάριτι τοῦ Κυρίου, ὡς στρουθίον ὑποχείριον, ὁ Σατανᾶς δεδεμένος γεγένηται τοῖς νηπίοις, καὶ θηλάζουσιν, ὧν ἐκ στόματος κατηρτίσατο αἶνον ὁ Θεός· [Tē chariti tou Kyriou, hōs strouthion hypocheirion, ho Satanas dedemenos gegenētai tois nēpiois, kai thēlazousin, hōn ek stomatos katērtisato ainon ho Theos] <i>By the grace of the Lord, like a tame sparrow,</i> Satan <i>has been bound for children and sucklings, out of whose mouth God has perfected his praise.</i> Chrysostom: Δήσας τὸν ἐχθρὸν ὁ Κύριος ἡμῶν δέδωκε καταπαίζειν αὐτοῦ, ὡς στρουθίου, τοῖς στραφεῖσι, καὶ γενομένοις ὡς τὰ παιδία. [Dēsas ton echthron ho Kyrios hēmōn dedōke katapaizein autou, hōs strouthiou, tois strapheisi, kai genomenois hōs ta paidia.] <i>To those who have been converted and have become as infants, our Lord has handed over the adversary, bound, to be mocked like a sparrow.</i> Alluding to this, the author of the life of <i>Pachomius</i>, chapter 49, attributes these words to the devils: <i>Since the wonderful incarnation</i> of Christ <i>took place on earth, we have begun to exist wholly without strength</i>; <i>so that we are mocked like sparrows by those who believe in his name.</i> Leaving these aside, we ask only whether the <i>Hebrew</i> is well rendered: <i>Will you play with him as with a sparrow? and will you bind him for your young ones?</i> That a mention of the sparrow is fitting here is suggested by that very well-known line of <i>Catullus</i>,

‎<i>Sparrow, darling of my girl, with whom she is wont to play, whom to hold in her lap, to whom, as it seeks it, to give her fingertip and to provoke its sharp bites.</i>

‎Hence <i>Arnobius</i>, book 7: <i>as little boys</i> (<i>to spare their feelings and keep from crying</i>) <i>are given little sparrows, poplar toys, loaves, with which they can divert themselves.</i> And an <i>Arab poet</i>, whom <i>Ibno Rasik</i> cites in the book entitled غرايب [gharāyib] <i>garaibo</i>, that is, <i>Curiosities</i>:

‎كعصفورة في كف طفل يسوخها * حياض الردي والطفل يلهو ويلعب * [ka-ʿuṣfūratin fī kaffi ṭiflin yasūkhuhā * ḥiyāḍa al-radī wa-l-ṭiflu yalhū wa-yalʿabu *]

‎<i>Like a hen sparrow in a boy's hand, which he plunges into the evil troughs while he jests and plays.</i> Yet in <i>Job</i> there is no need to understand the <i>sparrow</i> precisely, since there is no lack of other birds with which boys and girls are equally amused. <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Captivi</i>, Act 5:

‎— — <i>as jackdaws, or ducks, or quails are given to patrician boys to play with.</i>

‎<i>Pliny</i>, writing to <i>Clemens</i> about the son of <i>Regulus</i> in Letter 2 of book 4: <i>"The boy had many ponies, both harnessed and unharnessed; he had dogs large and small; he had nightingales, parrots, blackbirds."</i> Julius Capitolinus <i>in</i> Clodius Albinus: <i>"Seven small eagles were brought from the nest and placed, as if in play, around the boy's cradle."</i> <i>Pausanias</i> in the <i>Corinthiaca</i>: Κόκκυγα δὲ ἐπὶ τῷ σκήπτρῳ καθῆσθαί φασι, λέγοντες [II, 729] τὸν Δία, ὅτε ἤρα παρθένου τῆς Ἥρας ἐς τοῦτον τὸν ὄρνιθα ἀλλαγῆναι· τὴν δὲ ἅτε παίγνιον θηρᾶσαι· [Kokkyga de epi tō skēptrō kathēsthai phasi, legontes ton Dia, hote ēra parthenou tēs Hēras es touton ton ornitha allagēnai; tēn de hate paignion thērasai] <i>"They say the cuckoo sits on the sceptre</i> of Jove <i>because</i> Jupiter, <i>seized with love for the maiden</i> Juno, <i>turned himself into that bird, which the girl caught as a plaything."</i> Hence <i>Jupiter Coccygius</i>, whom the same author mentions further on. Accordingly <i>Jerome</i> explains the passage of <i>Job</i> we mentioned thus: <i>"Will you play with him as with a bird? Or will you bind him for your maidservants?"</i>, making no mention of a <i>sparrow</i>. And most of the more recent interpreters follow the same.

‎III. The third passage is <i>Ps 10:1</i> (<i>in the Hebrew XI, 1.</i>), where the <i>Greek</i> version has Μεταναστεύου ἐπὶ τὰ ὄρη ὡς στρουθίον· [Metanasteuou epi ta orē hōs strouthion], and the Vulgate, <i>"Flee to the mountain like a sparrow."</i> The <i>Arabic</i> also has مثل العصفور [mithl al-ʿuṣfūr], <i>like a sparrow.</i> And <i>Apollinaris</i> in [col. 153] his Metaphrase:

‎Στρουθὸν ὀρεσσιβότοισιν ὅπως θρώσκοντα νομῆσι, [Strouthon oressibotoisin hopōs thrōskonta nomēsi]

‎<i>"Like a sparrow leaping in mountain pastures."</i> And <i>Theodoret</i> in his Commentary: Τί δή ποτέ φησι παραινεῖτέ μοι φυγεῖν, καὶ τὰ ὄρη περινοστεῖν στρουθίου δίκην ἐπτοημένου; [Ti dē pote phēsi paraineite moi phygein, kai ta orē perinostein strouthiou dikēn eptoēmenou?] <i>"Why," he says, "do you urge me to flee and wander over the mountains like a frightened sparrow?"</i> But <i>Jerome</i> does better, as I at least think, with <i>"Fly over to the mountain like a bird"</i>, as do most of the more recent interpreters. For the sparrow is not a mountain bird. And <i>David</i>, who is the subject here, says that <i>Saul</i> is pursuing him <i>as one would hunt</i>, not a sparrow, but <i>the bird Kore in the mountains</i>, 1 Sam 26:20, that is, the <i>woodcock</i>, as we have shown. It would be vain to object that near Lake <i>Larius</i> in <i>Italy</i> there is a bird which they call the mountain sparrow. For that bird is not truly a sparrow, but of the blackbird kind, as <i>Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] proves at length, p. 608. Nor is this passage of <i>Galen</i>, <i>De ingenio sanitatis</i> ch. 2, more to the point: <i>"Among birds, partridges are praised, together with all mountain sparrows."</i> For there not sparrows properly so called are meant, which are very hot and incite lust, but those small birds in general that live in the mountains. For it is agreed that small birds are sometimes called στρουθία [strouthia]. <i>Moschopulus</i>, Περὶ σχεδῶν [Peri schedōn] p. 178: Τρώγλη· ἡ τρύπα, ἀφ᾽ οὗ τρωγλίτης, ὃς καὶ στρουθὸς λέγεται· στρουθία δὲ οὐδετέρως πάντα τὰ μικρὰ τῶν ὀρνίθων. [Trōglē; hē trypa, aph᾽ hou trōglitēs, hos kai strouthos legetai; strouthia de oudeterōs panta ta mikra tōn ornithōn.] So also <i>Phavorinus</i> under Στρουθία [Strouthia].

‎IV. The fourth passage is <i>Ps. LXXXIV</i>, 4. גם צפור מצאה בית, for which the <i>Greeks</i> have Καὶ γὰρ στρουθίον εὗρεν ἑαυτῷ οἰκίαν [Kai gar strouthion heuren heautō oikian]. And the <i>Arabic</i> in the same sense: لان العصفور وجد له مسكنا [li-anna al-ʿuṣfūr wajada lahu maskanan]. And <i>the Vulgate translator</i>: <i>"For the sparrow has found itself a home."</i> And <i>Jerome</i>: <i>"Since the sparrow has found a home."</i> And <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his <i>Lexicon</i> proves from this passage that צפור <i>tsippor</i> is not a general name for all birds but proper to a certain species, because in the following hemistich דרור <i>deror</i> is the <i>swallow</i>, as he at least asserts. <i>"Therefore,"</i> he says, אחר שאחרון פרט הראשון גם כן פרט, [II, 730] <i>"since the latter</i> (that is, דרור <i>deror</i>) <i>is the name of a particular bird, so also is the former</i> (namely צפור <i>tsippor</i>)." Accordingly in his <i>Commentaries</i> he likewise holds that צפור there is the little bird which the <i>Spaniards</i> call פשׂרא <i>paxaro</i>, that is, the <i>sparrow</i>. So this verse is to be rendered according to <i>Kimchi</i>'s mind: <i>"Even the sparrow has found a home, and the swallow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young, namely your altars, O God of hosts."</i> We agree with this view, except that we have interpreted דרור <i>deror</i> as the <i>wood pigeon</i> instead of the <i>swallow</i>. And by the name <i>altars</i>, by synecdoche of part for whole, we have understood the <i>tabernacle</i>, on whose beams both sparrows and wood pigeons nested, as has already been said elsewhere.

‎V. The fifth passage is <i>Ps 102:8 "I have kept watch, and I have become</i> כצפור בודד על גג", where the <i>Greeks</i> have ὡσεὶ στρουθίον μονάζων ἐπὶ δώματι· [hōsei strouthion monazōn epi dōmati], and <i>the Vulgate translator</i>, <i>"like a solitary sparrow on the roof"</i>; and one of the <i>Arabic</i> versions, <i>"I was in my solitude</i> كالعصفور [ka-l-ʿuṣfūr] <i>like a sparrow upon the roof"</i>; and the other, <i>"I have become like a sparrow that stays alone upon the roof."</i> And <i>Apollinaris</i>:

‎Ἢ στρουθόν τιν᾽ ἄϋπνον ἐόντ᾽ ἐν δώματι μοῦνον, [Ē strouthon tin᾽ aypnon eont᾽ en dōmati mounon]

‎<i>Or some sparrow, which is sleepless, alone on the roof.</i> For Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] said above that the sparrow nests تحت السقوف خوف من الجوارح [taḥta al-suqūf khawf min al-jawāriḥ] <i>under the roof, for fear of birds of prey.</i> And Suidas, under Νυκτικόραξ [Nyktikorax], describes the sparrow as δειλὸν ὄρνεον [deilon orneon], <i>a timid bird,</i> and one that ὑπὸ ἀγωνίας ἐλαύνει τὸν ὕπνον [hypo agōnias elaunei ton hypnon], <i>drives away sleep out of anxiety.</i> These things will seem to bear most closely on this passage, in which David, as some would have it, says that he kept watch like a sparrow that is alone on the roof. The same point is thought to be supported by the fact that, instead of <i>passer solitarius</i> [solitary sparrow], Augustine reads <i>passer singularis</i> [single sparrow]. In Greek this would be στρουθὸς μόνιος [strouthos monios], or στρουθίον [col. 154] μόνιον [strouthion monion]. And the vernacular word <i>moineau</i> for the sparrow will seem to have been formed from μόνιος [monios]. Finally, around Trent there is a bird which the common people call the <i>passer solitarius</i> [solitary sparrow], which wanders alone and usually stays on roofs. Accordingly, like many of the ancients, nearly all the more recent interpreters, Latin, French, Italian, German, Dutch and English, understand the sparrow here. Jerome, however, renders it otherwise: <i>I was like a solitary bird upon the roof.</i> Nor is there any of the Hebrews who points out that the sparrow is meant here, as in the preceding passage. And indeed I think nothing is further from the Prophet's mind. For these words belong entirely to the description of a nocturnal bird. I am surprised that this has not yet occurred to the interpreters, since all the circumstances of the passage openly proclaim it. For whoever speaks in this Psalm, since, worn out by heavy grief, he was fleeing the company of men and pouring out continual groans from the depths of his breast, compares himself with funereal birds, which are both solitary and have something mournful and ominous in their voice. <i>By reason of the voice of my groaning,</i> he says, <i>my bones cleave to my flesh. I am become like a pelican of the wilderness. I was like an owl</i> (or [II, 731] <i>a horned owl</i>) <i>of the desert places.</i> So he speaks in the two verses immediately preceding. Then, continuing the comparison he has begun, he says: <i>I kept watch, and was like a solitary bird upon the roof. I kept watch</i>, that is, <i>I spent a sleepless night.</i> Accordingly the Chaldee has there שקדת כל ליליא, <i>I kept watch all night.</i> That is, like the light-shunning nocturnal birds, which, because they hate the light, after hiding by day come out of their hiding places at night to watch for prey. The Philosopher, <i>Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 34: Γλαῦκες δὲ, καὶ νυκτικόρακες, καὶ τὰ λοιπὰ, ὅσα τῆς ἡμέρας ἀδυνατεῖ βλέπειν, τῆς νυκτὸς μὲν θηρεύοντα τὴν τροφὴν αὐτοῖς πορίζεται· [Glaukes de, kai nyktikorakes, kai ta loipa, hosa tēs hēmeras adynatei blepein, tēs nyktos men thēreuonta tēn trophēn autois porizetai] <i>Owls and nycticoraces, and the other birds that cannot see by day, obtain their food by hunting at night.</i> Hence Athenaeus must be corrected, who condenses these words thus, book 8, ch. 12: Γλαῦκες δὲ, καὶ κόρακες ἡμέρας ἀδυνατοῦσι βλέπειν, διὸ νύκτωρ τὴν τροφὴν ἑαυτοῖς θηρεύουσιν· [Glaukes de, kai korakes hēmeras adynatousi blepein, dio nyktōr tēn trophēn heautois thēreuousin]; and instead of κόρακες [korakes] one must write νυκτικόρακες [nyktikorakes]. Accordingly all writers attribute almost continual wakefulness to nocturnal birds. And in Aristophanes' <i>Lysistrata</i> a certain woman speaks thus:

‎Ἐγὼ δ᾽ ὑπὸ τῶν γλαυκῶν γε τάλαιν᾽ ἀπόλλυμαι, Ταῖς ἀγρυπνίαισι κακκαβιζουσῶν ἀεί. [Egō d' hypo tōn glaukōn ge talain' apollymai, Tais agrypniaisi kakkabizousōn aei.]

‎<i>But I, wretched woman, have perished because of the owls, which were always hooting in the night watches.</i> And <i>Aelian, Hist.</i> book 1, ch. 29, on the owl: Νύκτωρ μὲν ἀγρυπνεῖ· [Nyktōr men agrypnei] <i>By night it keeps awake.</i> And of the same bird <i>Varro, De ling. Lat.</i> book 4: <i>By night it sings and keeps awake.</i> With this in view, then, the <i>Psalmist</i> says: <i>I have watched, and was like a solitary bird upon the roof.</i> Some, however, refer this to the sparrow, as though it kept awake for fear, which <i>Suidas</i> has from <i>Theodoret</i>. But you will read nothing of the kind in those who have written on the nature of birds. And those who keep sparrows at home know that they sleep at night, like all birds except the nocturnal ones. Otherwise, since they are awake by day, they would never sleep at all. Moreover, צפור בודד, <i>a solitary bird</i>, is rightly said of the eagle-owl or the owl, because it lives in desert places, as was said in the preceding verse. Hence <i>Damir</i>: وكنية الانثي ام الخراب [wa-kunyat al-unthī umm al-kharāb] <i>And the female</i> (owl) <i>is surnamed mother of desolation.</i> And further on: البوم اصناف وكلها تهب الخلوة بانفسها والتفرد [al-būm aṣnāf wa-kulluhā thb al-khalwa bi-anfusihā wa-l-tafarrud] <i>There are several species of owls, all of which love seclusion and solitude.</i> Therefore, just as the <i>wild ass</i> is called בודד <i>boded</i>, that is, <i>solitary</i>, in <i>Hos 8:9</i>, because it dwells in the wilderness, by the same reasoning the eagle-owl or owl is here rightly called צפור בודד, <i>bird boded</i>, that is, <i>solitary</i>, since it nests in places very far removed from human traffic. Accordingly <i>Pliny</i>, book 29, ch. 4, mocking the magi who prescribe remedies for the hair from the egg of the eagle-owl, says: <i>Who, I ask, could ever have seen an eagle-owl's egg, when to have seen the bird itself is a por-</i> [col. 155] <i>tent?</i> And <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] on الصدي [al-ṣadā], that is, the <i>eagle-owl</i>: طاير يصر بالليل يقفر قفرانا [ṭāyir yaṣirr bi-l-layl yaqfur qafrānan] <i>It is a bird that shrieks at night and lives in desert</i> [II, 732] <i>places</i>. Indeed, to this point belongs the fact that in <i>Arabic</i> it is called صدي <i>tsada</i>, as in <i>Chaldee</i> צדיא <i>tsadja</i>. For צדו <i>tsadu</i> or צדות <i>tsaduth</i> means to the <i>Chaldeans</i> <i>waste</i>, <i>desolation</i>; and the verb צדה <i>tseda</i> means <i>to be laid waste</i>, <i>to be made desolate</i>. In this sense it is used in the <i>Niphal</i> by <i>Zephaniah</i>, ch. 3, v. 6. Thus from the very name which the <i>Chaldeans</i>, <i>Syrians</i> and <i>Arabs</i> gave the eagle-owl, you may infer that it is a solitary bird, living in desolate places. But the eagle-owl is also called a <i>solitary bird</i> because it is not a gregarious animal but one that wanders alone. Hence the <i>Poet</i>, in book 4 of the <i>Aeneid</i>:

‎<i>And the eagle-owl alone on the rooftops with funereal song was seen to lament, and to draw out its long notes into weeping.</i>

‎But why should the sparrow be called <i>solitary</i>, since it is a gregarious bird and nests under the roofs of houses? I admit that within the sparrow kind some are wild. Hence <i>Alexander of Myndus</i> teaches, in <i>Athenaeus</i> book 9, δύο γένη εἶναι τῶν στρουθῶν, τὸ μὲν ἥμερον, τὸ δὲ ἄγριον [dyo genē einai tōn strouthōn, to men hēmeron, to de agrion], <i>that there are two kinds of sparrows, one domestic, the other wild</i>. But wild sparrows do not betake themselves to the roofs of houses, as does the bird of which the <i>Psalmist</i> speaks, whom he describes upon the roof. Therefore, if a sparrow is meant, it cannot be the wild sparrow but only the domestic one. They answer that the domestic sparrow is called <i>solitary</i> because it has been bereaved of its mate. This might be tolerable if the sparrow, like the turtledove or the dove, remained widowed after losing its spouse. But sparrows live by other customs: it is a most lustful animal, so given to <i>Venus</i> that it covers the female, if not a hundred times, as the <i>Arabs</i> report, at least seven times in an hour. Hence the <i>poets</i> yoke it to the chariot of <i>Venus</i>. Bound by no law, then, it rushes into promiscuous <i>love</i> and has no fixed spouse. So, as it was never a husband, neither does it become a widower. That is why the books of the <i>Philosophers</i> say nothing of a solitary sparrow. And those err by the whole breadth of heaven who derive the <i>French</i> name of the sparrow from the <i>Greek</i> μόνιος [monios]. For μόνιος [monios] is an epithet of the boar, not of the sparrow. And to us <i>moineau</i> is something quite different, namely μοναχίσκος [monachiskos], <i>monachulus</i> [little monk]. By this name the male is called, because, like monks, it seems to be hooded. Something else again is the <i>passer solitarius</i> [solitary sparrow] of the <i>people of Trent</i>. For it belongs not to the sparrows but to the blackbird kind, which the <i>Philosopher</i> describes as βαιὸς [baios], <i>dusky</i>, and ἐπὶ τῶν πετρῶν, καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν κεράμων τὰς διατριβὰς ποιῶν [epi tōn petrōn, kai epi tōn keramōn tas diatribas poiōn], <i>living on rocks and roof-tiles</i>. So this is nothing to the point. Now if you ask why the <i>Psalmist</i> describes a nocturnal bird, say the eagle-owl or the owl, upon the roof, the answer is ready. For although eagle-owls and owls nest in desert places, yet at night they are accustomed sometimes to come to towns and cultivated places and to sound their funereal song from the roofs and gables of houses. There is no one who has not heard this at some time. So nothing is more frequent in the <i>poets</i>. Ovid, <i>Metam.</i> book 6, Fable 7:

‎[II, 733] — — — — <i>and on the roof the unholy eagle-owl brooded, and sat on the gable of the bridal chamber.</i>

‎And in the <i>Ibis</i>, verse 223:

‎<i>The nocturnal eagle-owl sat on the opposite gable.</i>

‎<i>Tibullus</i>, book 1, <i>Elegy</i> 6:

‎— — — <i>let the violent screech-owl sing from the roofs.</i>

‎<i>Propertius</i>, book 4, in the Letter of <i>Arethusa</i> to <i>Lycotas</i>:

‎<i>Or whether the owl moaned, perched on the neighbouring beam.</i>

‎And the greatest <i>Poet</i>, book 1 of the <i>Georgics</i>:

‎— — — — <i>from the highest rooftop</i> <i>the owl plies her late songs in vain.</i>

‎And in the passage cited a little earlier:

‎<i>And alone on the rooftops the eagle-owl with funereal song.</i>

‎But examples of this kind also occur everywhere in the <i>Historians</i>. <i>Lampridius</i>, on the [col. 156] death of the emperor <i>Commodus</i>: <i>An eagle-owl was also caught above his bedchamber, both at</i> Rome <i>and at</i> Lavinium. <i>Above the bedchamber</i>, that is, <i>above the roof of the bedchamber</i>. <i>Dio</i> also, in his <i>Commodus</i>: Ὁ βύας ἐπ᾽ αὐτοῦ ἔβυξεν [Ho byas ep᾽ autou ebyxen], <i>the eagle-owl hooted upon it</i> (that is, upon the roof of the <i>Capitol</i>). For so some read instead of ἀπ᾽ αὐτοῦ [ap᾽ autou], <i>from it</i>. And in his <i>Augustus</i> an eagle-owl portended the war of <i>Actium</i>, flying into the temple of <i>Concord</i>, καὶ ἐπὶ τοὺς ἄλλους ἐπικαθήμενος [kai epi tous allous epikathēmenos], <i>and perching on the others</i> (that is, on the roofs of other buildings). Finally <i>Ammianus</i>, book 30, on the death of <i>Valentinian</i>: <i>While he was still staying at</i> Sabaria, <i>an eagle-owl perched on the rooftops of the royal bath and hooted funereal notes</i>, etc. We conclude, therefore, that the <i>Psalmist's</i> solitary bird, which keeps watch at night and perches on roofs, is not the sparrow, as they would have it, but the eagle-owl or the owl. The sparrow does indeed nest under the roof, but not על גג, <i>upon the roof</i>, as the <i>Psalmist</i> says.

‎VI. The sixth passage is <i>Ps 104:16</i>: <i>The trees of God shall be satisfied, the cedars of</i> Lebanon, <i>which he planted, where</i> צפורים tsipporim <i>make their nests</i>. <i>Trees of God</i>, that is, <i>very lofty trees</i>. So also <i>cedars of God</i>, Ps 80:11; <i>mountains of God</i>, Ps 36:7; <i>flame of God</i>, Song 8:6, that is, <i>a most vehement flame</i>; <i>wrestlings of God</i>, Gen 30:5, that is, <i>most severe wrestlings</i>. Thus <i>trees of God</i> and <i>cedars of Lebanon</i> are put forward as synonyms, since cedars are among the tallest of trees. And the <i>trees of God</i> and <i>cedars of Lebanon</i> are said to be <i>satisfied</i>, namely with rain, of which verse 13 speaks. And God is said to have <i>planted</i> them, like the aloe trees in <i>Num 24:6</i>, because they grow of their own accord, by God's will alone, without human labour and care. It is added that צפורים <i>tsipporim</i> <i>nest</i> in them, that is, <i>sparrows</i>, as many think. For so the LXX: Ἐκεῖ [II, 734] στρουθία ἐννοσσεύσουσι [Ekei strouthia ennosseusousi]. And <i>Symmachus</i>: Ὅπου στρουθία ἐννοσσεύσει [Hopou strouthia ennosseusei]. And the <i>Vulgate translator</i>: <i>There the sparrows shall make their nests.</i> And <i>Apollinaris</i>:

‎Κεῖσε νεηγενέων πέλεται στρουθῶν ἀλεωρὴ [Keise neēgeneōn peletai strouthōn aleōrē],

‎<i>There is the refuge of newly hatched sparrows.</i> And both Arabic versions: هناك عششت العصافير [hunāka ʿashshashat al-ʿaṣāfīr] <i>There the sparrows nest.</i> But <i>Aquila</i>, it seems, does better: Ἐκεῖ ὄρνεα νοσσεύσουσιν [Ekei ornea nosseusousin]. And <i>Jerome</i>: <i>There the birds shall make their nests.</i> For the summit of <i>Lebanon</i>, where the cedars are, is inaccessible to sparrows, which, as we have already shown, are not mountain birds.

‎VII. The seventh passage is <i>Psal. CXXIV</i>, 7: <i>Our soul has escaped</i> כצפור, <i>like a</i> tsippor, <i>from the snare of the fowlers</i>. The Greeks: ὡς στρυθίον [hōs strythion]; the Vulgate: <i>like a sparrow</i>; the Arabic likewise: مثل العصفور [mithl al-ʿuṣfūr] <i>like the sparrow</i>; Apollinaris: στρουθοῦ πανομοίϊος [strouthou panomoiios], <i>entirely like a sparrow</i>; but Jerome: <i>as a bird</i>. This I approve more, because sparrows and similar little birds are caught with birdlime rather than with a snare. This is taught by <i>Baal Aruch</i> [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome] [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh] on the word שפשף, which signifies <i>a pole smeared with birdlime for catching little birds</i>.

‎VIII. The eighth is <i>Eccles. XII</i>, 4 (var. 6), where the <i>Greeks</i> say of the old man: Καὶ ἀναστήσεται εἰς φωνὴν τοῦ στρουθίου [Kai anastēsetai eis phōnēn tou strouthiou], <i>And he shall rise up at the voice of the sparrow.</i> But Jerome: <i>And he shall rise up at the voice of the bird.</i> And the Chaldee: <i>And you shall be roused from your sleep</i> על עסק קול דעופא, <i>because of the voice of the bird</i>, that is, at the song of a bird, which some take to be the <i>cock</i>, and cockcrow to be meant. Just as the cock, which wakes people with its morning song, is simply called <i>ales</i> [the bird] in these lines of the <i>Poet of the Amores</i>, book 1, <i>Eleg.</i> 6:

‎<i>And now Lucifer drives forth his frosty axles,</i> <i>and the bird rouses wretched men to their work.</i>

‎Others prefer to understand the song of the <i>sparrow</i> or of a <i>little bird</i>, by which, however faint, the old man is woken from sleep, while the young sleep so deeply that they do not hear even the crash of thunder. Nor is it easy to decide which version is sounder. For we have amply refuted those who deny that the cock, or any of the larger birds, is signified by the word צפור. It can also be taken of the <i>morning chatter of birds</i>, which in <i>Arabic</i> is called by a most proper word, [col. 157] زقزقة [zaqzaqa] <i>zakzaka</i>. Alcamus: الزقزقة صوت طير عند الصبح [al-zaqzaqa ṣawt ṭayr ʿinda al-ṣubḥ] <i>the chatter of birds about dawn</i>.

‎IX. The ninth passage reads thus, <i>Thren. III</i>, 32: <i>My enemies hunt me</i> כצפור, <i>like a</i> tsippor, <i>deservedly</i> [sic]. Here the <i>Greeks</i>: ὡς στρουθίον [hōs strouthion]; but <i>Jerome</i>: <i>like a bird</i>. This I would rather take of the larger birds, on the catching of which effort is more willingly spent, and with greater profit.

‎[II, 735] In <i>Tobit</i> the <i>Latin</i> copy disagrees with the <i>Greek</i> on this word, <i>Tob 2:9</i> (var. <i>11.</i>). For in the <i>Greek</i> <i>Tobit</i> says, Καὶ οὐκ ᾔδειν ὅτι στρουθία ἐν τῷ τοίχῳ ἐστί· καὶ, τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν μου ἀνεωγότων, ἀφώδευσαν τὰ στρουθία θερμὸν εἰς τοὺς ὀφθαλμούς μου [Kai ouk ēdein hoti strouthia en tō toichō esti; kai, tōn ophthalmōn mou aneōgotōn, aphōdeusan ta strouthia thermon eis tous ophthalmous mou]; <i>And I did not know that there were sparrows in the wall; and, my eyes being open, the sparrows voided warm dung into my eyes.</i> But the <i>Latin</i> relates that it happened one day that, when <i>Tobit</i> had fallen asleep beside a wall, <i>warm dung fell upon him as he slept from a swallows' nest.</i> I do not see how these can be reconciled otherwise than by saying that στρουθία [strouthia] in the <i>Greek</i> there are not <i>sparrows</i> but <i>little birds</i>, just as <i>Moschopulus</i> and <i>Phavorinus</i> asserted above that every kind of <i>little bird</i> is called στρουθία [strouthia] in <i>Greek</i>. Or else that by the name of <i>sparrows</i> <i>swallows</i> are meant, after the manner of the <i>Arabs</i>, who call the swallow عصفور الجنة [ʿuṣfūr al-janna] <i>the sparrow of Paradise.</i> Similar is the case of <i>Prov 26:2</i>, where some render the <i>Hebrew</i> דרור <i>deror</i> as <i>swallow</i>, but the <i>Greek</i> interpreters as στρουθοὺς [strouthous], <i>sparrows.</i>
