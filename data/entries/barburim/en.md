# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 19: What the barburim abusim on the table of Solomon were (1 Kgs 4:23) (vol. 2, pp. 698–708)

### ‎[II, 698] [col. 126] Chapter 19 [col. 127]

### ‎<i>What the</i> ברבורים אבוסים, <i>fattened barburim, at the table of Solomon were, 1 Reg. IV, 23.</i> [var. <i>V, 3.</i>].

‎As with the Roman <i>ancilia</i>, so in the various interpretations of the word ברבורים <i>barburim</i> in 1 Reg. IV, 23. [var. V, 3.], the false ones have been fashioned so closely to resemble the true that it is not easy to tell the one from the other. From an inspection of the passage itself it is clear that the ברבורי׳ <i>barburim</i> are listed among the animals that were consumed daily at the table of <i>Solomon</i>. For the sacred text reads: <i>And the provisions of</i> Solomon <i>for each day were ten oxen,</i> etc., <i>besides harts, and roebucks, and buffaloes,</i> ברבורים אבוסים, <i>and fattened</i> barburim. <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and <i>Mercerus</i> [=Jean Mercier (d. 1570), commentaries on Job and Proverbs] explain this as <i>all the choicest animals.</i> Apuleius would have said <i>pastus electiles</i> [choice fare], as in book 10 of the <i>Metamorphoses</i>: <i>Nor could the ass, who alone was present, be affected by such food, and yet every day the choice fare was nowhere to be found.</i> The <i>Greek</i> interpreters were of the same opinion; two versions of theirs survive. One has ἐκλεκτῶν σιτευτὰ [eklektōn siteuta], the other ὀρνίθων ἐκλεκτῶν νομάδων [ornithōn eklektōn nomadōn]. But in both ἐκλεκτῶν [eklektōn] stands for ברבר׳ <i>barburim,</i> and in the second they supply ὀρνίθων [ornithōn] on their own. As if <i>choice fattened birds</i> were meant, and these <i>of the gallinaceous kind.</i> For that is what ὄρνιθες [ornithes] are in that passage, as we proved above <sup>a)</sup>. So too the <i>Talmudists</i>, in the tractate <i>Bava Metsia</i>, ch. 7, fol. 86b, understand choice oxen or choice hens, fattened in a particular way. The passage reads: מאי ברבר׳ אבוס׳ אמר רב שאובסין אותן [col. 128] בעל כרחן ושמואל אמר שאובסין ועומדין מאליהם ורבי יוחנן אמר מביאים תור ממרעיתו בדלא אניס ותרנגולת מאשפתו בדלא אסינא אמר רבי יוחנן מובחר שבבהמות שור מובחר שבעופות תרנגולת אמר אמימר זגתא אוכמתא בי בטניתא דמשתכחא ביני עצרי ולא מציא פסיא קניא, <i>What are fattened</i> barburim? Rab <i>said: Those which they fatten by force</i> (that is, with food forced into the mouth). <i>But</i> Samuel <i>said: they are fattened as they stand by themselves</i> (that is, left to themselves, with no force applied). <i>And</i> Rabbi Iochanan <i>said: They bring an ox from its pasture which has not yet worked</i> (in tilling the land), <i>and a hen from its dunghill which has not yet laboured</i> (in rearing chicks). <i>Again</i> Rabbi Iochanan <i>said: The choicest of cattle is the ox, and the choicest of birds is the hen.</i> Amimar <i>said: A black hen, pot-bellied</i> (var. <i>fattened at home</i>), <i>which is found among the winepresses</i> (where it grows fat on grape-pips), <i>and cannot step over a reed,</i> because of its fatness. Hence <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in his Commentary on the books of <i>Kings</i>: וברברים אבוסים יש מרבותינו אומרים תרנגולין פתומין, <i>Fattened</i> barburim: <i>there are some of our Doctors who say they are fat cocks.</i> <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] understand <i>capons</i>, because העוף המסורס הוא שיתפטם ביותר, <i>a castrated bird grows [II, 699] fatter.</i> On this opinion see further what we adduced above <sup>b)</sup> on the second passage of Chapter 16.

‎In the <i>Aruch</i> [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], <i>Rabbi Judah</i> explains ברבורים <i>barburim</i> as מיני ביברים, <i>kinds of those animals that are kept in vivaria.</i> This is very ambiguous. It is uncertain whether he means fish, birds or wild beasts. For although the <i>Hebrew</i> ביבר <i>bibar</i>, Greek βιβάριον [bibarion] (French <i>vivier</i>), is taken by the <i>Hebrews</i> <sup>c)</sup> and <i>Greeks</i> <sup>d)</sup> of a later age for a <i>fishpond</i>, the word <i>vivarium</i> once extended much more widely. Its scope, namely, embraced any enclosures in which living animals were fed. Thus <i>Gellius</i>, book 2, chapter 20, distinguishes three kinds of vivaria: aviaries (<i>les volieres</i>), hare-warrens (<i>les garennes</i>) and fishponds (<i>les estangs</i>). From the same author, however, it may be gathered that <i>vivaria</i> rather concerned wild beasts, like the παράδεισοι [paradeisoi] of the <i>Greeks</i>. <i>“Vivaria,”</i> he says, <i>“as they are now called, are certain enclosed places in which wild beasts are fed alive.”</i> Likewise: <i>“The vivaria, as the common people now say, are what the Greeks call</i> παραδείσους [paradeisous].<i>”</i> The <i>Hebrews</i> define exactly so what a ביבר <i>bibar</i> is. <i>Aruch</i>: ביבר הוא פרדס שמאספין שם חיות וחפירה שמאספין שם דגין ומקום מקורה ששם עופות, <i>“A</i> bibar <i>is a park in which wild beasts are gathered; also a pit in which fish are collected; and finally a roofed place in which there are birds.”</i> This is taken from the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>On the Sabbath</i>, chapter 13, folio 106b, where the <i>Gemara</i> says: ביברין של חיות ושל עופות ושל דגין אין צדין מהם ביום טוב, <i>“From</i> bibarin (or <i>vivaria</i>) <i>of wild beasts, birds and fish one may not hunt on a festival day.”</i> Therefore the explanation of <i>R. Judah</i>, by which he defines ברבורים <i>barburim</i> as <i>kinds of animals that are kept in vivaria</i>, is far too broad. I add that it is hardly safe, because it rests entirely on a most empty play on the name. For <i>R. Judah</i> thought that ביבר <i>bibar</i> and ברבור <i>barbur</i> were words of the same origin, and hence that ביבר <i>bibar</i> was said for ברבר <i>birbar</i>, the letter <i>Resh</i> after the first being dropped, in the way <i>Lamed</i> has disappeared in the <i>Chaldee</i> or <i>Syriac</i> words ܓܓܘܠܬܐ [גגולתא], and ܩܝܩܘܠܬܐ [קיקולתא], and ܨܘܨܠܬܐ [צוצלתא], and ܫܘܫܠܬܐ [שושלתא]. Yet it is perfectly plain that the Hebrew ביבר <i>bibar</i>, the Greek βιβάριον [bibarion], the Spanish <i>bivar</i> and the French <i>vivier</i> derive from the <i>Roman</i> word <i>vivarium</i>. Nevertheless, following this view, <i>Castalio</i> [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] rendered the passage we are dealing with thus: <i>“His provision for each day was thirty cors of fine flour,”</i> etc., <i>“besides harts, roes, wild oxen and vivaria.”</i> In this respect, however, he is more inept than the <i>Hebrews</i>, since he put <i>vivaria</i> for the animals that are kept in vivaria. That is utterly absurd. For vivaria themselves are not [col. 129] among foods; nor would they be called אבוסים, <i>fattened.</i> And so <i>Castalio</i> passed over this word entirely untouched. Note in passing that in the <i>Aruch</i> the passage of <i>R. Judah</i> in which he explains the <i>Hebrew</i> ברברים as מיני ביברים is cited from <i>Vayikra Rabbah</i>, the chapter וכי ימוך אחיך, [II, 700] which is the thirty-fourth. Yet it appears nowhere there in the <i>Venice</i> edition of 1545, the only one I have been able to see. So either the <i>Aruch</i> is mistaken, or this edition is defective.

‎The third interpretation is that of those for whom ברברים <i>barburim</i> are <i>birds</i> in general, as if אברברים <i>abarburim</i>, from אבר <i>eber</i>, that is, <i>from wing</i>. This seems to be followed by <i>Josephus</i>, the <i>Chaldee</i>, the <i>Syriac</i>, the <i>Arabic</i>, the <i>Vulgate</i>, <i>R. Levi</i> and <i>Mordecai</i>. So in their opinion ברבור <i>barbur</i> would stand for אברבר <i>abarbur</i>, of the pattern of אספסף <i>asapsuph</i>. But it is almost without precedent for the first radical to drop out of nouns formed from quiescent <i>Pe Aleph</i> verbs.

‎<i>Baal Aruch</i> [=Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome, the ʿArukh] gives the fourth interpretation from the book <i>Vajekra Rabba</i>, already cited, as follows: רבנן אמרו עוף טהור הוא ומעולא הוא והיה בא מברבריאה והיה עולה על שולחן שלמה בכל יום, <i>Our teachers said that it is a clean and excellent bird, which was brought from</i> Barbaria <i>and was served daily at the table of</i> Solomon. And Kimchi on the books of <i>Kings</i>: ובדברי רז״ל עוף הבא מברבריא ונקראים ברבורים על שם מקומם, <i>In the words of our teachers of blessed memory, it was a bird brought from</i> Barbaria, <i>called</i> barbur (or <i>barbar</i>) <i>after the name of its place</i>. And in the <i>Lexicon</i> he cites the very same words as <i>Baal Aruch</i>. Elias [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] too, in the <i>Thisbi</i>: אומרין שהוא עוף שמן מברבריאה, <i>They say it is a fat bird from</i> Barbaria, and the rest, as in the others. This interpretation ought not to have been met with such hissing by the learned, as though the name <i>Barbaria</i> for a particular region had been born only today or yesterday. For it is supposed that the <i>Arabs</i> not long ago gave the name <i>Barbaria</i> to <i>Numidia</i>, or, as some hold, to the whole part of <i>Africa</i> that lies along the <i>Mediterranean</i> Sea from <i>Egypt</i> as far as <i>Gades</i>. Anyone curious about such matters will find the reasons for this name in <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)], <i>Exercitation</i> 51 against Cardano, in <i>Leo Africanus</i>, book 1, chapter 2, and in <i>Marmol</i>, <i>Africa</i>, book 1, chapter 6. And indeed the name <i>Barbaria</i> for the part of <i>Africa</i> we have mentioned is not to be found in Strabo, nor in <i>Mela</i>, nor in Pliny, nor in <i>Marcianus</i>, nor in <i>Ptolemy</i>, nor in any <i>geographer</i> of antiquity. Yet it is utterly false that it was given by the <i>Arabs</i>, since it was already current in the age of <i>Justinian</i>, long before the <i>Arabs</i> went there. Hence <i>Anastasius Sinaita</i>, a contemporary of <i>Justinian</i>, in his <i>Anagogical Contemplations on the Hexaemeron</i>: Ἐν τοῖς πλησιοχώροις Αἰγύπτου τοῖς ἐν Πενταπόλει τῇ Βαρβαρικῇ λεγομένοις ἀδύτοις [En tois plēsiochōrois Aigyptou tois en Pentapolei tē Barbarikē legomenois adytois]. In these words the <i>Cyrenaic Pentapolis</i> is placed in the <i>Barbaria</i> of <i>Africa</i>. And those whom the <i>Vandals</i> around that time transferred from Mount <i>Aurasius</i> in <i>Mauretania</i> to <i>Sardinia</i> were called <i>Barbaricini</i>. So testify <i>Procopius</i> in book 2 of the <i>Vandal War</i>, and <i>Gregory</i>, <i>Epistles</i> 25 and 27 of book 3. But I also find in the writings of the ancients another <i>Barbaria</i>, whose name is much older: namely the <i>Ethiopian Barbaria</i>, which is in turn twofold. One is <i>Barbaria</i> properly so called, the other the <i>Troglodytic</i>. Both [II, 701] lie along the sea: the latter along the <i>Arabian</i>, <i>Adulic</i> and <i>Avalite</i> gulfs, the former along the <i>Barbaric</i> gulf. By the name <i>Barbaria</i> <i>Ptolemy</i> and <i>Marcianus of Heraclea</i> understand only the former. But others also count among the <i>Barbarians</i> the dwellers by the <i>Arabian</i> Sea and the <i>Troglodytes</i>. Thus <i>Arrian</i> in the <i>Periplus</i> places the <i>Barbarians</i> and <i>Barbaria</i> around <i>Berenice</i>, <i>Orine</i>, the island of Diodorus, <i>Ptolemais Theron</i>, and the emporium of <i>Avalites</i>, which are places of the <i>Troglodytica</i>. And <i>Stephanus</i>, in his book <i>On Cities</i>, writes that <i>Barbaria</i> is παρὰ τὸν Ἀράβιον κόλπον [para ton Arabion kolpon], where the <i>Troglodytica</i> is. The <i>Arab geographer</i> likewise describes ارض [col. 130] بربرة [arḍ barbara], <i>the Barbaran land</i>, or <i>land of Barbaria</i>, الي البحر اليماني [ilā al-baḥr al-yamānī] <i>towards the Yemenite sea</i>, that is, the <i>Arabian</i>, Clim. [=climate (section) of al-Idrīsī's geography, in the Latin Geographia Nubiensis] 1, section 6. And in the preceding chapter he describes مدن الحبشة الساحلية [mudun al-ḥabasha al-sāḥiliyya] <i>the coastal towns of the Abyssinians</i> الي ما اتصل بها من عمارات البربرة [ilā mā ittaṣala bihā min ʿimārāt al-barbara] <i>until one reaches the inhabited places of Barbaria.</i> And Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] on the word بربر [barbar] <i>Barbar</i>: بربر جيل البرابرة وهم بالغرب وامة اخري بين الحبوش والزنج يقطعون مذاكير الرجال ويجعلونها مهور نسايهم وكلهم ولد كيس غيلان او هم بطنان من حمير صنهاجة وكتامة صاروا الي البربر ايام فتح افريقيس الملك افريقية [barbar jīl al-barābira wa-hum bi-l-gharb wa-umma ukhrā bayna al-ḥubūsh wa-l-zanj yaqṭaʿūna madhākīr al-rijāl wa-yajʿalūnahā muhūr nisāyihim wa-kulluhum walad kays ghaylān aw hum baṭnān min ḥimyar ṣanhāja wa-kutāma ṣārū ilā al-barbar ayyām fatḥ ifrīqīs al-malik ifrīqiya] Barbar, <i>the nation of the</i> Barbarians, <i>who are in the west.</i> <i>There is also another nation between the</i> Abyssinians <i>and the</i> Zinj, <i>which cuts off men's genitals and assigns them as dowry to their wives</i> (just as <i>David</i> acquired a wife for himself with a dowry of two hundred foreskins, <i>1 Sam 18:25 27.</i> and <i>2 Sam 3:14</i>). <i>All of them are sons of</i> Cais Gailan, <i>or of the stock of</i> Humeir (from whom the <i>Homerites</i> are named). <i>But</i> Sunhagia <i>and</i> Cutama <i>went into</i> Barbaria <i>when the</i> African <i>king opened up Africa.</i> And more recent <i>geographers</i> too describe, in the <i>Avalite</i> gulf, a part of the <i>Troglodytica</i>, a city <i>Barbara</i>, or <i>Barbora</i>, and an island of the same name next to the city, at which <i>Varthema</i> says he landed after setting sail from <i>Zeila</i>, <i>Navigat.</i> book 2, ch. 16. And <i>Duarte Barbosa</i> has much about the same in his <i>India</i>, in the chapter <i>On Barbora.</i> From this <i>Galen</i> is easily reconciled with <i>Dioscorides</i>: the former reports that ginger is brought ἀπὸ τῆς Βαρβαρίας [apo tēs Barbarias], <i>from Barbaria</i>; the latter, <i>from the Troglodytica.</i> For <i>Barbaria</i> and <i>Troglodytis</i> are the same region. Not knowing this, Pliny misunderstood what he had read in the <i>Greeks</i>, that <i>Troglodytic</i> myrrh is also called <i>Barbaran</i>, as if they meant that this kind of myrrh was <i>squalid and barbarous in appearance.</i> For so he interprets it in book 12, chapter 16. Whereas, on the contrary, in <i>Dioscorides</i> it is said to be διαυγὴς [diaugēs], <i>shining</i> or <i>translucent.</i> Hence in <i>Isidore</i>, book 17, ch. 8, it is commended for its purity: <i>Myrrh</i>, he says, <i>Troglodyte, from an island of</i> Arabia, <i>where a better and purer kind is gathered.</i> Accordingly it is praised in these words in the old Hymn <i>On the Epiphany of the Lord: Incense to God, myrrh</i> Troglodyte <i>for burial.</i> For that is how it should be read, not, [II, 702] as commonly, <i>trocletem</i>, as <i>Cassander</i> rightly pointed out long ago. <i>Troglodytic</i> myrrh was therefore called <i>Barbaran</i>, not as squalid in appearance, but by reason of its native soil. Nor is there any other reason why at <i>Gen 10:3</i> the <i>Jerusalem</i> translator renders <i>Thogarma</i> as ברבריא <i>Barbaria</i> than a deceptive play on the names <i>Thogarma</i> and τρώγλην [trōglēn], whence <i>Troglitis</i> or <i>Troglodytis</i>. From this it is more than sufficiently clear that the name <i>Barbaria</i> comprises the whole seacoast of <i>Eastern Ethiopia</i>, from the borders of <i>Egypt</i> as far as the promontory of <i>Rhapta</i>. It seems to have received this name because of its uncivilized customs. <i>Arrian</i>: Ἀτακτότεροι δὲ οἱ κατοικοῦντες τὸν τόπον Βάρβα- [col. 131] ροι [Ataktoteroi de hoi katoikountes ton topon Barbaroi]. Or because of its barbarous and uncouth speech, if one can call speech what is more truly the squeaking of bats. Herodotus, <i>Muse</i> 4: Οἱ Τρωγλοδύται Αἰθίοπες γλῶσσαν οὐδεμίῃ ἄλλῃ παρομοίην νενομίκασι, ἀλλὰ τετρύγασι καθάπερ αἱ νυκτερίδες [Hoi Trōglodytai Aithiopes glōssan oudemiē allē paromoiēn nenomikasi, alla tetrygasi kathaper hai nykterides]. Pliny, book 5, ch. 8: <i>Their</i> (food) <i>is the flesh of serpents, and they have a squeak, not a voice: so wholly do they lack the exchange of speech.</i> <i>Martianus Capella</i>, book 6: <i>They squeak rather than speak</i>. As Solinus: <i>Ignorant of speech, they squeak rather than speak</i>. Rightly, then, were they called <i>Barbarians</i>. For properly so are called οἱ δυσεκφόρως, καὶ σκληρῶς, καὶ τραχέως λαλοῦντες [hoi dysekphorōs, kai sklērōs, kai tracheōs lalountes], as Strabo shows at length on that verse of Homer <sup>e)</sup> in which he calls the <i>Carians</i> Βαρβαροφώνους [Barbarophōnous]. In this very sense the <i>Greek, Chaldee, Arabic and Latin</i> translators at <i>Ps 114:1</i> call <i>barbarous</i> the people which the <i>Hebrews</i> call עם לועז. For to the <i>Hebrews</i> לועז is <i>one who speaks a foreign tongue, and that absurd and uncouth.</i> Nor do the lexicographers of the <i>Arabs</i> explain بربار [barbār] <i>barbar</i> otherwise. <i>Giggeius</i> [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)]: بربار [barbār] (barbar) <i>garrulous, making a din, clamouring.</i> بربرة [barbara] (barbara) <i>the bleating of goats, triviality, the speech of an angry man, which is usually abrupt with ill-articulated words,</i> as it is in <i>Seneca</i>. Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: بربر [barbara] (<i>barbara</i>, verb) <i>he grumbled as angry men do:</i> which is taken from Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ]. But Alcamus preceded <i>Giggeius</i>; his words are: البربرة صوت المعز وكثرة الكلام والجلبة والصياح [al-barbara ṣawt al-maʿz wa-kathrat al-kalām wa-l-jalaba wa-l-ṣiyāḥ] (Albarbara) <i>the voice of the goat, much talking, clamour and shouting.</i> Therefore the name <i>Barbaria</i> was as much <i>Chaldee</i> and <i>Arabic</i> as <i>Greek</i>, and so was very well known to the <i>Jews</i> and <i>Syrians</i>, in whose books it occurs everywhere. Examples from the <i>Talmud</i> and the paraphrasts are found in <i>Baal Aruch</i>; from the <i>Ielammedenu</i> in Buxtorf [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum]; from <i>Semoth Rabba</i> and <i>Esther Rabba</i> in <i>David Cohen de Lara</i>. To these I add from <i>Gorionides</i>, on <i>Aretas</i>, king of the <i>Arabs</i>, about to fight against <i>Herod</i>, book 5, ch. 18 and 19: <i>He gathers all his army, the army</i>, that is, <i>of Arabia</i>, וצבא ברבר, <i>and the army of barbaria.</i> And shortly after, the <i>Jews</i> bring back much spoil from the <i>Arabs</i>, ומן ברבר, <i>and from the Barbarians.</i> In that age, of course, part of <i>Barbaria</i> was subject to the rule of the <i>Arabs</i>. Indeed even the more remote <i>Barbarians</i> [II, 703] as far as <i>Rhapta</i> acknowledged the kings of <i>Arabia Felix</i> as their lords. Hence <i>Arrian</i> on <i>Rhapta</i> and the region around <i>Rhapta</i>: Νέμεται δὲ αὐτὴν κατά τι δίκαιον ἀρχαῖον, ὑποπίπτουσαν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τῆς πρώτης γινομένης Ἀραβίας, ὁ Μωφαρίτης τύραννος [Nemetai de autēn kata ti dikaion archaion, hypopiptousan tē basileia tēs prōtēs ginomenēs Arabias, ho Mōpharitēs tyrannos]· <i>The</i> Mopharite <i>tyrant possesses it, subject by some ancient right to the kingdom of the first</i> Arabia. I think the same <i>Barbarians</i> are meant by the <i>Talmudists</i> when, in the tractate <i>Iebamoth</i>, fol. 63b, they say that the foolish people of whom <i>Moses</i> speaks in <i>Deut 32:21</i> are אנשי ברבריא שמהלכין ערומין בשוק, <i>the men of</i> Barbaria, <i>who walk naked through the streets.</i> For they were either naked or half-covered with a hide. <i>Dionysius</i> on the <i>Erembi</i>, or <i>Troglodytes</i>, verse 965.

‎Γυμνοὶ, καὶ κτεάνων ἐπιδεύεες· [Gymnoi, kai kteanōn epideuees] — —

‎<i>Naked, and lacking possessions.</i> And Festus:

‎<i>And with limbs uncovered, they ever creep up the steep roofs of their dwellings.</i> — — —

‎And <i>Strabo</i>, in book 16: Γυμνῆται δὲ, καὶ δερματοφόροι, καὶ σκυταληφόροι διατελοῦσιν· [Gymnētai de, kai dermatophoroi, kai skytalēphoroi diatelousin] <i>They live naked, or clad in skins and carrying clubs.</i> In <i>Africa</i>, therefore, there was a twofold <i>Barbaria</i>: one western, extending as far as <i>Gades</i>; the other eastern, extending to the <i>Arabian Gulf</i> and the eastern <i>Ocean</i>, as far as <i>Rhapta</i>. It is certain, moreover, that the <i>Hebrews</i> were speaking of the latter, not the former, when they held that the ברברים <i>barburim</i>, or <i>barbarim</i>, were named after <i>Barbaria</i>, from which they had been brought to <i>Solomon</i>. For this interpretation is taken from the book <i>Vajekra Rabba</i>, whose author, <i>Rabba</i>, [col. 132] son of <i>Nachmoni</i>, flourished around the three hundredth year of <i>Christ</i>. In that age there is no mention of a <i>Barbaria</i> in the west. Nor does he speak on his own authority; rather, he cites his predecessors, <i>Judah</i> and <i>Berechiah</i>. Besides, by whatever name those <i>Barbarians</i> who live in the west of <i>Africa</i> may be called, <i>Solomon</i> had no trade with them. For <i>Solomon</i> fitted out his fleet not on the <i>Mediterranean</i> but on the <i>Red Sea</i>, at <i>Aelana</i> and <i>Ezion-geber</i>. From there the route led not to the western but to the eastern <i>Barbarians</i>; and from there guinea fowl could have been brought and called ברברים <i>barburim</i>, or <i>barbarim</i>, that is, <i>Barbarian</i> or <i>Barbaric birds</i>. For at a time when these birds were very rare and scarce, a certain island of the <i>Red Sea</i> belonging to the <i>Barbaria of the Troglodytes</i> was extremely productive of them. This is recorded by reliable authors: besides <i>Agatharchides</i>, by <i>Diodorus</i> and by <i>Artemidorus</i> as cited in <i>Strabo</i>. Strabo's words in book 16 run thus: Φησὶ δ᾽ Ἀρτεμίδωρος ἀπὸ Ἡρώων πόλεως πλέουσι κατὰ τὴν Τρωγλοδυτικὴν πόλιν εἶναι Φιλοτέραν, etc. Εἶτα Μύος ὅρμον, καὶ Ἀφροδίτης ὅρμον καλεῖσθαι λιμένα μέγαν, τὸν εἴσπλουν ἔχοντα σκολιόν· προκεῖσθαι δὲ νήσους τρεῖς, δύο μὲν ἐλαίαις κατασκίους, μίαν δ᾽ ἧττον κατάσκιον, μελεαγρίδων μεστήν· [Phēsi d' Artemidōros apo Hērōōn poleōs pleousi kata tēn Trōglodytikēn polin einai Philoteran, etc. Eita Myos hormon, kai Aphroditēs hormon kaleisthai limena megan, ton eisploun echonta skolion; prokeisthai de nēsous treis, dyo men elaiais kataskious, mian d' hētton kataskion, meleagridōn mestēn] <i>Artemidorus says that for those sailing from the</i> City of the Heroes <i>there is, near the</i> Troglodytic <i>city, Philotera</i>, etc. <i>Then a large harbour</i> [II, 704] <i>with a winding entrance, which is called the Mouse's Anchorage, and the Anchorage of</i> Venus. <i>Before it lie three islands, two shaded with olive trees, the third less shaded, full of guinea fowl.</i> And <i>Diodorus</i>, book 2, p. 120: Ὑπέρκεινται δὲ τούτου νῆσοι τρεῖς, ὧν δύο μὲν πλήρεις εἰσὶν ἐλαιῶν, καὶ σύκων· μία δὲ λειπομένη τῷ πλήθει τῶν προειρημένων δένδρων· πλῆθος δ᾽ ἔχουσα τῶν ὀνομαζομένων μελεαγρίδων. [Hyperkeintai de toutou nēsoi treis, hōn dyo men plēreis eisin elaiōn, kai sykōn; mia de leipomenē tō plēthei tōn proeirēmenōn dendrōn; plēthos d' echousa tōn onomazomenōn meleagridōn] <i>Beside it lie three islands, two of which abound in olives and figs; but one, less fertile in these trees, is filled with the birds called guinea fowl.</i> And, older than both, <i>Agatharchides</i>, <i>On the Red Sea</i>, book 5, ch. 42: Ἐν οἷς καὶ νῆσοι τρεῖς εἰσι προσκειμέναι· ὧν αἱ μὲν ἐλαίαις πεπύκνωνται, μία δὲ ἧττον μὲν δασεῖα, τῶν δὲ καλουμένων μελεαγρίδων ἐκτρέφουσα πλῆθος. [En hois kai nēsoi treis eisi proskeimenai; hōn hai men elaiais pepyknōntai, mia de hētton men daseia, tōn de kaloumenōn meleagridōn ektrephousa plēthos] <i>Where three islands lie nearby: two of them are thick with olives; one is indeed less wooded, but it nourishes an abundance of the birds they call guinea fowl.</i> So it appears that this island was most famous for its yield of these birds.

‎What exactly meleagrides are is not sufficiently established. Some think they are the same bird as the <i>African</i> or <i>Numidian</i> hens. <i>Varro</i>, <i>Rei Rust.</i> book 3, ch. 9: <i>"The</i> African <i>hens are large, speckled, humpbacked; the</i> Greeks <i>call them</i> μελεαγρίδας [meleagridas]." <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, ch. 26, after relating that the <i>Memnonides</i> birds fight at the tomb of <i>Memnon</i>, says: <i>"In like manner the meleagrides fight in</i> Boeotia" (namely at the tomb of <i>Meleager</i>). <i>"This is a humpbacked kind of hen of Africa, sprinkled with speckled feathers,"</i> etc. <i>Columella</i>, however, distinguishes them thus in book 9, chapter 2: <i>"The African hen, which most call the</i> Numidian, <i>is like the meleagris, except that it bears a red helmet and crest on its head, both of which are blue in the meleagris."</i> But they also differ in their place of origin. For the <i>African</i> is found nowhere else, whereas the meleagris is a <i>Greek</i> bird, known already in the heroic age. And in the fables of the <i>Greeks</i> a twofold origin of it is handed down. Some write that the sisters of <i>Meleager</i>, others that the companions of the maiden <i>Iocallis</i>, worshipped on the island of <i>Leros</i>, were changed into these birds. <i>Suidas</i>: Λέγουσι δὲ οἱ μὲν τὰς ἀδελφὰς τοῦ Μελεάγρου μεταβαλεῖν εἰς τὰς μελεαγρίδας ὄρνιθας· οἱ δὲ τὰς συνήθεις Ἰοκάλλιδος τῆς ἐν Λέρνῃ παρθένου, ἣν τιμῶσι δαιμονίως [Legousi de hoi men tas adelphas tou Meleagrou metabalein eis tas meleagridas ornithas; hoi de tas synētheis Iokallidos tēs en Lernē parthenou, hēn timōsi daimoniōs]. For ἐν Λέρνῃ [en Lernē] read, at my own risk, ἐν Λέρῳ [en Lerō]. For that <i>Iocallis</i> is the very one of whom <i>Clytus</i> speaks in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 14: Περὶ δὲ τὸ ἱερὸν τῆς παρθένου ἐν Λέρῳ εἰσὶν οἱ καλούμενοι ὄρνιθες μελεαγρίδες [Peri de to hieron tēs parthenou en Lerō eisin hoi kaloumenoi ornithes meleagrides]; <i>"Around the temple of the maiden in</i> Leros <i>there are birds called meleagrides."</i> Nor should <i>Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] be heeded, who, [col. 133] in order to refer it to <i>Pallas</i>, reads ἐν Ὠλέρῳ [en Ōlerō], because in the city of <i>Crete</i>, <i>Olerus</i>, there was a temple of <i>Pallas</i>, from which come Ὠλερία Ἀθηνᾶ [Ōleria Athēna] and Ὠλερία [Ōleria], a festival sacred to <i>Pallas</i>, in <i>Stephanus</i>, <i>De urbibus</i>. For the meleagrides were not in the city of <i>Olerus</i> but on the island of <i>Leros</i>. Hence <i>Aelian</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 4, ch. 42: Ἴσασι, he says, οἱ τὴν νῆσον οἰκοῦντες τὴν Λέρον [Isasi hoi tēn nēson oikountes tēn Leron]; <i>"Those who inhabit the island of</i> Leros <i>know"</i>, namely why those who worship <i>Diana</i> abstain from eating meleagrides. And book 5, ch. 27: Τὰς δὲ ἐν Λέρῳ μελεαγρίδας [II, 705] ὑπὸ μηδενὸς ἀδικεῖσθαι τῶν γαμψωνύχων ὀρνέων λέγει Ἴστρος [Tas de en Lerō meleagridas hypo mēdenos adikeisthai tōn gampsōnychōn orneōn legei Istros]; <i>"</i>Ister <i>related that the meleagrides on</i> Leros <i>are harmed by none of the birds of prey."</i> And <i>Antoninus Liberalis</i>, <i>Metamorphosis</i> 2, on the sisters of <i>Meleager</i>: Αὐτὰς Ἄρτεμις ἁψαμένη ῥάβδῳ μετεμόρφωσεν εἰς ὄρνιθας, καὶ ἀπῴκισεν εἰς Λέρον τὴν νῆσον ὀνομάσασα μελεαγρίδας [Autas Artemis hapsamenē rhabdō metemorphōsen eis ornithas, kai apōkisen eis Leron tēn nēson onomasasa meleagridas]; <i>"</i>Diana <i>touched them with a rod and changed them into birds, and removed them to the island of</i> Leros, <i>giving them the name meleagrides."</i> But writers wrongly confuse the <i>Lerian</i> meleagrides with the <i>Aetolian</i>. For the <i>Lerian</i> ones were kept in honour of <i>Iocallis</i>, who was worshipped on <i>Leros</i>, and the <i>Aetolian</i> ones around <i>Calydon</i> and <i>Pleuron</i> in honour of <i>Meleager</i>. Hence <i>Statius</i>, <i>Thebaid</i>, book 4, line 103:

‎<i>"And Meleagrian</i> Pleuron, <i>bewailed by kindred birds."</i>

‎And <i>Menodotus of Samos</i> in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 14, relates that peacocks were first produced on <i>Samos</i> and carried from there elsewhere, ὡς καὶ οἱ ἀλεκτρυόνες ἐν τῇ Περσίδι, καὶ αἱ καλουμέναι μελεαγρίδες ἐν τῇ Αἰτωλίᾳ [hōs kai hoi alektryones en tē Perside, kai hai kaloumenai meleagrides en tē Aitōlia], <i>"as the cocks in</i> Persia, <i>and in</i> Aetolia <i>those called meleagrides."</i> So I cannot at all understand why <i>Pliny</i>, in the place cited above, set the meleagrides and the tomb of <i>Meleager</i> in <i>Boeotia</i>. For <i>Meleager</i>, leader of <i>Aetolia</i>, had nothing in common with the <i>Boeotians</i>, who lived far from there. From <i>Clytus</i>, <i>Athenaeus</i> has a long description of the meleagrides, yet so ambiguous that one cannot tell whether he describes the <i>African</i> or <i>Numidian</i> hen (today the <i>Barbary hen</i>), or the turkey (which our people call the <i>Indian cock</i>), or a third kind of bird unlike either. Indeed, it suits neither that ὁ τόπος ἑλώδης [ho topos helōdēs], <i>a marshy place</i>, is assigned for rearing the meleagris. Nor does what we read, that the bird is ἄστοργον πρὸς τὰ ἔκγονα τὸ ὄρνεον, καὶ ὀλιγωρεῖν τῶν νεωτέρων, ὥς τε ἀνάγκη τοῖς ἱερεῦσιν ἐπιμελεῖσθαι αὐτῶν [astorgon pros ta ekgona to orneon, kai oligōrein tōn neōterōn, hōs te anankē tois hiereusin epimeleisthai autōn], <i>"a bird without any affection for its offspring, which neglects its chicks so much that the priests must take care of them."</i> As for flavour, the <i>poets</i> extol the <i>African</i> hen above all among the delicacies of the ancients. <i>Horace</i>, <i>Epode</i> 2:

‎<i>"May no</i> African <i>bird go down into my belly,</i> <i>nor</i> Ionian <i>attagen,</i> <i>more pleasing than,"</i> etc.

‎Petronius:

‎<i>"For your palate the peacock is fed in confinement,</i> <i>for you the</i> Numidian <i>hen, etc."</i>

‎<i>Juvenal</i>, Satire 11:

‎<i>"Our little apprentice does not know how to filch a morsel of roe, nor the wing of an</i> African <i>bird."</i> — —

‎<i>Statius</i>, book 1 of the <i>Silvae</i>, on the <i>Saturnalia of Domitian</i>: [II, 706] <i>"Among which there suddenly fall in flight</i> <i>immense clouds of birds through the stars,</i> <i>those which the sacred</i> Nile <i>and the rugged</i> Phasis, <i>those which the</i> Numidians <i>gather under the damp</i> South Wind."

‎<i>Martial</i>, book 3, <i>Epigram</i> 57:

‎<i>And the painted partridge, and the speckled</i> Numidian <i>hens.</i>

‎And <i>Epigram</i> 76:

‎<i>Neither</i> Libya <i>nor</i> Phasis <i>sends you birds.</i>

‎And book 13, <i>Epigram</i> 46:

‎<i>If we had birds of</i> Libya <i>and of</i> Phasis, <i>you would receive them: but as it is, receive birds of the farmyard.</i>

‎Yet of these <i>Varro</i> says: <i>These were the latest to come from the kitchen into the gluttons' dining-room, owing to the fastidiousness of men.</i> And Pliny: <i>They are the most recent of foreign birds to be admitted to the table, on account of their unpleasant smell.</i> So rarity alone seems to have raised their price. <i>Varro</i> teaches us this in these words: <i>They sell at a high price because of their scarcity.</i> [col. 134] Likewise <i>Petronius</i>:

‎<i>The bird sought from</i> Phasian <i>Colchis</i> <i>and the</i> African <i>fowl please the palate</i> <i>because they are not easy to get.</i> — — <sup>R1</sup>

‎So much for the various interpretations of the word ברבורים <i>barburim</i>. Of these, although the third and fourth may be tolerated, the first pleases me most. I mean that most ancient one, according to which ברבורים אבוסים are all the <i>choicest</i> <i>fattened fowl</i>. For, to say nothing of the agreement of <i>the Elders</i> [=the translators of the Septuagint] with the <i>Talmudists</i> in asserting it, analogy requires it. Just as from חרר charar, <i>to burn</i>, comes חרחור <i>charchur</i>, <i>extreme burning</i>, Deut 28:22, so from ברר barar, <i>to choose</i>, comes ברבור <i>barbur</i>, <i>a most choice thing</i>; the first radical is doubled so that the meaning is intensified. This is customary in these languages. Thus, since קלל means <i>to be light</i> or <i>to be worthless</i>, קלקל is <i>a most worthless, most light, most despised thing</i>. And the <i>Jews</i>, murmuring, call the manna so in contempt, <i>Numer. XXXI</i>, 5. And from צלל, <i>to overshadow</i>, comes צלצל, <i>a denser shadow</i>. And from רבב, <i>to make great</i>, come רברבים [II, 707], the <i>magnates</i>, who stand out most among the people. And from כידור, <i>spark</i>, comes כרכר, the <i>Carchedonian stone</i>, a gem that sparkles exceedingly. <i>Publilius Syrus</i> in <i>Petronius Arbiter</i>:

‎<i>Why do you desire Carchedonian stony fires, except that they may sparkle?</i> — — —

‎And from דקק, <i>to make thin</i>, comes דקדוק, <i>a subtle disquisition</i>. Hence <i>Grammar</i> is so called by the <i>Hebrews</i>, because it is a subtle and precise examination of speech. Moreover, this explanation yields a most fitting sense. Solomon's <i>provisions for each day were thirty cors of fine flour, sixty cors of meal, ten fat oxen, twenty pasture-fed oxen, and a hundred sheep, besides harts, roebucks, and bubali, and all the choicest fattened fowl</i>, that is, those animals which were selected from among the rest to be fattened in a special way and set on the royal table. The ancients understood this chiefly of birds, both because small birds hold the first place in the delicacy market, and in order to distinguish them from those already mentioned, namely oxen, sheep, harts, roebucks and bubali. Yet this is not necessary, since the very method of fattening sufficiently distinguishes these from the rest; from it they are called אבוסים <i>abusim</i>, that is, <i>fattened at the stall</i>, not, like the others, in the pastures. For אבוס <i>ebus</i> is a <i>stall</i> (manger), <i>Iob. XXXIX</i>, 12. <i>Prov. XIV</i>, 14. and <i>Es. I</i>, 3. Hence אבס <i>abas</i> means <i>to fatten livestock</i>, not in any way whatever, but <i>tied at the stall</i>, and indeed <i>with food put and thrust into the mouth</i>, if we believe the <i>Talmudists</i>. <i>Aquila</i> renders it φατνιάζεσθαι [phatniazesthai], and <i>Theodotion</i> φατνεύεσθαι [phatneuesthai]. But there was no need to coin new words, since σιτεύειν [siteuein] means this. For σιτευτὰ [siteuta] is said of <i>animals that are fattened in confinement</i>. Hence <i>Plutarch</i>, <i>Sympos.</i> 4, Question 1: Ὅσα δὲ σιτεύουσι καθείρξαντες [hosa de siteuousi katheirxantes], <i>Whatever they fatten shut up</i>. So the same <i>Theodotion</i> renders שור אבוס as βοῦν σιτευτὸν [boun siteuton], quite rightly. And in <i>Luc. XV</i>, 23. the prodigal's father orders τὸν μόσχον τὸν σιτευτὸν [ton moschon ton siteuton] to be killed, that is, השור האבוס: out of many oxen or calves, the one that had been fattened in this way. For the article placed before it is not idle. And <i>Matth. XXII</i>, 4. τὰ σιτιστὰ τεθυμένα [ta sitista tethymena], that is, האבוסות. Accordingly, one of the <i>Greeks</i> renders ברבורים אבוסים as ἐκλεκτῶν σιτευτά [eklektōn siteuta]. Somewhat parallel to the passage under discussion is another, <i>Nehem. V</i>, 18., in which for <i>Nehemiah's</i> daily food are prepared <i>one ox, six choice sheep, and birds</i>. <i>Choice</i> in Hebrew is בררות, which with the first letter doubled could have been called ברברות. For בררות and ברברות are the same, except that in ברברות the meaning is more intense because of the doubled letter. So if ברורות means <i>choice</i>, ברברות would mean <i>most choice</i> <sup>R2</sup>.

‎[II, 708] I shall now say why I think that ברברים אבוסים are neither <i>fattened hens</i>, nor <i>guinea fowl</i>, nor <i>birds</i> in general. 1. Although among the later <i>Hebrews</i> [col. 135] אבס <i>abas</i> is said also of birds that are fattened, yet in Scripture אבוס <i>ebus</i> is the <i>manger</i> of <i>cattle</i> or <i>beasts of burden</i>, not a <i>fattening coop for birds.</i> And אבס <i>abas</i>, derived from it, is applied only to cattle. Hence, when those who had come with <i>Cadmus</i> occupied the island of <i>Euboea</i>, as is found in <i>Strabo</i>, their leader was called אבס <i>Abas</i>, that is, <i>fattener of cattle.</i> From this, with a <i>Greek</i> inflection, the inhabitants themselves were called <i>Abantes</i> <sup>R3</sup>. For the greatest <i>Poet</i> calls them by no other name. So too the island was called Εὔβοια [Euboia] from oxen, and Ἀργιβόειος [Argiboeios] in <i>Aelian</i>, book 12, chapter 36. For that this island was long ago famous for its ox pastures is shown by the oxen driven off from <i>Euboea</i> by <i>Autolycus</i>, of which <i>Apollodorus</i> speaks in book 2 of the <i>Bibliotheca</i>, and <i>Tzetzes</i>, Chil. 8. Accordingly, at the beginning of the <i>Peloponnesian</i> War the <i>Athenians</i> πρόβατα, καὶ ὑποζύγια εἰς τὴν Εὔβοιαν διεπέμψαντο [probata, kai hypozygia eis tēn Euboian diepempsanto], <i>sent their sheep and beasts of burden to</i> Euboea, as <i>Thucydides</i> has it in book 2; and on the coinage of the <i>Euboeans</i> an ox's head was engraved. So <i>Antonius Augustinus</i> testifies in Dialogue 3 <i>On Coins.</i> But on <i>Euboea</i> see more in our <i>Chanaan.</i> 2. If by ברברים אבוסים the Sacred Writer meant to signify birds, of which there had been no mention before, there appears no reason why, content with epithets alone, he did not expressly name צפורים, that is, <i>birds</i>, so that there would be no room for doubt. 3. The same objection can be raised regarding the gallinaceous kind. For no one would say that ברברים are <i>cocks</i> or <i>hens</i>. 4. If the ברבורים had their name from <i>Barbary</i>, they would not be called <i>barburim</i>, but <i>barbarim.</i> 5. And although the eastern coast of <i>Africa</i> long ago bore the name of <i>Barbary</i>, yet that this was already in use in the age of <i>Solomon</i> cannot be proved by any argument. 6. Finally, who would believe that guinea fowl, or birds of <i>Barbary</i>, whatever they may have been, came into the reckoning of <i>Solomon</i>'s provisions rather than so many others native to <i>Judaea</i> and the neighbouring regions, which were set daily on the royal table? 7. But there was no need to descend so far. Otherwise fish too, and vegetables, and the fruits of trees, and every kind of delicacy ought to have been listed together. <i>Josephus</i> saw this in part. And so he added fish to the birds on his own account. Ταῦτα πάντα, he says, πάρεξ τῶν ἐπ᾽ ἄγρας ἐλάφων λέγω, καὶ βουβάλων, καὶ τῶν πτηνῶν, καὶ ἰχθύων, ἐκομίζετο καθ᾽ ἡμέραν τῷ βασιλεῖ· [Tauta panta, parex tōn ep᾽ agras elaphōn legō, kai boubalōn, kai tōn ptēnōn, kai ichthyōn, ekomizeto kath᾽ hēmeran tō basilei] <i>All these, besides wild animals taken in the hunt, I mean deer and bubali, and birds, and fish, were brought daily to the king</i>, book 8, ch. 2 <i>Antiqu.</i>

‎R1 *(p. 706)* On the <i>meleagrides</i> [guinea fowl] compare what SCHNEIDER said on Aelian's <i>History of Animals</i> IV, 42, to which he added some things in the <i>Appendix to the Commentaries on the Remains of the Books of Frederick II and the chapters of Albertus Magnus on the art of hunting with birds</i>, Leipzig 1789, p. 163. That the <i>meleagrides</i> are the <i>Numidae</i> of Linnaeus (Syst. Nat. vol. I, part II, p. 744, 13th ed.), <i>Perlhühner</i> [guinea fowl], was shown, following <i>Perrault</i>, by PALLAS in <i>Spicil. Zoolog.</i> III, p. 13 ff.

‎R2 *(p. 707)* Yet the opinion of J. D. Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer] in the <i>Supplements</i>, vol. I, p. 228, does not seem to be dismissed: that ברברים, or, as it is written in 14 of Kennicott's codices, ברבורים, are animals of the fields, forests and deserts which we usually catch by hunting, as opposed to domestic [II, 708] ones, from the Chaldee בָּרָא, Syriac ܒܪܐ [ברא], Arabic بَرّ [barr], <i>field, desert.</i> What he added to commend this opinion should be read in his own work.

‎R3 *(p. 708)* There is nothing about <i>Cadmus</i> in that passage of Strabo, as Michaelis observed, loc. cit. p. 227, who quoted the Greek words themselves; and he should certainly be compared on what Bochart adduces here.
