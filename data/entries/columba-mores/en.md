# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 4: The dove's simplicity, gentleness, chastity and timidity (vol. 2, pp. 549–556)

### ‎[II, 549] Chapter IV.

### ‎<i>On the dove's simplicity, gentleness, chastity and timidity.</i>

‎Now, as we are about to treat of the habits of doves, we shall begin with the famous saying of <i>Christ</i>, <i>Matt 10:16</i> Γίνεσθε φρόνιμοι ὡς οἱ ὄφεις, καὶ ἀκέραιοι ὡς αἱ περιστεραί [Ginesthe phronimoi hōs hoi opheis, kai akeraioi hōs hai peristerai]· <i>Be ye prudent as serpents, and simple as doves.</i> By <i>Tzetzes, Chiliad 9</i>, Hist. 263, φρόνιμοι [phronimoi] and ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] are taken as meaning the same thing. For after he has asserted that they are called prudent when, like serpents, they guard their head, that is, their faith in God, he immediately adds:

‎Ἀκέραιοι δὲ γίνεσθε, ὡς αἱ περιστεραί μοι· Καὶ τοῦτο πάλιν τὸ αὐτὸ δηλοῖ τε, καὶ σημαίνει. Ἀκέραιοι γὰρ λέγονται περιστεραὶ τοιώς δε, Φρόνιμοι, ὥσπερ λέγεται περιστερὰ τοῦ Νῶε. [Akeraioi de ginesthe, hōs hai peristerai moi; Kai touto palin to auto dēloi te, kai sēmainei. Akeraioi gar legontai peristerai toiōs de, Phronimoi, hōsper legetai peristera tou Nōe.]

‎<i>But be ye simple for me as doves. And this again indicates and signifies the same thing. For doves are called simple, prudent in such a way as Noah's dove is spoken of.</i> That is, the dove which returned to the ark, that is, to the church, from which one must not depart. Nothing more absurd could have been devised. For, like the serpent and the dove, so φρόνιμος [phronimos] and ἀκέραιος [akeraios] are in no way synonyms. Nor did <i>Christ</i> join them as synonyms, but as opposites, in a striking antithesis, such as <i>Martial</i> uses when he says, in book 10, <i>Epigr. 47</i>:

‎<i>Prudent simplicity, friends who are equals.</i>

‎For just as prudence without simplicity degenerates into cunning, so simplicity without prudence is nothing but foolishness and stupidity. <i>The prudent</i> in Hebrew are ערומים, and <i>the simple</i> פתיים, or פתאים. <i>Solomon</i> often sets these two against each other. Thus <i>Prov 1:4.</i> לתת לפתאים ערמה, <i>to give prudence to the simple.</i> Prov 8:5 <i>O ye simple, understand prudence.</i> Prov 14:15. פתי, <i>the simple believes every word</i>, וערום, <i>but the prudent considers his step.</i> Prov 19:25. ופתי יערים, <i>And the simple will become prudent.</i> Prov 22:3. ערום, <i>The prudent, when he sees evil, hides himself</i>, ופתיים, <i>but the simple pass on and are punished.</i> And, [col. 20] just as the serpent is called ערום, φρόνιμος [phronimos], Gen 3:1, so the dove is called פתה, <i>simple</i>, Hos 7:11 <i>And</i> Ephraim <i>has become</i> כיונה פתה אין לב, <i>like a simple dove, lacking heart</i>, that is, <i>senseless.</i> To these two passages <i>Christ</i> no doubt alluded. You may object that both are put as vices: that the serpent is called ערום not because it is prudent but because it is crafty and wily, and the dove פתה not because it is simple but because it is foolish, and אין לב, <i>senseless</i>, as immediately follows. I reply, first, that both [II, 550] ערום and פתי or פתה are neutral words, which according to their subject may also turn to praise. Thus ערום means not only <i>crafty</i> and <i>cunning</i> but also <i>prudent</i>, and ערמה <i>prudence</i>, as the passages of <i>Solomon</i> cited above amply show. Conversely פתי signifies not only ἄφρονα [aphrona], <i>foolish</i>, but also ἀκέραιον [akeraion], <i>simple</i>, as in <i>Ps 116:6.</i> שמר פתאים יהוה, <i>the Lord preserves the simple.</i> <i>Christ</i> could therefore use these words in praise as well. Second, neither the serpent's prudence nor the dove's simplicity is praised by <i>Christ</i> on its own and torn from the other; they are praised only when joined together, so that the one supplies what is lacking in the other, or corrects what is excessive. In the same way, from two drugs that are harmful separately a third is compounded that is useful and wholesome, because they temper each other. Hence the ancients so often join these two. <i>Gregory of Nazianzus</i>, on his father, <i>Oration</i> 19: Τὸ γὰρ τοῦ ὄφεως φρόνιμον περὶ τὸ κακὸν τῷ τῆς περιστερᾶς ἀκεραίῳ περὶ τὸ καλὸν ἀναμίξας, οὐδὲ τὸ φρόνιμον εἴασε κακοῦργον γενέσθαι. οὔτε τὸ ἁπλοῦν ἀνόητον, ἀλλ᾽ ἓν ἀρετῆς εἶδος, ὡς μάλιστα ἐνῆν, ἐξ ἀμφοτέρων ἀπηκριβώσατο. [To gar tou opheōs phronimon peri to kakon tō tēs peristeras akeraiō peri to kalon anamixas, oude to phronimon eiase kakourgon genesthai. oute to haploun anoēton, all᾽ hen aretēs eidos, hōs malista enēn, ex amphoterōn apēkribōsato.] <i>Having joined the serpent's prudence regarding evil with the dove's simplicity regarding good, he so conducted himself that he let neither prudence degenerate into cunning wickedness nor simplicity into stupidity, but from both, as far as was possible, he produced a single form of virtue, perfect and complete in every respect. Jerome</i>, to <i>Paulinus</i> on the training of a monk: <i>Have the simplicity of the dove, so as not to contrive snares against anyone, and the cunning of the serpent, so as not to be tripped up by the plots of others. Isidore of Pelusium</i>, book 3, epist. 175: Ἡ φρόνησις τῇ ἁπλότητι κιρνωμένη θεῖόν τι χρῆμα· φημὶ δὲ ἐντελεστάτην ἀρετὴν ἀποτελεῖ· εἰ δὲ θατέρα θατέρας χωρισθείη, ἡ μὲν εἰς πονηρίαν ἐμπίπτει, ἡ δὲ εἰς μωρίαν τελευτᾷ· [Hē phronēsis tē haplotēti kirnōmenē theion ti chrēma; phēmi de entelestatēn aretēn apotelei; ei de thatera thateras chōristheiē, hē men eis ponērian empiptei, hē de eis mōrian teleuta;] <i>Prudence tempered with simplicity produces something divine, that is, a virtue complete in every respect. But if the one is separated from the other, the former falls into perversity, while the latter ends in folly.</i> Faustus, <i>On Free Will</i>, book 2, last chapter: <i>Let us make man simple, let us make him astute: simple, so that being crafty he may not inflict evil; astute, so that being incautious he may not fall into it. Gregory</i> on <i>Job</i>, book 1, ch. 2: <i>He necessarily joined both in the admonition, so that the cunning of the serpent might equip the simplicity of the dove, and in turn the simplicity of the dove might temper the cunning of the serpent. Sidonius</i>, book 7, Epist. 13, on <i>Lupus</i>, bishop <i>of Troyes</i>: <i>In the church he keeps the simplicity of the dove, in the forum the cunning of the serpent. To the good he is judged prudent, to the wicked wary, to neither crafty. St Bernard</i> [=Bernard of Clairvaux], Epist. 327, to <i>Pope Innocent: in the church, then, simplicity must be kept, in the forum cunning; here, lest you be deceived, there, lest you deceive.</i> See more on this subject in <i>Caesarius, Dial. 4, Quaest. 190.</i> Further, the simple are here called not ἁπλοί [haploi] but ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi]. Yet it comes to the same thing. For ἀκέραιος [akeraios], formed from κεράω [keraō], <i>misceo</i> [I mix], properly signifies that which has nothing external and foreign mixed into it. Some take ἀκέραιος [akeraios] to be the same as ἀκερᾶς [akeras] or ἀκέρατος [akeratos], [II, 551] <i>that which has no horns</i>, and hence is ἀβλαβής [ablabēs] and <i>harmless</i>, a metaphor taken from animals that strike with the horn. So the <i>Arabic</i> and <i>Ethiopic</i> translators seem to have taken it: in the former ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] are ودعا [wudaʿā], in the latter የዋሃን፡ [yawāhān], that is, <i>mild</i> and <i>gentle</i>. But the first etymology seems more regular. Nor is it quite fitting that <i>hornless</i> should be said for <i>harmless</i>, since eagles, lions and many other wild beasts [col. 21] that have no horns possess many ways of doing harm, which they employ to destroy other animals. Moreover, the simple are opposed to the prudent, but the mild and gentle are not. So if you render <i>mild</i>, the grace of the opposition will be lost, which is evident here between the serpent's prudence and the dove's simplicity. Of the same kind is that of <i>Paul</i>, Rom 16:19: <i>I would have you</i> σοφούς [sophous] <i>wise in what is good</i>, ἀκεραίους δέ [akeraious de], <i>but simple in what is evil</i>. The illustrious <i>Beza</i> had rendered <i>innocent</i> in his first editions, but in later ones he rightly changed it to <i>simple</i>, so that the opposition might stand <sup>R1</sup>. To the same effect is the fact that in that very ancient manuscript of <i>Beza</i> ἁπλούστατοι [haploustatoi], <i>most simple</i>, was read for ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi]. Nor is it a slight point that, just as the serpent's being called <i>prudent</i> is taken from <i>Moses</i>, so the dove's being called <i>simple</i> is taken from <i>Hosea</i>. But no passage of the <i>Prophets</i> occurs where it is called <i>mild</i>. Thus the <i>Christian Arabs</i> seem to have understood less well what ἀκέραιοι [akeraioi] means here than the <i>Muslim</i> doctors, who render بلها [bulhan] <i>bulhan</i>, that is, <i>simple</i>. So in the book of the priest <i>Achmed</i> entitled الزهد [al-zuhd], that is, <i>abstinence</i>, <i>Iazid</i>, son of <i>Malzara</i>, says that <i>Jesus</i> commanded his disciples ان يكون بلها في الله مشل الحمام [an yakūna bulhan fī Allāh mashl al-ḥamām] <i>that they should be bulhan</i> (that is, <i>simple</i>) <i>in God like doves</i>. Thus we render بلها [bulhan] <i>bulhan</i>, with the most learned of the <i>Arabs</i>, from the singular ابله [ablah] <i>ablaho</i>, to which كيس [kayyis] <i>chaison</i> is opposed, that is, <i>cunning</i> and <i>crafty</i>. And that it is to be so taken is shown by what follows in the same place: ليس شي ابله من الحمام وذلك انك تاخذ فرخيه من تحته فتذبحهما ثم يعود الي مكانه فيفرخ فيه [laysa shay ablah min al-ḥamām wa-dhālika annaka taʾkhudh farkhayhi min taḥtihi fa-tadhbaḥuhumā thumma yaʿūd ilā makānihi fa-yufarrikh fīhi] <i>Nothing is simpler than the dove. For even if you kill the young taken from under it, it nevertheless soon returns to its place and hatches young in it.</i> The very same thing the <i>Fathers</i> write on the passage of <i>Hosea</i> we have cited. <i>Theodoret</i>: Ἐκείνη τῶν ἰδίων νεοττῶν ἀφαιρουμένη οὐκ ἀφίπταται, ἀλλὰ τοῖς αὐτοῖς προστρέχει χωρίοις. [Ekeinē tōn idiōn neottōn aphairoumenē ouk aphiptatai, alla tois autois prostrechei chōriois.] And following him <i>Theophylact</i>: <i>When its young are taken away it does not fly off, but remains in the same places.</i> Somewhat differently <i>Jerome</i>: <i>Other birds hasten to protect their young even at the risk of their own life, and when they see a hawk, a snake, a raven or a crow approach their nest,</i> [II, 552] <i>they flutter this way and that, throw themselves at it, tear it with beak and claws, and bear witness to a parent's grief with plaintive cry. The dove alone does not grieve for its young when they are taken away, nor seek them.</i> But how truly, I do not quite know, since a kind of dove or wood-pigeon, الورشان [al-warashān] <i>alvvarsen</i>, is described by the <i>Arabs</i> بالحنو علي اولاده حتي انه ربما قتل نفسه اذا راها في يد القانص [bi-l-ḥunuww ʿalā awlādihi ḥattā annahu rubbamā qatala nafsahu idhā rāhā fī yad al-qāniṣ] <i>as burning with such affection for its young that it sometimes kills itself when it sees them in the fowler's hand.</i> So it is found in <i>Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān].</i> And in general writers commend the dove for its love of its offspring. <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, ch. 34, on the male and female dove: <i>Both have equal love for their offspring; often for this reason there is chastisement, when the female is slow in going in to the young.</i> On which more in <i>Aristotle, Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 7; <i>Aelian, Hist.</i> book 3, ch. 45; again <i>Aelian, Var.</i> book 1, ch. 15; <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9, ch. 11; <i>Porphyry</i>, Περὶ ἀποχῆς [Peri apochēs], etc., book 3; which there is no room to copy out here. Others assert that the dove's simplicity is evident in the fact that it does not avoid nets, nor the snares laid for it, [col. 22] when it comes down to feed. And with this stupidity of the dove they would have <i>Hosea</i> compare the sloth of the <i>Israelites</i>, who took no precaution against dangers and threw themselves into the power of their enemies. However that may be, this simplicity of the dove was recognized also by οἱ ἔξωθεν [hoi exōthen]. Thus on that line of <i>Juvenal</i>,

‎— — — <i>censure harasses the doves,</i>

‎the old <i>Scholiast</i> says: <i>He referred "doves" to simplicity.</i> And in the <i>Suda</i> ἡμένη πελειὰς [hēmenē peleias], <i>a sitting dove</i>, is used as a proverb ἐπὶ τῶν ἁπλουστάτων [epi tōn haploustatōn], <i>of the most simple-minded.</i> This is, of course, διὰ τὸ εὔηθες τοῦ ζώου [dia to euēthes tou zōou], <i>because of the creature's stupidity</i>, as <i>Eustathius</i> has it on <i>Iliad</i> ψ΄ [psi]. The proverb, however, is not used of every dove, but of those that sit beside the decoy dove. As the <i>Suda</i> says, it is κατὰ ἀντιπαρεξέτασιν τῆς παλευτρίας [kata antiparexetasin tēs paleutrias], <i>by contrast with the decoy dove</i>, that is, the dove that fowlers misuse to lure the others. <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Birds</i>:

‎Τὰς περιστερὰς θ᾽ ὁμοίως ξυλλαβὼν εἵρξας ἔχει Κἀπαναγκάζει παλεύειν δεδεμένας ἐν δικτύῳ· [Tas peristeras th' homoiōs xyllabōn heirxas echei / Kapanankazei paleuein dedemenas en diktyō]

‎<i>And likewise he seizes the doves, keeps them confined, and forces them, bound in a net, to lure others.</i> And there the <i>Scholiast</i> says: Εἰώθασιν ἐκτυφλοῦντές τινα τῶν ὀρνέων ἱστάναι ἐν δικτύῳ, ὅπως τῇ φωνῇ προσκαλεῖτο τὰ ὁμοιογενῆ· [Eiōthasin ektyphlountes tina tōn orneōn histanai en diktyō, hopōs tē phōnē proskaleito ta homoiogenē] <i>They are accustomed to keep a blinded bird in a snare, so that by its voice it may call others of its kind.</i> That doves were used for this purpose is asserted not only by <i>Aristophanes</i> but also by the <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 7: Αἱ περισταὶ, he says, ζῶσι καὶ ὀκτὼ ἔτη, αἱ τετυφλωμέναι ὑπὸ τῶν παλευτρίας τρεφόντων αὐτάς· [Hai peristerai … zōsi kai oktō etē, hai tetyphlōmenai hypo tōn paleutrias trephontōn autas] <i>Doves that are blinded and kept as decoys live as long as eight years.</i> Now, if you consult more recent writers, what has been said about the simplicity of doves and the ease of catching them will be considerably confirmed by what [II, 553] Cadamosto [=Alvise Cadamosto, Navigazioni (1455-56)] relates happened to him, <i>Navigat.</i> ch. 40, when he first landed on the <i>Canary</i> Islands. <i>The doves</i>, he says, <i>were caught at will by a man's hand. For they were not frightened at the sight of a man.</i> And in chapter 6, on the island of <i>Madeira</i>: <i>I remember hearing from the inhabitants that when the island was first settled there was a very great abundance of doves there. To catch them the fowlers used no other device than a hook tied to a pole, so that they might be drawn to them from the trees on which they perch there; the birds were not frightened but full of wonder, as at something never seen before.</i>

‎Meanwhile, those who take ἀκεραίους [akeraious] in <i>Christ's</i> saying to mean <i>mild</i> and <i>gentle</i> have followed a good many <i>Grammarians</i>, for whom ἀκέραιον [akeraion], said of an animal, means <i>hornless</i>, as well as <i>kindly</i> and <i>harmless</i>. Beyond this, they have had regard to the nature of the dove, which is in truth a most mild and gentle animal. Accordingly <i>Ovid</i> calls it <i>placid</i>, <i>Metam.</i> book 7, Fable 12. And <i>Cyril</i> says that the dove πρὸς ἄκρον ἥκειν πρᾳότητος [pros akron hēkein praotētos], <i>reaches the highest summit of gentleness</i>, in book 15 <i>On Adoration</i>. Indeed, many think it entirely lacks gall. Hence <i>Clement</i> calls it ἄχολον [acholon], <i>Paedag.</i> book 1, ch. 5; and <i>Isidore</i>, <i>Orig.</i> book 12, calls doves <i>gentle birds without gall.</i> Of the same kind is what <i>Tertullian</i> writes, <i>On Baptism</i> ch. 8, that <i>the dove, even bodily, lacks gall itself.</i> And <i>Sedulius</i>, book 2 of the <i>Carmen</i>, on the Baptism of <i>Christ</i>:

‎<i>And it befits the Gentle One, the greatly Mild, to come forth through the bird that lacks gall.</i> — —

‎And <i>Horapollo</i>, <i>Hieroglyph.</i> book 1, ch. 54: Ἱστορεῖται δὲ ὅτι οὐ χολὴν ἔχει τοῦτο τὸ ζῶον [Historeitai de hoti ou cholēn echei touto to zōon], <i>It is also reported that this animal lacks gall.</i> Yet he himself refutes this in book 2, ch. 45, saying that the dove ἐν ὀπισθίοις τὴν χολὴν ἔχειν [en opisthiois tēn cholēn echein], <i>has its gall at the back.</i> And <i>Galen</i>, in the book <i>On Black Bile</i>, says those people are ridiculous who think the dove lacks gall because it does not have τὴν ἐπὶ τῷ ἤπατι κύστιν [tēn epi tō hēpati kystin], <i>the gall bladder next to the liver.</i> Nor did this escape the <i>Philosopher</i>, whose words are, <i>Hist.</i> book 2, ch. 15: Ἔνιοι πρὸς τῇ κοιλίᾳ ἔχουσιν, οἱ δὲ πρὸς τοῖς ἐντέροις τὴν χολὴν, οἷον περιστερά [Enioi pros tē koilia echousin, hoi de pros tois enterois tēn cholēn, hoion peristera]. Hence <i>Pliny</i>, book 11, ch. 37: <i>In some the gall is attached only to the intestine, as in doves.</i>

‎Another point to be noted here in doves is their conjugal chastity, which the ancients praise remarkably. <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 7: Οὔτε γὰρ συν- [col. 23] δυάζεσθαι θέλουσι πλείοσιν, οὔτε προσλείπουσι τὴν κοινωνίαν, πλὴν ἐὰν χῆρος, ἢ χήρα γένηται· [Oute gar syndyazesthai thelousi pleiosin, oute proleipousi tēn koinōnian, plēn ean chēros ē chēra genētai] <i>For they will not pair with more than one, nor do they ever abandon their partnership, except through widowhood.</i> <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, ch. 34: <i>Chastity is foremost with them, and adultery is known to neither. They do not violate conjugal fidelity, and they keep a common home. Unless unmated or widowed, a dove does not leave its nest.</i> <i>Propertius</i>, book 2, <i>Eleg.</i> 12:

‎[II, 554] <i>So let doves joined in love be my example, male and female, a whole marriage.</i>

‎Of the turtledove and the wood pigeon the same <i>Philosopher</i> writes: Ἔχει δὲ τὸν ἄῤῥενα ἡ τρυγὼν τὸν αὐτὸν καὶ φάττα, καὶ ἄλλον οὐ προσίενται [Echei de ton arrhena hē trygōn ton auton kai phatta, kai allon ou prosientai], <i>The turtledove too, and the wood pigeon, have one male, and do not admit another.</i> Indeed, <i>Porphyry, On Abstinence from Animal Food</i> book 3, and <i>Phile, On Animals</i> ch. 19, report that wood pigeons punish adultery with death. Both take this from <i>Aelian, Hist.</i> book 3, ch. 44, where he adds that turtledoves keep the same law scrupulously, but that white doves kill only the males caught in adultery, taking pity on the females. <i>Clement</i>, too, in the second book of the <i>Stromata</i>, reports that there are certain animals ἃ τὴν ἐπιμιξίαν οὐ ποιεῖται πρὸς πολλὰ καὶ ἀναίδην, ἀλλὰ πρὸς ἓν, καὶ ὁμόφυλον· οἷαι αἱ πελειάδες, καὶ αἱ φάσσαι, καὶ τὸ τρυγόνων γένος, καὶ ὅσα τούτοις παραπλήσια [ha tēn epimixian ou poieitai pros polla kai anaidēn, alla pros hen, kai homophylon; hoiai hai peleiades, kai hai phassai, kai to trygonōn genos, kai hosa toutois paraplēsia], <i>which do not mate with many and shamelessly, but with one, and that of the same kind, as are doves, and wood pigeons, and the turtledove kind, and those that are like them.</i> What is more, they say that the black dove remains a widow after the death of its mate, and that for this reason among the <i>Egyptians</i> it was the symbol of a woman who shrinks from a second marriage. <i>Horus</i> book 2, ch. 30: Γυναῖκα χήραν ἐπιμείνασαν ἄχρι θανάτου θέλοντες σημῆναι, περιστερὰν μέλαιναν ζωγραφοῦσιν· αὕτη γὰρ οὐ συμμίγνυται ἑτέρῳ ἀνδρὶ ἕως οὗ χηρεύει [Gynaika chēran epimeinasan achri thanatou thelontes sēmēnai, peristeran melainan zōgraphousin; hautē gar ou symmignytai heterō andri heōs hou chēreuei] <i>When they wish to signify a woman who remains a widow until death, they paint a black dove: for this bird, as long as it is widowed, does not join with another male.</i> Accordingly <i>Tertullian</i> tries to establish his monogamy by the example of the dove: <i>A bird</i>, he says, <i>not only harmless but also chaste, which one male alone knows.</i> But the later <i>Fathers</i>, with great unanimity, transferred this very thing to turtledoves, for what reason I do not know. <i>Origen</i> on the <i>Song of Songs</i>, Homily 2: <i>They say that the nature of turtledoves is such that the male does not approach any female besides one, nor does the female accept more than one male; so that, if it happens that one is taken away and the other survives, the love of mating is extinguished together with the mate. Basil</i> in the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, Homily 8: Τὴν τρυγόνα φασὶ διαζευχθεῖσάν ποτε τοῦ ὁμόζυγος, μηκέτι τὴν πρὸς ἕτερον καταδέχεσθαι κοινωνίαν, ἀλλὰ μένειν ἀσυνδύαστον, μνήμῃ τοῦ ποτε συζευχθέντος τὴν πρὸς ἕτερον κοινωνίαν ἀπαρνημένην· [Tēn trygona phasi diazeuchtheisan pote tou homozygos, mēketi tēn pros heteron katadechesthai koinōnian, alla menein asyndyaston, mnēmē tou pote syzeuchthentos tēn pros heteron koinōnian aparnēmenēn] <i>They say that the turtledove, once separated from its mate, never enters into union with another, but remains single without a mate, refusing marriage with another in remembrance of the partner it has lost. Gregory of Nazianzus</i> in the <i>Precepts to Virgins</i>, Poem 3:

‎Πυνθάνομ᾽ ὡς πτερόεσσα τρυγὼν τρυγόνος φιλίοιο Χηρωθεῖσ᾽ ὁμόλεκτρον ἑὸν ποθέουσα σαόφρων, Οὐ δέχεται πόσιν ἄλλον ἑῆς καθύπερθε καλιῆς· [Pynthanom᾽ hōs pteroessa trygōn trygonos philioio Chērōtheis᾽ homolektron heon potheousa saophrōn, Ou dechetai posin allon heēs kathyperthe kaliēs]

‎<i>I hear that the female of the turtledove, once widowed of her mate, restrains herself out of longing for her spouse and admits no other mate into her nest. Chrysostom</i>, Homily [II, 555] <i>On the Turtledove</i>, <i>English</i> edition, vol. 7, p. 264: Φασί τινες τὴν τρυγόνα φίλανδρον εἶναι, καὶ φίλαγνον· ὥς τε κἂν συμβῇ τὸν ταύτης ἄῤῥενα ὑπὸ ἀετοῦ ἀναλωθῆναι, ἢ ὑπὸ ἰξευτοῦ θηρευθῆναι, ταύτην ἑτέρῳ ἀνδρὶ μὴ ἐπιμίγνυσθαι, ἀλλ᾽ αὐτὸν ποθεῖν, αὐτὸν ἀναμένειν, καὶ τῇ τούτου μνήμῃ συναποθνήσκειν. [Phasi tines tēn trygona philandron einai, kai philagnon; hōs te kan symbē ton tautēs arrena hypo aetou analōthēnai, ē hypo ixeutou thēreuthēnai, tautēn heterō andri mē epimignysthai, all᾽ auton pothein, auton anamenein, kai tē toutou mnēmē synapothnēskein.] <i>Some say that the female turtledove is held by love for her mate and is so devoted to chastity that, if her male happens to be taken by an eagle or by a fowler, she does not mate with another, but longs for and awaits him alone, and even dies together with the memory of him.</i> So also <i>Gregory of Nyssa</i>, Homily 3 on the <i>Song</i>; <i>Epiphanius</i> in the <i>Physiologus</i>, ch. 10; <i>Philo of Carpasia</i> on <i>Cant. 1.</i>; <i>Ambrose</i> in the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, book 5, ch. 19, and on <i>Psalm. CXVIII.</i>; <i>Jerome</i>, <i>Against Jovinian</i>, book 1, ch. 17; <i>Justus of Urgel</i>, [col. 24] <i>Theodoret</i>, <i>Aponius</i> and <i>Gregory the Great</i> on <i>Song 1:10</i>; <i>Eustathius</i> on the <i>Hexaemeron</i>; <i>Ildefonsus of Toledo</i>, Sermon <i>on the Purification of St Mary</i>, if it is really his; <i>Theophylact</i> on <i>Luke</i>, ch. 2; <i>Pisides</i>, <i>On the Creation of the World</i>, line 998; <i>Geoffrey, Abbot of Vendôme</i>, Sermon 7; <i>Michael Glycas</i>, <i>Annals</i>, section 1; <i>Bernard</i> on the <i>Song</i>, Sermons 40 and 59, and the Sermon <i>on the Nativity of St Mary, On the Aqueduct</i>; the <i>Baal Hatturim</i> [=Jacob ben Asher (d. 1343), commentary on the Torah] on <i>Lev 12:6</i>; <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on <i>Ps 74:19</i>; <i>Damir</i> in the chapter <i>On</i> شفنين [shifnīn] <i>siphnin</i>, that is, the <i>turtledove.</i> All these, I say, attribute to the turtledove what the older writers had written of the dove alone: that she is so devoted to one mate that, when he dies, she does not admit another. As a result, those who have commented on the <i>Song of Solomon</i> have not sufficiently understood why <i>Solomon</i> so often designates the bride by the name of his dove. Thus <i>Song 2:14</i>: <i>My dove, in the clefts of the rock</i>, etc. And <i>Song 5:2</i>: <i>Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove.</i> And <i>Song 6:8</i>: <i>My dove, my perfect one, is but one</i>, etc. By this, clearly, he wished to signify that she was loved by him alone, and that he in turn was uniquely dear to her, just as among doves there is a remarkable love between mates and a very firm law of marriage. If this was less true in the type, that is, in <i>Solomon</i>, who had wives by the ἑκατοντάδας [hekatontadas], it is nevertheless most true ἐν τῷ τυπωθέντι [en tō typōthenti], that is, in <i>Christ</i>: just as we acknowledge him alone as bridegroom, so he has one church, which he has betrothed to himself for ever in righteousness, judgment, lovingkindness and mercy, <i>Hos 2:19.</i> Hence the <i>Apostle</i>, 2 Cor 11:2: <i>I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin</i> to Christ.

‎Although המה properly means <i>to make a noise</i> and <i>to be in tumult</i>, nevertheless the <i>Hebrew</i> מעי המו in <i>Song 5:4</i> the <i>Greeks</i> render ἡ κοιλία μου ἐθροήθη [hē koilia mou ethroēthē], and <i>Jerome</i>, <i>My belly trembled.</i> So too in <i>Ezek 7:16</i>, where the same Jerome has, <i>They shall be on the mountains like doves, all trembling</i>, and <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], <i>They shall be on the mountains themselves like doves of the valleys, all trembling</i>: the words <i>all trembling</i> correspond to the <i>Hebrew</i> כלם המות. And in <i>Hos 11:11</i>, for ויחרדו you may read in <i>Junius</i>, <i>they shall come running in trepidation</i>, etc., <i>like a dove from the land</i> of Assyria. In others: <i>they shall tremble</i>, or <i>they shall fear</i>, or <i>they tremble like a dove</i>, etc. These expressions exactly capture the dove's nature. For the dove [II, 556] is a most fearful animal. Hence <i>Varro</i> on doves, <i>On Agriculture</i>, book 3, ch. 7: <i>Because of their natural fear they seek the highest places on roofs.</i> And a little later: <i>For nothing is more timid than a dove.</i> And in <i>Ovid</i>, <i>Metam.</i> book 5, Fable 10, the nymph <i>Arethusa</i> says of herself and of <i>Alpheus</i> pursuing her:

‎<i>So I ran, so that wild one pressed upon me,</i> <i>As the dove flees the hawk on trembling wing,</i> <i>And as the hawk is wont to harry trembling doves.</i>

‎And <i>Tristia</i>, book 1, <i>Elegy</i> 1:

‎<i>The dove, once wounded by your talons, hawk,</i> [col. 24] <i>is terrified by the slightest whirring of a wing.</i>

‎And <i>Valerius Flaccus</i>, in the eighth book of the <i>Argonautica</i>, of Medea:

‎<i>But lo! the maiden, after the manner of a frightened dove,</i> <i>When, encompassed by the huge shadow of a bird of prey,</i> <i>She falls trembling upon whatever man she meets. Just so she,</i> <i>Struck with heavy fear, threw herself into the midst,</i> etc.

‎As the Chorus in <i>Sophocles' Ajax</i>, line 139:

‎— — — πεφόβημαι Πτηνῆς ὡς ὄμμα πελειάς. [pephobēmai / Ptēnēs hōs omma peleias.]

‎<i>I fear, like the eye of the winged dove.</i> And another <i>poet</i> in the <i>Suda</i>, under Πελειάδες [Peleiades]:

‎Δειλαί τοι δείλοισιν ἐφεδρεύουσι πέλειαι, [Deilai toi deiloisin ephedreuousi peleiai,]

‎<i>Timid doves sit upon timid [eggs].</i> To this belongs the fact that the dove is so often called τρήρων [trērōn] by <i>Homer</i>, ἐπεὶ διὰ τὴν ἀθένειαν εὐλαβὴς ἡ ὄρνις αὕτη, τρεῖν δ' ἐστὶ τὸ εὐλαβεῖσθαι [epei dia tēn atheneian eulabēs hē ornis hautē, trein d' esti to eulabeisthai], <i>because on account of its weakness this bird is fearful, for</i> τρεῖν [trein] <i>means to</i> [col. 25] <i>fear.</i> So <i>Athenaeus</i> in book 9. Nor otherwise the old <i>Scholiast on Homer</i>, <i>Eustathius</i> on <i>Odyssey</i> μ' [mu], <i>Hesychius, Suidas, the Etymologicum,</i> etc. And <i>Apollonius</i> imitates <i>Homer</i> thus, in book 3 of the <i>Argonautica</i>, verse 541:

‎Τρήρων μὲν φεύγουσα βίην κίρκοιο πελειὰς, Ὑψόθεν Αἰσονίδεω πεφοβημένη ἔμπεσε κόλποις· [Trērōn men pheugousa biēn kirkoio peleias, Hypsothen Aisonideō pephobēmenē empese kolpois]

‎<i>A timid dove, fleeing the violence of a hawk, fell terrified from on high into the bosom of</i> Aeson's son.
