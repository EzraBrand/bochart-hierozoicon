# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 13: The ravens that brought bread and meat to Elijah at the brook Cherith, 1 Kgs 17 (vol. 2, pp. 806–811)

### ‎[II, 806] Chapter XIII. [col. 214]

### ‎<i>On the ravens that brought Elias bread and meat near the brook Cherith, 1 Reg. XVII, 3.</i>

‎Now we must speak of <i>Elias</i>, to whom, while he was hiding at the brook <i>Cherith</i> near the <i>Jordan</i>, הערבים, the <i>ravens</i>, at God's command, brought bread and meat twice a day, <i>1 Reg. XVII, 3. 4. 5. 6.</i> Because this has seemed scarcely credible to captious minds, there are those who deny that <i>ravens</i> are meant by the <i>Hebrew</i> word ערבים <i>orebim</i>. Thus, according to <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], some explain it as <i>merchants</i>, as in <i>Ezek 27:27</i>, where we have ערבי מערבך, that is, <i>those who trade in your wares</i>. For others ערבים are <i>Arabs</i>, as in <i>2 Chr 21:16</i> and <i>Neh 4:1.</i> In <i>Bereshith Rabba</i>, ch. 33, we read עיר היא בתחום ביתשאן ושמה ערבו, <i>There is a town named</i> Arabo <i>in the vicinity of Bethshan</i> (or <i>Scythopolis</i>), as <i>Rabbi Judah</i> writes; its citizens are thought to be called הערבים here. For the <i>Arabic</i> translator too, ערבים <i>Orebim</i> are not ravens but the name of a people. For he renders <i>1 Reg. XVII, 4.</i>: <i>I have commanded</i> لعوربيم [li-ʿwrbym] the Orebimites <i>to feed you</i>. And in verse six: وكان العوربيم [wa-kāna al-ʿwrbym] <i>And it came about that the Orebimites brought him bread and meat</i> <sup>R1</sup>. But it is easy to show that these interpretations have nothing sound in them. For although <i>Ezekiel</i> [II, 807] describes the <i>Tyrian</i> merchants with the periphrasis ערבי מערבך, <i>those who trade in your wares</i>, ערבים by itself nowhere means <i>merchants</i>. Nor would God have said in general terms, <i>I have commanded merchants</i>, but rather <i>I have commanded the merchants of this or that place to feed you.</i> The other renderings are refuted by the location of the place where this miracle occurred. For near the <i>Jordan</i>, where <i>Elias</i> was hiding, there were no <i>Arabs</i>, no <i>Orebimites</i>, nor any town named <i>Arabo</i>, as far as I know. Moreover, <i>Arabs</i> are not called ערבים <i>Orebim</i> in <i>Hebrew</i>, but ערבים <i>Arbim</i>; and as for the town ערבו <i>Arabo</i>, if there ever was one (which is not yet established to my satisfaction), its inhabitants would have been called ערבויים <i>Arabojim</i> <sup>R2</sup>, not <i>Orebim.</i> Furthermore, <i>Elias</i>, ordered to hide there, would not truly have been hidden if the local inhabitants had known of his hiding place. And this hiding place would soon have been disclosed to King <i>Ahab</i>, who was searching for him everywhere with great care <sup>R3</sup>.

‎Here, therefore, the הערבים are nothing other than true <i>ravens</i>, as, after [col. 215] the <i>Greeks</i>, they are understood by <i>Josephus</i>, the <i>Chaldee</i>, the <i>Syriac</i>, <i>Jerome</i>, most of the <i>Hebrews</i>, and all the Doctors of the Church without exception. God says that he commanded them: <i>I have commanded the ravens</i> (he says) <i>to feed thee there.</i> God is indeed said to command even brute animals, as in <i>2 Chr 7:13. If I command the locust to devour the land.</i> Amos 9:3 <i>And I will command the serpent, and he shall bite them.</i> And Jonah 2:10 (var. 11.) <i>God spoke to the fish, that it should vomit out</i> Jonah <i>upon the land.</i> But in the same way he commands even inanimate things: the heavens, for instance, <i>Isa 45:12</i>; the clouds, <i>Job 38:11. Ps 78:23. Isa 5:6</i>; the dawn, <i>Job 38:12</i>; the sword, <i>Amos 9:4.</i> To <i>command brute animals</i>, or <i>clouds</i>, etc., is to use them to carry out his decrees. Kimchi therefore rightly says here: וטעם צויתי ששמתי בלבם להביא לחם ובשר שתי פעמים ביום, <i>I have commanded</i> (the ravens), <i>that is, I have put it into their mind to bring bread and meat twice a day.</i> But it is in a very different way that he says, in the ninth verse of the same chapter, that he commanded the widow to feed <i>Elias</i>. For he had commanded her either by an express word, or by inclining her mind to charity and piety toward the servant of God. It should further be noted that several ravens, not one, were appointed for this task, as <sup>a)</sup> <i>Eutychius of Alexandria</i> holds; for one would not have sufficed to carry the food <i>Elias</i> needed. For in the life of <i>Paul the Hermit</i> by Saint <i>Jerome</i>, among [II, 808] certain other things which I confess I do not understand, there is also this: that a single raven brings a whole loaf, enough for the meal of two men, namely <i>Antony</i> and <i>Paul</i>. <i>They look up</i>, he says, <i>and see that a raven had settled on a branch of the tree, which, flying gently down from there, laid a whole loaf before their astonished faces. After it had gone, Well,</i> said Paul, <i>the Lord has sent us a meal,</i> etc. <i>For sixty years now I have always received a piece of half a loaf; but at your coming</i> Christ <i>has doubled the ration for his soldiers.</i> Here, however, the case is different. For it is not one raven bringing food to several, but several bringing it to one. That food was bread and meat. Many of the ancients hold that these were brought separately: bread in the morning and meat in the evening. <i>Athanasius</i> in the <i>Synopsis</i>: Οἱ κόρακες ἔφερον αὐτῷ, διὰ ῥήματα Κυρίου, πρωΐας μὲν ἄρτους, δείλης δὲ κρέα [Hoi korakes epheron autō, dia rhēmata Kyriou, prōïas men artous, deilēs de krea]. <i>Theodoret</i> on <i>3 Kings</i>, Question 52: Προσέφερον γὰρ οἱ κόρακες ἄρτους μὲν ἕωθεν, δείλης δὲ κρέα [Prosepheron gar hoi korakes artous men heōthen, deilēs de krea]. <i>Augustine</i>, Sermon 146 <i>De tempore</i>: <i>The raven brought the servant of God bread in the morning, meat in the evening.</i> <i>Eutychius</i>, as cited above: وكان غراب ياتيه في كل يوم بالغداة بخبز وبالعشا بلحم ياكله [wa-kāna ghurāb yaʾtīhi fī kull yawm bi-l-ghadāh bi-khubz wa-bi-l-ʿashā bi-laḥm yaʾkuluhu] <i>And it was a raven that brought him every day bread in the morning and meat in the evening, to eat.</i> This is indeed what the <i>Greek</i> version has in most copies; but the <i>Complutensian</i> has more correctly what is found in the <i>Hebrew</i>, <i>Chaldee</i>, <i>Syriac</i>, <i>Arabic</i> and <i>Roman</i> text: ἄρτους καὶ κρέας τὸ πρωῒ, καὶ ἄρτους καὶ κρέας τὸ δείλης [artous kai kreas to prōi, kai artous kai kreas to deilēs]. For God willed that, amid the utmost scarcity of everything, both bread and meat should be brought to his servant, so that the meal might be complete and his providence in preserving him might shine out the more. To the same purpose is the fact that the ravens brought these meals to the <i>Prophet</i> twice a day, morning and evening, namely dinner and supper, at fixed hours. For that both were customary among the <i>Jews</i> is clear from <i>Luke 14:12.</i> And a midday meal is mentioned in <i>Gen 43:25</i> <i>Ruth 2:14</i> and <i>Acts 10:9–10.</i>

‎The <i>Hebrews</i> in <i>Kimchi</i> anxiously ask where the ravens brought the bread and meat from. Some hold that they were taken from the table of <i>Ahab</i>, others from that of <i>Jehoshaphat</i>. As if the <i>prophet</i>, brought up in the utmost frugality, had needed royal fare. I would rather say that these things were set aside for the <i>prophet</i> by some one of those seven thousand who <sup>b)</sup> had not bowed the knee before <i>Baal</i>, whom God [col. 216] willed to be privy to so great a secret. Yet it is safest to answer with the author of the <i>Mirabilia Scripturae</i>, book 2, chapter 15: <i>But whence that raven brought that meat and bread, let him see to it who entrusted it with such a task.</i> Elsewhere <sup>c)</sup> an angel of God sets bread and water before this same <i>Elias</i> when he was hungry in the desert, which you will say was more fitting, and Θεοπρεπέστερον [theoprepesteron]. But God also willed the same [II, 809] service to be performed by ravens, to teach that all creatures are subject to his command <sup>R4</sup>. But the raven, you will say, is an unclean bird, defiling everything by its touch. Quite so, since it mostly feeds on carcasses, and the ceremonial law counts it among the unclean birds. But <i>what God has cleansed must not be held unclean</i>, Acts 10:15. The same God who had enacted this law also abrogated it, or at least dispensed <i>Elias</i> from observing it for a time. Although there was no need of such a dispensation. For the observance of ceremonies could be set aside when the necessity of life pressed. Thus <i>Samson</i> was permitted to eat the honeycomb which bees had built in the dead lion, as <i>Theodoret</i> observes on this passage. And <i>David</i>, when hungry, ate without scruple the bread τῆς προθέσεως [tēs protheseōs], <i>which</i> otherwise <i>it was not lawful to eat except for the priests alone</i>, <i>Luc. VI</i>, 4. And accordingly it was rightly decreed that the <i>Jews</i>, when attacked by the enemy on the sabbath day, might fight and repel force with force, <i>1 Macc 2:41.</i> For life was at stake. And <i>the Sabbath is for man, not man for the Sabbath.</i> Mark 2:27. The author of the <i>Mirabilia</i> asserts that the ravens serving <i>Elias</i> made amends for the fault committed in the flood. <i>The ravens</i>, he says, <i>are commanded to minister</i> to the prophet, <i>so that that bird might be seen to expiate the fault it had committed in the flood, by becoming a faithful servant</i> to Elias, <i>it which had before been negligent and deceitful</i> toward Noah. <i>Chrysostom</i>, in the Sermon <i>On Elias</i>, holds that the <i>Prophet</i> was like the ravens in this: that as the raven is a μισότεκνον [misoteknon] animal, so <i>Elias</i> hated his children, namely the <i>Jews</i>, for their sins, and, having shut heaven by his prayers, had imposed a long famine on them. And that God therefore wished to soften the <i>prophet's</i> mind, exasperated beyond measure, by the example of the ravens, which, harsh and μισότεκνοι [misoteknoi] though they are, yet with a changed disposition now took pity on the <i>prophet</i> and supplied him with food lest he perish of hunger: as if he had said to <i>Elias</i>, Μὴ τοσαύτη παρὰ σοὶ μετὰ Ἰουδαίων μισοτεκνία, ὅση τοῖς κόραξι κατὰ τῶν νεοσσῶν τῶν οἰκείων. ἀλλ᾽ ὅρα νῦν μεταβεβλημένους τοὺς κόρακας. ὅρα τὴν ἐκείνων, ὦ Ἠλία, φιλανθρωπίαν. οἱ περὶ τοὺς οἰκείους ἀπανθρωποὶ νεοσσοὺς, σοῦ γίνονται ξενοδόχοι φιλότιμοι. Ἄτοπόν ἐστιν, ὦ Ἠλία, τοὺς μὲν κόρακας μεσίτας γίνεσθαι πρὸς σὲ τῆς παρ᾽ ἐμοῦ φιλανθρωπίας· σὲ δὲ μεσίτην τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις μὴ γίνεσθαι τῆς παρ᾽ ἐμοῦ κατ᾽ [II, 810] αὐτῶν τιμωρίας. Αἰδέσθητι τὴν τῶν κοράκων μεταβολὴν, καὶ γένου περὶ τοὺς Ἰουδαίους χρηστός. [Mē tosautē para soi meta Ioudaiōn misoteknia, hosē tois koraxi kata tōn neossōn tōn oikeiōn. all᾽ hora nyn metabeblēmenous tous korakas. hora tēn ekeinōn, ō Ēlia, philanthrōpian. hoi peri tous oikeious apanthrōpoi neossous, sou ginontai xenodochoi philotimoi. Atopon estin, ō Ēlia, tous men korakas mesitas ginesthai pros se tēs par᾽ emou philanthrōpias; se de mesitēn tois Ioudaiois mē ginesthai tēs par᾽ emou kat᾽ autōn timōrias. Aidesthēti tēn tōn korakōn metabolēn, kai genou peri tous Ioudaious chrēstos.] <i>Do not hold the</i> Jews, <i>your children, in as great hatred as the ravens hold their chicks. Yet see the ravens now changed. See</i>, O Elias, <i>their kindness. They who are inhuman toward their own chicks show themselves generous hosts toward you. It is absurd</i>, O Elias, <i>that the ravens should be mediators of my kindness toward you, while you do not intercede for the</i> Jews, <i>whom I am punishing. Marvel at the change in the ravens, and be kind toward the</i> Jews. There are some among more recent writers who hold that God the more justly exacted this service from the ravens, that they should feed his <i>prophet</i>, because of the greater care with which he feeds them when, still chicks, they are abandoned by their parents, as they prove from the words of <i>Job</i>, <i>David</i>, and <i>Christ</i> himself, which we have explained otherwise.

‎The brook <i>Cerith</i>, or <i>Carith</i>, at which this miracle took place, is thought to be the same as the brook <i>Kana</i>, or <i>of the reed-bed</i>, mentioned in <i>Josh 16:8</i> and <i>XVII, 9.</i> I would readily believe this, and that it was called קנה <i>kana</i> from the abundance of reeds in which <i>Elias</i> lay hidden. Like the one of whom the greatest of poets says,

‎— — <i>You lay hidden behind the sedge.</i>

‎And the brook כרית <i>Cerith</i> is named from the verb כרה <i>cara</i>, which the <i>Greek</i> translators at <i>Job 40:25</i> render very aptly by ἐνσιτεῖσθαι [ensiteisthai], that is, <i>to be fed</i> or <i>to eat</i>, because at it the <i>Prophet</i> was miraculously fed by ravens. If this conjecture is true, a prolepsis [col. 217] must be recognized in these words: <i>Hide thyself by the brook Cerith.</i> However that may be, from the sacred text we know this much: that this brook was near the <i>Jordan</i>, and to the east of <i>Samaria</i>. So <i>Josephus</i> was not paying attention when he wrote that <i>Elias</i> withdrew εἰς τὰ πρὸς νότον μέρη [eis ta pros noton merē], <i>to the parts that lie towards the</i> south <sup>R5</sup>. And far more absurdly <i>Eutychius</i>, confusing this retreat of <i>Elias</i> with his forty-day fast on Mount <i>Horeb</i>: وهرب ايلياس النبي علي تخوم الاردن الي جبل حوريب وهو طور سينا وسكن في شعب عين وكان يشرب الما من ذلك الشعب [wa-haraba Īliyās al-nabī ʿalī tukhūm al-Urdunn ilī jabal Ḥūrīb wa-huwa Ṭūr Sīnā wa-sakana fī shiʿb ʿayn wa-kāna yashrabu al-mā min dhālika al-shiʿb] <i>And</i> Elias <i>the prophet fled through the borders of the</i> Jordan <i>to Mount</i> Horeb, <i>which is Mount</i> Sinai, <i>and dwelt by the cleft of a spring, from which he drank water.</i> To these words he adds the rest about the raven bringing bread and meat.
