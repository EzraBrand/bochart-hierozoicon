# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 13: The hunter partridge in a cage (Sirach 11) (vol. 2, pp. 645–647)

### ‎[II, 645] [col. 89] Chapter 13.

### ‎<i>What is</i> πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ [perdix thēreutēs en kartallō], <i>the hunter partridge in a cage, Sir 11:31 var. 37.</i>

‎Having dealt with the spurious partridge, we shall say a little about the genuine one, with reference to the well-known passage of <i>Sirach</i>, <i>Sir 11:31</i> var. 37: Πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς ἐν καρτάλλῳ, οὕτως καρδία ὑπερηφάνου, καὶ ὡς ὁ κατάσκοπος ἐπιβλέπει πτῶσιν [Perdix thēreutēs en kartallō, houtōs kardia hyperēphanou, kai hōs ho kataskopos epiblepei ptōsin]; <i>Like a hunter partridge in a cage, so is the heart of the proud man, who like a spy watches for a fall.</i> The sense is that a proud man, when another has been driven to ruin, exults over him in the same way as the hunter partridge in a cage exults over the birds of its own kind that it has led into the snare. For there πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς [perdix thēreutēs] is a tame partridge, which by its arts lures wild partridges into the cage or the snares. <i>Aristotle, History</i> book 9, ch. 8: Καὶ τῶν περδίκων δ᾽ οἱ τιθασσοὶ τοὺς ἀγρίους πέρδικας ὀχεύουσι, καὶ ἐπικορίζουσι [kai tōn perdikōn d᾽ hoi tithassoi tous agrious perdikas ocheuousi, kai epikorizousi] (read ἐπικοῤῥίζουσι [epikorrizousi]) καὶ ὑβρίζουσιν. Ἐπὶ δὲ τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρδικα ὠθεῖται τῶν ἀγρίων ὁ ἡγεμὼν, ἀντιάσας ὡς μαχούμενος, τούτου δ᾽ ἁλόντος ἐν ταῖς πηκταῖς, πάλιν προσέρχεται ἄλλος, ἀντιάσας τὸν αὐτὸν τρόπον [kai hybrizousin. Epi de ton thēreutēn perdika ōtheitai tōn agriōn ho hēgemōn, antiasas hōs machoumenos, toutou d᾽ halontos en tais pēktais, palin proserchetai allos, antiasas ton auton tropon]. <i>Tame partridges also tread the wild partridges, and beat them, and treat them with insult. And the leader of the wild ones rushes at the hunter partridge, advancing against it as if to fight. When he has been caught in the wicker cage, another in turn comes forward against it in the same way.</i> You see in both places τὸν θηρευτὴν πέρ- [col. 90] δικα [ton thēreutēn perdika], <i>the hunter partridge</i>, and the <i>wicker cage</i>. For that is properly κάρταλλος [kartallos] in <i>Sirach</i>, as αἱ πήκται [hai pēktai] is in <i>the Philosopher</i>. <i>Hesychius</i>: Κάρταλον [Kartalon] (read Κάρταλλον [Kartallon]): τὸ πλεκτὸν ἀγγεῖον [to plekton angeion] (I would prefer πηκτὸν [pēkton], as in <i>Aristotle</i>) ἐν τοῖς ὀψαρτυτικοῖς κλωβός [en tois opsartytikois klōbos]. Quite correctly. For a <i>cage</i> of this kind is also called κλωβὸς [klōbos], as in <i>Hebrew</i> כלוב <i>club</i>, and in <i>Syriac</i> ܩܠܘܒܝܐ [קלוביא] <i>klubja</i>. Thus <i>Ierem. V</i>, 27: ככלוב מלא עוף, <i>like a cluub</i> (cage) <i>teeming with birds</i>. Here the <i>Syriac</i> has ܐܝܟ ܩܠܘܒܝܐ [איך קלוביא], <i>like a klubja</i> (although the <i>Greeks</i> have ὡς παγὶς ἐφεσταμένη [hōs pagis ephestamenē], and the <i>Arabic</i> مثل فخ منصوب [mithl fakhkh manṣūb] [II, 646] ܡܨܝܕܬܐ [מצידתא] <i>like a set snare</i>, and Jerome <i>like a trap</i>). For the subject is a cage into which birds are lured, as is clear from the whole context. For the verse immediately preceding reads: <i>For among my people are found those who lie in wait like fowlers, setting snares to catch men.</i> And this verse itself: <i>Like a</i> club <i>teeming with birds, so their houses are full of deceit</i>, that is, of things gained and acquired by deceit. Thus, a point that has escaped the <i>lexicographers</i>: just as כלוב <i>club</i> occurs in the <i>Hebrew</i> text of <i>Jeremiah</i>, so also in <i>Greek</i> writers κλουβὸς [kloubos] or κλωβὸς [klōbos] occurs elsewhere too, either for this deceitful cage or for any cage at all. <i>Oppian</i> in the <i>Ixeutica</i> [III, 14.], on the aegithus: <i>It is caught</i> κλωβῷ [klōbō], <i>in a cage, in which another bird, caught earlier, is shut up to lure it by its voice.</i> An old <i>poet</i> in <i>Suidas</i>, under Ἀμφιῤῥῶγας [Amphirrhōgas]: Κλωβοὺς τ᾽ ἀμφιῤῥῶγας [Klōbous t᾽ amphirrhōgas], <i>cages broken on all sides.</i> <i>Philostorgius</i> book 10, ch. 11 (in my manuscript, which is often more correct than the printed copy), on the <i>Egyptian</i> dwarf: Ὁ δὲ Αἰγύπτιος οὕτω κατεβραχύνετο, ὥστε μηδ᾽ ἀχαρίστως τοὺς ἐν τοῖς κλουβοῖς πέρδικας ἐκμιμεῖσθαι, καὶ συναθύρειν αὐτῷ πρὸς ἔριν ἐκείνους [Ho de Aigyptios houtō katebrachyneto, hōste mēd᾽ acharistōs tous en tois kloubois perdikas ekmimeisthai, kai synathyrein autō pros erin ekeinous]; <i>The</i> Egyptian <i>was so stunted that he imitated, not unpleasingly, the partridges shut up in clubs</i> (or <i>cages</i>), <i>which themselves contended with him in play.</i> And <i>Nicephorus</i>, book 12, ch. 37, says that the same man was παραπλήσιον πέρδικι, καὶ μὴ ἀχάριστον ἐν κλωβῷ τὴν ἐκείνου προσομιλίαν [paraplēsion perdiki, kai mē acharislon en klōbō tēn ekeinou prosomilian]. And <i>Cedrenus</i>, Paris edition, p. 592 B: Ζῶον ἦν τι πτῆνον ἐν κλωβῷ κατὰ τὰ βασίλεια αἰωρούμενον [Zōon ēn ti ptēnon en klōbō kata ta basileia aiōroumenon]. <i>There was a certain bird in the palace, hung up in a clobus</i> (that is, a <i>cage</i>). The Glosses of <i>Philoxenus</i>: Κλωβὸς [Klōbos], <i>cavea</i> [cage]. Therefore a very learned man wishes in vain to change κλουβοῖς [klouhois] in <i>Philostorgius</i> to κλοιοῖς [kloiois] or κλυβοῖς [klybois]. And the translator of <i>Nicephorus</i> rendered the words ἐν κλωβῷ [en klōbō] quite absurdly: <i>In a globe, namely of men.</i> The <i>Talmudists</i>, in the tractate <i>Kilaim</i>, call a cage for catching birds פלצור <i>paltsur</i>, and the Romans <i>transenna</i>. <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Bacchides</i>, act 4, the scene <i>Nimio</i>:

‎<i>Now this thrush is going for the worm from the trap. He will hang there nicely today, so well have I set the snare.</i>

‎And in the <i>Persa</i>, act 4, the scene <i>Cui homini</i>:

‎<i>Today I shall lead this man into the trap with clever tricks.</i>

‎And in the <i>Rudens</i>, act 4, the scene <i>Quam mox</i>:

‎<i>O</i> Gripus, Gripus, <i>in men's lifetime there come to be very many traps, in which they are caught by tricks</i> <sup>R1</sup>.

‎[II, 647] Moreover, others also mention the hunter partridge. Except that instead of "hunter" <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9, calls it θηρεύοντα πέρδικα [thēreuonta perdika], <i>a hunting partridge</i>. Pliny <sup>a)</sup> calls it the <i>fowler's</i> informer, or rather his <i>decoy</i> [<i>illicem</i>], as <i>Turnebus</i> <sup>b)</sup> and <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] <sup>c)</sup> advise reading. Aelian <sup>d)</sup>, in the same sense, says τὸν παλεύοντα πέρδικα [ton paleuonta perdika]. <i>Eustathius of Antioch</i> says τὸν πέρδικα συμπράττοντα τοῖς θηρευταῖς πρὸς τὴν ἄγραν [ton perdika sympratonta tois thēreutais pros tēn agran]. <i>Theodoret</i>, in the <i>Catena</i> on <i>Jeremiah</i>, says πέρδικα θηρατικὸν [perdika thēratikon], adding that such birds are obtained from <i>Babylon</i>. Indeed, this kind of fowling was already in use in the East long ago. Accordingly, in <i>Xenophon</i>, book 1 of the <i>Cyropaedia</i>, <i>Cambyses</i> addresses his son <i>Cyrus</i> thus: Ὄρνιθες δὲ ἐπεπαίδευτό σοι, ὥς τέ σοι μὲν τὰ συμφέροντα ὑπερητεῖν, τὰς δὲ ὁμοφύλους ὄρνιθας ἐξαπατᾷν [Ornithes de epepaideuto soi, hōs te soi men ta sympheronta hypērētein, tas de homophylous ornithas exapatan], <i>"You had trained birds so that they would both serve you in what was useful and deceive birds of the same kind."</i> Whoever wants more on this subject should consult those we have cited. Also <i>Albertus Magnus</i>, where he treats of the <i>cubeth</i> <sup>e)</sup>, that is, the <i>female partridge</i>, [col. 91] in Arabic القبج [al-qabj], which he says, when shut in a cage, lures the males to itself. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], however, attributes this very thing to the male in these words: في طبع الذكر ان يخدع امثاله بقرقرته ولهذا اتخذه الصيادون في اشراكهم ليكثر القرقرة فيجتمع الحجل فيقعن معه [fī ṭabʿ al-dhakar an yakhdaʿa amthālahu bi-qarqaratihi wa-li-hādhā ittakhadhahu al-ṣayyādūn fī ashrākihim li-yukthira al-qarqara fa-yajtamiʿa al-ḥajal fa-yaqaʿna maʿahu] <i>"It is the nature of the male to lure birds like itself by its call; therefore hunters take it with them, so that by its frequent calling it may gather the female partridges, which fly to it."</i> But that both sexes are used for this, with varying success, is shown at length by <i>the Philosopher</i>, Aelian and <i>Athenaeus</i> in the places cited above. The <i>Arabic</i> version of <i>Sirach</i>, instead of القبج في القفص [al-qabj fī al-qafaṣ] <i>a partridge in a cage</i>, has, without any sense, الصنج في الرقص [al-ṣanj fī al-raqṣ] <i>a sistrum</i>, or <i>a cymbal in the dance</i>. That this arose through the ignorance of scribes is shown by the similarity of the letters. For <i>Pagnino, Castalio, Arias, Camerarius, Jansenius</i> and others, πέρδιξ θηρευτὴς [perdix thēreutēs] is <i>a captured partridge</i>, as though it were πέρδιξ θηρευθεὶς [perdix thēreutheis]. By the same error the <i>Syriac</i> has ܐܝܟ ܩܩܒܢܐ ܕܨܝܕܐ [איך קקבנא דצידא] <i>like a partridge taken in hunting</i>; and the <i>Vulgate</i>, <i>"as a partridge is led into the cage."</i>
