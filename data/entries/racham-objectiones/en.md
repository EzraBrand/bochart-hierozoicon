# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 27: Answers to what can be objected to the chapter before (vol. 3, pp. 65–78)

### ‎[III, 65] [col. 311] CHAPTER 27.

### ‎<i>A reply to the objections that can be made to the preceding chapter.</i>

‎Since we have so far proved that רחם <i>racham</i> is the <i>vulture</i>, some objections seem possible from which it would follow that it is a bird different from the vulture. First, the <i>Arabs</i>, when they define the <i>racham</i>, say expressly that it is an <i>eagle</i>. <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: رخمة طاير النسر [rakhma ṭāyir al-nasr] "The rachama <i>is the eagle bird</i>", that is, of the eagle kind. <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī]: الانوق العقاب الرخمة [al-anūq al-ʿuqāb al-rakhma] "Anuk, <i>eagle</i>, <i>rachama</i>." Accordingly, in <i>Alkazuinius</i> [col. 312] the <i>rachama</i>, when about to give birth, is said to bring from <i>India</i> a stone that has another stone in its belly and relieves the pains of birth, that is, the ἀετίτης [aetitēs] stone, or <i>eagle-stone</i>. For it is described thus. Pliny, book 10, chapter 3: <i>"This stone is pregnant, another stone sounding within, in its womb, when you shake it."</i> Dioscorides, book 5: Ἀετίτης λίθος, ὁ ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι ἦχον ἀποτελῶν, ὡς ἑτέρου ἐγκύμων λίθου ὑπάρχων· [Aetitēs lithos, ho en tō kineisthai ēchon apotelōn, hōs heterou enkymōn lithou hyparchōn] <i>"The eagle-stone, which makes a sound when moved, as though pregnant with another stone conceived within it."</i> And a little later: Ἐν δὲ τῷ καιρῷ τῆς ἀποτέξεως, περίαπτε τῷ μηρῷ, καὶ ἐκτὸς ὠδίνων τέξεται. [En de tō kairō tēs apotexeōs, periapte tō mērō, kai ektos ōdinōn texetai] <i>"At the hour of birth tie it to the thigh</i> (of the woman in labour), <i>and she will give birth without pain."</i> Chrysermus in Stobaeus, Discourse 98: Ἐν Εὐφράτῃ, ποταμῷ τῆς Παρθίας, γεννᾶται λίθος ἀετίτης καλούμενος· ὃν αἱ μαῖαι ταῖς δυστοκούσαις ἐπὶ τὰς γαστέρας ἐπιτιθέασι, καὶ παραχρῆμα τίκτουσιν ἄτερ ἀλγηδόνος. [En Euphratē, potamō tēs Parthias, gennatai lithos aetitēs kaloumenos; hon hai maiai tais dystokousais epi tas gasteras epititheasi, kai parachrēma tiktousin ater algēdonos] <i>"In the</i> Euphrates, <i>a river of</i> Parthia, <i>a stone is produced called</i> aëtides, <i>which midwives place on the bellies of women in difficult labour, and at once they give birth without pain."</i> Further, since the <i>Greeks</i> call it ἀετίτης [aetitēs], or <i>eagle-stone</i>, from the eagle, and not γυπίτης [gypitēs], or <i>vulture-stone</i>, from the vulture, it seems to follow that this stone is brought by the eagle, not by the vulture. And this is what the ancient writers assert. Pliny, in the place cited: <i>"In the nests of the first three kinds of eagles and of the fifth the aëtites stone is built in."</i> And book 30, ch. 14: <i>"The aëtites stone, found in the nest of the eagle."</i> And book 36, chapter 21: <i>"The aëtites stone has great fame from the meaning of its name.</i> [III, 66] <i>It is found in the nests of eagles."</i> Sextus: <i>"The eagle's stone is found in its belly or nest."</i> Isidore, <i>Origines</i>, book 16, chapter 5: <i>"Aëtites</i> (read <i>Aëtitae</i>) <i>stones are found in the nests of eagles."</i> Horus on the eagle, book 2, chapter 46: Ἐκεῖνος ἀπὸ θαλάττης, ἢ ἀπὸ γῆς λίθον ἐπαίρει, καὶ τίθησιν εἰς τὴν ἰδίαν νεοττίαν· [Ekeinos apo thalattēs, ē apo gēs lithon epairei, kai tithēsin eis tēn idian neottian] <i>"It takes up a stone from the land or the sea and brings it into its own nest."</i> Philostratus, book 2, chapter 7: Τὰ δὲ τῶν ὀρνίθων τίς οὐκ οἶδεν, ὡς ἀετοὶ μὲν, καὶ πελαργοὶ, καλιὰς οὐκ ἂν πήξαιντο, μὴ πρότερον αὐταῖς ἐναρμόσαντες, ὁ μὲν τὸν ἀετίτην λίθον, ὁ δὲ τὸν λυχνίτην; [Ta de tōn ornithōn tis ouk oiden, hōs aetoi men, kai pelargoi, kalias ouk an pēxainto, mē proteron autais enarmosantes, ho men ton aetitēn lithon, ho de ton lychnitēn] <i>"Who does not know what birds do: that eagles and storks never build their nests without placing stones in them, the former the aëtites, the latter the lychnites stone?"</i> Aelian too, <i>History</i>, book 1, chapter 35, writes that eagles place in their nests τὸν λίθον, ὅσπερ οὖν ἐξ αὐτῶν ἀετίτης κέκληται [ton lithon, hosper oun ex autōn aetitēs keklētai], <i>"the stone which is called aquiline after them."</i> But the <i>Arabs</i> themselves also teach that this stone is found in the eagle's nest. Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter on العقاب [al-ʿuqāb] <i>Alokab</i>, which is a species of eagle: حجر العقاب حجر يشبه نوي التمر هندي اذا حرك سمع له صوي فاذا كسر لا يري فيه شي يوجد في عش العقاب والعقاب يجلبه من بلد الهند [ḥajar al-ʿuqāb ḥajar yushbih nawā al-tamr hindī idhā ḥurrika sumiʿa lahu ṣawī fa-idhā kusira lā yurā fīhi shay yūjad fī ʿushsh al-ʿuqāb wa-l-ʿuqāb yajlibuhu min balad al-hind] <i>"The stone of</i> alokab (or <i>of the eagle</i>) <i>is a stone like the kernel of the tamarind; when it is moved a sound is heard, yet when it is broken nothing is seen in it. It is found in the nest of</i> alokab (<i>the eagle</i>). <i>And</i> alokab (<i>the eagle</i>) <i>brings it from</i> India." And <i>Alkazuinius</i> himself, in the chapter <i>On</i> نسر [nasr], or <i>the eagle</i>: اذا كان اوان بيضها فالنسر الذكر يمشي الي بلاد الهند وياتي بحجر يوجد في [col. 313] بعض جبالها ويترك تحت الانثي لتخف عليه الالم [idhā kāna awān bayḍihā fa-l-nasr al-dhakar yamshī ilā bilād al-hind wa-yaʾtī bi-ḥajar yūjad fī baʿḍ jibālihā wa-yutrak taḥt al-unthā li-takhiff ʿalayhi al-alam] <i>"When the eagle is about to lay its eggs, the male goes off to</i> India <i>and brings from there a stone that is found in some mountains of that land, and places it under the female to relieve her pains."</i> Therefore, since in the same <i>Alkazuinius</i> the bird <i>rachama</i> is said to bring this very stone from <i>India</i>, he seems altogether to have taken the <i>rachama</i> for a true <i>eagle</i>. Third, since we have proved, both from reasons and from the authority of the ancients, that in the list of forbidden birds דיה <i>daja</i> is the <i>vulture</i>, if רחם <i>racham</i> is also the <i>vulture</i>, the same bird will be forbidden twice by <i>Moses</i> under different names in the same sequence of discourse, which does not seem tolerable. Fourth, it can be said that there is nothing in the vulture that answers to the name רחם, derived from <i>mercy</i> and natural στοργή [storgē] towards the young, since the vulture is a rapacious and carnivorous bird,

‎— — <i>monstrous with its hooked beak,</i>

‎as the <i>poet</i> has it, and so utterly foreign to those most tender affections with which a mother burns for her offspring. Hence <i>Antoninus Liberalis</i>, Fable [III, 67] 21, rightly writes that the vulture is πάντων ὀρνίθων ἔχθιστον θεοῖς τε, καὶ ἀνθρώποις· καὶ διὰ παντὸς ἵμερον αὐτῷ κρέως καὶ αἵματος ἐνέβαλον ἀνθρωπείου [pantōn ornithōn echthiston theois te, kai anthrōpois; kai dia pantos himeron autō kreōs kai haimatos enebalon anthrōpeiou], <i>of all birds the most hateful to gods and men; in which</i> (the gods) <i>implanted a perpetual craving for human flesh and blood.</i> Indeed, in <i>Photius</i>, Author 223, <i>Theodore</i>, or rather <i>Diodorus</i>, bishop of <i>Tarsus</i>, denies that the vulture feeds its young. Διὰ τί δέ τινα τῶν ζώων [Dia ti de tina tōn zōōn], he says, οὐ τρέφει τὰ γεννήματα, ὥσπερ πτηνῶν γύπας, καὶ κόρακες; [ou trephei ta gennēmata, hōsper ptēnōn gypas, kai korakes?] <i>Why do some animals not feed their offspring, as among birds vultures and ravens?</i> And below: Τίς δὲ κόρακας, καὶ γύπας ἐδίδαξεν ἵπτασθαι· οὓς οὐδὲ τρέφειν οἱ γεγεννηκότες πεφύκασι; [Tis de korakas, kai gypas edidaxen hiptasthai; hous oude trephein hoi gegennēkotes pephykasi?] <i>Who taught ravens or vultures to fly, whom their parents are not even accustomed to feed?</i> But these objections will be refuted with no trouble.

‎Among the <i>Arabs</i>, they say, رخم [rakham] <i>racham</i> is نسر [nasr] <i>neser</i>, that is, the <i>eagle</i>, not the <i>vulture.</i> We answer that some <i>Arabs</i>, though not all, define it so. For <i>Damir</i> expressly says الرخمه طاير ابقع يشبه النسر في الخلقة [al-rakhma ṭāyir abqaʿ yushbih al-nasr fī al-khilqa]: <i>The</i> rachama <i>is a variegated bird, approaching the nature of the eagle.</i> Thus in <i>Damir</i>'s opinion it is not the same bird as the eagle, but merely similar to it. This is most true of the vulture, of which <i>Philes</i> [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)] says:

‎Οὗ τήν τε κεφαλήν γε, βασιλεῦ, καὶ τὸ στόμα, Κἂν μακρόθεν βλέπῃ τις, αἰετὸν λέγει· [Hou tēn te kephalēn ge, basileu, kai to stoma, Kan makrothen blepē tis, aieton legei;]

‎<i>Whose head and beak, O king, if anyone sees them from afar, he calls it an eagle.</i> Especially among the kinds of eagle, the percnopteri have a vulture-like appearance. Hence they are called γυπαίετοι [gypaietoi], <i>vulture-eagles.</i> Aristotle, <i>History</i>, book 9, chapter 32: Ἔστι δ᾽ ἕτερον γένος (ἀετῶν) περκνόπτερος, etc. γυπὶ ὅμοιος· ὀρειπέλαργος καλεῖται, καὶ γυπαίετος [Esti d᾽ heteron genos (aetōn) perknopteros, etc. gypi homoios; oreipelargos kaleitai, kai gypaietos]. <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 3: <i>Of the fourth</i> (eagle) <i>kind is the percnopterus, also called oripelargus, of vulture-like appearance.</i> Others call them <i>aegypii</i>. <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i>, book 2, chapter 46: Τοὺς δὲ αἰγυπιοὺς, ἐν μεθορίῳ γυπῶν ὄντας καὶ αἰετῶν [Tous de aigypious, en methoriō gypōn ontas kai aietōn]: <i>The aegypii, which are intermediate between vultures and eagles.</i> Moreover, great writers hold that eagles, through mixed mating, at length degenerate into vultures. Pliny: <i>Sea eagles have no kind of their own, but are born from the mixed mating of eagles. What is born of them in turn has its kind among the ossifrages, from which smaller vultures are produced; and from these the great ones, which do not breed at all.</i> This is taken from these words of Aristotle's <i>Marvels</i>: Ἐκ τοῦ [col. 314] ζεύγους δὲ τῶν ἀετῶν θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων ἁλιαίετος γίνεται παραλλὰξ, ἕως ἂν σύζυγα γένηται, ἐκ δὲ τούτων οἱ περκνοὶ, καὶ γῦπες. οὗτοι δὲ οὐκέτι διορίζουσι περὶ τοὺς γύπας, ἀλλὰ γεννῶσι τοὺς μεγάλους γύπας· οὗτοι δ᾽ εἰσὶν ἄγονοι· [Ek tou zeugous de tōn aetōn thateron tōn agonōn haliaietos ginetai parallax, heōs an syzyga genētai, ek de toutōn hoi perknoi, kai gypes. houtoi de ouketi diorizousi peri tous gypas, alla gennōsi tous megalous gypas; houtoi d᾽ eisin agonoi;] But <i>Pliny</i> rendered this passage of <i>Aristotle</i> in such a way that whatever was difficult (namely the words θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων [thateron tōn agonōn] and παραλλὰξ ἕως ἂν σύζυγα γένηται [parallax heōs an syzyga genētai]) he skipped over with a carefree leap. [III, 68] I think the first words, namely θάτερον τῶν ἀγόνων [thateron tōn agonōn], are corrupt, and that θάτερον τῶν ἀγενῶν [thateron tōn agenōn] should be read instead, so that the sense of the whole passage is: "From a pair of eagles, one ignoble chick is born in alternation, namely the sea eagle. And when σύζυγα [syzyga] have been born, that is, two degenerate eagles, or two sea eagles, from them are born ossifrages; from the ossifrages, percni and vultures, whose kind changes no further. For these beget other vultures, but larger and sterile ones." Since it follows from what has been said that there is a close kinship between eagles and vultures, no one will be surprised that these birds are often confused by writers and one taken for the other. To prove this by examples: that the liver of <i>Prometheus</i> was devoured by an eagle is related by <i>Hesiod</i>, <i>Theogony</i>, verse 523; <i>Aeschylus</i> in <i>Prometheus Bound</i>, verse 996; <i>Apollonius</i>, <i>Argonautica</i>, book 2, verse 1254, and book 3, verse 851; <i>Accius</i> in <i>Prometheus</i>; <i>Apollodorus</i>, books 1 and 2; <i>Diodorus</i>, book 4; <i>Philostratus</i>, book 2; <i>Plutarch</i> in the book <i>On Rivers</i>, under <i>Phasis</i>; <i>Lucian</i> in <i>Prometheus</i> and in <i>Sacrifices</i>; <i>Pherecydes</i>, book 2; <i>Agroetas</i> in <i>Scythica</i>, book 13; <i>Duris</i> and <i>Herodorus</i> in the Scholiast on <i>Apollonius</i>; <i>Servius</i> on <i>Virgil</i>'s sixth <i>Eclogue</i>, etc. Yet <i>Fulgentius</i>, in book 2 of the <i>Mythologies</i>, describes to us <i>Prometheus offering his ever-renewed liver to a fierce vulture</i>, and cites as authorities <i>Nicagoras</i> in the book <i>Distemithea</i> (I read <i>On Prometheus</i>), <i>Aristoxenus</i> in the book of <i>Epimetheia</i>, and <i>Petronius Arbiter</i> in these <i>Hendecasyllables</i>:

‎<i>The vulture that roams through the depths of the liver</i> <sup>a)</sup><i>, and tears at the breast and the innermost fibres, is not the one the idle</i> <sup>b)</sup> <i>poets speak of, but the evils of the heart: envy and excess.</i>

‎There, however, instead of <i>There is none whom the warm</i> or <i>the charming poets call</i>, some read, not badly, <i>There is none whom</i> Tityus, <i>etc.</i> If this reading is accepted, <i>Petronius</i> would be speaking not of <i>Prometheus</i> but of <i>Tityus</i>, of whom the greatest <i>poet</i> says in book 6 of the <i>Aeneid</i>:

‎— <i>and a monstrous vulture with hooked beak, pecking at the immortal liver.</i> — —

‎And long before, the father of fables, <i>Odyss.</i> λ' [l]:

‎Γῦπε δέ μιν ἑκάτερθε παρημένῳ ἧπαρ ἔκειρον, Δέρτρον ἔσω δύνοντες, [Gype de min hekaterthe parēmenō hēpar ekeiron, Dertron esō dynontes,]

‎<i>And two vultures, perched on either side, were digging into his liver, having pierced the skin within.</i> Perhaps, then, a corrupt reading of <i>Petronius'</i> words deceived <i>Fulgentius</i>. But consider the passage of <i>Valerius Flaccus</i> on the Promethean herb, <i>Argonautica</i> book 2, line 357:

‎[III, 69] — <i>it brings forth the Caucasian flower from the blood of the</i> Promethean <i>flesh, herbage nourished by the thunder,</i> <i>which that sacred gore, amid the snows and the grim frosts,</i> <i>hardens and puts forth, when the vulture, its gullet sated,</i> <i>rises from the crags and sprinkles it from its open beak.</i>

‎If, I say, you compare this passage of <i>Valerius</i> with these lines of <i>Apollonius</i> on this same herb, <i>Argonautica</i> book 3, line 850:

‎Πρωτοφυὲς τό γ᾽ ἀνέσχε καταστάξαντος ἔραζε Αἰετοῦ ὠμηστέω κνημοῖς ἐνὶ Καυκασίοισιν, Αἱματόεντ᾽ ἰχῶρα Προμηθῆος μογεροῖο. [Prōtophyes to g' anesche katastaxantos eraze Aietou ōmēsteō knēmois eni Kaukasioisin, Haimatoent' ichōra Promēthēos mogeroio.]

‎you will plainly see that <i>Valerius</i> has made a vulture out of the eagle, just as among our own writers <i>Theophylact</i> does when he wants the eagles of which <i>Christ</i> speaks in <i>Matth. XXIV</i>, 28. to be taken as vultures. Ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα, he says, συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή [Epi nekron sōma … synagontai oxeōs hoi aetoi, hoi gypes dēladē]. <i>To a corpse the eagles, that is, the vultures, quickly gather. Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] [col. 315] also observes that <i>Albertus</i> and certain other more recent writers often confuse the vulture with the eagle. And <i>Albertus</i> defends himself with the authority of <i>Aristotle</i>, who, he writes, assigns the eagle and the vulture to the same genus; this <i>Gesner</i> rightly denies. Yet <i>Albertus</i> had a reason for believing it. For he had read neither the <i>Greek</i> text of <i>Aristotle</i> nor a <i>Latin</i> version made from the <i>Greek</i> text, but one made from the <i>Arabic</i>. Such were the times in which <i>Albertus</i> lived. Then there was no one among the <i>Latins</i> who read the <i>Greek</i> writers in <i>Greek</i> or rendered them into <i>Latin</i>: only the <i>Arabs</i> were consulted, who had translated into their own language whatever existed in <i>Greek</i>, and from their versions barbarous translators rendered the <i>Greek</i> works into <i>Latin</i> after a fashion. The <i>Arabs</i>, moreover, extend the word نسر [nasr] <i>neser</i>, which properly means <i>eagle</i>, even to the <i>vulture</i>. Hence they sometimes ascribe to the <i>neser</i> things that are proper to the vulture. Thus what <i>Alkazuinius</i> says belongs to the vulture: النسر يتبع العساكر لطعمه في لحم القتال [al-nasr yatbaʿu al-ʿasākir li-ṭuʿmihi fī laḥm al-qitāl] that the neser <i>follows armies, in order to feed on the flesh of the slain.</i> Likewise, ياكل الجيف حتي لا يقدر علي الطيران [yākulu al-jiyaf ḥattā lā yaqdiru ʿalā al-ṭayarān] <i>that it gorges itself on carcasses so much that it can no longer fly.</i> But these words of <i>Damir</i> too, in the chapter نسر [nasr] <i>Neser</i>, are proper to the vulture rather than to the eagle: وهو حاد البصر يري الجيفة من اربع ماية فرسخ وكذلك حاسة شمه الي النهايه لكنه اذا شم الطيب مات لوقت [wa-huwa ḥādd al-baṣar yarā al-jīfa min arbaʿ māya farsakh wa-kadhālika ḥāssat shammihi ilā al-nihāya lākinnahu idhā shamma al-ṭīb māta li-waqtihi] <i>It has very keen sight, so that it sees a carcass from four hundred parasangs. Its sense of smell is likewise extremely intense. Accordingly, when it smells perfume, it dies on the instant.</i> For in sight the [III, 70] eagle is indeed strongest, as was proven in its place <sup>c)</sup>; but the power of smell is believed to be greater in the vulture. Hence <i>Lucretius</i>, book 4:

‎<i>Though bees are drawn from far by the scent of honey,</i> <i>and vultures by carcasses.</i> — —

‎And <i>Apuleius</i> in the first <i>Apology</i>: <i>But if he has caught with his nostrils the smell of home cooking, he surpasses dogs and vultures in keenness of scent. For to what dog, to what vulture would anything of the</i> Alexandrian <i>sky be perceptible as far away as the borders of the</i> Aeenses<i>?</i> And <i>Sidonius</i>, book 7, letter 14: <i>They display subtlety of smell, as if the vulture did not excel in scent.</i> Hence the verse in common use:

‎<i>The vulture excels in smell, the spider in touch.</i>

‎<i>Isidore</i>, however, in <i>Etymologies</i> book 12, chapter 7, joining eagles to vultures, says: <i>"Vultures, like eagles, perceive carcasses even beyond the seas."</i> But what follows makes it plain that in writing this he had sight rather than smell in view: <i>"For, flying higher, they see from on high many things that are hidden by the darkness of the mountains."</i> But, to pass over the rest, at least what the <i>Arab</i> adds, that the <i>neser</i> or <i>eagle</i> is harmed by the smell of ointment, is clearly written by the ancients only of the vulture. Thus <i>Aristotle</i> in the <i>Mirabilia</i>: Λέγεται καὶ τοὺς γῦπας ὑπὸ τῆς τῶν μύρων ὀσμῆς ἀποθνήσκειν [Legetai kai tous gypas hypo tēs tōn myrōn osmēs apothnēskein]; <i>"They say also that vultures are killed by the smell of ointments."</i> <i>Theophrastus</i>, <i>On the Causes of Plants</i> book 6, chapter 4, asserts that certain animals ὑπὸ τῶν ἡδίστων ἡμῖν ἀναιρεῖσθαι, καθάπερ οἱ γῦπες ὑπὸ τῶν μύρων [hypo tōn hēdistōn hēmin anaireisthai, kathaper hoi gypes hypo tōn myrōn], <i>"are destroyed by those things which are most pleasant to us, as vultures by ointments."</i> And in the book <i>On Odours</i>: Πονεῖν δ᾽ ἔνια φαίνεται ταῖς ὀσμαῖς, καὶ ταῖς [col. 316] εὐωδίαις, εἴπερ ἀληθὲς τὸ ἐπὶ τῶν γυπῶν [Ponein d᾽ enia phainetai tais osmais, kai tais euōdiais, eiper alēthes to epi tōn gypōn]; <i>"Some creatures also appear to be harmed by smell and fragrance, if what is reported of vultures is true."</i> <i>Pliny</i>, book 11, chapter 53: <i>"Vultures, which are put to flight by ointment, seek other odours."</i> Aelian, <i>History</i> book 3, chapter 7: Εὐωδία δὲ, καὶ μῦρον γυψὶν αἰτία θανάτου [Euōdia de, kai myron gypsin aitia thanatou]; <i>"Fragrance and ointment bring death to vultures."</i> And book 4, chapter 18: Γυπῶν γε μὴν τὸ μῦρον ὄλεθρός ἐστι [Gypōn ge mēn to myron olethros esti]; <i>"Ointment is the bane of vultures."</i> <i>Clement</i>, <i>Paedagogus</i> book 2, chapter 8: Μὴ λάθωμεν, ὥσπερ οἱ γῦπες τὰ μύρα βδελυττόμενοι [Mē lathōmen, hōsper hoi gypes ta myra bdelyttomenoi]; <i>"Let us not unwittingly, like vultures, abhor ointments."</i> Eustathius <i>on the</i> Hexaemeron: Φασὶ δὲ τὸν γῦπα τὰ ῥόδινα μύρα χρισθέντα ἀποθνήσκειν [Phasi de ton gypa ta rhodina myra christhenta apothnēskein]; <i>"They say that the vulture, anointed with rose ointment, is killed."</i> Philes, <i>On Animals</i> chapter 2, where he treats of vultures:

‎Οἱ τῷ μύρῳ θνήσκουσι, κανθάρων δίκην [Hoi tō myrō thnēskousi, kantharōn dikēn]· [III, 71] <i>"Who are killed by ointment, like beetles."</i> You see, reader, that this is everywhere reported of vultures, nowhere of eagles. It is evident, therefore, that the <i>Arabs</i>, who attribute this same thing to the <i>neser</i>, meant by the name <i>neser</i> not the <i>eagle</i> alone but also the <i>vulture</i>. And so, since among the <i>Arabs</i> the name <i>neser</i> is general to <i>eagles</i> and <i>vultures</i>, it does not follow from their counting the <i>rachama</i> among the species of the <i>neser</i> that the <i>rachama</i> is not taken by them for a <i>vulture</i>. What of the fact that even <i>the Philosopher</i> counts this kind of vulture among the eagles, as will be shown below, although he assigns it a vulturine form and habits, and accordingly calls it γυπαίετον [gypaieton]?

‎II. The second objection was this: that the <i>rachama</i> in <i>Alkazuinius</i> brings from <i>India</i> the stone <i>abutacion</i>, hollow and resonant, and useful for childbirth; and since this can be no other than the ἀετίτης [aetitēs] stone, or <i>eagle-stone</i>, the bird which brings it also seems to be an eagle. <i>I reply</i> that the ancients indeed attributed this to the eagle alone, but that some centuries ago more recent writers attributed it also to the vulture. <i>Jerome</i>, or whoever is the author of the Epistle <i>On the Paschal Candle</i> under <i>Jerome's</i> name: <i>"The vulture, when it has begun to lay eggs, first takes from the land of</i> India <i>the instintium: and it is like a nut, and has within something which moves and gives out a sound. This it places by itself, and it produces many young, but one remains."</i> <i>Eustathius</i> on the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, of the vulture: Καὶ ἔγγυος (read ἔγκυος) γενόμενος πορεύεται εἰς τὴν Ἰνδικὴν, καὶ λαβὼν τὸν εὐτόκιον λίθον οὕτω καλούμενον, ἐπάνω αὐτοῦ καθέζεται, συνεχόμενος ὠδῖσι, καὶ ἀποτίκτει. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ λίθος περιφερὴς ὡς κάρυον· καὶ κινούμενος ἦχον ἐκπέμπει ὡς κώδων, ἔνδοθεν ἑτέρου λίθου κινουμένου [Kai engyos (engkyos) genomenos poreuetai eis tēn Indikēn, kai labōn ton eutokion lithon houtō kaloumenon, epanō autou kathezetai, synechomenos ōdisi, kai apotiktei. Esti de ho lithos peripherēs hōs karyon; kai kinoumenos ēchon ekpempei hōs kōdōn, endothen heterou lithou kinoumenou]. <i>"When</i> the vulture <i>has conceived, it sets out for</i> India, <i>and takes up the stone which they call</i> eutocion, <i>on which it sits in the pains of labour, and gives birth. The stone is round like a nut; and when it is shaken it gives out a sound like a bell, another stone moving inside."</i> He rightly calls it εὐτόκιον λίθον [eutokion lithon] from its property, because it <i>promotes childbirth</i>, though others prefer ὠκυτόκιον [ōkytokion]. <i>Harpocration of Alexandria</i>: Ὠκυτόκιος λίθος ἐστὶν ἐοικὼς τῷ ἀετίτῃ, μικρότερος δὲ ἐκείνου· πλὴν ἀπηχῶν ὡς ἐκεῖνος ἐν τῷ κινεῖσθαι παρὰ τὸ οὖς. ἔστι δὲ καὶ λεῖος τῇ θέᾳ [Ōkytokios lithos estin eoikōs tō aetitē, mikroteros de ekeinou; plēn apēchōn hōs ekeinos en tō kineisthai para to ous. esti de kai leios tē thea]. <i>"The</i> ocytocius <i>stone is like the aetites, but smaller than it; yet, when shaken by the ear, it sounds in the same way. And it appears smooth."</i> <i>Dioscorides</i> uses this word several times, but only as an adjective: as when he writes in book 5 that jaspers are φυλακτήρια περίαπτα, καὶ ὠκυτόκια μηρῷ περιαπτόμενα [phylaktēria periapta, kai ōkytokia mērō periaptomena], <i>"among the amulets that are tied on, and, tied to the thigh, hasten childbirth."</i> From these, correct the meaningless words, in the Epistle <i>On the Candle</i>, <i>Instintium</i>; and in <i>Alkazuinius</i> ابوطاقيون [abūṭāqiyūn]. And write instead اوطغيون [awṭghiyūn] εὐτόκιον [eutokion], or, if you prefer, اقوطغيون [aqūṭghiyūn] ὠκυτόκιον [ōkytokion].

‎III. To the third objection we reply that דיה <i>daja</i> and רחם <i>racham</i> are prohibited separately by <i>Moses</i>, although both words mean <i>vulture</i>, because there are [col. 317] different species of vultures. For while דיה <i>daja</i> is the <i>black vulture</i>, as we proved above [III, 72] <sup>d)</sup>, which in <i>Arabic</i> is called انوق [anūq] <i>anuk</i>, רחם <i>racham</i> is طاير ابقع [ṭāyir abqaʿ] a <i>variegated bird</i>, as <i>Damir</i> writes, since it is blackish only in its wings and white in the rest. So <i>Bellunensis</i> and <i>Giggeius</i> [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)] define it. Accordingly a horse which, from the mixture of its colours, we call in the vernacular a <i>pica</i> [magpie, piebald] is called in <i>Arabic</i> ارخم [arkham] <i>archam</i>, and a sheep so variegated رخما [rakhmā] <i>rachma</i>. Alcamus: شاة رخما بيضا راسها سودا سيرها [shāh rakhmā bayḍā rāsuhā sūdā sayruhā] "A <i>rachma</i> <i>sheep</i>, <i>which has a white head and the rest black</i>." Likewise فرس ارخم [faras arkham] an <i>archamo horse</i>, which is variegated in this way. The name is taken, as it were, from the bird, in which the colours are likewise mixed. Hence a man foremost in these studies rightly conjectures that this word signifies <i>Aristotle's</i> περκνόπτερον [perknopteron], or ὀρειπέλαργον [oreipelargon]; it is called <i>percnopterus</i> <i>from its black wings</i>, and <i>oripelargus</i>, that is, <i>mountain stork</i>, because, like a stork, it is partly white and partly black. The <i>Philosopher</i> counts this same bird among the eagles in such a way that it is named γυπάετος [gypaetos], that is, <i>vulture-eagle</i>, because in outward appearance it is γυπὶ ὅμοιος [gypi homoios], <i>like a vulture</i>, and has the habits of a vulture. I cannot understand how the <i>English</i> translator got wind of this, who has <i>Gier-eagle</i>, that is, <i>vulture-eagle</i>, here. Does he follow <i>Gesner</i>, who had seen <i>Bellunensis</i>? There are also some among the <i>Arabs</i> for whom the <i>rachama</i> is the <i>ossifrage</i>. Our <i>Golius</i> [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: "<i>It is explained by</i> <i>Meidanus</i> <i>and others by</i> the Persian استخوان رند [ustukhwān rand], <i>that is</i>, <i>ossifrage</i>." The Arabic Pandectarius: "<i>Arathamathi</i>, <i>that is</i>, <i>breaking bones</i>." <i>Arathamathi</i> is corrupted from الرخمة [al-rakhma] <i>arrachamath</i>, and <i>breaking bones</i> is the <i>ossifrage</i>. But the former is true.

‎IV. To the fourth objection, that the name רחם <i>racham</i>, derived ἀπὸ τῆς φιλοστοργίας [apo tēs philostorgias], is ill suited to the vulture, since it is a most savage bird, <i>I reply</i> that the vulture has a bad name among the common people and is held to be a savage bird because it feeds on human flesh; but the <i>Greeks</i> and <i>Egyptians</i>, who looked more deeply into the nature of the vulture, pronounce very differently about it. <i>Herodorus Ponticus</i>, in <i>Plutarch's</i> <i>Romulus</i> and in the <i>Roman Questions</i>, proves by three arguments that the vulture is of all animals of its kind δικαιότατον, καὶ ἀβλαβέστατον [dikaiotaton, kai ablabestaton], <i>the most just, and the least harmful</i>. The first is that οὐδενὸς ἅπτεται ζῶντος, οὐδὲ ἀποκτίννυσιν ἔμψυχον οὐδὲν, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, καὶ τὰ νυκτινόμα, χρῆται δὲ τοῖς ἄλλοις ἀποθανοῦσιν [oudenos haptetai zōntos, oude apoktinnysin empsychon ouden, hōs aetoi, kai hierakes, kai ta nyktinoma, chrētai de tois allois apothanousin], <i>it touches nothing living, nor kills any animate creature, as eagles and hawks and nocturnal birds do, but feeds only on carcasses</i>. The second, that καὶ τούτων τὰ ὁμόφυλα παρίησι· πετεινοῦ γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἑώρακε γῦπα γευόμενον, ὡς ἀετοὶ, καὶ ἱέρακες, μάλιστα τὰ συγγενῆ διώκουσι, καὶ κόπτουσιν· [kai toutōn ta homophyla pariēsi; peteinou gar oudeis heōrake gypa geuomenon, hōs aetoi, kai hierakes, malista ta syngenē diōkousi, kai koptousin], <i>even among carcasses it passes over those of its own kind: for no one has seen birds tasted by a vulture, which eagles and hawks above all pursue and kill.</i> [III, 73] The third, that ἀνθρώποις ἀβλαβέστατός ἐστιν, οὔτε καρπὸν ἀφανίζων, οὔτε φυτὸν, οὔτε ζῶον ἥμερον κακουργῶν [anthrōpois ablabestatos estin, oute karpon aphanizōn, oute phyton, oute zōon hēmeron kakourgōn], <i>it does very little harm to men, since it neither destroys crops nor plants, nor injures any tame animal.</i> Following this, <i>Eustathius</i> on the <i>Hexaëmeron</i> observes σπέρματα μὴ βλάττειν αὐτὸν, μηδέ τι ἔμψυχον διαλυμαίνεσθαι, καὶ μηδὲ νεκρῷ προσέρχεσθαι πετεινῷ διὰ τὴν συγγένειαν [spermata mē blattein auton, mēde ti empsychon dialymainesthai, kai mēde nekrō proserchesthai peteinō dia tēn syngeneian], <i>that it neither damages sown fields, nor harms any animate creature, nor approaches the carcass of any bird on account of kinship.</i> Hence the vulture was held by the <i>Romans</i> to be a bird of good omen. And <i>Hercules</i> too (according to <i>Herodorus</i> in <i>Plutarch</i>) πάντων μάλιστα γυψὶν ἐπὶ πράξεως ἀρχῇ φανεῖσιν ἔχαιρεν [pantōn malista gypsin epi praxeōs archē phaneisin echairen], <i>was delighted above all when vultures appeared at the outset of his undertakings.</i> What of the fact that the <i>Indians</i> threw the bodies of the dead to the vultures, as <i>Sextus the Philosopher</i> reports in book 3 of the <i>Pyr-</i> [col. 318] <i>rhonian Hypotheses</i>, chapter 14? That something of the kind was also customary among the <i>Persians</i> is proved at length by <i>Brissonius</i> at the end of book 2 of <i>De regno Persico</i>. And that the <i>Spaniards</i> did the same is attested by these lines of <i>Silius</i>, book 13, line 470:

‎<i>In the</i> Iberian <i>land, as they say, this is the ancient custom:</i> <i>the foul vulture consumes the lifeless bodies.</i>

‎This is to be understood of those who had fallen in war, fighting bravely for their country. For so the same poet says in book 3, line 340:

‎<i>The</i> Celts <i>came too, who had joined their name to the</i> Iberians. <i>For them it is an honour to have fallen in battle, and to burn the body such a one is a sacrilege. They believe they are borne to heaven and the gods above if the hungry vulture tears at their limbs as they lie.</i>

‎And Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 10, chapter 22: Βαρκαῖοι, γένος δὲ τοῦτό ἐστιν Ἑσπέριον, τῶν ἀποθανόντων νόσῳ τοὺς νεκροὺς ὑβρίζοντες, ὡς ἀνάνδρως καὶ μαλακῶς τεθνεώτων, θάπτουσι πυρί· τοὺς δὲ ἐν πολέμῳ βίον καταστρέψαντας, ὡς καλοὺς, καὶ ἀγαθοὺς, καὶ ἀρετῆς μετειληχότας, γυψὶ προβάλλουσιν ἱερὸν τὸ ζῶον εἶναι πεπιστευκότες. [Barkaioi, genos de touto estin Hesperion, tōn apothanontōn nosō tous nekrous hybrizontes, hōs anandrōs kai malakōs tethneōtōn, thaptousi pyri; tous de en polemō bion katastrepsantas, hōs kalous, kai agathous, kai aretēs meteilēchotas, gypsi proballousin hieron to zōon einai pepisteukotes.] "The Barcaei, <i>a Spanish people, burn with fire, by way of insult, those who have died of some disease, as having died in a womanly and cowardly fashion; but those who have fallen in war, as good and brave men of outstanding virtue, they cast to the vultures, because they consider this animal sacred."</i> There are, indeed, no <i>Barcaei</i> in <i>Spain</i>; but we should read Βακκαῖοι [Bakkaioi], as in <i>Stephanus</i>: Βακκαῖοι Ἱσπανίας ἔθνος [Bakkaioi Hispanias ethnos], <i>"the Baccaei, a Spanish people"</i>, namely those whom others call <i>Vaccaei</i>. Nor is there any doubt that Aelian wrote it so; after inserting some remarks about <i>Romulus</i>' twelve vultures, he adds that among the <i>Egyptians</i> the vulture is sacred to <i>Juno</i>, and that the head of <i>Isis</i> is adorned with its feathers, as are τῶν προπυλαίων ὀρόφους [tōn propylaiōn orophous], <i>the gables of the porches</i>.

‎But there is something else which I would not have the reader pass over carelessly, since it lends great weight to our interpretation. <i>Horapollo</i> reports that these same [III, 74] <i>Egyptians</i> in their <i>hieroglyphics</i> signified a merciful person by a painted vulture. His words are in book 1, chapter 11: Μητέρα δὲ γράφοντες, ἢ ἐλεήμονα, γῦπα ζωγραφοῦσι. [Mētera de graphontes, ē eleēmona, gypa zōgraphousi.] <i>"To signify a mother, or a merciful person, they paint a vulture."</i> And so that you may know that the mercy meant is precisely that which the <i>Hebrews</i> call by the proper name רחם <i>rechem</i>, that is, τὴν πρὸς τὰ τέκνα φιλοστοργίαν [tēn pros ta tekna philostorgian] [tender love toward one's young], the same writer gives this reason why a merciful person is depicted by the image of a vulture: <i>Because,</i> namely, <i>during the hundred and twenty days in which the mother rears her young, she almost never flies out, but is concerned only with her chicks and their feeding. Moreover, during those days, when she has nothing with which to sustain them,</i> τὸν ἑαυτῆς μηρὸν ἀνατεμοῦσα, παρέχει τοῖς τέκνοις τοῦ αἵματος μεταλαμβάνειν, ὡς μὴ ἀπορήσαντα τροφῆς ἀναιρεθῆναι [ton heautēs mēron anatemousa, parechei tois teknois tou haimatos metalambanein, hōs mē aporēsanta trophēs anairethēnai], <i>"cutting open her own thigh, she offers her blood to her chicks to suck, so that they may not perish from lack of food."</i> <i>Pisides</i> relates the same things at verse 1064, where he speaks of vultures:

‎Τὸν μηρὸν ἐκτέμνοντες, ἡματωμένοις Γάλακτος ὁλκοῖς ζωπυροῦσι τὰ βρέφη. Καί μοι δοκεῖ τὸν γῦπα σεμνῦναι πλέον. Τῇ συμπαθείᾳ τῆς γόνης τετμημένον, ῎Η τὸν μέγιστον εἰς ἀσέλγειαν Δία Τμηθέντα μηρὸν εἰς γονὴν ὀλεθρίαν. [Ton mēron ektemnontes, hēmatōmenois / Galaktos holkois zōpyrousi ta brephē. / Kai moi dokei ton gypa semnynai pleon. / Tē sympatheia tēs gonēs tetmēmenon, / Ē ton megiston eis aselgeian Dia / Tmēthenta mēron eis gonēn olethrian.]

‎<i>"Cutting out the thigh, they revive their young with a bloody stream of milk. And in my eyes the vulture, which cuts itself out of pity for its offspring, is worth more than that greatest</i> Jupiter, <i>who for the sake of a destructive offspring</i> (that is, <i>Bacchus</i>) <i>cut open his own thigh."</i> This, then, is the true reason for the name רחם <i>racham</i>; the <i>Fathers</i> distorted its story so as to attribute to the pelican what was proper to the vulture. Of the same bird's devotion to its chicks <i>Vincent</i>, <i>Doctrinale</i>, book 16, chapter 170, says: <i>"It feeds its chicks until they can fly, and exposes itself to danger for them; it even wounds with its talons, and strikes with its wings, one stronger than itself that wants to carry them off."</i> Nor was that supreme στοργὴ [storgē] [col. 319] and devotion of the vulture toward its young unknown to the ancient <i>Greeks</i>. A notable passage on this is in <i>Aeschylus</i>' <i>Agamemnon</i>, but very badly handled by the copyists and the translator. There he says that <i>Agamemnon</i> and <i>Menelaus</i> sailed to <i>Troy</i> on account of the abduction of <i>Helen</i>,

‎Τρόπον αἰγυπιῶν· οἵ τ᾽ ἐκπατίοις Ἄλγεσι παίδων ὕπατοι λεχέων στροφοδινοῦνται, Πτερύγων ἐρετμοῖσιν ἐρεσσόμενοι, δεμνιοτήρη Πόνον ὀρταλίχων ὀλέσαντες. — — [Tropon aigypiōn; hoi t᾽ ekpatiois / Algesi paidōn hypatoi lecheōn strophodinountai, / Pterygōn eretmoisin eressomenoi, demniotērē / Ponon ortalichōn olesantes. — —]

‎<i>"Like vultures, which, in their distraught grief for their</i> stolen <i>chicks, wheel round in circles high above their nests, rowing with the oars of their wings, having lost the toil they had spent in guarding the</i> [III, 75] <i>bed</i> (or <i>nest</i>) <i>of their chicks."</i> Instead of ἄλγεσι παίδων [algesi paidōn], <i>"grief for the chicks"</i> (supply <i>stolen</i>), the editions absurdly have ἄλγεσσι ποδῶν [algessi podōn], <i>"pain of the feet."</i> This is why the <i>Latin</i> translator strays as far as possible from the <i>poet's</i> meaning. But Stephanus' edition rightly has Ἄλγεσι παίδων [Algesi paidōn], and the old scholiast reads no differently. Homer had preceded <i>Aeschylus</i>: in <i>Odyssey</i> π, verse 216, he relates that <i>Ulysses</i> and <i>Telemachus</i>, in their mutual embrace, wept more abundantly than ossifrages or vultures whose chicks peasants have taken away before they were fledged for flight. The words are:

‎Κλαῖον δὲ λιγέως ἀδινώτερον, ἤ τ᾽ οἰωνοὶ Φῆναι, ἢ αἰγυπιοὶ γαμψώνυχες, οἷσί τε τέκνα Ἀγρόται ἐξείλοντο, πάρος πετεεινὰ γενέσθαι. [Klaion de ligeōs adinōteron, ē t᾽ oiōnoi / Phēnai, ē aigypioi gampsōnyches, hoisi te tekna / Agrotai exeilonto, paros peteeina genesthai.]

‎Why he joined ossifrages to vultures, as equally devoted to their young, is very well known. For in <i>Oppian</i>, book 1 of the <i>Halieutica</i>, we read that the ossifrage groans and wails like a woman if anyone has stolen one of its chicks, and thereafter does not leave the nest, neglecting food. Nor is it merciful only toward its own chicks, but toward those of others as well. <i>For the chicks cast out by eagles</i> (says <i>Pliny</i>) <i>are taken up by the kindred race of the ossifrage, which rears them with its own.</i> And <i>Antigonus</i>, Story 52: Ὁ δὲ Ἀριστοτέλης φησὶ τὴν φήνην τοὺς ἐκβληθέντας τῶν νεοττῶν ὑπὸ τοῦ ἀετοῦ λαμβάνειν, καὶ ἐκτρέφειν [Ho de Aristotelēs phēsi tēn phēnēn tous ekblēthentas tōn neottōn hypo tou aetou lambanein, kai ektrephein]· Aristotle <i>says that the ossifrage takes up and rears the chicks cast out by the eagle.</i> Namely in <i>History</i>, book 9, chapter 34: Ἡ δὲ καλουμένη φήνη ἐστὶν ἤπιος, καὶ τὰ τέκνα ἐκτρέφει, καὶ τὰ αὐτῆς, καὶ τὰ τοῦ ἀετοῦ [Hē de kaloumenē phēnē estin ēpios, kai ta tekna ektrephei, kai ta autēs, kai ta tou aetou]· <i>The bird called</i> phene (that is, <i>the ossifrage</i>) <i>is kindly, and besides its own it also rears the chicks of the eagle.</i> Ἤπιος [ēpios] here is the same as <i>pius</i> [devoted, kindly], and refers to devotion and mercy toward the chicks it rears, so that it is almost the same as רחום <i>rachum.</i> So <i>Paul</i> uses it in <i>1 Thessal. II,</i> 7: Ἐγενήθημεν, he says, ἤπιοι ἐν μέσω ὑμῶν, ὡς ἂν τροφὸς θάλπῃ τὰ ἑαυτῆς τέκνα [Egenēthēmen ēpioi en mesō hymōn, hōs an trophos thalpē ta heautēs tekna]. Thus there is a great similarity of habits between the vulture and the ossifrage. Hence it came about that <i>the Philosopher</i> in the book of <i>Marvels</i>, and <i>Pliny</i> following him, asserts that vultures are begotten from ossifrages. And not a few count the ossifrage among the species of vultures, among them the <i>Kiranides</i>, Gesner and <i>Dalechamp</i>: just as we have already said that some of the <i>Arabs</i> interpret رخمة [rakhama] <i>rachamam</i>, instead of <i>vulture</i>, as <i>ossifrage</i>. Meanwhile it is already clear from this that <i>Diodorus of Tarsus</i> is gravely mistaken when he writes that vultures οὐ τρέφειν τὰ γεννήματα [ou trephein ta gennēmata], <i>do not rear their offspring:</i> unless by the name <i>vultures</i> he means <i>eagles</i>; although even of eagles it is not simply true, as we have shown in its place <sup>e)</sup>.

‎The vulture's clemency and kindness also appears most clearly from the fact that it protects not only its own offspring but also other birds from the hawk, if we believe <i>Cassiodorus</i> [III, 76], book 2, Letter 19. <i>Alas!</i> he says, <i>devotion is found in birds which is abandoned by the human condition. The vulture itself, whose life is the carcass of another, a body of such great size, is not shown to be hostile even to small birds:</i> <i>rather it strikes with its wings the hawk that pursues the life of feathered birds, tears it with its beak, and strives with its whole weight to come to the aid of those in danger.</i> And a little later, dealing with slaves who had killed their master: <i>Let him therefore become the food of the devoted vulture, who could cruelly desire the death of his shepherd.</i> You see, reader, that the vulture is called <i>devoted</i> and set forth as an example of devotion, as one that takes into its protection not only its own chicks but any birds whatever, [col. 320] so as to keep them safe and sound from every danger as far as it can. Pliny writes something similar of the kestrel, book 10, chapter 37: <i>The kestrel defends doves and terrifies hawks by a natural power, so much that they flee its sight and its voice.</i>

‎But Scaliger [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)], in his Exercise 231 against Cardano, taught me another proof of στοργῆς [storgēs] in the vulture, in these words: <i>They say that the African vulture in</i> Libya <i>and on Mount</i> Atlas, <i>where they call it</i> sner, <i>lives so long that, stripped of its feathers, it is fed by its own young; so that the</i> Greeks <i>must now coin another word than</i> ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein], <i>namely</i> ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein]. Since these words harm no one, I do not understand why they so stirred that cymbal of the world, <i>Leo Allatius</i>, that he took from them material for raging without restraint against a man by far the greatest, in his <i>Notes</i> on <i>Eustathius' Hexaemeron</i>, p. 225. <i>Most wittily</i>, he says, <i>he jests at the</i> Greeks, <i>as if on the strength of this story they must coin another name than</i> ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein], <i>namely</i> ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein]. <i>Yet so great a man, corrector and propagator of the</i> Greek <i>and</i> Latin <i>languages,</i> ἀποῤῥοιβδεῖ [aporrhoibdei], καὶ τοῖς ἐκ τρίποδος βδέει λιβανωτοὺς [kai tois ek tripodos bdeei libanōtous], <i>and fancies that he smells sweet. The name the</i> Greeks <i>are to coin ought to be taken from the vulture, whose nature and character are to be expressed, not from birds, most of whom</i> [quorum plerique] <i>are found to be undutiful to their parents.</i> In this, note the words <i>quorum</i> (of birds) <i>plerique impii</i>. For Scaliger's critic did not know that <i>aves</i> [birds] is of feminine gender, which boys learn under the cane. But these are trifles compared with what follows: <i>But to</i> Scaliger <i>everything sounds as it pleases him, and so does this. And</i> Scaliger <i>coins it thus, but a</i> Greek <i>does not; if he wished to form it from the vulture, he would certainly not say, in correct form,</i> ἀντιγυπίζειν [antigypizein]. And a little later, as though he had done the job well: <i>Behold</i> (he says) <i>the smatterers of</i> Greeklings <i>whom you unjustly tear apart against all right! Such as they are, they nevertheless recognize both your ignorance and your trifles. Sell smoke to others; they possess the light.</i> As if he tears apart the <i>Greeks</i> who writes that they must now say ἀντοιωνεῖν [antoiōnein] of the vulture, as ἀντιπελαργεῖν [antipelargein] of the stork. For that is all that Scaliger wrote. So there was no reason for him to stir up such tragedies. Nor should it have escaped a <i>Greek</i> that many centuries ago οἰωνὸν [oiōnon] was rendered γῦπα [gypa] by most <i>Grammarians</i>. The <i>Etymologicum magnum</i>: Οἰωνός· ὄρνις, ὁ γύψ· παρὰ τοῦ οἰῶ τὸ μονῶ γίνεται. Φασὶ γάρ τινες, ὅτι καὶ χωρὶς μίξεως πέφυκε πολλάκις τίκτειν [Oiōnos; ornis, ho gyps; para tou oiō to monō ginetai. Phasi gar tines, hoti kai chōris mixeōs pephyke pollakis tiktein], etc. The unpublished <i>Lexicon of Cyril</i>, which I have in my possession: Οἰωνός· ὁ γὺψ [Oiōnos; ho gyps], etc., almost as [III, 77] in the <i>Etymologicum</i>. The Scholiast of <i>Sophocles</i> on the <i>Ajax</i>, line 842: Οἰωνοῖς· ὄρνισιν· κυρίως δὲ οἱ γῦπες [Oiōnois; ornisin; kyriōs de hoi gypes]. <i>Oppian's Scholiast</i> on line 29 of book 1 of the <i>Halieutica</i>: Οἰωνοί· τὰ ὄρνεα, κυρίως δὲ οἱ γῦπες, ἀπὸ τοῦ οἰῶ τὸ μονῶ. Μεμονωμένον γὰρ συλλαμβάνει ὑπὸ τοῦ ἀνέμου, μὴ ὄντος ἄῤῥενος [Oiōnoi; ta ornea, kyriōs de hoi gypes, apo tou oiō to monō. Memonōmenon gar syllambanei hypo tou anemou, mē ontos arrhenos]. <i>Io. Tzetzes</i> on <i>Hesiod's Works and Days</i>: Οἰωνοὶ κυρίως οἱ γῦπες, ὡς οἰοὶ, ἤτοι μόνοι, δίχα συνουσίας, ὠὰ ὑπηνέμια γεννῶντες [Oiōnoi kyriōs hoi gypes, hōs oioi, ētoi monoi, dicha synousias, ōa hypēnemia gennōntes]. And in <i>Chiliad</i> 13, History 474:

‎Γῦπες κυρίως οἰωνοί. θῆλυ γὰρ πᾶν τὸ γένος. Οἶαι, καὶ μόναι, κύουσαι ὠὰ, μηδ᾽ ἐξ ἀῤῥένων. Ὠὰ γὰρ ὑπηνέμια γεννῶσιν, ὡς προεῖπον. Οὕτω κυρίως οἰωνοὶ καλοῦνται μὲν οἱ γῦπες. Καὶ πάντα δὲ τὰ ὄρνεα καταχρηστικωτέρως. [Gypes kyriōs oiōnoi. thēly gar pan to genos. Oiai, kai monai, kyousai ōa, mēd᾽ ex arrhenōn. Ōa gar hypēnemia gennōsin, hōs proeipon. Houtō kyriōs oiōnoi kalountai men hoi gypes. Kai panta de ta ornea katachrēstikōterōs.]

‎If you knew this, my <i>Allatius</i>, with what face did you charge Scaliger with ignorance, when he assumed as true what the most learned of your countrymen assert so plainly and with such unanimous agreement? Or, if you did not know it, since you are ignorant of things most hackneyed and on everyone's lips, with what face did you dare to match yourself against such a man, not fearing that the well-known line would be thrown at you,

‎— — <i>No match for</i> Achilles <i>in the encounter</i>,

‎and your people's proverb,

‎Νεκροῦ σῶμα λέοντος ἐφυβρίζουσι λαγωοί. [Nekrou sōma leontos ephybrizousi lagōoi.]

‎Meanwhile I admit what no one is unaware of, that οἰωνὸν [oiōnon] is often taken for any <i>bird</i>, especially if it is <i>rapacious</i> or <i>carnivorous</i>: as at the very beginning of the <i>Iliad</i>,

‎Οἰωνοῖσί τε πᾶσι [Oiōnoisi te pasi]. — — [col. 321]

‎that is, τοῖς σαρκοφάγοις ὄρνισι [tois sarkophagois ornisi], especially γυψὶ, καὶ κόραξιν [gypsi, kai koraxin], as the old <i>Scholiast</i> has it there. Hence Hesychius: Οἰωνοί· σαρκοφάγα ὄρνεα, καὶ πάντα, γῦπες, κόρακες [Oiōnoi; sarkophaga ornea, kai panta, gypes, korakes]. But this too is very well known, that the name of a genus is often restricted to a species. Thus ὄρνις [ornis], <i>bird</i>, is often the <i>hen</i>. Θὴρ [Thēr], <i>wild beast</i>, is often the <i>lion</i>. Θηρία [Thēria], <i>wild beasts</i>, are often <i>serpents</i>, or even <i>vipers</i>, whence Θηριακά [Thēriaka]. Ὑποζύγιον [Hypozygion], <i>beast of burden</i>, is used everywhere by the <i>Greek</i> translators for the <i>ass</i>, and following them by <i>Matthew</i>, <i>Peter</i>, etc. For us <i>jumentum</i> is a <i>mare</i>. For you today ἄλογον [alogon] is a <i>horse</i>, which seems already to have prevailed in the age of Hesychius, whose words are: Καπητόν· παράβλημα ἀλόγων [Kapēton; parablēma alogōn]; for which I would read Κάπη· τὸ παράβλημα ἀλόγων [Kapē; to parablēma alogōn], that is, <i>fodder of horses</i>. Since Κάπη [Kapē] commonly means a <i>horses' manger</i>, Hesychius had found it used somewhere for the <i>fodder usually put before horses in the manger</i>.
