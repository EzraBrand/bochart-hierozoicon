# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 10: The swallow (vol. 2, pp. 602–613)

### ‎[II, 602] [col. 59] CHAPTER X.

### ‎<i>On the Swallow.</i>

‎With תור <i>thor</i>, that is, the <i>turtledove</i>, are joined סיס <i>sis</i> and עגור <i>agor</i>, as birds that are likewise migratory, in these words of <i>Jer 8:7. Both</i> thor <i>and</i> sis <i>and</i> agor <i>have kept the time of their coming.</i> And since the <i>turtledove</i> has been dealt with, it follows that we treat of <i>sis</i> and <i>agor</i>. These words occur again in the song of King <i>Hezekiah</i>, <i>Isa 38:14. Like</i> sis (<i>he says</i>) <i>and</i> agor <i>I chirped, I moaned like a dove.</i> But elsewhere there is no mention of them. And although in both places סוס is written in the text with <i>Vau</i>, yet in <i>Jeremiah</i> the Hebrews read סיס <i>sis</i>, with <i>Iod</i>. This reading I strongly approve, both so that סיס <i>sis</i> may differ from the word סוס <i>sus</i>, which means <i>horse</i>, and because סיס <i>sis</i> seems better to express the nature of the thing signified by this name <sup>R1</sup>. This very point, since it is in question, must now be examined. <i>Pagninus, Munster, Buxtorf, Schindler, Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] and <i>Tremellius</i> [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], the English, the Italians, etc., and most other modern interpreters consistently render סיס <i>sis</i> as <i>crane</i>. So, of course, the most learned of the <i>Hebrews</i> hold, <i>Jarchi [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac), called Jarchi by the Christian Hebraists], Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], following <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] in the <i>Chaldee Paraphrase</i>, which has כורכיא <i>kurkeja</i> for סיס <i>sis</i>. Further, that כורכיא <i>kurkeja</i> is the <i>crane</i> is clear both from the <i>Talmud</i> and from the <i>Arab</i> writers, who write كركي [kurkī] <i>kurki.</i> For כורכיא <i>kurkeja</i> you sometimes find כרוכיא <i>krukeja</i>, as in the tractate <i>Kiddusim</i>, ch. 2, fol. 44a. Very similar to this word is the word כרוכא <i>kruka</i>, which occurs in <i>Jonathan</i> at <i>Lev 11:22</i> for חגב <i>chagab</i>, or <i>cicada</i>. It once also stood in the <i>Jerusalem Paraphrase</i>, from which it is cited by <i>Elias</i> in the <i>Methurgeman.</i> Hence arose an amusing error in <i>Symmachus</i>, who, taking the words כרוכא <i>kruka</i> and כרוכיא <i>krukeja</i> indiscriminately, made a <i>cicada</i> out of the <i>crane</i> in <i>Jeremiah</i>. Hence <i>Jerome</i> on <i>Jeremiah:</i> Symmachus <i>translated it 'cicada', which in</i> Hebrew <i>is called</i> סיס <i>sis.</i> And in the same place <i>Theodoret</i>: Τρυγὼν, καὶ τέττιξ, καὶ χελιδὼν, ἀγροῦ στρουθία, ἐφύλαξαν καιροὺς εἰσόδων αὐτῶν· [Trygōn, kai tettix, kai chelidōn, agrou strouthia, ephylaxan kairous eisodōn autōn] <i>The turtledove, and the cicada, and the swallow,</i> [II, 603] <i>little birds of the field, have observed the time of their coming.</i> After <i>Chrysostom</i> quoted these very words in the Homily <i>On the Turtledove</i>, as if from <i>Jeremiah</i>, he adds by way of commentary: Τρυγόνα λέγων τὴν πάνσεμνον ἐκκλησίαν, χελιδόνα τὸν φιλέρημον Ἰωάννην, τέττιγα τὸν εὔλαλον Παῦλον [Trygona legōn tēn pansemnon ekklēsian, chelidona ton philerēmon Iōannēn, tettiga ton eulalon Paulon]. <i>Calling the venerable church the turtledove, the dweller in the desert,</i> John, <i>the swallow, and the eloquent</i> Paul <i>the cicada.</i> This error arose from another error. For those who render סיס <i>sis</i> as <i>crane</i> are as mistaken as those who render it <i>cicada</i>: since סיס <i>sis</i> is neither the <i>crane</i> nor the <i>cicada</i>, but the <i>swallow</i>. This is proved first by the agreement of the older interpreters, which carries great weight with me when no compelling reason opposes it, and here none presents itself. As older interpreters I here name: 1. the Greek <i>Elders</i>, by whom סיס <i>sis</i> is rendered χελιδὼν [chelidōn] in both places; 2. <i>Theodotion</i>; 3. <i>Jerome</i>, who follow the same; 4. that very <i>Symmachus</i> for whom סיס <i>sis</i> in <i>Jeremiah</i> is the <i>cicada</i>. For by him <i>Hezekiah</i>'s words כסוס עגור are rendered ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλεισθεῖσα [hōs chelidōn enkleistheisa], <i>like a swallow shut in.</i> 5. Add to these the <i>Arabic translator</i>, for whom סיס <i>sis</i> is سنونوه [sunūnūh] <i>sununua</i>, that is, <i>swallow</i>. In <i>Abi Ubid</i>, Century 1, Proverb 32, it is written سنونية [sunūniya] <i>senunia</i>, and the late <i>Erpenius</i> ὁ μακαρίτης [ho makaritēs] had rightly explained it as <i>swallow</i>, but only by conjecture. <i>That it is the name of a bird</i>, he says, <i>is clear enough; but of what kind I do not know. I translated it</i> swallow, <i>because the Chaldee</i> סנוניתא <i>senunitha is usually so translated. Otherwise the swallow is called</i> خطاف [khuṭṭāf] chottaph, etc. To meet these doubts of a man by far the most learned in <i>Arabic</i> letters, and once most deserving of my gratitude, I wish to explain the word more precisely. خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i> is the <i>swallow</i> in general, so called from the swiftness of its flight, like the <i>Chaldee</i> חטפיתא <i>chatphitha</i>. But of this there are several species, which the <i>Arabs</i> in <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] define thus: الخطاطيف انواع منها نوع يالف السواحل عند البحر يحفر بيتها هناك ويعشش هناك وهو صغير الجثة دون عصغور الجنة ولونه رمادي [al-khaṭāṭīf anwāʿ minhā nawʿ yaʾlaf al-sawāḥil ʿinda al-baḥr yaḥfir baytahā hunāka wa-yuʿashshish hunāka wa-huwa ṣaghīr al-jutha dūna ʿaṣghūr al-janna wa-lawnuhu ramādī] [col. 61] والناس يسمونه سنونوا بضم السين المهملة ونونين ومنها نوع لخضرته علي ظهرة بعض حمرة اصغر من الدرة تسميه اهل مصر الخضري لخضرته يغتات الغراش والذباب ونحو ذلك ومنها طويل الاجنحة رقيقها يالف الجبال وياكل النمل وهذا النوع يقال له السايم مغرب سامه ومنهم من يسمي هذا النوع السنونوا الوحدة سنونوة وهو كثير في المسجد الحرام بمكة يعشش في سقف المسجد في مقام ابراهيم وباب بني شيبة وبعض الناس [wa-al-nās yusammūnahu sunūnū bi-ḍamm al-sīn al-muhmala wa-nūnayn wa-minhā nawʿ li-khuḍratihi ʿalā ẓahrih baʿḍ ḥumra aṣghar min al-durra tusammīhi ahl Miṣr al-khuḍarī li-khuḍratihi yaghtāt al-gharāsh wa-al-dhubāb wa-naḥw dhālika wa-minhā ṭawīl al-ajniḥa raqīquhā yaʾlaf al-jibāl wa-yaʾkul al-naml wa-hādhā al-nawʿ yuqāl lahu al-sāyim mughrib sāmih wa-minhum man yusammī hādhā al-nawʿ al-sunūnū al-waḥda sunūnūa wa-huwa kathīr fī al-masjid al-ḥarām bi-Makka yuʿashshish fī saqf al-masjid fī maqām Ibrāhīm wa-bāb banī Shayba wa-baʿḍ al-nās] [II, 604] يزعم انه الطير الابابيل الذي عذب الله به اصحاب الغيل [yazʿum annahu al-ṭayr al-abābīl alladhī ʿadhdhaba Allāh bihi aṣḥāb al-ghīl] <i>There are various species of swallows. One of them lives on the shores of the sea, and there digs out its home and nests. It is smaller in body than the bird of Paradise, and ash-grey in colour. It is called</i> sununu, <i>with</i> Sin Semol <i>given damma and two</i> Nun. <i>There is also a green species with some redness on the back, smaller than a parrot, which the</i> Egyptians <i>on account of its colour call</i> chadra, <i>that is, green. It feeds on butterflies, flies and other similar small creatures. There is also another species, with long and slender wings, a dweller in the mountains, which eats ants. These they call</i> semaim, <i>from the singular</i> semama. <i>But some also call them</i> sununu. <i>The singular of unity is</i> sununua. <i>Of this species there are many in the</i> Charam <i>temple of the city of</i> Mecca, <i>which nest in the roof of the temple, at the station of</i> Abraham <i>and at the gate of the sons of</i> Seiba. <i>Some assert that these are the birds called</i> ababil, <i>by which God punished the companions of the elephant:</i> namely by throwing on their heads حجارة من سجيل [ḥijāra min sijjīl] <i>stones of siggil.</i> So they call stones baked and hardened in the fire of hell, inscribed with the names of those on whom they fell. Their leader <i>Abraheth</i>, king of the <i>Abyssinians</i> in the interior of <i>Arabia Felix</i>, in the very year in which <i>Muhammad</i> was born, had come riding a great elephant with large forces in order to destroy the temple of <i>Mecca</i>. The pseudo-prophet touches on this in passing in <i>Azoara</i> 115, entitled الغيل [al-ghīl] <i>The Elephant.</i> But these matters belong elsewhere. Here it suffices to have noted that it is clear from what has been adduced that سنونوة [sunūnūa] <i>sununua</i> properly denotes the <i>sand martin</i> [<i>hirundo riparia</i>] <sup>R2</sup>, of which <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, ch. 33: <i>The third kind of them is those which hollow out banks and so nest within.</i> And book 30, ch. 4: <i>Yet by far the most efficacious are the young of the bank</i> (swallows). <i>So they call those that nest in hollows of riverbanks.</i> The <i>riparia</i> [bank swallow] is called in <i>Greek</i> δρεπανὶς [drepanis], from its sickle-shaped feet. So the <i>Philosopher</i> calls it, <i>Hist.</i> [col. 62] book 1, ch. 1. But they add that by the same name سنونوة [sunūnūa] <i>sununua</i> are also signified those swallows which nest in mountains and temples. These are the rustic or field swallows of <i>Pliny</i>, on which see <i>Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], p. 565 <sup>R3</sup>. Meanwhile it is certain that this name sometimes extends more widely and is used for any swallow. Thus the stone اليرقان [al-yaraqān] <i>icterias</i>, that is, by which jaundice sufferers are healed, is by another name called حجر السنونوة [ḥajar al-sunūnūa] <i>the stone of the sununua</i>, that is, <i>of the swallow</i>, because it is found in its nest. For the <i>Arabs</i> dye the swallow's young with saffron; and the father, suspecting that they suffer from jaundice, brings that stone from <i>India</i> into the nest and places it under the young. <i>Damir</i> describes it as small, streaked with red lines that verge on black. So too the author <i>On the Wonders of Nature</i>, where, as <i>Damir</i> notes, صنونو [ṣunūnū] <i>sununu</i> is wrongly read with <i>Sad</i>. And at <i>Bar 6:21</i> the <i>Arabic translator</i> rendered these <i>Greek</i> words, Ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν [II, 605] αὐτῶν ἐφίπτανται χελιδόνες [Epi tēn kephalēn autōn ephiptantai chelidones], thus: السنونو تلمسهم علي راسهم [al-sunūnū talmisuhum ʿalā raʾsihim] <i>Assununu</i> (or <i>swallows</i>) <i>touch them upon their heads. Touch</i>, that is, ἐφάπτονται [ephaptontai], as he read instead of ἐφίπτανται [ephiptantai], <i>fly upon.</i>

‎It has been proved so far that for most of the ancients סיס <i>sis</i> is the <i>swallow</i>. Against them <i>Jonathan</i> is cited in vain, as though he had rendered it כורכיא, that is, <i>crane</i>. For he correctly explained the two words סיס <i>sis</i> and עגור <i>agor</i> by כורכיא <i>kurkeja</i> and סנוניתא <i>sununitha</i>, that is, <i>crane</i> and <i>swallow</i>. For as סיס <i>sis</i> is the <i>swallow</i>, so עגור <i>agor</i> is the <i>crane</i>, as will be shown in the following chapter. But he inverted the order, and put the <i>swallow</i>, which comes first in the <i>Hebrew</i> text, in the second place. This often happens with translators. And, not to seek examples further afield, in the passage of <i>Baruch</i> which we cited a little earlier, for the <i>Greek</i> words νυκτερίδες, χελιδόνες [nykterides, chelidones], <i>bats and swallows</i>, the <i>Arabic</i> has, in inverted order, السنونو والوطاويط [al-sunūnū wa-l-waṭāwīṭ] <i>swallows and bats</i>. Accordingly we add the <i>Chaldee</i> also, whom the <i>Syriac</i> followed, to those of the ancients for whom סיס <i>sis</i> is the <i>swallow</i>. Thus there is now none of the early translators who does not agree with the rendering we maintain.

‎To this authority of the ancients is added a clear reason. The word סיס <i>sis</i> is ὀνοματοποιητικὴ [onomatopoiētikē], by which the <i>Hebrew</i> intended to imitate, after a fashion, the swallow's ψιθυρισμὸν [psithyrismon]. So with the <i>Italians</i> around <i>Venice</i>, for whom <i>zisilla</i> is the <i>swallow</i>, and <i>zisillare</i> means to τιτυβίζειν [titybizein] or ψιθυρίζειν [psithyrizein] in the manner of a swallow. The author of the <i>Philomela</i> said <i>zinzilulare</i>:

‎<i>The wren and the bee-eater, and</i> Procne <i>with red breast, Know how to</i> zinzilulare <i>with a like measure.</i>

‎As though the voice of <i>Procne</i>, that is, of the <i>swallow</i>, were <i>zizi</i>, from which <i>zizilulare</i> ought to have been said, as in <i>Italian zisillare</i>. But for the sake of euphony an epenthetic N was added, which is frequent in <i>Roman</i> words. Such are <i>lingo</i> from λείχω [leichō], <i>linquo</i> from the <i>Aeolic</i> λείκω [leikō] for λείπω [leipō], <i>tango</i> from θίγω [thigō], <i>pango</i> from πάγω [pagō] for πήγω [pēgō], <i>densus</i> from δασὺς [dasys], <i>pinguis</i> from παχὺς [pachys], etc. <sup>R4</sup>.

‎Nor is it of slight weight that the <i>Egyptians</i> relate in their fables that <i>Isis</i> was changed into a swallow. <i>Plutarch</i> in the <i>Isis</i>: Αὐτὴν δὲ γενομένην χελιδόνα (φασὶ) [Autēn de genomenēn chelidona (phasi)]

‎[II, 606] τῇ κίονι περιπέτεσθαι, καὶ θρηνεῖν· [tē kioni peripetesthai, kai thrēnein] <i>And she, having become a swallow</i> (they say), <i>flew around the pillar and lamented.</i> For <i>Isis</i> plainly alludes to <i>sis</i>. And the fabulous metamorphoses of the gods often rest on allusions of words. Thus <i>Anubis</i> was represented with a dog's head, because in <i>Hebrew</i> נובח <i>nobeah</i> means <i>barking</i>. And king <i>Apis</i> was worshipped in a bull, because of the allusion to אביר <i>abir</i>: for the <i>Hebrews</i> so name the <i>bull</i>. Hence the <i>LXX</i> at <i>Ier. XLVI,</i> 15: Διὰ τί ἔφυγεν ὁ Ἄπις [Dia ti ephygen ho Apis], for the <i>Hebrew</i> [col. 63] מדוע נסחף אבירך, as they read in place of אביריך. And in the story of the <i>Gigantomachy</i> the gods, having taken flight into <i>Egypt</i> for fear of the <i>giants</i>, changed themselves into various forms. <i>Ovid</i>, book 5 of the <i>Metamorphoses</i>:

‎<i>Jupiter, she said, became leader of the flock, whence even now Libyan</i> Hammon <i>is fashioned with curved horns: The</i> Delian <i>hid in a raven, the offspring of</i> Semele <i>in a goat, The sister of</i> Phoebus <i>in a cat, the daughter of</i> Saturn <i>in a snow-white cow, </i>Venus <i>in a fish, the</i> Cyllenian <i>in the wings of an ibis.</i>

‎<i>Antoninus Liberalis</i>, <i>Fab.</i> 28, from <i>Nicander</i>: Ἀπόλλων μὲν ἐγένετο ἱέραξ, Ἑρμῆς δὲ ἴβις, Ἄρης δὲ λεπιδωτὸς ἰχθὺς, Ἄρτεμις δὲ αἴλουρος [Apollōn men egeneto hierax, Hermēs de ibis, Arēs de lepidōtos ichthys, Artemis de ailouros] (so it must be read instead of σίλουρος [silouros], as is clear from <i>Ovid</i>) τράγῳ δὲ εἰκάζεται Διόνυσος, ἔλλῳ δὲ Ἡρακλῆς, βοὶ δ᾽ Ἥφαιστος, μυγάλῃ δὲ Λητώ [tragō de eikazetai Dionysos, ellō de Hēraklēs, boi d' Hēphaistos, mygalē de Lētō]: Apollo <i>was changed into a hawk</i>, Mercury <i>into an ibis</i>, Mars <i>into a scaly fish</i>, Diana <i>into a cat</i>, Bacchus <i>into a goat</i>, Hercules <i>into a fawn</i>, Vulcan <i>into an ox</i>, Latona <i>into a shrew-mouse.</i> We do not undertake to explain all of these here, but only a few, for whose invention the occasion was taken from paronomasias. <i>Jupiter</i>, the supreme God, was changed into a ram, because אל <i>el</i>, <i>God the strong</i>, and איל <i>ajil</i>, <i>ram</i>, are paronyms, and the plural of both is אלים <i>elim</i>. <i>Bacchus</i>, the <i>Osiris</i> of the Egyptians, into a he-goat, because שעיר <i>seir</i>, in Hebrew <i>he-goat</i>, alludes to <i>Osiris</i>. <i>Diana</i> into a cat, because in <i>Egyptian</i> ΒΟΥΒΑΣΤΟΣ was the cat, and <i>Bubastis</i> was Diana. The one is in <i>Stephanus</i>, under the word Βούβαστος [Boubastos]: Οἱ δὲ Αἰγύπτιοι βούβαστον τὴν αἴλουρόν φασι [Hoi de Aigyptioi boubaston tēn ailouron phasi]: <i>but the Egyptians call the cat bubastos</i>; the other in <i>Herodotus</i>, book 2, ch. 156: Αἰγυπτιστὶ δὲ Ἀπόλλων Ὧρος, Δημήτηρ δὲ Ἶσις, Ἄρτεμις δὲ Βούβαστις [Aigyptisti de Apollōn Hōros, Dēmētēr de Isis, Artemis de Boubastis]: <i>in Egyptian Apollo is Horus, Ceres Isis, Diana Bubastis.</i> Hence it is that in the <i>Herbarium of Apuleius</i>, ch. 10, the Egyptians call <i>Artemisia</i> Βουβαστέως καρδίαν [Boubasteōs kardian], <i>the heart of Bubastis</i>, that is, the herb dear to <i>Diana</i>, as its <i>Greek</i> name also displays. <i>Juno</i> took the shape of a cow. For we have observed in its place that עשתרות, from which <i>Juno</i> was called <i>Astarte</i>, signifies not only flocks of sheep but also herds of cattle, and that this is why in <i>Sanchuniathon</i> <i>Astarte</i> ἐπέθηκε τῇ ἰδίᾳ κεφαλῇ βασιλείας παράσημον κεφαλὴν ταύρου [epethēke tē idia kephalē basileias parasēmon kephalēn taurou], <i>placed a bull's head on her own head as the insignia of royalty.</i> That <i>Venus</i> hid in a fish they refer to <i>Atargatis</i>, as if she were <i>Dagon</i>, to whom דג <i>dag</i>, that is, <i>fish</i>, gave the name; but I approve this less, for reasons given elsewhere.

‎[II, 607] <i>Hezekiah</i> in his illness says that he chirped like a swallow [Isa 38:14], because the swallow's voice seems to have something plaintive and mournful about it. All the <i>poets</i> proclaim this with one voice. <i>Hesiod</i>, in the <i>Works</i>, verse 366:

‎Τὸν δὲ μετ' ὀρθρογόη Πανδιονὶς ὦρτο χελιδὼν, [Ton de met' orthrogoē Pandionis ōrto chelidōn,]

‎<i>After him, lamenting at dawn, came forth the swallow</i>, daughter of Pandion. Hence <i>Suidas</i>: Ὀρθρογόη· χελιδών [Orthrogoē: chelidōn]. Others prefer ὀρθογόη [orthogoē]. <i>Hesychius</i>: Ὀρθογόη· ἡ χελιδών [Orthogoē: hē chelidōn]. Accordingly, from this, Ὀρθογόν· χελιδών [Orthogon: chelidōn] must be corrected in the manuscript <i>Lexicon of Cyril</i> in my possession. In the <i>Etymologicum</i> ὀρθογόη [orthogoē] is rendered ἡ μεγάλως θρηνοῦσα [hē megalōs thrēnousa], <i>she who laments greatly</i>. So too in <i>Phavorinus</i>, who adds that it is said of the swallow. <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Frogs</i>:

‎Δὴ χείλεσιν ἀμφιλάλοις Δεινὸν ἐπιβρέμεται Θρηΐκια χελιδὼν, Ἐπὶ βάρβαρον ἑζομένη πέταλον, Κελαδεῖ δ' ἐπίκλαυτον ἀηδόνιον νόμον, [Dē cheilesin amphilalois Deinon epibremetai Thrēikia chelidōn, Epi barbaron hezomenē petalon, Keladei d' epiklauton aēdonion nomon,]

‎<i>Now with chattering lips</i> <i>the</i> Thracian <i>swallow</i> <i>resounds heavily</i>, <i>sitting upon a barbarian leaf</i>, <i>and sings a mournful, shrill song.</i>

‎Moschus, <i>Idyll 3</i>:

‎Οὐδὲ τόσον θρήνησεν ἀν' ὤρεα μακρὰ χελιδὼν, [Oude toson thrēnēsen an' ōrea makra chelidōn,]

‎<i>Nor did the swallow lament so much across the great mountains</i>. Palladas, <i>Anthol.</i> book 1, ch. 60:

‎Τίπτε πανημέριος, Πανδιονὶ κάμμορε κούρη, [col. 64] Μυρομένη κελαδεῖς τραυλὰ διὰ στομάτων. [Tipte panēmerios, Pandioni kammore kourē, Myromenē keladeis traula dia stomatōn.]

‎<i>Why all day long</i>, O <i>daughter</i> of Pandion, <i>unhappy girl</i>, <i>do you sing lamenting, lisping songs from your mouth?</i> Accordingly, on these lines of <i>Theocritus</i> in the <i>Thalysia</i>,

‎— — — — ἁ δ' ἐλολυγὼν Τηλόθεν ἐν πυκινῇσι βάτων τρύζεσκεν ἀκάνθαις, [ha d' elolygōn Tēlothen en pykinēsi batōn tryzesken akanthais,]

‎<i>And the</i> ololygon <i>moaned far off in the dense thorns of the brambles</i>, ὀλολυγὼν [ololygōn] is explained by the <i>Scholiast</i> as ἡ χελιδὼν ἡ τὸν Ἴτυν ὀλοφυρομένη [hē chelidōn hē ton Ityn olophyromenē], <i>the swallow bewailing</i> Itys. And <i>Agathias</i>, <i>Anthol.</i> book 7, <i>Epigr.</i> 65, addresses the swallows thus:

‎Ἀλλ' Ἴτυλον κλαίοιτε κατ' οὔρεα, καὶ γοάοιτε, [All' Itylon klaioite kat' ourea, kai goaoite,]

‎<i>But bewail</i> Itylus <i>in the mountains</i>, <i>and lament</i>. Nor do the <i>Roman</i> poets speak otherwise. <i>Catullus</i> to <i>Ortalus</i>: [II, 608] <i>Such as beneath the dense shade of the branches</i> the Daulian bird <i>sings, moaning the fate of the lost</i> Itylus.

‎<i>Horace</i>, book 4, <i>Ode</i> 12:

‎<i>The unhappy bird builds her nest</i>, <i>tearfully moaning</i> Itys, etc. — — —

‎C. <i>Albinovanus</i> to <i>Livia</i>:

‎<i>The</i> Daulian <i>bird bewails</i> Thracian Itys.

‎<i>Ovid</i>, <i>Tristia</i> book 5, <i>Eleg.</i> 1:

‎<i>It is something to relieve a fatal ill by words;</i> <i>this makes</i> Procne <i>and the halcyons plaintive.</i>

‎<i>Seneca</i> in <i>Hercules Oetaeus</i>, Act 1:

‎— — — <i>as the</i> Daulian <i>bird is wont</i> <i>to weep for her son</i> <i>beneath the</i> Ismarian <i>shade.</i> — —

‎And in <i>Agamemnon</i>, Act 3:

‎<i>Not the</i> Bistonian <i>bird that</i> <i>sits on the rooftops, chattering</i> <i>bewails the impious thefts of her dread husband.</i>

‎And in <i>Hercules Furens</i>, Act 1:

‎<i>And the</i> Thracian <i>concubine</i> <i>longs to give her wings to the new sun</i> <i>among her plaintive nests.</i> — —

‎And in the <i>Octavia</i>, near the beginning:

‎— — — — <i>now take up again your accustomed laments, and outdo the halcyons of the sea, outdo too the</i> Pandionian <i>birds. For your fortune is heavier than theirs.</i> — — —

‎<i>Martial</i> to <i>Faustus</i>, book 10, <i>Epigram</i> 51.

‎The Attic <i>concubine</i> <i>weeps</i> for Ismarian Itys.

‎<i>Statius, Thebaid</i> book 8, line 616.

‎<i>Thus, when the</i> Pandionian <i>birds return to their faithful lodgings and the home they left when winter drove them out,</i> [II, 609] <i>they stand upon their nests and recount to the roofs the beginnings of their old fate; and that broken and tearful murmur men take for words, and yet that voice is not unlike words.</i>

‎And book 12, line 488.

‎— — — <i>the</i> Getic <i>birds do not complain more to the hospitable roofs in broken speech.</i>

‎Against these, however, it is objected that <i>Plato</i>, refuting those who say that swans θρηνοῦντας τὸν θάνατον ὑπὸ λύπης ἐξάδειν [thrēnountas ton thanaton hypo lypēs exadein], <i>sing for grief as they bewail their death</i>, says: Οὐδὲν ὄρνεον ᾄδει ὅταν πεινῇ, ἢ ῥιγοῖ, ἤ τινα ἄλλην λύπην λυπεῖται· οὐδὲ αὐτὴ ἥ τε ἀηδὼν, καὶ χελιδὼν, καὶ ὁ ἔποψ, ὃν δή φασι διὰ λύπην θρηνοῦντα ᾄδειν [Ouden orneon adei hotan peinē, ē rhigoi, ē tina allēn lypēn lypeitai; oude autē hē te aēdōn, kai chelidōn, kai ho epops, hon dē phasi dia lypēn thrēnounta adein] (read οὓς δὴ φ. δ. λ. θρηνοῦντας ᾄδειν [hous dē ph. d. l. thrēnountas adein]), <i>No bird sings when it is hungry, or cold, or sad for any other reason; not even the nightingale, the swallow and the hoopoe, which they say sing mournfully for grief.</i> Accordingly <i>Proclus</i> cannot bear that the swallow should be called ὀρθογόη [orthogoē] by <i>Hesiod</i>, and wants to read ὀρθοβόη [orthoboē], <i>crying in the morning</i>, so that the <i>Poet</i> may not seem to disagree with <i>Plato</i>. But <i>Tzetzes</i> answers otherwise: Ὑμεῖς δ' ἴσμεν καὶ δίχα μάρτυρος Πλάτωνος, ὅτι οὐδὲν ὄρνεον λυπούμενον ᾄδει· ἀλλὰ δεινῶς, καὶ ῥητορικῶς ὁ ποιητὴς τὴν λαλιὰν τῆς χελιδόνος θρῆνον εἶναι λέγει κατὰ τὸν μῦθον τῆς τοῦ Πανδίονος ἐκείνης θυγατρός [Hymeis d' ismen kai dicha martyros Platōnos, hoti ouden orneon lypoumenon adei; alla deinōs, kai rhētorikōs ho poiētēs tēn lalian tēs chelidonos thrēnon einai legei kata ton mython tēs tou Pandionos ekeinēs thygatros]. <i>We know even without</i> Plato <i>as witness that no bird sings in grief. But the Poet skilfully and rhetorically calls the swallow's song a lament, in keeping with the fable of</i> Pandion's <i>daughter.</i> So he, as though the swallow were said to mourn only because of the fable. Indeed, as <i>Pausanias</i> says in the <i>Attica</i>, σφίσι τὴν ἐς ἀηδόνα, καὶ χελιδόνα, μεταβολὴν ἐπεφήμισαν, ὅτι οἶμαι, καὶ αὗται αἱ ὄρνιθες ἐλεεινὸν, καὶ θρήνῳ ὅμοιον ᾄδουσιν [sphisi tēn es aēdona, kai chelidona, metabolēn epephēmisan, hoti oimai, kai hautai hai ornithes eleeinon, kai thrēnō homoion adousin], <i>I think the common fable</i> about <i>Pandion's</i> daughters, <i>that one of them was changed into a nightingale and the other into a swallow, arose from the fact that each bird utters pitiful and mournful songs.</i> [col. 65] Therefore nothing is to be changed in <i>Hesiod</i>. And the swallow would absurdly be called ὀρθοβόη [orthoboē] or ὀρθροβόη [orthroboē], since it is not a clamorous bird but one that twitters softly and ψιθυρίζουσα [psithyrizousa]. Hence <i>Sidonius</i>, book 2, letter 2, spoke of Procne <i>twittering among the rafters.</i> Likewise an unknown <i>Poet</i> in the <i>Anthology</i>,

‎Τραυλὰ μινυρομένη Πανδιονὶ κάμμορε φωνᾷ, [Traula minyromenē Pandioni kammore phōna,]

‎<i>O wretched daughter of</i> Pandion, <i>twittering songs with lisping voice.</i> Similar is what <i>Pollux</i> says in the chapter <i>On the voices of birds</i>, χελιδόνας ψιθυρίζειν [chelidonas psithyrizein], <i>that swallows whisper</i>. And that ψιθυρισμὸς [psithyrismos], or that <i>twittering</i> if you prefer, seems to have something in common with the complaints and murmurs that escape us in grief or for any other cause. Hence some of the ancients in <i>Plutarch</i>'s <i>Table Talk</i> hold that by the swallow <i>Pythagoras</i> meant ψιθύρους [psithyrous], that is, <i>whisperers</i> or informers who secretly whisper into the ear. Still, what <i>Plato</i> says is true: swallows [II, 610] do not sing thus for grief. For a bird's song, however mournful it may seem, is the effect not of grief but of nature.

‎Now a few words on the migration of swallows in <i>Ierem. VIII,</i> 7. That they are migratory birds is known to the bleary-eyed and the barbers, and it has been observed in every age. <i>Homer</i> in the Εἰρεσιώνη [Eiresiōnē], quoted by <i>Herodotus</i> in his life,

‎Νεῦμαι σοι, νεῦμαι ἐνιαύσιος, ὥστε χελιδὼν, [Neumai soi, neumai eniausios, hōste chelidōn,]

‎<i>I return to you every year, like the swallow.</i> Anacreon, <i>Ode</i> 33,

‎Σὺ μὲν, φίλη χελιδὼν [Sy men, philē chelidōn] <i>You, dear swallow,</i> Ἐτησίη μολοῦσα [Etēsiē molousa], <i>coming every year,</i> Θέρει πλέκεις καλίην [Therei plekeis kaliēn], <i>weave your nest in summer,</i> Χειμῶσι δ' εἶς ἄφαντος [Cheimōsi d' eis aphantos]. <i>And in winter you do not appear.</i>

‎Aristotle, <i>Hist.</i> book 8, ch. 12: Ἀπαίρουσι δὲ, etc. καὶ οὐ χειμάζουσι, καὶ αἱ χελιδόνες, καὶ αἱ τρυγόνες [Apairousi de, etc. kai ou cheimazousi, kai hai chelidones, kai hai trygones]; <i>Both swallows and turtledoves depart, and do not winter among us.</i> The author <i>To Herennius</i>: <i>Swallows are at hand in summertime; driven off by winter, they withdraw.</i> <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 24: <i>Swallows too depart in the winter months.</i> Damir: وهو من الطيور القواطع [wa-huwa min al-ṭuyūr al-qawāṭiʿ] <i>It is one of the migratory birds.</i> <i>Columella</i>, in his prose <i>On the Cultivation of Gardens</i>, book 12, chapter 3: <i>Soon, when the winter solstice is over, after an interval of forty days, around the arrival of the swallow, when</i> Favonius <i>is already rising.</i> And book 10, chapter 14: <i>While the rising of</i> Arcturus <i>and the arrival of the swallow promise more favourable weather.</i> In <i>Pliny</i>, book 2, chapter 47: <i>Some call Favonius, on the</i> 7th <i>day before the Calends of</i> March, <i>Chelidonias, from the sighting of the swallow. Favonius,</i> that is, <i>Zephyr.</i> Hence <i>Horace</i>, book 1, Epistle 7, <i>to Maecenas</i>:

‎<i>If you will allow me, with the</i> Zephyrs <i>and the first swallow.</i>

‎And <i>Oppian</i> on the swallow, book 3 of the <i>Halieutica</i>, line 244:

‎— — εἰαρινὴ Ζεφύρου πρωτάγγελος ὄρνις, [eiarinē Zephyrou prōtangelos ornis,]

‎<i>The spring bird, first messenger of</i> Zephyr. And <i>Nonnus</i>, book 2 of the <i>Dionysiaca</i>:

‎— — εἰαρινοῖο φίλη Ζεφύροιο χελιδὼν, [eiarinoio philē Zephyroio chelidōn,]

‎<i>The swallow, friend of the spring</i> Zephyr. And in book 47:

‎Καὶ Ζεφύρου καλὸς ὄρνις ὑπωροφίην χέε μολπὴν, [Kai Zephyrou kalos ornis hypōrophiēn chee molpēn,]

‎<i>And the beautiful bird of</i> Zephyr <i>poured out its song beneath the roof.</i> As if alluding to the <i>Greek</i> proverb Νέα χελιδὼν [Nea chelidōn], <i>A new swallow</i>, which <i>Suidas</i>, by a lapse of memory, cites from the <i>Birds</i> of <i>Aristophanes</i>, although it is read nowhere but in the <i>Knights</i>, Act 1, in these words: [II, 611] Σκέψασθε παῖδες, οὐχ' ὁρᾶθ'; ὥρα, νέα χελιδὼν, [Skepsasthe paides, ouch' horath'; hōra, nea chelidōn,] [col. 66]

‎<i>Look, boys, don't you see? It is spring, a new swallow.</i> On which the <i>Scholiast</i>: Ἔστιν ἔαρος ἀρχή· δοκεῖ γάρ πως ἅμα τῷ ἔαρι φαίνεσθαι χελιδών. [Estin earos archē; dokei gar pōs hama tō eari phainesthai chelidōn.] <i>It is the beginning of spring, for the swallow seems to appear together with the spring.</i> So in the <i>Birds</i> (which may have misled <i>Suidas</i>) the appearing swallow teaches

‎Ὅτε χρὴ χλαῖναν πωλεῖν ἤδη, καὶ ληϊδάριόν τι πρίασθαι, [Hote chrē chlainan pōlein ēdē, kai lēidarion ti priasthai,]

‎<i>When the cloak is to be sold, so that a summer garment may be bought.</i> And in the <i>Peace</i> the <i>Chorus</i> sings:

‎— — ὅταν ἠρινᾷ μὲν [hotan ērina men] — — <i>when indeed with spring</i> Φωνῇ χελιδὼν [Phōnē chelidōn] <i>voice the swallow</i> Ἑζομένη κελαδῇ. [Hezomenē keladē.] <i>sitting resounds.</i>

‎Similar to which are these words of <i>Stesichorus</i>, as the <i>Scholiast</i> notes: Ὅταν ἦρος ὥρᾳ κελαδῇ χελιδών· [Hotan ēros hōra keladē chelidōn;] <i>When the swallow shall resound in springtime.</i> Nor are these words of <i>Simonides</i> in <i>Athenaeus</i> far off: Ἄγγελε κλυτὰ ἔαρος ἁδυόδμου κυανέα χελιδὼν [Angele klyta earos hadyodmou kyanea chelidōn], <i>O famed messenger of sweet-scented spring, dark-blue swallow</i><sup>*)</sup>. And in <i>Theognis</i>, book 3, on the sacrifices of the <i>Rhodians</i>, the song which οἱ χελιδονίζοντες [hoi chelidonizontes] sang from door to door, that is, <i>the beggar-minstrels who announced that the swallow had come</i> (in Hesychius χελιδονισταὶ [chelidonistai]), begins thus: Ἦλθ', ἦλθε χελιδὼν καλὰς ὥρας ἄγουσα, καὶ καλοὺς ἐνιαυτοὺς, ἐπὶ γαστέρα λευκὰ κἀπὶ νῶτα μέλαινα· [Ēlth', ēlthe chelidōn kalas hōras agousa, kai kalous eniautous, epi gastera leuka kapi nōta melaina;] <i>The swallow has come, has come, with white belly and black back, bringing fair seasons and fair years.</i> See the rest there. Accordingly, as <i>Theon</i> writes on <i>Aratus</i>, the ancients painted the <i>northern</i> Fish with a swallow's head and called it ἰχθὺν χελιδόνιαν [ichthyn chelidonian], because by its rising it signified the prelude of spring. To the same point belong these words of <i>Aelian</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 1, ch. 52: Χελιδὼν δ' ἄρα τῆς ὥρας τῆς ἀρίστης ὑποσημαίνει τὴν ἐπιδημίαν· [Chelidōn d' ara tēs hōras tēs aristēs hyposēmainei tēn epidēmian;] <i>The swallow signals the arrival of the most beautiful season of the year.</i> And <i>Ovid</i>, almost at the end of book 2 of the <i>Fasti</i>:

‎<i>Am I deceived, or has the swallow, harbinger of spring, come?</i>

‎And <i>Columella</i> in the <i>Garden</i>:

‎<i>And the swallow will have sung of spring's arrival from her nest.</i>

‎And <i>Calpurnius</i>, <i>Eclogue</i> 5:

‎— — <i>in early spring, when the birds are already beginning to twitter, and the returning swallow plasters her nests with mud.</i>

‎And an unknown <i>poet</i> among the <i>Epigrams</i> of the ancients, book 4:

‎<i>The frisky kid and the chattering swallow announce springtime, and the bowl of milk, and the green grass.</i> [II, 612] And in book 5, <i>Avienus</i>:

‎<i>For when in early spring the hard earth loosens itself,</i> <i>and the swallow, winged one, chirps sweetly in the hollow rooftops.</i>

‎But if only one or two swallows appear, the proverb of the <i>Greeks</i> is known from <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>Ethics</i> book 1, chapter 6: Μία χελιδὼν ἔαρ οὐ ποιεῖ [mia chelidōn ear ou poiei], <i>One swallow does not make a spring.</i> Among us the swallow's stay is usually six months. Pliny, book 10, chapter 25: <i>Birds differ greatly in their seasons. Some are perennial, like doves; some stay six months, like swallows; some three months, like thrushes and turtledoves.</i> That is, swallows come at the beginning of spring and depart at the end of summer. Pliny on <i>Arcturus</i>, book 18, chapter 31: <i>Let the sign of the rising of that star be observed in the departure of the swallows.</i> <i>Arcturus</i>, moreover, rises in the month of <i>September</i>. Galen: Ἔστι μὲν ὡραιότατον τῆς ἐλάτης τὸ σπέρμα περὶ τὴν ἐπιτολὴν τοῦ Ἀρκτούρου, ὅστις καιρὸς ἐν Ῥώμῃ ὁ καλούμενος μὲν Σεπτέμβριός ἐστι [esti men hōraiotaton tēs elatēs to sperma peri tēn epitolēn tou Arktourou, hostis kairos en Rhōmē ho kaloumenos men Septembrios esti]. It is also around the <i>autumn</i> equinox. Theophrastus on the carob, <i>History of Plants</i> book 4, chapter 2: Ἀνθεῖ περὶ Ἀρκτοῦρον, καὶ ἰσημερίαν [anthei peri Arktouron, kai isēmerian]; or (as Pliny says more explicitly, book 2, chapter 47) <i>Arcturus rises eleven days before the equinox.</i> So when swallows migrate is well enough established, but where they migrate is not likewise. <i>Isidore</i> says in general: (the swallow) <i>flies across the seas and stays there.</i> <i>Anacreon</i>, in the place cited above:

‎Χειμῶνι δ' εἷς ἄφαντος, Ἢ Νεῖλον, ἢ 'πὶ Μέμφιν. [cheimōni d' heis aphantos, ē Neilon, ē 'pi Memphin.] <i>But in winter you are not seen,</i> [col. 67] <i>Whether you make for</i> the Nile <i>or</i> Memphis.

‎Herodotus, book 2, chapter 22, on <i>Ethiopia</i> below <i>Egypt</i>: Ἰκτῖνοι δὲ, καὶ χελιδόνες δι' ἔτεος ἐόντες οὐκ ἀπολείπουσι [iktinoi de, kai chelidones di' eteos eontes ouk apoleipousi]; <i>Kites and swallows are there the whole year round, and do not depart in winter.</i> However that may be, among them it is a grave crime to desert the departing flock, if these lines of Martial, book 5, <i>Epigram</i> 68, are true:

‎<i>When the</i> Attic birds <i>sought their winter retreats in their accustomed manner, one bird stayed behind in the nests: the crime was detected when they returned in springtime, and her own kind tore the deserter to pieces.</i>

‎But those that remain also face no slight danger from the cold. Pliny, in the passage already cited on the star <i>Arcturus</i>: <i>Let the sign of the rising of that star be observed in the departure of the swallows; for those caught behind perish.</i> Yet it is certain that some remain in sunny places; not all of these die, but they are stripped bare and lose their feathers. Accordingly <i>the Philosopher</i>, <i>History of Animals</i> book 8, chapter 16, after teaching that kites and [II, 613] swallows betake themselves in winter to warmer places when these are not too far away, says: Οἱ δὲ ποῤῥοτέρω ὄντες τῶν τοιούτων οὐκ ἐκτοπίζουσι, ἀλλὰ κρύπτουσιν ἑαυτούς· ἤδη γὰρ ὠμμέναι εἰσὶ πολλαὶ χελιδόνες ἐν ἀγγείοις [hoi de porrhoterō ontes tōn toioutōn ouk ektopizousi, alla kryptousin heautous; ēdē gar ōmmenai eisi pollai chelidones en angeiois] (read ἐν ἄγκεσι [en ankesi], or ἐν ἀγκείαις [en ankeiais]) ἐψιλωμέναι πάμπαν [epsilōmenai pampan]; <i>But the birds that are farther from such places do not migrate from there, but hide themselves: for many swallows have already been seen in valleys, completely featherless.</i> And following him Pliny, book 10, chapter 24: (swallows) <i>go off into neighbouring places, seeking the sunny recesses of the mountains, and have already been found there naked and featherless.</i> To such a swallow <i>Claudian</i> compares the hairless, moth-eaten head of <i>Eutropius</i>, an already worn-out eunuch, in these words, book 1 <i>Against Eutropius</i>:

‎<i>Or like the winter swallow that, its feathers falling in the icy frosts, dies upon the trunk of a tree.</i>

‎But when the <i>Prophet</i> says that swallows know the time of their coming or their departure, it need not be understood of all of them without exception. It suffices that it holds for most, so that by this example we may be reminded how rightly <i>Hesiod</i> said:

‎— — καιρὸς δ᾽ ἐπὶ πᾶσιν ἄριστος [kairos d' epi pasin aristos],

‎and one of the Seven Sages whom <i>Greece</i> celebrated: Καιρὸν γνῶθι [kairon gnōthi], <i>Know the time</i>. These are the very words of the sacred prophet.

‎I add one point: in the <i>Greek</i> copy of <i>Tobit</i>, chapter 2, verse 11, we read that τὰ στρουθία [ta strouthia], <i>little birds</i>, dropped dung into his eyes. <i>Jerome</i> rightly saw that this refers to swallows, and renders it thus: <i>It happened that, as he slept, warm dung fell from a nest of swallows onto his eyes.</i> For this is peculiar to the swallow: the mother herself either throws the droppings of the chicks out of the nest while they are small, or teaches them to void outside the nest when they are somewhat bigger. <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>History of Animals</i>, book 9, chapter 7: Καὶ κόπρον τὸ πρῶτον αὐταὶ ἐκβάλλουσιν· ὅταν δὲ αὐξηθῶσι μεταστρέφοντας ἔξω διδάσκουσι τοὺς νεοττοὺς προΐεσθαι [Kai kopron to prōton autai ekballousin; hotan de auxēthōsi metastrephontas exō didaskousi tous neottous proiesthai]. This is rendered by <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 33, as follows: <i>With remarkable cleanliness they carry out the excrement of the chicks; and when these are older they teach them to turn round and discharge their fullness outside.</i> Nor is this statement of the <i>Arabs</i> in <i>Damir</i> unlike it: ولا يلقي في عشه زبلا بل يلقيه الي خارج فانا اكبر فراخه علمها ذلك [wa-lā yulqī fī ʿushshihi ziblan bal yulqīhi ilā khārij fa-anā akbar firākhihi ʿallamahā dhālik] <i>It does not void its dung into the nest but outside, and it teaches this very thing to its chicks when they have grown up.</i> Something similar is recorded to have happened to <i>Gorgias</i>, for a swallow dropped dung on him. <i>Plutarch</i>, <i>Table Talk</i>, book 8, chapter 7: [col. 68] Γοργίας δὲ ὁ Σοφιστὴς, χελιδόνος ἀφείσης ἐπ᾽ αὐτὸν ἀπόπατον, ἀναβλέψας πρὸς αὐτὴν, Οὐ καλὰ ταῦτα (εἶπεν) ὦ Φιλομῆλα [Gorgias de ho Sophistēs, chelidonos apheisēs ep᾽ auton apopaton, anablepsas pros autēn, Ou kala tauta (eipen) ō Philomēla]· <i>Gorgias the Sophist, when a swallow had let fall its dung on him, turned his eyes towards it and said, "This is not becoming [II, 614], Philomela."</i> The <i>Philosopher</i> praises this as an apt saying in the <i>Rhetoric</i>, book 3, chapter 3, because ὄρνιθι μὲν, εἰ ἐποίησεν, οὐκ αἰσχρὸν, παρθένῳ δὲ αἰσχρόν· εὖ οὖν ἐλοιδόρησεν, εἰπὼν ὃ ἦν ἀλλ᾽ οὐχ᾽ ὅ ἐστιν [ornithi men, ei epoiēsen, ouk aischron, parthenō de aischron; eu oun eloidorēsen, eipōn ho ēn all᾽ ouch ho estin], <i>for a bird, if it had done it, it is not shameful, but for a maiden it is shameful. So he rebuked her well, saying what she had been, not what she then was.</i> Nor should anyone be surprised that the swallow is given the name <i>Philomela</i>, although it is very commonly believed that Philomela is the nightingale. For the matter is uncertain, and many of the ancients state explicitly that <i>Procne</i> was changed into a nightingale and <i>Philomela</i> into a swallow. I could prove this without any trouble, if that were the subject at hand. But we now hasten to other matters.
