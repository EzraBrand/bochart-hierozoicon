# Part 2, Book 4, chapter 1: The names and kinds of the locusts (vol. 3, pp. 251–271)

### ‎[III, 251] [col. 441] Chapter I.

### ‎<i>The names and kinds of locusts.</i>

‎As the land of the <i>Hebrews</i> is most fertile in locusts, so is their language in names for <i>locusts</i>. For, to say nothing of the <i>Talmudic</i> ones, which occur in great number, in the writings of the <i>Prophets</i> alone the doctors count ten. They are: 1. ארבה <i>arbe.</i> 2. גוב <i>gob</i>, or גובי <i>gobai.</i> 3. גזם <i>gazam.</i> 4. חגב <i>chagab.</i> 5. חנמל <i>chanamal.</i> 6. חסיל <i>chasil.</i> 7. חרגל <i>chargal.</i> 8. ילק <i>jelek.</i> 9. סלעם <i>solam.</i> 10. צלצל [col. 442] <i>tselatsal.</i> Since the origins of these words are for the most part little known, we have thought it worthwhile to explain them here in passing: for the thing lies hidden in the words, and much that pertains to the nature of these animals is drawn out of their names.

‎I. The first, ארבה <i>arbe</i>, <i>Aben Ezra</i> [=Abraham ibn Ezra] on <i>Leviticus</i> rightly derives מלשון רבוי, <i>from the meaning of multitude. For</i> רבה raba means <i>to be much</i> or <i>to be multiplied.</i> Hence ארבה <i>arbe</i>, with <i>Aleph</i> prefixed, as often. Nor is it obscure why locusts took their name from <i>abundance</i>, since no animal is more prolific. Hence of the <i>Midianites</i> [col. 443] Iud. VI, 5: <i>And they came like locusts, they and their camels without number.</i> And Judg 7:12: <i>They were spread out in that valley like locusts in multitude.</i> And Ps 105:34: <i>And the locust came, and the bruchus, of which there was no number.</i> And Jer 46:23, of the trees of the forest: <i>Because they are more than locusts, and there is no number of them.</i> And LI, 14: <i>Surely I will fill you with men, as with bruchi.</i> And Joel 1:6, of the locusts that were to lay waste <i>Judaea</i>: <i>A nation is come up against your land, strong and without number.</i> And Nahum III, 15: <i>Be as numerous as the bruchus, as numerous as the locust.</i> And [III, 252] Jdt 2:11: <i>They gathered to them like locusts, and like the sand of the earth: for they were innumerable for multitude.</i> And of the locusts of <i>Egypt</i>, Exod 10:5: <i>They shall cover the face of the earth, so that no one can see the earth.</i> Hence Jerome on <i>Joel</i>: <i>What is more innumerable than locusts?</i> And the common proverb of the <i>Arabs</i>, مال كالدبا [māl ka-l-dabā] <i>Wealth</i> (or <i>cattle</i>) <i>like locusts</i>, that is, very abundant. But these things are known to everyone <sup>R1</sup>.

‎II. But why the locust is called in <i>Hebrew</i> גוב <i>gob</i>, or גובי <i>gobai</i>, Amos 7:1 and Nahum III, 17., and in <i>Arabic</i> جاب [jāb] <i>gab</i>, or جابي [jābī] <i>gabi</i>, I would still not know, had not the <i>Arabs</i> taught me, among whom the verb جبا [jabā] <i>gaba</i> means <i>to emerge from the earth</i>, which is characteristic of locusts. Aristotle, <i>History</i> book 5, chapter 28: Ὅταν τέκωσιν (αἱ ἀκρίδες) ἐνταῦθα γίνονται σκώληκες ὠοειδεῖς, οἳ περιλαμβάνονται [Hotan tekōsin (hai akrides) entautha ginontai skōlēkes ōoeideis, hoi perilambanontai] [III, 253] ὑπό τινος γῆς λεπτῆς, ὥσπερ ὑφ᾽ ὑμένος· ἐν ταύτῃ δὲ ἐκπέτονται [hypo tinos gēs leptēs, hōsper hyph᾽ hymenos; en tautē de ekpetontai]. <i>When the locusts have laid, there arise there egg-shaped grubs, which are enclosed by a very thin earth, as by a little membrane, from which they fly out.</i> Gaza: <i>When this is split open, the locusts emerge and fly out.</i> Pliny, book 11, ch. 29: <i>These lay their eggs, packed close, in the ground in autumn, letting down the stalk of their spine. They last through the winter under the ground; and at the following end of spring they send forth small ones,</i> etc. To the same I refer the fact that by the <i>Ethiopians</i> and <i>Troglodytes locusts</i> are called አንበጣ፡ [ʾanəbaṭā] <i>anbat.</i> For in <i>Arabic</i> نبط [nabaṭa] <i>nabata</i> is <i>to gush forth</i>, and <i>to issue</i> and <i>emerge from the earth.</i> This indeed is proper to springs, but it is also transferred to other things καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs] <sup>R2</sup>.

‎III. The third name of the <i>locust</i>, גזם <i>gazam</i>, Joel 1:4 and II, 25. and Amos 4:9, the <i>Hebrews</i> derive from גוז <i>guz</i> or גזז <i>gazaz</i>, which means <i>to shear</i> and <i>to cut off</i>. And they hold that the final <i>Mem</i> is servile, although they produce no similar noun. For חנם, <i>gratis</i> [freely], ריקם, <i>frustra</i> [in vain], דומם, <i>quiete</i> [quietly], יומם, <i>interdiu</i> [by day], אמנם, <i>vere</i> [truly], which they cite here, are not nouns but adverbs. Therefore the true root is the verb גזם <i>gazam</i>, which the <i>Talmudists</i> sometimes use in the sense of <i>cutting off</i> and <i>pruning</i>: as in <i>Avoda Zara</i> fol. 50b, אין מגזמין וסכין שמן בגזום, <i>They do not prune the branches of trees, but they smear oil on the place of the pruning.</i> So in <i>Arabic</i>, in Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], جَزَمَ النحل [jazama al-nakhl], <i>He pruned the palm.</i> In <i>Ethiopic</i> too it occurs for ἐκκόπτειν [ekkoptein], Matt 3:10 and VII, 19. Luke 3:9 and XIII, 7. 9. In these places too the subject is the cutting down of trees. From this, then, locusts were named, because with their very sharp teeth they gnaw not only grass, crops and the leaves of trees, but also the bark and the more tender twigs of the trees themselves, as if they were cutting them off. Pliny, in the place cited above: <i>Scorching many things by their touch, but gnawing everything with their bite, even the doors of houses.</i> Jerome on Joel ch. 1: <i>The Jews think that in the days of Joel so innumerable a multitude of locusts came upon Judaea that they filled everything, and left, I will not say the crops, but not even the bark and branches of the vines and trees.</i> And also Amos 4:9: <i>Your gardens, and your vineyards, and your fig groves, and your olive groves</i> יאכל הגזם, <i>the Gazam</i> (or <i>locust</i>) <i>shall eat.</i> Accordingly [col. 444] Pisides attributes to the swarms of locusts a μυριόστομον ξίφος [myriostomon xiphos], <i>a sword of ten thousand edges.</i> So much according to the mind of the <i>Hebrews</i>, who with the greatest agreement hold that גזם <i>gazam</i> is a species of <i>locust</i>: following, of course, the Chaldee, for whom <i>gazam</i> is זחלא, the <i>creeping locust</i>, [III, 254] or ἄπτερος [apteros], as we shall show below. The <i>Syriac</i> also renders it ܡܫܘܛܐ [משוטא] in the same sense. For ܡܫܘܛܐ [משוטא] is the Chaldee שמוטא inverted. And שמוטא is counted among the species of <i>locust</i>, as is the <i>Hebrew</i> חסיל, for which it stands in Jonathan [=Targum Jonathan] on 1 Reg. VIII, 37. and Joel 1:4 <sup>R3</sup>. But for the <i>Greeks</i> and Jerome גזם <i>gazam</i> is κάμπη [kampē], <i>caterpillar.</i> Although Fuller [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)] in his <i>Miscellanea</i>, book 5, ch. 20, tries to defend this version with many arguments, he by no means persuades me: both because the <i>Hebrews</i>, <i>Chaldeans</i> and <i>Syrians</i> think otherwise, and because the name גזם <i>gazam</i>, formed from the <i>pruning of branches</i>, seems to suit the caterpillar less than the locust, whose bite is stronger. For the bite of the caterpillar <i>scorches</i> rather than cuts off. Columella:

‎— — — <i>and the caterpillar creeps through the gardens,</i> <i>And, advancing over them, scorches the seedlings with its bite.</i>

‎Therefore, just as it is called ERUCA <i>from gnawing</i> [<i>erodere</i>], so it is also called URUCA <i>from burning</i> [<i>urere</i>]. The old <i>Latin-Greek Glosses</i> edited by Stephanus, p. 347: <i>Uruca</i>, κάμπη [kampē]. And the <i>Greek-Latin</i> ones, p. 501: Κάμπη λαχάνου [Kampē lachanou], <i>Eruca</i>, <i>uruca.</i> And p. 516: Λαχάνων κάμπη [Lachanōn kampē], <i>urica.</i> Since these three places confirm one another, none of them ought to have been suspect to Fuller <sup>R4</sup>.

‎IV. Aben Ezra on <i>Leviticus</i> asserts that the fourth name of the <i>locust</i>, חגב <i>chagab</i>, is known מלשון ישמעאל, <i>from the language of Ishmael</i>, that is, <i>Arabic</i>: which is utterly false. For although among the <i>Arabs</i> there are very many names for <i>locusts</i>, none similar to this one occurs. Yet the origin of the word may be <i>Arabic</i>. For [III, 255] the <i>Arabic</i> verb حجب [ḥajaba] <i>chagaba</i> means <i>to veil</i>. Thus Avicenna [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], in the chapter <i>On the anatomy of the eye</i>, teaches that the ἀραχνοειδῆν [arachnoeidēn] tunic is thin for this reason, that if it were dense, يحجب الضو عن الجليدية [yaḥjub al-ḍaw ʿan al-jalīdiyya] <i>it would veil the light from the glacial humour</i>, or <i>crystalline</i>, that is, it would keep the light from it as if by a veil spread before it. Hence حجاب [ḥijāb] <i>chigab</i> is the <i>veil</i>, Matt 27:51 and Heb 6:19, and حاجب [ḥājib] <i>chagib</i>, that is, <i>doorkeeper</i>, Gen 43:16 19. in the <i>Paris</i> edition; because in closing the door he as it were draws a veil over it. I would therefore think that locusts were called חגבים <i>chagabim</i> from the same source, because when they fly in swarms they seem to draw a veil over the light of the sun, as we shall show below on Joel 2:20 <sup>R5</sup>.

‎V. The fifth, חנמל <i>chanamal</i>, Ps 78:47, is taken for the <i>locust</i> by <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] and <i>R. Chasan</i>, whom <i>Aben Ezra</i> cites. But others more rightly hold that <i>chanamal</i> is something analogous to <i>hail</i>, so that, as usual, the two hemistichs of the same verse correspond (ἀντίστροφα [antistropha]). They read: <i>And he destroyed</i> בברד, <i>with hail their vines, and their sycomores</i> בחנמל, <i>with Chanamal.</i> So the <i>Hebrew</i> word for word. Accordingly <i>Aben Ezra</i> understands hailstones here. But <i>Saadias</i> and <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] understand what the <i>Arabs</i> call יאלצקיע, that is, <i>hoarfrost</i> <sup>R6</sup>. Quite rightly. For so the ancient <i>Greeks</i>: Καὶ τὰς συκαμίνους αὐτῶν ἐν τῇ πάχνῃ [Kai tas sykaminous autōn en tē pachnē]. Hence the Vulgate translator: <i>And their mulberry trees with hoarfrost.</i> And the old <i>Arabic Psalter</i> in manuscript in my possession: وتوثهم بالصقيع [wa-tūthahum bi-l-ṣaqīʿ]. And the <i>Ethiopic</i>: ወበለሶሞን፡ በአስሐትያ፡ [wabalasomon baʾasəḥatəyā]; all in the same sense. Moreover, in the <i>Midrash</i> it is asserted that חנמל <i>chanamal</i> is said by metathesis for נחמל <i>nachamal</i>, because נח ומל <i>nach vemal</i>, it <i>rests</i> and <i>cuts</i>. Both of these suit hoarfrost very well. Hence <i>Theophrastus</i>, book 5, <i>De causis</i>, ch. 19, comparing snow with hoarfrost, says: Ἡ μὲν οὐκ ἐπιμένει, ἀλλ᾽ ἀποτήκεται ἀπὸ τῶν [col. 445] κλημάτων, καὶ τῶν βλαστῶν· ἡ δὲ πάχνη [Hē men ouk epimenei, all᾽ apotēketai apo tōn klēmatōn, kai tōn blastōn; hē de pachnē] (ἐπιμένουσα [epimenousa], for this must be supplied for the opposition to hold) ταῦτ᾽ ἀποκαίει [taut᾽ apokaiei], <i>the former does not stay long, but melts and slips off the shoots and buds; but hoarfrost</i> (staying long) <i>burns them.</i> And below: Τμητικοτέρα δοκεῖ ἡ πάχνη τῆς χίονος εἶναι [Tmētikotera dokei hē pachnē tēs chionos einai], <i>Hoarfrost seems to have a greater power</i> [III, 256] <i>of cutting than snow.</i> <i>Pliny</i> too writes that hoarfrost <i>persidere</i> [settles in], that is, <i>stays settled long</i>. The words are in book 17, ch. 24: <i>Hoarfrost is more destructive by nature, because once fallen it stays settled.</i> It is therefore very consistent with the truth that the word חנמל signifies <i>hoarfrost</i> rather than the <i>locust</i>. Ἐρυσίβη [erysibē], <i>blight</i>, is something else. Yet in the <i>Greek Scholiast</i> on the <i>Psalms</i>: ἐρυσίβη ἐςὶ κατὰ τὸν Ἡρωδιανὸν τὸ ἐπικάθιζον τοῖς καρποῖς παχνῶδες, καὶ διαφθεῖρον αὐτοὺς [erysibē esti kata ton Hērōdianon to epikathizon tois karpois pachnōdes, kai diaphtheiron autous], <i>blight, according to</i> Herodian, <i>is that frost-like thing which settles on the fruits and spoils them</i> <sup>R7</sup>.

‎VI. The sixth name of the <i>locust</i>, חסיל <i>chasil</i>, is formed from the verb חסל <i>chasal</i>, which means <i>to consume</i> and is properly said of the locust. So in the <i>Hebrew</i> text, Deut 28:38: <i>You shall cast much seed into the field and gather little,</i> כי יחסלנו הארבה, <i>for the locust shall consume it.</i> And in the <i>Chaldee of Onkelos</i> [=Targum Onkelos]: ארי יחסליניה גובא. And Nahum III, 16., in <i>Jonathan</i>: יחסלונך כגובא, <i>They shall consume you like the locust.</i> Hence, with the meaning somewhat altered, חסל also occurs frequently in the sense of <i>finishing</i> and <i>completing</i>, as if <i>to consummate</i> were nearly the same as <i>to consume</i> <sup>R8</sup>.

‎[III, 257] VII. Where the seventh, חרגל <i>chargol</i>, comes from, the <i>Hebrews</i> are wholly ignorant. But in <i>Arabic</i> حرجل [ḥarjala] <i>chargala</i> means <i>to be long</i>, or <i>to move and stretch out in a long line</i>. Hence حرجل [ḥarjal] <i>chargal</i>, or حرجلة [ḥurjula] <i>chorgola</i>, in <i>Alcamus</i>, الجماعة من الخيل والقطعة مي الجراد [al-jamāʿa min al-khayl wa-l-qiṭʿa my al-jarād]: <i>of horses it means a column; and of locusts, a swarm</i>. <i>Cadamustus</i>, <i>Navigations</i>, ch. 13, writes that such a swarm sometimes extends over twelve miles. And although in <i>Europe</i> they extend less far, yet even there immense columns of locusts are sometimes seen. <i>Aldrovandi</i> [=Ulisse Aldrovandi, Ornithologia (1599–1603)], book 4, <i>On insects</i>, p. 423: <i>They write that in the year</i> 1475 <i>a huge mass of locusts coming from</i> Hungary, Moravia <i>and</i> Silesia <i>roamed over the</i> Sieradz <i>and</i> Łęczyca <i>region and</i> Masovia, etc., <i>with swarms continuous for a mile and a half in breadth and as much again in length</i>, that is, for three miles. <i>Surius</i>, at the year 1541, on the locusts around <i>Poland</i>: <i>These flew forth in such a multitude that they covered two miles in length and breadth a cubit deep, so that by their density they shut out even the brightness of the sun from that whole tract of land</i>. So it is not without reason that these columns of locusts take their name from a long line. Yet <i>Moses</i> does not use חרגל <i>chargol</i> for a <i>swarm of locusts</i>, but for <i>a particular kind of locust</i>. So too in the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>On the Sabbath</i>, fol. 67, in the <i>Mishnah</i>: יוצאין בביצת החרגל ובשן של שועל ובמסמר מן הצלוב משום רפואה, <i>They go out</i> (lawfully on the Sabbath day) <i>with the egg of the</i> chargol, <i>and with the tooth of a fox, and with a nail from a gallows, for the sake of healing</i> (hung on the body); and there the <i>Gemara</i> says that the egg of the <i>chargol</i> was used לשיחלא. And the <i>Gloss</i> says that שיחלא is כאבה און והרלי באזן, <i>pain of the ear, or a disease in the ear</i> <sup>R9</sup>.

‎[III, 258] VIII. The eighth is ילק <i>jelek</i>, <i>from licking</i>, derived from שילוק בלשונו, because <i>with its tongue it licks</i> the crops and the fruits of the earth. So <i>Aben Ezra</i> on <i>Joel</i>. The <i>Arabs</i> agree with this: for them the verb لحس [laḥasa] <i>lahasa</i> means <i>to lick</i>. And the noun لحس [laḥs] <i>lahas</i>, that is, <i>a licking</i>, is said of the locust, which consumes the produce of the earth by licking it, as the moth does wool. <i>Alcamus</i>: اللحس اكل الدود الصوف واكل [col. 446] الجراد الخضر [al-laḥs akl al-dūd al-ṣūf wa-akl al-jarād al-khuḍar]. Similar is the fact that in <i>Giauhari</i> [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] العثة [al-ʿuththa], the <i>moth</i> (Hebr. עש), is defined as دويبة تلحس الصوف [duwaybba talḥasu al-ṣūf], <i>a little animal that licks wool</i>, that is, <i>gnaws</i> it. Hence in Scripture <i>to lick</i> sometimes means the same as <i>to consume</i>. So <i>Num 22:4. This multitude will lick up everything that is around us, as the ox licks up the grass of the field.</i> And <i>1 Reg. XVIII, 20. Then the fire of the Lord fell and consumed the burnt offering, and the wood, and the stones, and the very dust, and also licked up the water that was in the trench</i> <sup>R10</sup>.

‎IX. The ninth, סלעם <i>solam</i>, is for <i>Aben Ezra</i> a kind of <i>locust</i> that יעלה בסלעים, <i>climbs the rocks</i>, as though it were formed from סלע with <i>Mem</i> added at the end of the word; but we have already shown that they cite no noun of this form. Not to mention that there is no species of <i>locust</i> more given to rocks than another. We therefore think that in סלעם the final <i>Mem</i> belongs to the root, as in גזם, discussed above <sup>a)</sup>. For in <i>Chaldee</i> סלעם means <i>to devour</i> and <i>to consume</i>, like the <i>Hebrew</i> בלע, in place of which it frequently occurs in the <i>Paraphrasts</i>. [III, 259] Hence the <i>locust</i> is rightly called סלעם <i>solam</i>, just as in <i>Greek</i> it is called βροῦχος [brouchos] or βροῦκος [broukos], and ἀκρὶς [akris], on which see below <sup>R11</sup>.

‎X. The tenth is צלצל <i>tselatsal</i>, Deut 28:42. For the <i>Greeks</i> it is ἐρυσίβη [erysibē], for the <i>Vulgate</i> BLIGHT; but for all the <i>Hebrews</i>, for <i>Onkelos</i> and for <i>Erpenianus</i> [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] it is the locust. Many hold that it is so called from the noise of its wings, because צלל means <i>to tinkle</i>, and צלצלים are <i>cymbals</i> or <i>bells</i>. But צלל also means <i>to overshadow</i>. Hence it seems more probable to me that <i>locusts</i> are so called because they overshadow the sun, as we prove in its proper place <sup>b)</sup> from <i>Pliny</i> and others. No one observes that the sound of their wings comes close to the ringing of a cymbal or a bell <sup>R12</sup>.

‎So far we have reviewed many names of locusts, scattered here and there in the <i>Prophetic</i> books. Lest anyone think they are wholly synonymous, it should be known that most of them denote definite species. This is clear from <i>Joel 1:4</i>, where what the <i>gazam</i> left the <i>arbe</i> ate, and what the <i>arbe</i> left the <i>jelek</i> ate, and what the <i>jelek</i> left the <i>chasil</i> ate. So it appears that <i>gazam, arbe, jelek</i> and <i>chasil</i> are distinct species <sup>R13</sup>. Again, in <i>Lev 11:21–22.</i> four species of locusts are counted, namely <i>arbe, solam, chargol</i> and <i>chagab</i>; common to all of these is that they are winged, four-footed, and permitted as food <sup>R14</sup>. For the <i>Arabs</i> teach that locusts are [III, 260] distinguished in various ways. <i>Damir</i>: وهى اصناف فبعضه اصفر وبعضه ابيض وبعضه احمر وبعضه اسود وبعضه كبير الجثة وبعضه صغيرها [wa-hiya aṣnāf fa-baʿḍuhu aṣfar wa-baʿḍuhu abyaḍ wa-baʿḍuhu aḥmar wa-baʿḍuhu aswad wa-baʿḍuhu kabīr al-juththa wa-baʿḍuhu ṣaghīruhā] <i>There are various species of locusts. For some are yellow, some white, some red, some black. Some are of large body, and some of small.</i> But <i>Marcellus Virgilius</i> on <i>Dioscorides</i>, book 2, puts it much more eloquently: <i>Nature has varied locusts with wonderful artistry</i>, etc. <i>For there are some that are green, some black, some livid, some that display this colour in one part and that in another. There are some that only when flying reveal a colour previously hidden. There are some that fly with single wings, some with several, some that lack wings and leap, some that, deprived of both these motions, only walk. There are some with longer legs, some with shorter: and among these, some with more and shorter joints, some with fewer and longer. There are some that sing, some that are mute, some that are heard by day, some only in the dark. And, not to be too long, there are some that do no harm to man in farming and harmlessly let themselves be caught by boys. There are others, on the contrary, that bring pestilence and ruin to all crops throughout whole regions, and by the famine they cause have often forced peoples to abandon their ancestral fields</i> [col. 447]. Therefore, as there are different species of locusts, so there are also different names. Thus by reason of form or colour in <i>Arabic</i> سرعوف [surʿūf] <i>surouph</i> is the <i>long locust</i>, جندخ [jundukh] giondoch the <i>fat locust</i>, برقان [burqān] borkan the <i>variegated locust</i>, جردم [jardam] giardam the <i>green locust with a black head</i> <sup>R15</sup>; conversely, كدم [kudam] chudam the <i>black locust with a green head</i> <sup>R16</sup>. They are also distinguished by reason of place: حبشان [ḥubshān] <i>chobsan</i> is the <i>Ethiopian locust</i>; عرفان [ʿurfān] <i>orphan</i>, the one found in the sands of <i>Aligi</i> or <i>Dahnae</i>, [III, 261] near <i>Medina</i>; and by reason of sex, for the male <i>locust</i> is called عصغور [ʿuṣghūr] <i>usphour</i> <sup>R17</sup>, and عظاري [ʿaẓārī] <i>adari</i>, and عسقول [ʿusqūl] <i>uscoul</i>; the female دباسا [dibāsā] <i>dibasa</i>, and عيسا [ʿaysā] <i>aisa</i>; and by reason of age, as these words of <i>Damir</i> prove: اذا خرج الجرد من بيضة يقال لها الدبا <sup>R18</sup> فاذا طلعت اجنحته وكبرت فهو الغوغا وذلك حين عيوج بعضه في بعض فاذا بدت فيه الالوان واصفرت الذكور واسودت الاناث سمي جراد حينيذ [idhā kharaja al-jurd min bayḍa yuqāl lahā al-dabā fa-idhā ṭalaʿat ajniḥatuhu wa-kaburat fa-huwa al-ghawghā wa-dhālika ḥīna ʿywj baʿḍuhu fī baʿḍ fa-idhā badat fīhi al-alwān wa-iṣfarrat al-dhukūr wa-iswaddat al-ināth summiya jarād ḥīnaydh] <i>When the locust comes out of the egg, it is called</i> daba <sup>R18</sup>; <i>when the wings sprout and grow, it is called</i> gauga, <i>and that is when they swarm tumultuously over one another. But when they begin to be marked with various colours, so that the males turn yellow and the females black, then at last it is called</i> girad <sup>R19</sup>.

‎In Latin دبا [dabā] <i>daba</i> is the <i>bruchus</i>, غوغا [ghawghā] gauga the <i>attelabus</i>, and جراد [jarād] girad the <i>locust</i>, if these words of <i>Remigius of Auxerre</i> on <i>Joel</i> are true: <i>The offspring or young of the locust, before it has wings, is called</i> bruchus. <i>Then, when it has begun to fly a little, it is called</i> attelabus. <i>Then, when it has begun to fly fully, it becomes a</i> locust. And <i>Jerome</i> on <i>Nahum III</i> says much the same: <i>That the bruchus does nothing else but stays always on the ground and, without wings, serves food and belly. The attelabus, however, takes on at least modest wings, and though it cannot fly high, it is nevertheless noted to leap up from the ground, and finally, becoming a locust, it flies</i>, etc. And again: <i>The attelabus</i>, [III, 262] <i>which Aquila more expressively rendered</i> 'devourer', <i>is a small locust between the locust and the bruchus, creeping rather than flying on small wings, and always leaping.</i> For <i>Augustine</i> too, on <i>Psalm 104</i>, the bruchus is the offspring of the locust. <i>The plague</i>, he says, <i>of the locust and of the bruchus is one; since the one is the parent and the other the offspring.</i> And Isidore asserts that <i>almost the whole body of the bruchus is gathered into the belly</i>: that is, it is scarcely anything but belly, since it wholly lacks wings. He has this from <i>Gregory</i> on <i>Job</i>, book 33, ch. 17. Accordingly βροῦχος [brouchos], or βροῦκος [broukos], as the <i>Ionians</i> wrote it according to <i>Hesychius</i>, is so called παρὰ τὸ βρύκειν, τὸ ἐσθίειν [para to brykein, to esthiein], <i>from eating</i> or <i>devouring</i>, as μάσταξ [mastax] παρὰ τὸ μασᾶσθαι [para to masasthai], <i>from chewing</i>; as if it served nothing but the belly, being incapable of flight.

‎This is the view of the <i>Latin Fathers</i>. Which is surprising, since in the <i>Vulgate</i> version one reads at <i>Nahum III, 16.</i> <i>The bruchus spread out and flew away</i>, and in the <i>Greek</i>, Βροῦχος ὥρμησε, καὶ ἐξεπετάσθη [Brouchos hōrmēse, kai exepetasthē]. And at <i>Lev 11:22</i> the <i>bruchus</i> is counted among the winged [col. 448] locusts. Accordingly the <i>Greek Fathers</i> attribute flight and wings to the bruchus. Thus <i>Cyril</i> says of it on <i>Nahum</i> ch. 3: Φασὶ γὰρ, ὅτι, πιπτούσης χαλάζης, καὶ ὑετῶν καταρηγγνυμένων ἀδρανὴς εἰς πτῆσιν ὁ βροῦχος, καταδεδευμένων αὐτῷ τῶν πτερῶν [Phasi gar, hoti, piptousēs chalazēs, kai hyetōn katarēngnymenōn adranēs eis ptēsin ho brouchos, katadedeumenōn autō tōn pterōn]; <i>For they say that when hail falls and rains burst forth, the bruchus becomes weak for flight, its wings being soaked.</i> And <i>Theodoret</i> in the same place, of the same: Τῆς ἡλιακῆς προσβαλλούσης ἀκτῖνος ἀνίσταται, καὶ πετάννυσι τὰ πτερὰ, καὶ εἰς ἕτερον μεταβαίνει τόπον [Tēs hēliakēs proballousēs aktinos anistatai, kai petannysi ta ptera, kai eis heteron metabainei topon]; <i>When the ray of the sun strikes it, it rises, spreads its wings, and moves elsewhere.</i> Indeed the same author, on <i>Amos 7:1</i>, openly distinguishes the bruchus from the young of locusts: Ἐπιγονὴν δὲ ἀκρίδων, he says, ἐκάλησε τὸν Ἀσσύριον, βροῦχον δὲ τὸν Βαβυλώνιον [Epigonēn de akridōn … ekalēse ton Assyrion, brouchon de ton Babylōnion]; <i>He called</i> the Assyrian <i>the young of locusts, but</i> the Babylonian <i>the bruchus</i>. The <i>Greek</i> translators too, at <i>Lev 11:22</i>, seem to have distinguished the bruchus from the common locust not only by age but also by species. But the use of the word varied. For among the <i>Ionians</i> βροῦκος [broukos] was a species of locust, and among the <i>Cypriots</i> βροῦκας [broukas] was the green locust, but among the <i>Tarentines</i> the attelabus. <i>Hesychius</i>: Βροῦκος· ἀκρίδων εἶδος, Ἴωνες. Κύπριοι δὲ τὴν χλωρὰν ἀκρίδα βροῦκαν· Ταραντῖνοι δὲ ἀττέλεβον [Broukos; akridōn eidos, Iōnes. Kyprioi de tēn chlōran akrida broukan; Tarantinoi de attelebon], that is, ἀττέλαβον [attelabon]. Thus for <i>Theophrastus</i>, in the little book <i>On the animals that appear suddenly</i>, the βροῦκος [broukos] is a species of <i>attelabus</i>: Χαλεπαὶ μὲν οὖν, he says, αἱ ἀκρίδες, χαλεπώτεροι δὲ οἱ ἀττέλαβοι, καὶ τούτων μάλιστα οὓς καλοῦσι βρούκους [Chalepai men oun … hai akrides, chalepōteroi de hoi attelaboi, kai toutōn malista hous kalousi broukous]; <i>Locusts indeed are harmful, but attelabi are more harmful, and most of all those they call</i> bruci. So one must read, instead of the corrupt βαρούκτους [baroukt ous].

‎Thus for the older writers the <i>attelabus</i> cannot be the <i>immature young of the locust</i>, since they more than once mention the brood and eggs of the attelabus: <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>History</i> book 5, ch. 29: Ὁμοίως δὲ τίκτουσι καὶ οἱ ἀττέλαβοι [Homoiōs de tiktousi kai hoi attelaboi], <i>The attelabi too give birth in the same way.</i> And a little later: Φθείρεται δὲ αὐτῶν τὰ ὠὰ ὑπὸ τῶν μετοπωρινῶν ὑδάτων [Phtheiretai de autōn ta ōa hypo tōn metopōrinōn hydatōn]; <i>Their eggs are destroyed by the autumn rains.</i> Plutarch in <i>Isis</i>: Λήμνιοι [III, 263] κορύδους ἐτίμησαν τὰ τῶν ἀττελάβων εὑρίσκοντας ὠὰ, καὶ κόπτοντας [Lēmnioi korydous etimēsan ta tōn attelabōn heuriskontas ōa, kai koptontas]; the Lemnians <i>honoured larks, because they found the eggs of the attelabi and broke them.</i> The same author seems everywhere to take ἀττέλαβον [attelabon] or ἀττέλεβον [attelebon] for the <i>locust</i> in general: as in the <i>Symposiaca</i> book 2, ch. 3, where he says that the egg generates δίποδα τὸν ὄρνιν, καὶ ἄποδα τὸν ὄφιν, καὶ πολύποδα τὸν ἀττέλεβον [dipoda ton ornin, kai apoda ton ophin, kai polypoda ton attelebon], <i>the two-footed bird, the footless serpent, and the many-footed attelabus.</i> And a little below, when in the servile war in <i>Sicily</i> much blood had been shed and corpses lay rotting unburied on the ground, πλῆθος, he says, ἀττελέβων ἐξήνθησε, καὶ τὸν σῖτον ἔφθειρον, πανταχοῦ σχεδασθέντες ἐπὶ τὴν νῆσον [plēthos … attelebōn exēnthēse, kai ton siton ephtheiron, pantachou schedasthentes epi tēn nēson], <i>a multitude of attelabi sprang up, which, scattered everywhere over the island, ruined the crops.</i> Of the same kind is the fact that in <i>Eubulus</i>' riddle in <i>Athenaeus</i> book 10 the <i>ichneumon</i> is called ἀττελεβόφθαλμος [attelebophthalmos] instead of ἀκριδοφθάλμῳ [akridophthalmō]. Yet this is certain: the attelabus was small within the locust kind, and ἄπτερον [apteron, wingless]. <i>Hesychius</i>: ἀττέλεβος· ἀκρὶς μικρά [attelebos; akris mikra]. <i>Pliny</i> book 29, ch. 5: <i>The smallest of the locusts, without wings, which they call attelabi.</i> Hence the <i>Greek</i> translators at <i>Nahum III</i>, 17: Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος [Exēlato hōs attelabos], <i>It leapt like an attelabus</i>, attributing to it not flight but leaping. Indeed it seems to be named <i>from leaping</i>. It is well known that φ and β are often interchanged. Thus among the <i>Macedonians</i> Φίλιππος [Philippos] was Βίλιππος [Bilippos]; Φερονίκη [Pheronikē], Βερονίκη [Beronikē]; Φρύγες [Phryges], Βρίγες [Briges]. φάλαινα [phalaina] and <i>balaena</i> [whale] sound the same; so do ἀμφιλαφὴς [amphilaphēs] and ἀμφιλαβής [amphilabēs]. Πιστάκιον [Pistakion] is also written φιστάκιον [phistakion] and βιστάκιον [bistakion]. So I would think that ἀττέλαβον [attelabon] was the same as ἀττέλαφον [attelaphon], that is, <i>leaping deer</i>, or <i>leaping like a deer</i>. For ἄττειν [attein] is <i>to leap</i>, and ἔλαφος [elaphos] is <i>deer</i>, whose agility in leaping is praised at <i>Isa 35:6.</i> To this point is the fact that the <i>Talmudists</i> count among the species of locusts איילא קמצא, as if you said ἀκριδέλαφον [akridelaphon], or <i>deer-locust</i>. The <i>Arabic</i> translator of <i>Nahum</i>, who clings tenaciously to the <i>Greek</i> text, renders the <i>Greek</i> words Ἐξήλατο ὡς ἀττέλαβος [Exēlato hōs attelabos] thus: وثب مثل جرادة سودا [wathaba mithl jarāda sūdā] <i>It leapt like a black locust.</i>

‎Another name of the <i>locust</i>, ἀττάκης [attakēs] or ἀττακὸς [attakos] in <i>Levit. XI</i>, 22., might seem [col. 449] also to take its name from ἄττειν [attein], <i>from leaping</i>, as in the vulgar tongue <i>saltarella.</i> Unless ἀττακὸς [attakos] is thought to be the same as ἀστακὸς [astakos] in the dialect of the <i>Aeolians</i>, for whom πίστις [pistis] is πίττις [pittis], whence <i>fides</i> [faith]; and βλάστη [blastē] is βλάττη [blattē], whence <i>planta</i> [plant]. In favour of this are: 1. that among the sea locusts there is the ἀστακὸς [astakos], <i>astacus</i> (in Norman <i>houmar</i>); 2. that in the best Glosses <i>lucusta</i>, or <i>locusta</i>, is rendered ἀκρὶς [akris], ἀστακός [astakos]. Nor do these words of <i>Isidore</i>, otherwise very inept, mean anything else: <i>"The locust is so called because it has long legs like a spear [hasta]; hence</i> the Greeks <i>call it, both the marine and the terrestrial kind,</i> hastago." For it is evident that he was reaching after frigid wordplays between <i>locusta, hasta</i> and <i>astacus</i>, as though the <i>astacus</i>, his <i>hastago</i>, were a kind of terrestrial as well as of marine locust.

‎There is also in <i>Lev 11:22</i> a species of <i>locust</i> called ὀφιομάχης [ophiomachēs], <i>ophiomachus</i>, as if it <i>fought serpents.</i> However παράδοξον [paradoxon] this is, the <i>Philosopher</i> nevertheless affirms it in the <i>History</i> [III, 264], book 9, chapter 6: Πολλοὶ δὲ καὶ τὴν ἀκρίδα ἑωράκασιν, ὅτι ὅταν μάχηται τοῖς ὄφεσι, λαμβάνεται τοῦ τραχήλου τῶν ὄφεων [Polloi de kai tēn akrida heōrakasin, hoti hotan machētai tois ophesi, lambanetai tou trachēlou tōn opheōn]. Hence Pliny, book 11, chapter 29: <i>"Each one by itself kills a serpent, when it pleases, seizing its throat with its bite."</i> And <i>Simon Majolus</i>, bishop of <i>Volturara</i>, in <i>Dialogue</i> 10, relates that his gardener, coming from the field, saw a locust that had seized a serpent by the throat and so killed it without a fight. It should be noted that ὀφιομάχης [ophiomachēs] is put in the Law for the <i>Hebrew</i> חרגל <i>chargol</i>, or <i>argol.</i> For by the usage of the ancients it can be written either way. Thus the same river of <i>Mesopotamia</i>, חבר, is written in <i>Greek</i> Χαβόρας [Chaboras] and Ἀβόρας [Aboras]. The same people of <i>Arabia Felix</i>, חצרמות, Χατραμῶται [Chatramōtai] and Ἀτραμῶται [Atramōtai]. And among the <i>Leuco-Syrians</i> the <i>Alybes</i> are the same as the <i>Chalybes.</i> And the חסידים are called now <i>Chasidaei</i>, now <i>Asidaei</i>. And the bird חסידה <i>chasida</i> is written ἄσιδα [asida] in the <i>Greek</i> version of <i>Job.</i> So I think that the ancients wrote the <i>Hebrew</i> name of the <i>ophiomachus</i>, חרגול <i>chargol</i>, as <i>argol</i>, with the aspiration dropped. And from this arose the fable of the argolae ophiomachi, which they idly claim were serpents rather than locusts, and were called <i>argolae</i> because they were brought by <i>Alexander</i> from <i>Pelasgian Argos</i> into <i>Egypt</i> to kill the asps. So <i>Suidas</i> reports: Ἀργόλαι· εἶδος ὄφεων, οὓς ἤνεγκε Μακεδὼν ὁ Ἀλέξανδρος ἐκ τοῦ Ἄργους τοῦ Πελασγικοῦ εἰς Ἀλεξάνδρειαν, καὶ ἐνέβαλεν εἰς τὸν ποταμὸν, πρὸς ἀναίρεσιν τῶν ἀσπίδων [Argolai; eidos opheōn, hous ēnenke Makedōn ho Alexandros ek tou Argous tou Pelasgikou eis Alexandreian, kai enebalen eis ton potamon, pros anairesin tōn aspidōn]. Nor does he cite an authority. But I find something similar in the <i>Synopsis on the Life and Death of the Prophets</i>, which they falsely ascribe to <i>Dorotheus</i> the martyr, in the chapter <i>On Jeremiah.</i> But instead of a locust ophiomachus there was in <i>Argos</i> a locust scorpiomachus, of which the <i>Philosopher</i> says this in the book of <i>Marvels</i> [ch. 151.]: Ἐν Ἄργει δέ φασι γίνεσθαι ἀκρίδος τινὸς γένος, ὃ καλεῖται σκορπιομάχον· ὅταν γὰρ ἴδῃ τάχιστα σκορπίον, ἀνθίσταται αὐτῷ, ὡσαύτως δὲ καὶ ὁ σκορπίος ἐκείνῃ [En Argei de phasi ginesthai akridos tinos genos, ho kaleitai skorpiomachon; hotan gar idē tachista skorpion, anthistatai autō, hōsautōs de kai ho skorpios ekeinē]: <i>"In</i> Argos <i>they say there is a kind of locust, called scorpiomachus, which, as soon as it sees a scorpion, rises against it, as the scorpion in turn against it"</i> <sup>R20</sup>. See the rest there, and in <i>Stobaeus</i>, chapter 98, which is <i>On Diseases</i>, where he cites these very words, though not from <i>Aristotle</i> but from <i>Trophilus.</i>

‎Ἀκρὶς [Akris] too, the name common to all locusts, is taken in the same passage of <i>Leviticus</i> for a particular species. Because κὰρ [kar] or κάρη [karē] is <i>head</i>, some think it is called ἀκρὶς [akris] as if ἀκαρὶς [akaris], either from a small or from a large head, according as the prefixed <i>Alpha</i> has privative or intensive force. Others derive it παρὰ τὸ ἄκρας τῶν ἀσταχύων καὶ τῶν φυτῶν νέμεσθαι [para to akras tōn astachyōn kai tōn phytōn nemesthai]. So the <i>Etymologicum.</i> But for <i>Eustathius</i> ἀκρὶς [akris] is as if ἀγρὶς [agris], from γράω [graō], that is, ἐσθίω [esthiō]. Since these are rather far-fetched, it seems more probable to me [III, 265] that ἡ ἀκρίς [hē akris] is named ὀνοματοποιητικῶς [onomatopoiētikōs] from its chirping. In its fight with the scorpion the locust κύκλῳ περιϊοῦσα τρύζει περὶ αὐτὸν [kyklō periiousa tryzei peri auton], <i>"going round about it, chirps around it."</i> So the <i>Philosopher</i> has it in the passage just cited.

‎These are the species of locusts which the sacred translators mention. Many others could be added to them, drawn from the storehouses of the <i>Greeks</i>, if that were the matter in hand. For example, in <i>Aristophanes</i> the τετραπτερυλλίδες [tetrapterullides], so called from their four wings. And [col. 450] the πάρνοπες [parnopes] of the <i>Attic</i> countryside, from whose expulsion, as <i>Pausanias</i> relates in his <i>Attica</i>, <i>Apollo Parnopius</i> was named; just as the κόρνοπες [kornopes] of the <i>Oetaeans</i> gave its name to <i>Hercules Kornopion</i> in <i>Strabo</i>, book 13. And the μάσακες [masakes] and μολουρίδες [molourides] in <i>Nicander's Theriaca</i>, lines 416 and 802. And in <i>Theocritus, Idyll</i> 10, μάντις ἁ καλαμαία [mantis ha kalamaia], which others call ἀρουραία [arouraia], <i>the prophetess of the reeds</i>, or <i>of the fields.</i> So too in <i>Cyprus</i> the <i>Cerynetae</i> called <i>small cicadas</i> καλαμίνδας [kalamindas], as <i>Hesychius</i> attests under Καλαμίς [Kalamis]. But the <i>locust</i> was called not only καλαμαία [kalamaia] but μάντις καλαμαία [mantis kalamaia], because it foretells barrenness to the reeds, that is, the crops. So in <i>Arabic</i> a kind of locust is called جندب [jundab] <i>gindab</i>, which takes its name from جدب [jadb] <i>gidab</i>, that is, <i>a barren year.</i> To these must be added the <i>tryxalis</i>, of which Pliny speaks in book 30, chapter 6: <i>There is an animal like a locust, without wings, which is called</i> TRYXALIS <i>in Greek.</i> It <i>has no Latin name</i>, etc. (I would prefer <i>troxalis</i>, τρωξαλὶς [trōxalis], as it is written by Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 6, chapter 19.) And <i>Dioscorides'</i> ἀσίρακος [asirakos], in the chapter <i>On locusts</i> in book 2 (where some copies wrongly have ἡ ῥιξαχόσκη· [hē rhixachoskē]): a species of locust around <i>Leptis in Africa</i>, ἄπτερος [apteros] and μεγαλόκωλος [megalokōlos], <i>without wings and with long legs</i>, which in <i>Greek</i> is called ὄνος [onos], <i>ass</i>, but in the native tongue of the inhabitants (that is, in <i>Punic</i>) ἀσίρακος [asirakos] <sup>R21</sup>. From this, by the transposition of a single letter, came the <i>Talmudists'</i> אסקרא <i>askra</i>, or <i>iskra</i>, a kind of locust of which the doctors treat in the tractate <i>Cholin</i>, ch. 3, fol. 65a: מאי זחל אמר רבי אסקרא. <i>What is</i> zachal? Rabbi <i>said: Ascra.</i> That is, it is the same kind of locust as the <i>ascra.</i> And the <i>Gloss</i>: זחל מין חגב בלשון ארמי אסקרא ואין לו קרצולים בקטנותו וגדלים לאחר זמן, ZACHAL <i>is a species of locust, called</i> ASKRA <i>in Syriac, which does not have long legs while it is small, but in time they grow.</i> Then indeed it becomes μεγαλόκωλος [megalokōlos], as <i>Dioscorides</i> has it. Yet it is slow in its gait, whence it is called זחל by the <i>Hebrews</i> and ὄνος [onos] by the <i>Greeks</i>. In the <i>Arabic-Syriac-Latin Thesaurus of Thomas a Novaria</i>, for ܐܣܩܪܐ [אסקרא] <i>askra</i> is wrongly written ܐܣܩܕܐ [אסקדא] <i>askda</i>, and it is rendered in <i>Arabic</i> المذنب [al-mudhannab], <i>the tailed locust.</i> [III, 266] In <i>Hesychius</i> too there are many names of locusts: such as Ἄχηρον· ἀκρίδα, Κρῆτες [Achēron; akrida, Krētes]. (But in <i>Suidas</i> Ἀχήρας [Achēras], or ἀχώρας [achōras], is a kind of <i>worm</i>.) Βρέττανα φοβερὰ, ἀκρὶς μικρὰ ὑπὸ Κρητῶν [Brettana phobera, akris mikra hypo Krētōn]. Likewise, Θυλακοθρὼξ, μῦς, οἱ δὲ ἀκρίς· [Thylakothrōx, mys, hoi de akris], as if it gnawed sacks just as a mouse does. Likewise, Πετηλὶς, ἀκρίς· [Petēlis, akris], from the leaves which it consumes: for πέτηλον [petēlon] is a <i>leaf.</i> Likewise, Ὄρπας· τῆς ἀκρίδος ὁ γόνος, ἔνθεν γὰρ ὀπυίουσιν [Orpas; tēs akridos ho gonos, enthen gar opyiousin] (I read γάρον ποιοῦσιν [garon poiousin]), <i>Orpas, the offspring of the locust, from which they make garum:</i> namely, to drive them away or to destroy them. That this emendation is correct, gather from these words of <i>Democritus</i> in the <i>Geoponica</i>, book 13, chapter 1: Ἀποδιώξεις τὰς ἀκρίδιας, ἐὰν, γάρον ἐξ αὐτῶν σκευάσας, βόθρους ὀρύξῃς, καὶ ταύτας ἐγκαταβρέξῃς τῷ γάρῳ. μεθ᾽ ἡμέρας γὰρ ἐπελθὼν, εὑρήσεις αὐτὰς ἐν τοῖς βόθροις ὕπνῳ βεβαρημένας [Apodiōxeis tas akridias, ean, garon ex autōn skeuasas, bothrous oryxēs, kai tautas enkatabrexēs tō garō. meth' hēmeras gar epelthōn, heurēseis autas en tois bothrois hypnō bebarēmenas]. <i>You will drive away the locusts if, having made garum from them, you dig trenches and soak them with that garum. For when you go there after some days, you will find them in the trenches, heavy with sleep.</i> To the ORPAS of <i>Hesychius</i> the <i>Arabs'</i> عرفان [ʿurfān] <i>orphan</i> alludes, a kind of <i>locust</i> near <i>Medina</i>, of which above <sup>c)</sup>).

‎Since, then, it has so far been established that both the <i>Greeks</i> and the <i>Arabs</i> had various words by which they distinguished the kinds of <i>locusts</i> from one another, and since the same could be shown without any trouble for the <i>Chaldeans</i> and <i>Syrians</i>, why should we not believe that the <i>Hebrews</i> too designated different kinds of them by those different words whose origins we have laid open? Yet no one should expect definitions of each one, and the distinguishing marks (διακριτικὰς [diakritikas]) by which they differ from one another. The <i>Talmudic</i> doctors, it is true, discuss this matter at length in the tractate <i>Cholin</i>, ch. 3, fol. 65 <i>a. b.</i> and 66 <i>a.</i> But, as is their custom, they do so in words so obscure and intricate that hardly anything certain can be drawn from them. Such is what they profess to have learned from their masters: ארבה זה גובאי סלעם זה רשון [col. 451] חרגול זה ניפול חגב זה גדיאן, ARBE, <i>that is</i>, gobai; SOLAM, <i>that is</i>, rason; CHARGOL, <i>that is</i>, niphul; CHAGAB, <i>that is</i>, gadjan. The unknown explained by the more unknown. Again, because one reads in <i>Moses</i>, ARBE <i>after its kind</i>, SOLAM <i>after its kind</i>, CHARGOL <i>after its kind</i>, CHAGAB <i>after its kind</i>, they assign to each a kindred species. Thus the <i>locust</i> called צפורת כרמים <i>tsipporeth cheramim</i>, that is, <i>bird of the vineyards</i>, is said to be kindred to the <i>arbe</i>; and יוחנא ירושלמית, the <i>jochana of Jerusalem</i>, to the <i>solam</i>; and ערצוביא <i>artsubia</i>, to the <i>chargol</i>; and הרזבית <i>harzabith</i>, to the <i>chagab</i>. And after many circumlocutions they are noted to differ in this: that the <i>chargol</i> has גבחת וזנב, <i>a hump and a tail</i>; the <i>arbe</i> neither; the <i>solam</i> a hump but no tail; the <i>chagab</i> a tail without a hump. The same is asserted of the four kindred species. In the same place clean locusts are said to have four marks: ארבעה רגלים וארבע כנפים וקרצולים וכנפיו חופים את [III, 267] רובו, namely <i>four feet</i>, <i>four wings</i>, <i>longer legs</i>, <i>and wings that cover the whole body</i>. And the הצרצור, the <i>cricket</i>, is said to have these four marks and yet not to be clean, because the fifth mark is lacking, namely a longer head. See the rest there. Although <i>R. Selomo</i> not only read these things carefully but also illustrated them with notes, like the rest of the body of the <i>Talmud</i>, yet in his <i>Notes</i> on <i>Leviticus</i> he professes that he does not know the kinds of locusts. His words are אין אנו בקיאין בהם, <i>We are not versed in them</i>. And again, אין אנו יודעין להבדיל ביניהון, <i>We do not know how to distinguish them from one another</i>. Nor are the ancient versions of any help here. For the <i>Chaldee</i>, <i>Syriac</i>, <i>Arabic</i> and <i>Samaritan</i> translators either retain the <i>Hebrew</i> words or use others that are no better known. Nor is it sufficiently known, in the <i>Greek</i> and <i>Vulgate</i> versions, how the <i>locust</i>, <i>bruchus</i>, <i>attacus</i> and <i>ophiomachus</i> differ from one another; and much less whether the <i>Greek</i> and <i>Latin</i> words correspond accurately to the <i>Hebrew</i>. Yet that this was not unknown to the <i>Jews</i> down to the times of <i>Christ</i> is gathered from the fact that <i>John the Baptist</i> ate locusts, namely those whose eating he knew to be permitted in the Law of <i>Moses</i>. And this could not have been known otherwise than from the words of the <i>Prophet</i> rightly explained. But when, some time after <i>Christ</i>, <i>Jerusalem</i> was destroyed, the <i>Jews</i>, scattered here and there, so forgot their language that there are some things in the Law itself that are entirely unknown to the most learned of them.

‎Therefore, since it is not possible to inquire more precisely into the kinds of locusts, how much better it is here to confess our ignorance frankly than to be carried off the path with a very famous translator, who rendered the words of the Law thus, <i>Lev 11:20–21. 22. Every flying creeping thing that goes on four feet shall be an abomination to you. Yet this you shall eat of every flying creeping thing that goes on four feet: that which does not have legs above its feet with which to leap upon the earth. Of these you shall eat these: the locust after its kind, and the silkworm after its kind, and the beetle after its kind, and the cicada after its kind.</i> In this version there are many errors. First, in verse twenty-one, it changes an affirmative into a negative, against the most evident meaning of the Law. For in verse twenty the eating of every insect that flies and goes on four feet is forbidden. But in verse twenty-one an exception is made for flying insects which, besides four feet, have two leaping legs, such as the four kinds of locust named in verse twenty-two. Yet the translator proceeds as if, of the creeping things, or flying four-footed insects, the Law permitted only those that do not have those leaping legs. On that assumption, why does he at once add, <i>you shall eat the locust</i>, which everyone knows has those larger legs? Moreover, why are the <i>bombyx</i> [silkworm] and the <i>cantharus</i> [beetle], or <i>scarabaeus</i> [scarab], placed in this list? The former does not fly as long as it remains a silkworm; and neither is four-footed, neither [col. 452] has been used as food by any nation, much less by the <i>Jews</i>, whose cleanliness in eating was of the highest. We know that in <i>Ethiopia</i> and elsewhere there were ἀκριδοφάγους [akridophagous] and ὀφιοφάγους [ophiophagous]; [III, 268] but there is no one who has mentioned τῶν βομβυκοφάγων [tōn bombykophagōn] or τῶν κανθαροφάγων [tōn kantharophagōn]. Human nature has always shrunk from such foul and fetid foods. So that most learned man ought not to have set them on <i>Jewish</i> tables, and that as though on God's authority. I will say, however, why he rendered these words, אשר לו כרעים ממעל לרגליו, <i>which has legs above its feet</i>, with an added negation, <i>which does not have</i>, etc. It is well known that certain words of the <i>Prophets</i> are read otherwise than they are written. Among these is the little word לו, which the <i>Masoretes</i> note is written לא in fifteen places and read לו; of which the former means <i>not</i>, the latter <i>to it</i>. So in this place, while the text writes אשר לא כרעים, <i>which has not legs</i>, the margin has אשר לו כרעים, <i>which has legs</i>. It is certain that this reading must be followed, both from the whole sequence of the text and from the agreement of the translators. For all the <i>Hebrews</i> read thus. Nor do the <i>Greeks</i>, <i>Jerome</i>, the two <i>Chaldees</i> (<i>Jonathan</i> and <i>Onkelos</i>), the <i>Syriac</i>, the four <i>Arabic</i> translators (whom I was able to consult, two printed, and two in manuscript in <i>Sweden</i>), the <i>Samaritan</i>, etc., follow anything else, not to mention that the <i>Hebrew copy of the Samaritans</i> has in the text itself לו with <i>Vav</i>, not with <i>Aleph</i> <sup>R22</sup>.

‎<i>Moses</i> describes the clean locusts as flying and four-footed. Flying, to set them against the ἀπτέροις [apterois], such as the <i>attelabi</i> in <i>Pliny</i> and the <i>asiracus</i> in <i>Dioscorides</i> <sup>R23</sup>. The <i>Talmudists</i> assign them four wings. Hence it is that in <i>Greek</i> they are called τετραπτερυλλίδες [tetrapterullides], that is, <i>four-winged</i>. <i>Aristophanes</i> in the <i>Acharnians</i>:

‎Ἀλλ᾽ εἴ τι βούλει πρίασο τῶν ἐγὼ φέρω, Τῶν ὀρταλίχων, ἢ τῶν τετραπτερυλλίδων· [All᾽ ei ti boulei priaso tōn egō pherō, Tōn ortalichōn, ē tōn tetrapterullidōn]

‎<i>But buy, if you want any, of the things that I am carrying, cocks or four-wings.</i> And the old <i>Scholiast</i>, and from him <i>Suidas</i>, explain this word: Τετραπτερυλλίδων [Tetrapterylidōn]· τῶν ἀκρίδων [tōn akridōn]· παρὰ τὸ τέσσαρα ἔχειν πτερά [para to tessara echein ptera]. The two words τῶν ἀκρίδων [tōn akridōn], omitted in <i>Suidas</i>, are to be supplied from the <i>Scholiast</i>. You will be surprised, however, that <i>Moses</i> attributes only four feet to locusts, since others count six. <i>Aristotle</i>, in <i>De Partibus</i>, book 4, chapter 6, says this of locusts, gnats, etc.: Ἑξάποδα δὲ τὰ τοιαῦτα [III, 269] πάντα εἰσί [Hexapoda de ta toiauta panta eisi]. <i>But all these have six feet.</i> And <i>Damir</i> the Arab: ولجرادة ستة ارجل [wa-l-jarāda sitta arjul] <i>And the locust has six feet.</i> But these are easily reconciled. Of the locust's six feet, the two hindmost, which are longer, are fitted not for walking but for leaping. Accordingly <i>Moses</i> calls them <i>legs</i>, not <i>feet</i>: <i>You shall eat</i>, he says, <i>of every flying creeping thing that goes upon four feet, which has</i> כרעים ממעל לרגליו, <i>legs above its feet</i> (that is, <i>besides its feet</i>), <i>with which to leap upon the earth.</i> <i>Augustine</i>, therefore, in <i>Retractations</i>, book 2, chapter 15, observes that these flying creatures are called four-footed in the Law, because the Law <i>does not count among the feet the two hind legs with which locusts leap.</i> But <i>the Philosopher</i>, who calls them ἑξάποδα [hexapoda], expressly notes that among those six feet he also counts the parts by whose effort they leap: Ἑξάποδα δὲ τὰ τοιαῦτα πάντα εἰσὶ, σὺν τοῖς ἁλτικοῖς μορίοις [Hexapoda de ta toiauta panta eisi, syn tois haltikois moriois]. In these words it is plain that by ἁλτικὰ μόρια [haltika moria], <i>leaping parts</i>, are meant those two legs which <i>Moses</i> writes the locust has, לנתר בהן על הארץ, <i>to leap with them upon the earth</i> <sup>R24</sup>. <i>The Philosopher</i> calls them πηδάλια [pēdalia] in <i>History</i>, book 4, chapter 9, where we read αἱ ἀκρίδες τοῖς πηδαλίοις τρίβουσαι ποιεῖν τὸν ψόφον [hai akrides tois pēdaliois tribousai poiein ton psophon], <i>that locusts produce their sound by rubbing with their pedalia</i> (or <i>by the friction of their pedalia</i>). And the great <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] renders it <i>leaping legs</i>, as if they were so called ἀπὸ τοῦ πηδᾷν [apo tou pēdan], <i>from leaping.</i> Rather, πηδάλια [pēdalia] are <i>rudders</i>, by which name they are rightly called because they are [col. 453] πηδαλιώδη [pēdaliōdē], that is, <i>they imitate the shape of rudders.</i> This explanation is not ours but that of <i>the Philosopher</i> himself, who speaks thus in <i>De partibus</i>, book 4, chapter 6: Οὐκ ἔμπροσθεν δὲ, ἀλλ᾽ ὄπισθεν μόνον ἔχουσιν αἱ ἀκρίδες τὰ πηδαλιώδη [Ouk emprosthen de, all᾽ opisthen monon echousin hai akrides ta pēdaliōdē]. <i>Locusts have parts formed in the shape of rudders not in front, but only behind.</i> Indeed, just as locusts have two legs of this kind, so many ships of old had two rudders. <i>Aelian</i>, <i>Varia</i>, book 9, chapter 40, expressly asserts this of the ships of the <i>Carthaginians</i>: Δύο μὲν πηδάλια ἔχειν [Dyo men pēdalia echein], and accordingly also δύο κυβερνήτας [dyo kybernētas]. Hence it is that in <i>Heliodorus</i>, <i>Aethiopica</i>, book 5, <i>Theagenes</i> and <i>Chariclea</i>, carried in a <i>Tyrian</i> ship, put in at the <i>Cretan</i> promontory of <i>Ithaca</i>, τῶν πηδαλίων θάτερον ἀποβαλόντες [tōn pēdaliōn thateron apobalontes], <i>having lost one of the two rudders.</i> And of <i>Paul</i>'s ship <i>Luke</i> said in the plural τὰς ζευκτηρίας τῶν πηδαλίων [tas zeuktērias tōn pēdaliōn], <i>the bands of the rudders</i>, Acts 27:40. On these words I do not quite approve what the very learned commentator says: <i>He said rudders in the plural for the singular, since one ship does not have several rudders.</i> For we have proved that the ships of the <i>Phoenicians</i> (or, what is the same, the <i>Punic peoples</i>) were furnished with a double rudder. Such was <i>Paul</i>'s ship, if the <i>Vulgate</i> reads correctly at <i>Acts 27:2. Embarking on a ship of Adrumetum.</i> And in the <i>Ethiopic</i> ሐመር፡ አድሩመጠቂት፡ [ḥamar ʾadərumaṭaqit] <i>chamar Adrumatavith</i>, that is, <i>a ship from Adrumetum</i>, a very well-known <i>Punic</i> city on the coast of <i>Africa</i>. [III, 270] Or, if it was πλοῖον Ἀδραμυττηνὸν [ploion Adramyttēnon], <i>from Adramyttium in Mysia</i>, as the <i>Greek</i> has it, still it need not have had only one rudder. For this custom spread from the <i>Punic peoples</i> to others as well. Hence <i>Hyginus</i> on the ship <i>Argo</i>: <i>It has at the stern, at each of the rudders, at the first five stars, at the other four.</i> And <i>Germanicus</i> on the same: <i>Which, marked</i> (read <i>changed</i>), Pallas <i>figured in the sky, but from the rudders up to the mast.</i> And in <i>Apuleius</i>, <i>The Ass</i>, book 2, <i>Diophanes</i> on a ship, <i>Greek</i> as it seems, sunk off <i>Euboea</i>: <i>The very ship in which we were carried, having lost both its steering gear, sank headlong. Both its steering gear</i>, that is, <i>having lost both helms</i>: which escaped <i>Beroaldus</i>. <i>Petronius</i> too more than once speaks of the <i>rudders</i> of the <i>Tarentine</i> ship in the plural. And <i>Oppian</i>, in <i>Halieutica</i>, book 1, said that pilot fish leap around both sides of the ship <i>and the reins of the rudders, which are around toward the stern</i>,

‎— — — περί τε πρυμναῖα χαλινὰ Οἰήκων. [peri te prymnaia chalina Oiēkōn.] — — — — —

‎But see more in the very learned book of <i>the distinguished Scheffer</i>, <i>De militia navali</i>. Not without reason, therefore, does <i>the Philosopher</i> compare to the two rudders of ships those twin legs of the locusts that we have mentioned, by which they in some way steer their motion. For when these are extended, they are lifted up either for a leap or for flight. Indeed, as is stated in the same place, ὅταν κάμψαντ᾽ ἐκτείνῃ πάλιν, ἀναγκαῖον ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς ἤρθαι [hotan kampsant' ekteinē palin, anankaion apo tēs gēs ērthai]: <i>When they extend again what they have bent, they must be lifted from the ground.</i> Accordingly <i>Pisidas</i> compares them to a bow, which is drawn back to shoot arrows. His words are:

‎Καὶ συλλέγει τὰ κῶλα, καὶ τείνει πάλιν, Ὥσπερ τὰ νεῦρα τῶν βελῶν οἱ τοξόται· Καὶ τραυματοῖ τὸ σῶμα τῶν ἐναντίων Τοῖς ἀντιλόξοις τῶν σκελῶν ἀκοντίοις. [Kai syllegei ta kōla, kai teinei palin, Hōsper ta neura tōn belōn hoi toxotai; Kai traumatoi to sōma tōn enantiōn Tois antiloxois tōn skelōn akontiois.]

‎<i>And it draws in its limbs</i> (that is, those hind legs) <i>and stretches them out again, as archers do the strings of their arrows; and it wounds the body of its enemies with the slanting darts of its legs.</i> What these verses mean entirely escaped the very learned translator, who first rendered the opening thus: <i>Now it gathers corpses, now it scatters them.</i> And in the last verse he wants to read τῶν βελῶν [tōn belōn] instead of τῶν σκελῶν [tōn skelōn].

**Notes**

‎R1 *(p. 252)* That the name ארבה is sometimes used generally and comprises every kind of locust, and sometimes denotes a particular species, as in Joel 1:4. Ps 78:46, where it is distinguished from the other names of locusts and is mentioned only in second place, has been shown by Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde] (<i>Vermischte Sammlungen</i>, fasc. 2, ch. 7, part 2, p. 91. 92.) and by Ol. Gerh. Tychsen in his commentary on the locusts mentioned in the O. T., which is appended to Ignacio de Asso's little book on locusts, translated by him from Spanish into German, p. 88 (with this title: <i>Des Don</i> IGNACIO DE ASSO Y DEL RIO, <i>Königl. Spanischen General-Consuls in Holland, Abhandlung von den Heuschrecken und ihren Vertilgungsmitteln, aus dem Spanischen übersetzt, und mit einem Anhang von den biblischen Heuschrecken begleitet, von</i> OLUF GERH. TYCHSEN, Rostock 1787, 8vo). The same had long before been the opinion of Ed. Pococke [=Edward Pococke, Commentary on Hosea (1685)] in his <i>Commentary on Joel</i>, ch. 1, v. 4 (translated from English into Latin, Leipzig 1695, p. 7): "The word <i>Arbeh</i>," he says, "which we find placed here as though it were the name of a single species, we have in Lev 11:22 as though comprehending various species under it; whence you may rightly conjecture that this little word is sometimes used very generally, sometimes quite specifically, وقد يطلق الواحد منها علي الجميع مجازا [wa-qad yuṭlaqu al-wāḥid minhā ʿalī al-jamīʿ majāzan], <i>the name of one species being used</i> (as R. Tanchum puts it) <i>for all.</i>" Thus Pococke. If, however, ארבה is the name of a species, not only Oedmann but also Tychsen, loc. cit., holds that it signifies the <i>Gryllus gregarius</i> of Forsskål [=Peter Forsskål, Descriptiones animalium (1775)] (<i>Descript. Animall.</i> p. 81.). They think the Hebrew name was given to this species from רבה, <i>to be much</i>, because this species occurs in great numbers and migrates in swarms. I add Forsskål's words: "The <i>Gryllus gregarius</i> the Arabs everywhere call <i>Djerâd</i>, جيراد [jīrād], and the Jews living in Yemen asserted that it is the ארבה. — — At every step I stirred up whole swarms, so great was their number in these places at that time." ארבה is also the general name of the locust for J. D. Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer], in <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 6, p. 2228., but in Lev 11:22, where it is distinguished from חרגל, סלעם and חגב, it seems to him to be the locust that begins to fly after its first moult, whereas before that it creeps and lacks wings. But this opinion, which is supported neither by the agreement of the Arabic and Syriac dialects, nor by the authority of the ancient translators, nor by etymology, has been refuted at length by Tychsen (p. 62 ff. of the book cited) and Oedmann (p. 87 ff. and p. 122 ff.).

‎R2 *(p. 253)* To Michaelis, in <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 1, p. 249, גובי seems from its etymology to be the locust first creeping out of the earth, i.e. in its first stage, which is followed by four moults of the skin: the locust-worm that has not yet shed its skin. Against this opinion stands the agreement of the ancient translators, and the passage Nahum III, 17., where winged or full-grown locusts must be meant, as Tychsen rightly points out, p. 93; he himself interprets גובי as the <i>gryllus migratorius</i> of Linnaeus, but on what grounds he does not explain.

‎R3 *(p. 254)* משוטא מתאמר קמצא שאפא, <i>Moschuto is the name of the wingless locust</i>: this Hyde quotes from a certain Syriac lexicon in his notes on <i>Peritsol's Itinera mundi</i>, p. 125. It is clear that the <i>bruchus</i> is meant, that is, the young hatched from the locust's egg before it has wings. Nor, it seems, should the καμπη [kampē] of the Greek translators be interpreted otherwise. Michaelis, in <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 1, p. 290, connects ܡܫܘܛܐ [משוטא] with the Arabic مشط [mashaṭa], <i>he combed, he plundered,</i> but Tychsen, p. 86, with the Chaldee שוט, <i>to roam about.</i>

‎R4 *(p. 254)* Michaelis, however, also defends the meaning <i>eruca</i> [caterpillar], loc. cit.: "Notable," he says, "and classic for this opinion is the passage of Amos in which גזם is an insect harmful to vineyards, olive groves and fig trees." But locusts are no less harmful to such crops, as we shall see below, Chapter IV. And that גזם was rightly rendered <i>bruchus</i> by the Chaldee and the Syriac (see note 3.) I infer from the fact that in Joel 1:4 גזם is placed before the other names of locusts, as the one that begins the devastation of the fields, which suits the <i>bruchus</i> best. The most fitting sense of the Hebrew words יתר הגזם אכל הארבה here will therefore be: <i>what the bruchus left, the gryllus gregarius ate</i> (see note 1 above). Oedmann, too, is wholly for the <i>bruchus</i>, fasc. 2, ch. 6, p. 116 ff., and at the same time soundly refutes the opinion of those who take גזם for the <i>eruca</i> [caterpillar]. Tychsen, p. 88, interprets גזם as the <i>gryllus cristatus</i> of Linnaeus, on the authority of <i>Abrah. Iaphe</i>, a Jew of Safed.

‎R5 *(p. 255)* For Michaelis in the <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 3, p. 667 f., חגב is the fourth kind of locust, i.e. the locust of the fourth and last stage, named from حجب [ḥjb], <i>to veil</i>, because, once it flies, it <i>veils the sun and the sky.</i> Tychsen, however, p. 76 ff., considers חגב to be the same locust as the <i>gryllus coronatus</i> Linn., which received its name from <i>veiling</i> on account of the length of its wings. Since חגב is sometimes used in figurative speech to express the worthlessness and smallness of something (see Num 13:34. Esaj. XL, 22.), to Oedmann, p. 90, 91, it seems to be some smaller species of locust.

‎R6 *(p. 255)* R. Jonah [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim], or Abu'l-Walid, renders חנמל in the same way in his unpublished Lexicon, as Schnurrer [=C. F. Schnurrer, Dissertationes philologico-criticae (1790)] reports in his Dissertation <i>on Psalm 78</i> (which is included in the <i>Commentatt. Theologg.</i> edited <i>by Velthusen [=J. C. Velthusen, in Symbolae litterariae Kilonienses], Kuinoel and Ruperti</i>, vol. 1, p. 76 f.), in the note on v. 47.

‎R7 *(p. 256)* Michaelis in the <i>Supplemm.</i> p. 846 holds that חנמל are <i>larger ants</i>, a quadriliteral formed from נמלה, <i>ant</i>, with the letter Chet prefixed, which (to use Schnurrer's words, loc. cit.) we fear will not win the approval of many. Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791) and Clavis über den Jesaias (1793)] in the <i>Clavis Psalmorum</i> p. 198 suspects that two words, חן, compare Arabic حُنَن [ḥunan] <i>dung beetle,</i> and מל, compare مُلل [mulal] <i>dried out by heat</i>, have been joined into the single word חנמל.

‎R8 *(p. 256)* Michaelis in the <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 3, p. 862 ff. thinks the meaning <i>locust</i> must be rejected chiefly because חסיל is distinguished from ארבה in I Reg. VIII, 37. Joel 1:4. II, 25. But what if different species of locusts are designated by these words? And that this is truly so appears from Ps 78:46, where חסיל is parallel to ארבה, the best-known name of the <i>locust</i>, and from Isa 33:4, where חסילים correspond to גבים in the other member and are said to be gathered together with them, as we shall explain more fully below on Chapter 2. Michaelis himself, however, wavers between the <i>gryllo-talpa</i>, or <i>cricket with mole's feet</i> (in German <i>Maulwurfs-Grille, Rietwurm</i>), which is destructive to crops, and the <i>bruchus</i>, which would be called חסיל, <i>adult</i>, from חסל, <i>to complete</i>, transferred to adult age. For the <i>gryllo-talpa</i> he adduces the Syriac translator, who in Joel has ܢܘܣܠܐ [נוסלא], <i>cricket</i> (which, however, Michaelis refers to the <i>gryllo-talpa</i> without adequate reason); for the <i>bruchus</i>, the Vulgate, the Alexandrian translator at 2 Chr 6:28, and Aquila at Ps 78:46. Michaelis's opinion about the <i>gryllo-talpa</i> is refuted at length by Oedmann, fasc. 2, ch. 6, p. 138 ff., for whom חסיל is the <i>cimex Aegyptius</i> Linn., on the authority of the Arabic translator, who puts قمل [qaml], which he takes to be the name of the <i>bug</i>. Tychsen, p. 91, 92, interprets חסיל as the <i>gryllus verrucivorus</i> Linn. (Syst. Nat. vol. 1, part 4, p. 2067), relying on the meaning of the verb חסל, <i>he consumed entirely.</i> How uncertain this is anyone will see for himself, since this etymology fits almost all species of locusts. Paulus in the <i>Clavis Psalmorum</i> p. 197 thinks that חסיל is a <i>caterpillar</i> <i>that has crept out of the nymph</i>, or larva, [III, 257] compare Arabic خشل [khshl], <i>he rejected it as rather worthless</i>, a garment <i>was worn out</i>. Why I think the <i>locust</i> is to be understood I have already said above; but the species no one could determine today.

‎R9 *(p. 257)* For the Arabs حرجل [ḥarjal] is not only a <i>swarm of locusts</i>, but also a <i>wingless kind of locust</i>, <i>Gryllus</i> <i>onos</i> or <i>papus</i>, Linn. (Syst. Nat. vol. 1, part 4, p. 2068, 69), the same that, on the testimony of Dioscorides, was called ἀσίρακος [asirakos] around Leptis in Africa (עסקרא, عسكر [ʿaskar], <i>pedestrian</i>); see Bochart below, p. 450. Adderaihem in Casiri, <i>Biblioth. Arab. Hisp.</i> vol. 1, p. 320 a, confirms the same. Compare Hyde in the <i>Notes on Peritsol's Itinera mundi</i>, p. 125. Damir, however, does not even have the name حرجل [ḥarjal], as Tychsen reports, p. 73. Michaelis in the <i>Supplemm.</i> p. 911 thinks the <i>wingless kind of locust</i> is no different from <i>locusts not yet winged</i>, a strange error, since there are several species of locusts that lack wings entirely, such as <i>Gryllus papus</i>, <i>spinulosus</i>, <i>pedestris</i>, <i>onos</i>, Linn. From the sequence of the passage Lev 11:22, חרגל for Michaelis is the <i>locust not yet winged, after the third shedding of its skin</i>. He thinks the name חרגל is compounded from the Samaritan רגולה, <i>pedestrian</i>, and the Arabic خرج [kharaja], <i>he went out</i>, so that by etymology חרגל would be the <i>pedestrian locust</i> (creeping on the ground, as opposed to the winged one) that has <i>come out</i> of its skins after the third shedding. For Tychsen, p. 74, חרגל is the <i>gryllus gurges</i> of Asso, related to the gryllus verrucivorus Linn. Mere conjectures!

‎a) *(p. 258)* <i>p. 443.</i>

‎R10 *(p. 258)* Michaelis, in the <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 4, p. 1080, on the authority of the Alexandrian and the Vulgate translators, interprets ילק as <i>bruchus</i>. He says that the <i>bruchus</i> is a little grub that creeps under the ground and ravages roots with its bite (in German <i>Engerling</i>), which afterwards becomes winged and is called by us <i>Maykäfer</i>, after the month of May. But for Jerome, Augustine and Remigius of Auxerre the <i>bruchus</i> is <i>the young of the locust</i>, as is clear from what Bochart cites below, p. 447 (marginal note). The Greek βροῦκος [broukos] or βρῦχος [brychos], moreover, signifies now <i>the young of the locust</i>, now <i>a certain species of locusts</i>. And at Jer 51:27 the Alexandrian translators expressly put ἀκρίδα [akrida], the best-known name of the <i>locust</i>, for ילק. The Chaldee and the Syriac also understood a <i>locust</i>: the former renders it פרחא, <i>winged locust</i>, the latter ܣܡܐܢ ܠܐ ܦܪܚ [סמאן לא פרח], <i>locust not yet winged</i>. So far, then, is Bochart from assuming at his pleasure that ילק is a <i>locust</i>, as Michaelis asserts, that it is rather Michaelis himself who deserves censure, since he departed without reason from the authority of the ancients. Michaelis is refuted at length by Oedmann, Fasc. 2, Chapter 6, p. 126 ff., who himself takes ילק for the <i>gryllus cristatus</i> Linn. (Syst. Nat. vol. 1, part 4, p. 2074), common in those lands. He is moved chiefly by Jer 51:27, where ילק is called סמר, which Oedmann interprets as <i>serratus</i> [serrated] and refers to the four-cleft keel of the thorax. But Tychsen, p. 91, renders the same סמר as <i>horripilans</i> [bristling], which he considers an epithet of the <i>gryllus haematopus</i> Linn. (p. 2075), with its hairy-fringed thighs. Bochart will give more on ילק סמר below, Chapter 2.

‎b) *(p. 259)* <i>Below, ch. 5.</i>

‎R11 *(p. 259)* In the Talmudic tractate חלין <i>Cholin</i>, the סלעם is said to have a <i>bald head</i>, and the female to lack a sword-shaped tail. Since Tychsen finds these marks in the species of locust that Asso places third (and p. 5–6 of the German trans.) and calls <i>gryllus eversor</i>, he is persuaded, p. 69, that the Hebrew סלעם is that very species.

‎R12 *(p. 259)* For Michaelis, in the <i>Supplemm.</i> p. 2094, צלצל is the same as חסיל, the <i>gryllus with mole's feet</i>, the <i>gryllus talpiformis</i>, to which he thinks the name was given from its shrill and sharp sound, following the authority of the Syriac translator, who puts ܨܪܨܘܪ [צרצור], <i>cricket</i>. Against this opinion, however, not a few objections have been raised by Tychsen, p. 79–80, and by Oedmann, Fasc. 2, p. 140 ff., although he did not state his own opinion on צלצל. To Tychsen it seems an onomatopoeic name of the <i>gryllus stridulus</i> Linn. (vol. 1, part 4, p. 2078). The Alexandrians render it ἐρισυβην [erisybēn], which the Vulgate translator rendered <i>rubigo</i> [blight]. The same view was held by Jac. Hase in the <i>Biblioth. Brem.</i> Class 6, Fasc. 5, Diss. 1, § 9, who derives our word from צלה, <i>he roasted</i>, <i>he scorched</i>.

‎R13 *(p. 259)* هذه الاسما كلها قد تكون الاصناف من الجراد [hādhihi al-asmā kulluhā qad takūn al-aṣnāf min al-jarād] <i>all these names signify certain species of locusts</i>, says R. Tanchum in his <i>Commentary on Iocl I, 4.</i>, cited by Edw. Pococke on the same passage, p. 7.

‎R14 *(p. 259)* From this also appears at once the emptiness both of the opinion of Andr. Norrelius (formerly librarian to the King of the Swedes), who, in his <i>Diatriba de avibus esu licitis Lev 11:22</i>, Uppsala 1746, contradicting all the ancients and following no usage of the oriental languages, makes ארבה <i>doves</i>, סלעם <i>geese</i>, חרגל <i>the gallinaceous kind</i>, and חגב <i>sparrows</i>, [III, 260] understanding <i>the four on which they go</i> of the four toes of the feet; and of that of J. D. Michaelis, who, in the <i>Questions proposed to those about to travel to Arabia</i>, p. 84 ff., and in the <i>Supplemm. ad Lexx. [=J. D. Michaelis, Supplementa ad lexica Hebraica] Hebrr.</i>, where he treats the individual names, thinks that not certain species of locusts but four stages of their life are designated, not considering that locusts cannot be eaten before they have begun to fly, to say nothing of the other objections raised against this opinion by Tychsen, p. 62 ff., and Oedmann, Fasc. 2, p. 88 ff., who also refuted Norrelius's fancies at greater length than was necessary.

‎R15 *(p. 260)* Perhaps the <i>Gryllus viridis</i> of Forsskål (<i>Descript. Animall.</i> p. 81), <i>with depressed, fringed thorax, spiny fore legs, and a yellow spot on each elytron.</i> Common in the gardens at Alexandria.

‎R16 *(p. 260)* To these add, from Niebuhr's [=Carsten Niebuhr, Beschreibung von Arabien (1772)] <i>Descript. Arab.</i> p. 171, the <i>red locust, Dsjerâd áchmar</i> [جراد احمر] [jarād aḥmar], a migratory one, which, when it first arrives in the regions of Arabia, is small and lean, but after it has grown, to the great damage of the crops, is called <i>Dsjerâd Mükken</i> [جران مكن] [jarān mkn] [i.e. a locust laden with eggs], and is among the delicacies of the Arabs.

‎R17 *(p. 261)* Among the people of Basra, جراد عصفور [jarād ʿuṣfūr] is the male of the <i>Mukn</i> مكن [mkn], the fertile egg-bearer, on the testimony of Niebuhr, p. 171. I shall quote his own words: <i>At Basra the locust that the Arabs most like to eat was called</i> Mukn. <i>It was further added that this is the female, and very fat, or full of eggs, and that it is a strengthening food for men.</i> Dsjerad asfur <i>is the male of the</i> Mukn, <i>but lean, and is therefore not much used for eating at Basra.</i>

‎R18 *(p. 261)* Among the people of Oman, al-Hasa and Basra, دبا [dbā] is a certain species of locust, small but not edible, as Niebuhr reports, p. 172.

‎R19 *(p. 261)* Other names by which the Arabs are accustomed to distinguish the various stages of the locust's life are reported by Norberg in Letter 10 (in fascicle 2 of volume 6 of Björnståhl's Letters), p. 422. 423, German trans., as received from the mouth of a learned Maronite of Aleppo named <i>Germanus Conti</i>. — 1) <i>Beiad</i> [بياض [bayāḍ]], <i>the locust when it has just been hatched from the egg</i>; 2) <i>Sahaf</i> [زحّاف [zaḥḥāf], <i>creeping</i>, see Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)], <i>Lexic.</i> p. 1079], <i>when it begins to creep</i>; 3) <i>Taiah</i>, i.e. leaping, when it cannot yet fly up high but only springs up from the ground; and finally 4) <i>Tajar</i> [طاير [ṭāyir], <i>flying creature</i>], flying, when it fully begins to fly. — The Chaldee, Syriac and Arabic names of the locust have been carefully collected and briefly explained by Tychsen on <i>Ignacio de Asso</i>, p. 47 ff.

‎R20 *(p. 264)* "Our entomologists," says Beckmann on this passage, "have not yet examined this scorpion-fighter; yet those who know the way in which the insects belonging to the genera called <i>Sphex</i> and <i>Ichneumon</i> lie in wait for the larvae of other insects will agree with me that what our author has reported about it is not altogether far from the truth."

‎R21 *(p. 265)* Dioscorides does not actually say that this is an African name; his words are these: ἡ δε λεγομενη ἀκρις ἀσιρακος, ἡ ὀνος, ἀπτερος ἐτι, καὶ μεγαλυκωλος [hē de legomenē akris asirakos, hē onos, apteros eti, kai megalykōlos]. Nevertheless Bochart's conjecture is not improbable.

‎R22 *(p. 268)* In addition there is a very weighty testimony for the marginal reading: nature itself. For locusts have <i>two legs</i>, כרעים, rising above the four feet, with which they leap on the ground. The marginal reading is also defended by Aurivillius in the dissertation <i>On the Variant Reading of the Words</i> לא <i>and</i> לו <i>in the Biblical Codex</i>, §. 4, p. 472 f. of the Dissertations edited by Michaelis. Tychsen, in the <i>Appendix to Ignacio de Asso</i>, p. 56, note, thinks that the textual reading, לא, arose from the fact that the pronoun of the 3rd person singular masculine, לו, was formerly sometimes written with a paragogic א (לא), and that this difference of spelling is noted in the margin.
