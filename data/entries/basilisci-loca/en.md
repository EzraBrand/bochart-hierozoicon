# Part 2, Book 3, chapter 10: Five passages of Scripture on the basilisk are explained (vol. 3, pp. 187–193)

### ‎[III, 187] [col. 402] CHAPTER X.

### ‎<i>The five passages of Scripture on the basilisk are explained.</i>

‎Now, if we may run through the passages of Scripture about this serpent, the first is <i>Prov.</i> [col. 403] <i>XXIII, 32.</i>, where <i>Solomon</i> says of wine, <i>At the last it bites like a serpent</i>, וכצפעני יפרש, <i>and stings like a tsiphoni</i> (or <i>basilisk</i>). This is not to favour <i>Tatian</i>, the <i>Encratites</i> and the <i>Manichaeans</i>, who called <i>wine</i> the <i>gall of the dragon</i> and the <i>poison of the ancient serpent</i>, and abstained from it even in the Holy Supper. For here it is not the use of wine but its abuse that is condemned. God by wine <i>makes glad the heart of man</i>, Ps 104:15. Indeed wine is said <i>to cheer both God himself and men</i>, <i>Iud. IX, 13.</i>, namely in the sacrifices, in which it was used, <i>Exod 29:40</i> and <i>Num 15:5.</i> Some, however, would have the word אלהים <i>elohim</i> in that passage signify not the <i>true God</i> but <i>judges</i> and <i>magnates</i>, as in <i>Exod 21:6</i> and <i>XXII, 8. 9. 28.</i> and <i>Ps 82:6</i>; to these would be opposed אנשים, <i>men</i>, that is, <i>commoners</i>. In the same way, in <i>Gen 6:2</i> the <i>sons of men</i> are opposed to the <i>sons of God</i>, and in <i>Ps 82:6–7.</i> אדם (that is, <i>man</i>) to <i>gods and sons of the Most High</i>. The sense would then be that by drinking wine rich and poor, princes and subjects, judges and clients are cheered alike. This is exactly what the first of <i>Darius</i>'s bodyguards says of wine in <i>3 Ezra 3:19–20.</i> However that may be, the yield of wine is ascribed to God's goodness and liberality, <i>Jer 31:12</i> and <i>Hos 2:8.</i> And the <i>Israelites</i> are promised <i>the early and the late rain, that they may gather in their grain and wine</i>, Deut 11:14. And a cluster of grapes, brought into the wilderness, served them as a sample of the fertility of the land of <i>Canaan</i>, Num 13:24. Nor should it be said that what had been promised and permitted under the Law was forbidden under the Gospel. For <i>Christ</i>, invited to a wedding, changed water into wine to be offered to the guests, <i>John 2:8.</i> He himself drank wine many times with the <i>Apostles</i>, and at that Last Supper he even consecrated it to be the sacrament of his blood for the whole church. What is more, <i>Paul</i> recommends to <i>Timothy</i> the use of wine <i>for his stomach's sake</i>, 1 Tim. V, 23. But here a thing good in itself is condemned because of the evils of drunkenness. If anyone does not weigh these sufficiently, let him, like the boys of the <i>Lacedaemonians</i>, look at a drunken man: his disordered face, his tottering feet, his stammering tongue, his ungainly gestures, and his reason blinded and moved from its seat. Let him likewise consider the bleary eyes of gluttons, the nose suffused with bile, the rotten teeth, the foul breath, the trembling limbs, and the belly distended with fat; not to mention paralysis, gout, vertigo, convulsions, lethargy, apoplexy and a thousand swarms of diseases. However grave these are, they bring more disgrace than pain, because they are not accidental but self-inflicted, and are ascribed not to the weakness of nature but to the force of intemperance. Moreover, when reason is drowned in drink, the depraved passions are kindled. Hence <i>Solomon</i>, Prov 20:1: <i>Wine is a luxurious thing, and drunkenness</i> [III, 188] <i>riotous: whoever delights in it is not wise.</i> And Prov 23:29: <i>Who has woe? Who has evoë? Who has contentions? Who has babbling? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes? Those who tarry long at wine</i>, etc. And below <sup>a)</sup>: <i>Your eyes shall look upon strange women, and your heart shall utter perverse things.</i> Thus by such cups men are changed into beasts. Some into lions: like <i>Alexander</i>, who killed <i>Clitus</i>, the dearest of his friends, over the cups; and <i>Herod</i>, who, leaving a banquet, consigned a servant of God to death. Others into dogs: like <i>Nabal</i>, who, full of wine, barked at <i>David</i>. Others into swine, who wallow in foul pleasures: like <i>Lot</i>, who, when drunk, defiled himself by incestuous intercourse with his daughters. In short, by other vices the image of God is extinguished in us, but by drunkenness and gluttony even the image of man. Indeed, we are cast down below the cattle, which do not drink except when there is need, nor stir up thirst by art, but follow nature as their guide. Since, then, drunkenness is pernicious in so many ways, wine is not undeservedly [col. 404] said <i>to bite us like a serpent and to sting like a basilisk</i>. <i>Will sting</i> in Hebrew is יפרש, with right-hand <i>Sin</i>. Elsewhere this word means <i>to arrange</i> or to scatter; here it means <i>to sting</i>, from the <i>Chaldee</i> language <sup>R1</sup>, in which פרש <i>peras</i> is an <i>ox-goad</i> and פריש <i>paris</i> is a <i>spur</i>. Hence among the <i>Hebrews</i> too פרש paras is a <i>horseman</i>, who pricks his horse with spurs. But the verb פרש with left-hand <i>Sin</i> means <i>to spread out</i>. With this in view <i>Jerome</i> rendered, <i>As a regulus it will spread poisons</i>, and the <i>Greeks</i>, Ὥσπερ ὑπὸ κεράστου διαχεῖται αὐτοῦ ὁ ἰός [Hōsper hypo kerastou diacheitai autou ho ios]: <i>As by a cerastes its poison is spread</i> <sup>R2</sup>. But the <i>Chaldee</i> and the <i>Syriac</i> have here, <i>As a basilisk</i> דפרח, <i>which flies</i>, as if they had read יפרח for יפרש <sup>R3</sup>. This would be quite absurd, for no one mentions a <i>flying basilisk</i>. Far be it from us, then, to add wings to such a monster, which is so terrible by its creeping alone. According to the first version, <i>At the last it will bite like a serpent, and sting like a basilisk</i>, the two hemistichs are thoroughly ἀντίστροφα [antistropha]. For as <i>serpent</i> and <i>basilisk</i>, so <i>to bite</i> and <i>to sting</i> are nearly the same.

‎[III, 189] That is, a serpent is said <i>to prick with its bite</i>, because it has very sharp teeth. <i>Varro</i> in <i>Priscian</i>: <i>When the asp, brought near, has pricked.</i> Accordingly, the bite of the chersydrus and of the dragon is called νύγμα [nygma] in <i>Nicander's Theriaca</i>, that is, <i>a pricking</i>, at lines 362 and 446; just as in <i>Aelian's History</i>, book 15, chapter 13, the haemorrhous by its bite νύγμα ἐργάζεται [nygma ergazetai], <i>produces a puncture</i>, etc. And the three <i>Arabic</i> verbs لدغ [ladagha] <i>ladaga</i>, لسع [lasaʿa] <i>lasaa</i>, and وكع [wakaʿa] <i>vvachaa</i>, although they properly mean <i>to prick</i> and are often used of the scorpion, nevertheless also apply to the bite of a serpent, because it is a kind of pricking.

‎The second passage, <i>Isa 11:8. The infant shall delight itself over the hole of the asp, and the weaned child puts his hand upon the den of the basilisk</i>, we have already explained earlier <sup>b)</sup> in the chapter <i>On the Asp</i>. Here, therefore, note only that <i>the den of the basilisk</i> is called in <i>Hebrew</i> מאורת צפעוני, and that the word מאורת occurs nowhere else for <i>den</i>. That is why translators struggle with rendering it. The more recent ones, with <i>Jerome</i>, render it <i>den</i>; but the <i>Greeks</i> have κοίτην [koitēn], as the <i>Arabic</i> has مضجع [maḍjaʿ], <i>lair</i>, as if they had read מעונת <i>meonath</i> instead of מאורת <i>meürath</i>, which they render κοίτην [koitēn] in <i>Job 37:8</i> and <i>XXXVIII, 40.</i> and <i>Jer 10:22.</i> For the <i>Chaldee</i>, again, מאורת is something quite different, namely <i>the pupil of the eye</i>, as if from the root אור, which means <i>to be bright</i> or <i>to be illuminated</i>. Hence arose that paraphrase, על חיור גלגלי עיני חיוי חרמן חסילא ידוהי יושיט, <i>Upon the sight</i> (that is, <i>the surface</i>) <i>of the pupil of the eye of the basilisk the weaned child shall put his hand.</i> Hence <i>Aben-Ezra</i>: מאורת העין שהיא בעלת האור, Meürath, <i>the eye, because it is endowed with light.</i> That is, as <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] has it here: <i>when the serpent is in its hole, and in that dark place the pupil of its eye shines, the child, seeing the light from outside and thinking it a precious stone or crystal, stretches out his hand to take it.</i> So he, following the mind of the <i>Chaldee</i> translator. But the same author, in his own view, holds that מאורת, <i>den</i>, is named from אור, or <i>light</i>, which it admits through its opening; just as in <i>Iud. VI, 2.</i> the <i>Israelites</i> are said, out of fear of the <i>Midianites</i>, to have made for themselves in the mountains את המנהרות, that is, <i>caves</i>, so called because of the window through which נהרה, <i>light</i>, enters. In <i>Selomoh</i>, R. Menachem compares it with the word אור <i>ur</i>, which they explain as <i>valley</i> in <i>Gen 11:28</i> and <i>Isa 24:15.</i> I compare it with the <i>Arabic</i> أار [aʾār] <i>aar</i>, which the <i>Arabs</i>, in <i>Giggeius</i> [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)], interpret as <i>cave</i>. For our <i>Golius</i> [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)] teaches that آر [ār] is to be referred to the root أور [ʾawr]. Thus آر [ār] and מאורה are words of the same origin, however different their form <sup>R4</sup>.

‎[III, 190] III. The third passage is <i>Isa 14:29</i> (var. <i>XV, 2.</i>): <i>Rejoice not, thou whole</i> Palestine, <i>because the rod of him that smote thee is broken. For out of the serpent's root shall come forth</i> צפע, <i>a basilisk.</i> King <i>Uzziah</i> (under whom <i>Isaiah</i> had been called by God to the prophetic office, [col. 405] <i>Isa 1:1</i>) had for a time harassed the <i>Philistines</i> with a grievous war, tearing down the walls of <i>Gath</i>, <i>Jabneh</i> and <i>Ashdod</i>, and building cities in the midst of <i>Philistia</i>, <i>2 Chr 26:6.</i> But when he tried to usurp the priestly office, he was removed from the kingship and died in a separate house. He was succeeded by <i>Jotham</i> and <i>Ahaz</i>. Under Ahaz the <i>Philistines</i> recovered their spirit and strength, invaded the towns of the lowland and the south of <i>Judah</i>, and took <i>Beth-shemesh</i>, <i>Aijalon</i>, <i>Gederoth</i>, <i>Socho</i>, <i>Timnah</i> and <i>Gimzo</i> with the neighbouring villages, and settled there. For the Lord had brought the <i>Jews</i> low because of the impiety of <i>Ahaz</i>, <i>2 Chr 28:18–19.</i> But in the very year in which <i>Ahaz</i> died (Isa 14:28), <i>Isaiah</i>, full of God, foretells a great reversal of affairs soon to come under <i>Ahaz</i>'s successor, <i>Hezekiah</i>, who subdued the <i>Philistines</i> as far as <i>Gaza</i>, <i>2 Reg. XVIII, 8.</i> Accordingly the holy <i>Prophet</i> says here, <i>Rejoice not, thou whole</i> Palestine, <i>because the rod of him that smote thee is broken</i>; that is: All you <i>Philistines</i>, who once exulted at the deposition and death of King <i>Uzziah</i>, who had punished you severely, and who under <i>Ahaz</i> dared to rise against our people, rejoice no longer. For out of the serpent's root, that is, from the stock of <i>Uzziah</i>, who had stung you like a serpent, comes forth another serpent, namely <i>Hezekiah</i>, and he one of the most dangerous kind, a basilisk, whom to your very great harm you will find more grievous and more hostile than <i>Uzziah</i> himself. Hence <i>Jerome</i> on this passage: <i>Rejoice not</i>, O Philistia, <i>do not insult my people</i>, etc., <i>because the staff that used to strike thee seems broken by death, because the snake has been killed. For in his place will be born a more harmful one,</i> Hezekiah, <i>a regulus, which the</i> Greeks <i>call basilisk, who will kill thee with his look and slay thee with the breath of his mouth. For just as no bird can pass the regulus's gaze unharmed, but, however far off it may be, is swallowed by its mouth, so thou too shalt wholly perish at the sight of King</i> Hezekiah. <i>And he has kept the metaphor beautifully: because he had named a serpent and a regulus, he says that birds are devoured by its mouth and breath.</i> For in <i>Jerome</i>'s version the whole verse we are discussing reads thus: <i>For from the root of the snake shall come forth a regulus, and its seed swallowing the bird.</i> The holy man applies these last words to the story of the basilisk, of which <i>Solinus</i> says: <i>It corrupts even the very air, so that no bird flies through the air with impunity, infected as it is by its pestilential breath.</i> And from him <i>Isidore</i>: <i>At its look no flying bird passes unharmed; but, however far off it is, it is burned and devoured by its mouth.</i> And <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)]: انّ حاني مسكنها طاير سقط [inna ḥānī maskanihā ṭāyir saqaṭa] <i>A bird falls whenever it is opposite its lair.</i> And <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt]: ان طار طاير فوقها سقط عليها [in ṭāra ṭāyir fawqahā saqaṭa ʿalayhā] <i>If a bird flies over it, it falls upon it.</i> So also <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]. But these things are not very certain, nor, as far as I remember, were they reported by anyone before <i>Solinus</i>, who is not the best of authors. [III, 191] And <i>Isaiah</i> is cited for this in vain, since the <i>Hebrew</i> reads, as <i>Jerome</i> himself acknowledges in his <i>Notes</i>, ופריו שרף מעופף, <i>And its fruit a flying serpent</i> (or rather <i>hydrus</i>), which will be explained below. Whoever, then, first rendered those words <i>And its seed swallowing the bird</i> read שואף, which means <i>swallowing</i>, instead of שרף. But in truth there is no need here to search laboriously for reasons why King <i>Hezekiah</i> is compared with a <i>serpent</i> or a <i>basilisk</i>, since it is nothing new for a powerful king to be figured by a <i>serpent</i>. Thus among the <i>Egyptians</i>, who were neighbours of the <i>Philistines</i>, <i>Aelian</i> writes in <i>History</i>, book 6, chapter 38: Τοὺς βασιλεῖς ἐπὶ τῶν διαδημάτων φορεῖν πεποικιλμένας ἀσπίδας, τῆς ἀρχῆς αἰνιττομένους τὸ ἀνίκητον [Tous basileis epi tōn diadēmatōn phorein pepoikilmenas aspidas, tēs archēs ainittomenous to anikēton], <i>that the kings wore painted asps on their diadems, to signify the invincible power of their rule</i>; because, namely, τῶν ὑπ᾽ ἀσπίδος δηχθέντων οὐ μνημονεύεται οὐδεὶς ἐξάντης γεγονέναι τοῦ κακοῦ [tōn hyp᾽ aspidos dēchthentōn ou mnēmoneuetai oudeis exantēs gegonenai tou kakou], <i>of those bitten by an asp no one is ever recorded to have escaped.</i> And <i>Horapollo</i>, book 1, [col. 406] chapter 56: Βασιλέα δὲ κάκισον (read κράτισον) δηλοῦντες, ὄφιν ζωγραφοῦσι κοσμοειδῶς ἐσχηματισμένον, οὗ τὴν οὐρὰν ἐν τῷ στόματι ποιοῦσι· [Basilea de kakison (kratison) dēlountes, ophin zōgraphousi kosmoeidōs eschēmatismenon, hou tēn ouran en tō stomati poiousi] <i>To signify a most powerful king, they paint a serpent with its tail in its mouth in the shape of a circle</i>: namely, to indicate that he rules the whole circle of the earth. Read also chapters 57, 58 and 61, in all of which various virtues or duties of kings are signified by a serpent, whether watchful, coiled, half or whole. But of all kinds of serpents the basilisk is above all the figure of a king. This is signified by the <i>Arabic</i> name ملكة [malika] <i>melecha</i>, the <i>Greek</i> βασιλίσκου [basiliskou], and the <i>Latin</i> REGULUS. Thus <i>Nicander</i>, <i>Theriaca</i> line 397, calls it ἑρπηστῶν βασιλῆα [herpēstōn basilēa], <i>king of reptiles</i>. And <i>Pliny</i> describes it as <i>marked with a white spot on the head, as if distinguished by a kind of diadem.</i> And Solinus: <i>its head lined with white as if with a little mitre.</i> Galen too and <i>Aëtius</i>: θηρίον ἐπὶ τῇ κεφαλῇ τρεῖς ὑπεροχὰς ἔχον [thērion epi tē kephalē treis hyperochas echon], <i>a serpent having three prominences on its head.</i> And <i>Avicenna</i>: مكللة الراس [mukallalat al-rās] <i>with crowned head.</i> And <i>Damir</i>: علي راسه خطوط بيض مثل التاج [ʿalī rāsihi khuṭūṭ bīḍ mithl al-tāj] <i>having white lines on its head like a crown.</i> And <i>Alkazuinius</i>: قال جالينوس انها حية شقرا علي راسها ثلاث قنازع مثل التاج [qāla Jālīnūs innahā ḥayya shaqrā ʿalī rāsihā thalāth qanāziʿ mithl al-tāj] <i>Galen said that it is a reddish snake, and has on its head three crests in the manner of a crown</i> <sup>R5</sup>. They add that it does not, like the others, crawl prone with its whole body, [III, 192] but with its head and the middle part of its body raised. Pliny: <i>It does not propel its body with manifold coils like the other serpents, but advances lofty and erect in its middle.</i> Solinus: <i>When it moves, it crawls with the middle part of its body, while the other half is upright and lofty.</i> As if this were the emblem of kingship, as it was for the kings of the <i>Persians</i> τὴν τιάραν ὀρθὴν ἔχειν [tēn tiaran orthēn echein]. Moreover, every kind of reptile yields to it, however small it is, as though acknowledging it as king; for it puts them to flight with its hiss, as we showed above <sup>c)</sup>,

‎— — — <i>far and wide it drives off the whole</i> <i>crowd, and the basilisk reigns over the empty sand.</i>

‎So <i>Lucan</i> in book 9; this is set out at greater length by <i>Nicander</i>, and by <i>Archelaus</i> in <i>Aelian</i>. Indeed, this animal was even placed on the heads of the gods, as μόνον ἀθάνατον [monon athanaton], <i>alone immortal</i>, that is, by far the longest-lived of all of its kind; and, because it kills any other animal by its breath alone, it seemed ζωῆς καὶ θανάτου κυριεύειν [zōēs kai thanatou kyrieuein], <i>to hold the power of life and death.</i> I note this here because the <i>basilisk</i> is believed to be a symbol not only of King <i>Hezekiah</i> but also of <i>Christ</i> the θεανθρώπου [theanthrōpou, God-man]. Accordingly <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases the passage we are dealing with thus: <i>Do not rejoice, all of you</i>, Philistines, <i>because the dominion that you served has been broken; for from the sons of the sons</i> of Jesse <i>the Messiah will come forth.</i> In these words it is clear that <i>Messiah</i> is put for <i>basilisk</i>, as if the <i>Prophet</i> had meant <i>Christ</i> by the name <i>basilisk</i>, just as many centuries earlier <i>Moses</i> had signified him by the <i>serpent raised up in the desert.</i> In the same place <i>Jesse</i> stands for the word נחש <i>nahas</i>, which means <i>serpent</i>. And not without reason. For, as <i>Kimchi</i> observes, <i>David's</i> father <i>Jesse</i> had two names: he was called not only <i>Jesse</i> but also נחש <i>Nahas</i>. Hence in <i>2 Sam 17:25</i> we read that <i>Abigail</i> was the daughter of <i>Nahas</i>, who is the same as <i>Jesse.</i> Therefore in <i>Isaiah's</i> words, <i>From the root of</i> נחש nahas <i>a basilisk will come forth</i>, the <i>Chaldee</i> paraphrast understood the <i>Messiah</i>, who was to be born from the stock of <i>Jesse.</i>

‎IV. The fourth passage is <i>Isa 59:5.</i> ביצי צפעני בקעו, <i>They have hatched the eggs of the basilisk.</i> Here it is taken in a bad sense, as before in a good one. For the eggs of the basilisk are crimes and wicked deeds, which, conceived in thought, are hatched through deeds. Accordingly, on this passage <i>Kimchi</i> says: <i>He compares their thoughts and deeds with the eggs of the basilisk, from which, when hatched, basilisks come forth, just as their evil counsels and</i> [col. 407] <i>deeds are hatched.</i> And a little later: <i>Like the egg of a chick, which pecks at it with its beak, breaking the shell little by little [III, 193] until it comes out into the light: so they cling continually to evil thoughts until they have brought them to action.</i> There follows in the same passage of <i>Isaiah</i>: <i>Whoever eats of their eggs will die; and if one presses them, a</i> אפעה, <i>viper, will be hatched.</i> Thus in the same verse the same deeds are compared to the eggs of the <i>basilisk</i> and of the <i>viper</i>. Yet it does not follow from this, as <i>Kimchi</i> would have it, that אפעה and צפעני are names of the same serpent. On the contrary, צפעני is the <i>basilisk</i> and אפעה the <i>viper</i>, as has been proved so far. But it is customary in Scripture for the same thing to be signified by various species of serpents. So <i>Deut 32:33. Their wine is the gall of dragons, and the cruel poison of asps.</i> Job 20:16 <i>He will suck the gall of asps, and the tongue of the viper will kill him.</i> Ps 91:13 <i>You will walk upon the asp and the basilisk, etc.</i> Isa 11:8 <i>The infant will play over the hole of the asp, and over the den of the basilisk, etc.</i> Isa 14:29 <i>From the stock of the serpent a basilisk will come forth, and its fruit will be a flying hydrus.</i> This should be noted against <i>Gregory of Nazianzus</i>, who toward the end of the <i>Second Irenic Oration</i> writes as if a basilisk could be produced from an addled egg of an asp. For there that most elegant writer says: Ἐπειδ᾽ ἂν τὰ ὠὰ τῶν ἀσπίδων ῥήξαντες, ἢ συντρίψαντες λόγῳ σκληρῷ, καὶ ἀντιτύπῳ, οὔρια καὶ ἀνεμιαῖα ταῦτα ἐλέγχωμεν, καὶ τὸν ἐν αὐτοῖς τῆς ἀσεβείας κρυπτόμενον βασιλίσκον εἰς μέσον ἀγάγωμεν [Epeid' an ta ōa tōn aspidōn rhēxantes, ē syntripsantes logō sklērō, kai antitypō, ouria kai anemiaia tauta elenchōmen, kai ton en autois tēs asebeias kryptomenon basiliskon eis meson agagōmen]; <i>When, having broken the eggs of asps or crushed them by solid argument, we have shown them to be addled and wind-eggs, and have brought into the open the basilisk of impiety hidden within them.</i> He is alluding, namely, to these words of <i>Isaiah</i> according to the <i>Greek</i> edition: Ὠὰ ἀσπίδων ἔῤῥηξαν [Ōa aspidōn errhēxan], etc. καὶ ὁ μέλλων τῶν ὠῶν αὐτῶν φαγεῖν, συντρίψας οὔριον, εὗρεν καὶ ἐν αὐτῷ βασιλίσκον [kai ho mellōn tōn ōōn autōn phagein, syntripsas ourion, heuren kai en autō basiliskon]. In these words, however, that αὐτῶν [autōn] does not refer to the <i>asps</i> but to those <i>wicked men</i> whose deeds are compared in the first hemistich to the eggs of asps, in the second to the eggs of basilisks.

‎V. The last passage on the basilisk is <i>Jer 8:17. For I will send among you serpents, basilisks, against which there is no charm, and they shall bite you, says the Lord.</i> Here <i>the Chaldee</i> has: <i>For I will stir up against you</i> עממיא קטולין כחוין חרמנין דלית להון לחשא, <i>murderous peoples, like serpents, reguli, against which there is no charm.</i> Theodoret understands this of <i>enemies</i> who ταῖς ἡμετέραις οὐκ εἴκουσι παρακλήσεσιν [tais hēmeterais ouk eikousi paraklēsesin], <i>do not yield to our entreaties</i>; Jerome understands it of <i>the ancient serpent</i> and of <i>the hostile powers</i> to whom those who have despised God's commandments will be handed over. We prefer to understand it of the <i>Assyrians</i>, to whom the <i>Israelites</i> were soon to be handed over. In place of basilisks the <i>second edition</i> had σκοποὺς [skopous], <i>watchmen</i>, that is, צפים instead of צפענים. <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] idly claims that any serpent, once it has lived seventy years, becomes a צפע, a <i>basilisk</i>, and then stops its ear so as not to hear the charmer. He proves this from <i>Ps 58:5</i>, where, however, there is nothing about the צפע or <i>basilisk</i>. Rather, what we read there is that the פתן, the <i>asp, stops its ear</i> <sup>d)</sup>, etc., which has been fully explained above <sup>e)</sup>.
