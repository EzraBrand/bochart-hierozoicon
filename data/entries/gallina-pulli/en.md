# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 18: The hen's love for her chicks (vol. 2, pp. 695–697)

### ‎[II, 695] [col. 124] Chapter 18. [col. 125]

### ‎<i>On the hen's love for her chicks.</i>

‎It remains for us to say something about the hen's love for her chicks, in connection with the notable passage of <i>Christ</i>, <i>Matt 23:37</i> and <i>Luke 13:34</i>: "Jerusalem, Jerusalem, <i>how often would I have gathered your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you would not!</i>" <i>Hen</i> in Greek is ὄρνις [ornis], a word which among the older <i>Greeks</i> signified <i>bird</i> in general. Yet in <i>Plato</i>, <i>Laws</i> book 7, ὀρνίθων θρέμματα [ornithōn thremmata] are thought to be <i>the chicks of hens</i>. And in <i>Aristophanes</i>, in the <i>Wasps</i>, ὀρνίθων γάλα [ornithōn gala] is <i>hens' milk</i>. And in <i>Aristotle</i> οἱ ὄρνιθες [hoi ornithes] are <i>cocks</i>, when he writes in <i>Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 50: Ἐκτέμνονται δὲ οἱ ὄρνιθες κατὰ τὸ ὀῤῥοπύγιον [ektemnontai de hoi ornithes kata to orrhopygion], that is, <i>Cocks are castrated near the rump.</i> And αἱ ὄρνιθες [hai ornithes] are <i>hens</i> in <i>De generat. anim.</i> book 1, ch. 21, where he reports that some people περὶ τὰς ὄρνιθας τὰς γενναίας σπουδάζειν [peri tas ornithas tas gennaias spoudazein], <i>take pains to breed well-bred hens.</i> Nor is anything else meant in these words of <i>Theophrastus</i>, <i>De causis</i> book 2, ch. 15: Τὰ ὅλως εὔτοκα βραχύβια· καὶ τὰ ὑπερτοκήσαντα πολλάκις ἀπόλλυται, καὶ μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῶν τε συνανθρωπευομένων αἱ ὄρνιθες [ta holōs eutoka brachybia; kai ta hypertokēsanta pollakis apollytai, kai malista epi tōn te synanthrōpeuomenōn hai ornithes]. This should certainly be rendered thus: <i>All the most prolific animals are short-lived; and those whose breeding is excessive often perish, and hens above all among those that live with humans.</i> Nor in these words of <i>Chrysippus</i> in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9, ch. 4: Καθάπερ τινὲς τὰς λευκὰς ὀρνίθας τῶν μελανῶν ἡδίους εἶναι μᾶλλον [kathaper tines tas leukas ornithas tōn melanōn hēdious einai mallon]; <i>As some hold that white hens are tastier than black ones.</i> And he likewise proves, by adducing examples, that the old <i>comic poets</i> <i>Menander</i>, <i>Cratinus</i> and <i>Antiphanes</i> used it for <i>hens</i>. And in the same author, book 1, ch. 15, <i>Phylarchus</i> relates that τινὰς δυνάμεις στυτικὰς [tinas dynameis stytikas] sent by <i>Sandrocottus</i>, king of the <i>Indians</i>, to <i>Seleucus</i>, ὁρμὰς ἐμποιεῖν ὀρνίθων δίκην [hormas empoiein ornithōn dikēn], <i>excited sexual desire, such as is found in cocks.</i> A very great man noted on that passage that στυπτικὰς [styptikas] is wrongly printed; and that the translator substituted <i>Sandropyctus</i> for <i>Sandrocottus</i>, <i>Antiochus</i> for <i>Seleucus</i>, and <i>little sparrows</i> for <i>cocks</i>, so that nothing sound remains in this passage. Since, then, ὄρνις [ornis] in <i>Homer</i> and <i>Hesiod</i> is nothing other than <i>bird</i> in general, in <i>Plato</i>'s age it began to be used for the <i>gallinaceous</i> kind, and for some centuries was said of the <i>cock</i> as well as of the <i>hen</i>, until usage prevailed that it was the proper name of the <i>hen</i>. Hence <i>Myrtilus</i> in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9: Ἀλλὰ [II, 696] μὴν καὶ ὄρνιθας καὶ ὀρνίθια νῦν μόνον ἡ συνήθεια καλεῖ τὰς θηλείας [alla mēn kai ornithas kai ornithia nyn monon hē synētheia kalei tas thēleias]; <i>Now indeed custom has it that only the females</i> (of cocks) <i>are called</i> ὄρνιθες [ornithes] <i>and</i> ὀρνίθια [ornithia]. Therefore in <i>Matthew</i> and <i>Luke</i> ὄρνις [ornis] is not طير [ṭayr] or طاير [ṭāyir], <i>bird</i> in general, as the Vulgate translator renders it in <i>Luke</i>, and as one of the <i>Arabic versions</i> renders it in <i>Luke</i> and the other in both places, but دجاجة [dajāja], <i>hen</i>, as the Vulgate translator and Erpenianus [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] have it in <i>Matthew</i>. Accordingly the <i>Syriac</i> too consistently renders ܬܪܢܓܘܠܬܐ [תרנגולתא], and the <i>Ethiopic</i> ዶርሆ፡, that is, <i>hen</i>. So also <i>Cyprian</i>, <i>Hilary</i>, Jerome, Augustine, and most of the ancients, except <i>Ambrose</i> on <i>Luke</i>, where this passage is rendered thus: <i>How often would I have gathered your children, as a bird its nest under its feathers?</i> But this is most especially proper to the hen, that she not only sits on her eggs, like the other birds, but also gathers her chicks, once hatched, under her wings, so that they may be warmed and invigorated [col. 126] by their mother's heat. Hence it is that in <i>Arabic</i> she is surnamed ام حفصة [umm ḥafṣa], <i>mother of gathering</i>. So the two distinguished <i>lexicographers</i> Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] expressly write, and, among the writers on nature, Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], often cited by us. Here is a striking example of this hen's φιλοστοργία [philostorgia] from <i>Alpheus of Mytilene</i>, <i>Anthology</i> book 1, ch. 87.

‎Χειμερίαις νιφάδεσσι παλυνομένα τιθὰς ὄρνις Τέκνοις εὐναίας ἀμφέχεε πτέρυγας· Μέσφά μιν οὐράνιον κρύος ὤλεσεν. ἦ γὰρ ἔμεινεν Αἰθέρος οὐρανίων ἀντίπαλος νεφέων. Πρόκνη, καὶ Μήδεια, κατ᾽ ἀΐδος αἰδέσθητε, Μητέρες, ὀρνιθίων ἔργα διδασκόμεναι. [Cheimeriais niphadessi palynomena tithas ornis teknois eunaias amphechee pterygas; mespha min ouranion kryos ōlesen. ē gar emeinen aitheros ouraniōn antipalos nepheōn. Proknē, kai Mēdeia, kat᾽ aidos aidesthēte, mēteres, ornithiōn erga didaskomenai.]

‎<i>Sprinkled with the winter snows, the gentle hen spread her sleep-bringing wings over her chicks, until the cold of the sky killed her. Truly she held out as a fighter against the clouds of heaven. O mothers</i>, Procne <i>and</i> Medea, <i>blush in the underworld, as you learn the works of hens.</i> And the excellent writer <i>Plutarch</i>, in the book <i>On Affection for Offspring</i>, sets the matter before our eyes thus: Τὰς δὲ ἀλεκτορίδας ἐν τοῖς ὄμμασι καθ᾽ ἡμέραν ἔχομεν, ὃν τρόπον τὰ νεοττία περιέπουσι, τοῖς μὲν ἐνδῦναι χαλῶσαι τὰς πτέρυγας, τὰ δὲ ἐπιβαίνοντα τῶν νώτων, καὶ προσέρχονται πανταχόθεν ἀναδεχόμεναι, μετὰ τοῦ γεγηθὸς, ἢ καὶ προσφιλὲς ἐπιφθέγγεσθαι. κυνέας δὲ, καὶ δρακοντίας [Tas de alektoridas en tois ommasi kath᾽ hēmeran echomen, hon tropon ta neottia periepousi, tois men endynai chalōsai tas pterygas, ta de epibainonta tōn nōtōn, kai proserchontai pantachothen anadechomenai, meta tou gegēthos, ē kai prosphiles epiphthengesthai. kyneas de, kai drakontias] (read κύνας δὲ, καὶ δράκοντας [kynas de, kai drakontas], or δρακόντια [drakontia]) ἂν περὶ αὐτῶν φοβηθῶσι φεύγουσιν· ἂν δὲ περὶ τῶν τέκνων, ἀμύνονται, καὶ διαμάχονται παρὰ δύναμιν [an peri autōn phobēthōsi pheugousin; an de peri tōn teknōn, amynontai, kai diamachontai para dynamin]. <i>And hens, which are before our eyes every day: with what care they protect their chicks, letting down their wings for some to creep under, while others climb onto their backs! And they come up from every side to receive them, so as to testify their joy and love with their voice. Dogs and serpents, if they fear for themselves, flee; but if they fear for their young, they drive off the attacker and fight beyond their strength.</i> <i>Augustine</i> observes this very thing in these words in the <i>Gospel Questions</i>, book 1, question 36: <i>What you will find with greater difficulty in other animals, protecting her young with her wings she</i> [II, 697] <i>fights against the kite.</i> But he also often asserts that it is peculiar to the hen that she is so affected by the weakness of her chicks that she herself becomes weak and sick. Thus on <i>Psalm</i> 58: <i>He</i> (Christ) <i>was made weak even unto death, and took on the flesh of weakness, in order to gather the chicks of</i> Jerusalem <i>under his wings, as the hen becomes weak with her little ones.</i> And a little later: <i>Surely I speak of a well-known thing, which is before our eyes every day. How her voice grows hoarse! How her whole body becomes bristly, her wings droop, her feathers loosen!</i> And on <i>Psalm XC.</i>: <i>Many birds here breed before our eyes and warm their chicks; no bird becomes weak with her chicks as the hen does. Let your charity consider swallows, sparrows and storks. We see them outside their nests, and we do not know whether they have young. But the hen we recognize by the weakness of her voice and the loosening of her feathers: she is wholly changed by affection for her chicks; because they are weak, she makes herself weak.</i> And in <i>On the Words of the Lord</i>, Sermon 29: <i>That hen is the wisdom of the Lord. But it took on flesh in order to be suited to the chicks. See the hen, bristling in her feathers, wings lowered, voice broken, shaken, weary and languid, suiting herself to her chicks.</i> And in the <i>Questions</i> cited above: <i>This kind of animal has great affection for its young, so that, affected by their weakness, it too becomes weak.</i> Hence in <i>Euripides</i>, in the <i>Madness of Heracles</i>, <i>Megara</i>, greatly anxious about the children she had borne to <i>Hercules</i>, repeatedly says that she keeps them under her wings, like a hen brooding her chicks. The words are:

‎Οἵ θ᾽ Ἡράκλειοι παῖδες, οὓς ὑποπτέρους Σώζω νεοσσοὺς, ὄρνις ὡς ὑφειμένη· [Hoi th᾽ Hērakleioi paides, hous hypopterous Sōzō neossous, ornis hōs hypheimenē] <i>And the sons of</i> Hercules, <i>whom under my wings I keep, as a hen is wont to brood her chicks.</i>

‎I adduce these things here because they are very similar to the words of <i>Christ</i>, in which he compares his φιλανθρωπίαν [philanthrōpian] with the φιλοστοργίᾳ [philostorgia] of the hen, which with the greatest eagerness draws her chicks to herself, in order to keep them safe and sound under her wings from the threatening kite or hawk.
