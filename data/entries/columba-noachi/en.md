# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 6: Noah's dove, and the form of a dove at the baptism of Christ (vol. 2, pp. 560–571)

### ‎[II, 560] CHAPTER 6.

### ‎<i>On Noah's Dove, and on the Form of a Dove which appeared at the Baptism of Christ.</i>

‎<i>Noah</i>, shut up in the ark, since he had not learned from the raven what he longed to know, namely how far the waters of the flood had decreased, also sent out the dove, a docile bird and friendly to man, which flies long and far, <i>Ps. V</i>, 7. and gladly feeds on the ground, because it is one of the ἐκ τῶν σπερμολόγων [ek tōn spermologōn], and has this implanted in it by its nature, that it returns to its nest from anywhere, even from the most remote places. [col. 28] For these reasons, then, the dove was sent out by <i>Noah</i>; but it returned not long after, because it found no place of rest: for although the tops of the mountains had long since appeared, yet on an earth still λιμναζομένῃ [limnazomenē], as <i>Josephus</i> says, that is, either still standing under water, or at least full of pools and marshy, nothing was then firm. But the same dove, sent out again seven days later, returned toward evening, carrying an olive branch in its mouth. There are some among the <i>Hebrews</i> who will have it that this branch was plucked on the Mount of <i>Olives</i> near <i>Jerusalem</i>, or elsewhere in <i>Judaea</i>, which was untouched by the waters of the flood. <i>Bereshit Rabbah</i> ch. 33: מהיכן הביאה אותו רבי אבא בר כהנא אמר משבשושין שבארץ ישראל הביאה אותו רבי לוי אמר מהר המשחה הביאה אותו דלא טפת ארעה דישראל במבולא והוא שהקבה אומר ליחזקיאל ארץ לא מטוהרה היא ולא גושמה ביום זעם, <i>Whence did it</i> (the dove) <i>bring it</i> (the branch)<i>?</i> Rabbi Abba, <i>son of</i> Cahana (the priest), <i>said: It brought it from the shrubs of the land of</i> Israel. R. Levi <i>said: It brought it from the Mount of</i> Olives, <i>because the land of</i> Israel <i>was not submerged by the flood. As God, the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to</i> Ezekiel in chapter 22, verse 24: <i>That land is not cleansed, nor rained upon in the day of indignation.</i> These things are utterly absurd, since we read that even the highest mountains under the whole heaven were covered by the waters of the flood, <i>Gen 7:19.</i> Moreover, to what end would God have kept the land of <i>Canaan</i>, not yet holy, free from the inundation? To what end would he have ordered <i>Noah</i> to build the ark with such great labour? Why was he not rather ordered to withdraw there with the beasts, wild animals and birds? And if the waters of the flood spared the land of <i>Canaan</i>, what became of the inhabitants of that land? Did they too escape the common destruction, and do their descendants, forsooth, acknowledge another ancestor besides <i>Noah</i>, from whom <i>Moses</i> derived the whole human race? And how did God allow the survivors of so great a destruction to be driven out by the accursed <i>Canaan</i>, who not long after the time of the flood seized their dwelling place? Finally, since the waters rose so many cubits above the neighbouring mountains, what prevented them from overwhelming the land of <i>Canaan</i>? They will say this was done by the power of God, and I grant that it could have been done. But although we believe miracles when they are written, still no one is permitted to invent them for himself on his own authority, which these men openly do. For they cite <i>Ezekiel</i> in vain, who has not one γρῦ [gry] about the flood. [II, 561] He only, while reproaching the <i>Israelites</i> with various crimes and recounting the various punishments inflicted, or to be inflicted, on them, denies among other things that their land was, or would be, rained upon in the day of indignation. That is, in keeping with the threats of the Law, <i>Deuter. XI</i>, <i>17. Lest the Lord, his wrath kindled against you, shut up the heavens, so that there be no rain, and the land yield not its produce.</i> And of <i>the prophet Amos 4:7</i>, where God speaks thus: <i>I also withheld the rain from you,</i> etc. <i>And I caused it to rain upon one city, and upon another I caused it not to rain: one part was rained upon, and the part on which it did not rain withered.</i> This was shown by a famous example when, at Elijah's word, the heaven was shut, and neither dew nor rain came down upon <i>Judaea</i> for three years and more, <i>1 Reg. XVII.</i> and <i>XVIII.</i> But we argue in vain, since the very words of <i>Moses</i> amply show that the olive branch was plucked from those places that the flood had inundated. For thus <i>Moses</i> says of <i>Noah</i>, Genes. VIII, 8. <i>He sent forth the dove from him, to see whether the surface of the earth was relieved of the waters.</i> And in verse 11: <i>And the dove came to him in the evening; and behold, a plucked olive leaf in her mouth. And so</i> Noah <i>knew that the surface of the earth was relieved of the waters.</i> This would be falsely said if that leaf had been brought from a land that the flood had not touched. For it could have been brought from there even at the time when the waters elsewhere were at their very highest. From this it also follows that this leaf was not plucked in the earthly Paradise, as others of the <i>Jews</i> assert. <i>Bereshit Rabbah</i>, in the place cited above: רב ביבי אמר [col. 29] נפתחו לה שערי גן עדן והביאה אותו משם, R. Bibi <i>said: The gates of the garden of</i> Eden <i>were opened to it, and from there it brought this leaf.</i> The same opinion is attributed to a certain <i>Berechiah</i> in <i>Vayikra Rabbah</i> at the end of ch. 31. That is, if we believe the <i>Hebrews</i>, the garden of <i>Eden</i> was raised above the highest summits of the mountains, so that the waters of the flood did not reach that far. Many of the ancients incline to the same view. Among them is <i>Lactantius</i>, or rather <i>Venantius</i>, who speaks thus of the earthly Paradise in the poem <i>On the Phoenix</i>,

‎<i>And when the flood had drowned the world in its waves,</i> <i>it rose above the</i> Deucalionian <i>waters.</i>

‎This is also the position of most of the <i>Scholastics</i> on book 2 of the <i>Sentences</i>, Dist. 17; also of <i>Tostatus</i> and <i>Cajetan</i> on <i>Genesis</i>, of <i>Bellarmine</i> in the book <i>On the Grace of the First Man</i>, ch. 14, and of <i>Malvenda</i>, ch. 84 <i>On Paradise.</i> But we raise many objections to this view in a separate work, <i>On the Earthly Paradise.</i> From where, then, shall we say this olive leaf was brought? From <i>Assyria</i>, it seems, which in 2 Reg. XVII, 32. is said to be ארץ זית יצהר, or, as the <i>Chaldee</i> has it, ארע דויתהא עבדין משחא, <i>a land whose olives make oil</i>, that is, a land rich in olive trees, and very fruitful ones. For <i>Noah's</i> ark had come to rest on the mountains of <i>Ararat</i>, or of the <i>Gordyaeans</i>, which <i>Assyria</i> borders to the <i>south</i>. I would suppose that the dove, leaving the ark, flew off toward that quarter of the sky, drawn by the warmth of the <i>south</i> and also driven by the wind. For [II, 562] it is likely that the wind which God had raised to dry up the waters, <i>Gen. VIII,</i> 1., blew from the <i>north</i> toward the <i>south</i>. So in <i>Ovid</i>, at <i>Deucalion's</i> flood, <i>Jupiter</i>, appeased,

‎<i>Scattered the clouds, and, the storm-clouds removed by the</i> North Wind, <i>showed the lands to the sky, and the heavens to the lands.</i>

‎This dove, then, flying <i>south</i> into <i>Assyria</i>, could have plucked an olive leaf from some mountain, part of which the receding waters had laid bare.

‎If it is asked how an <i>olive branch with green leaves</i> could have <i>lasted so long</i> under the waters, I shall have two things to answer. First, although in <i>Jerome's version</i> Noah's dove is read to have carried in her mouth a branch with green leaves, yet in the <i>Hebrew</i> we read only that she had in her mouth עלה זית טרף, <i>an olive leaf plucked off.</i> Second, the ancients report that certain species not only of the olive but of other trees too live and stay green under water. <i>Theophrastus</i>, <i>Hist. Plant.</i> book 4, chapter 8: Ἐν δὲ τῇ θαλάττῃ ἐρυθρᾷ δενδρά τινα φύεται· καὶ καλοῦσιν αὐτὰ δάφνην, καὶ ἐλαίαν. Ἔστι δὲ ἡ μὲν δάφνη ὅμοια τῇ ἀρίᾳ, ἡ δὲ ἐλαία τῷ φύλλῳ· καρπὸν δὲ ἔχει ἡ ἐλαία παραπλήσιον δὲ ταῖς ἐλαίαις [En de tē thalattē erythra dendra tina phyetai; kai kalousin auta daphnēn, kai elaian. Esti de hē men daphnē homoia tē aria, hē de elaia tō phyllō; karpon de echei hē elaia paraplēsion de tais elaiais]. Pliny condenses this in book 13, ch. 25: <i>But in the Red Sea</i> (it is a marvel) <i>woods live, chiefly the laurel and the olive, bearing berries.</i> And <i>Theophrastus</i> again: Ἐν δὲ ταῖς νήσοις ταῖς ὑπὸ τῆς πλυμμυρίδος καταλαμβανομέναις, δένδρα μεγάλα πεφυκέναι ἡλίκα πλάτανοι· συμβαίνειν δὲ ὅθ᾽ ἡ πλημμυρὶς ἐπέλθοι, τὰ μὲν ἄλλα κατακρύπτεσθαι ὅλα, τῶν δὲ μεγίστων ὑπερέχειν τοὺς κλάδους [En de tais nēsois tais hypo tēs plymmyridos katalambanomenais, dendra megala pephykenai hēlika platanoi; symbainein de hoth᾽ hē plēmmyris epelthoi, ta men alla katakryptesthai hola, tōn de megistōn hyperechein tous kladous], etc. ἔχειν δὲ τὰ δένδρα φύλλον μὲν ὅμοιον τῇ δάφνῃ, ἄνθος δὲ τοῖς οἴσοις [echein de ta dendra phyllon men homoion tē daphnē, anthos de tois oisois] (read with Pliny τοῖς ἴοις [tois iois]) καὶ τῷ χρώματι, καὶ τῇ ὀσμῇ, καρπὸν δ᾽ ἡλίκον ἐλαίᾳ, καὶ τοῦτον εὐώδη σφόδρα, καὶ τὰ μὲν φύλλα οὐκ ἀποβάλλειν, τὸ δὲ ἄνθος, καὶ τὸν καρπὸν ἅμα τῷ φθινοπώρῳ γίνεσθαι· τοῦ δὲ ἔαρος ἀποῤῥεῖν [kai tō chrōmati, kai tē osmē, karpon d᾽ hēlikon elaia, kai touton euōdē sphodra, kai ta men phylla ouk apoballein, to de anthos, kai ton karpon hama tō phthinopōrō ginesthai; tou de earos aporrhein]. Pliny rendered this as follows, with the order somewhat changed: <i>In the same region the tide covers woods of islands, although they are taller than plane trees and the tallest poplars. Their leaves are those of the laurel, the flower is that of the violet in scent and colour, the berries are like olives, and they too have a pleasant scent; they grow in autumn, and the leaves never fall. The sea covers the smaller of these entirely; the tops of the largest stand out.</i> <i>Theophrastus</i> adds: Ἀλλὰ δὲ ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ θαλάττῃ πεφυκέναι, ἀείφυλλα μὲν, τὸν δὲ καρπὸν ὅμοιον ἔχειν τοῖς θέρμοις [Alla de en autē tē thalattē pephykenai, aeiphylla men, ton de karpon homoion echein tois thermois]. Hence Pliny: <i>Other trees too have been seen in the deep,</i> etc. <i>in the same sea, always retaining their leaves, their fruit like the lupine.</i> <i>Plutarch</i> too, in the book <i>On the Face in the Orb of the Moon</i>: Τῆς δὲ Γεδρωσίας καὶ Τρωγλοδύτιδος ἡ καθήκει πρὸς τὸν ὠκεανὸν, ἀφόρου διὰ ξηρότητα, καὶ ἀδένδρου παντάπασιν οὔσης, ἐν τῇ παρακειμένῃ καὶ παρακεχυμένῃ [col. 30] θαλάττῃ θαυμαστὰ μεγέθη φυτῶν τρέφεται, καὶ κατὰ βυθοῦ τέθηλεν· ὧν τὰ μὲν ἐλαίας, τὰ δὲ δάφνας, τὰ Ἴσιδος τρίχας καλοῦσιν [Tēs de Gedrōsias kai Trōglodytidos hē kathēkei pros ton ōkeanon, aphorou dia xērotēta, kai adendrou pantapasin ousēs, en tē parakeimenē kai parakechymenē thalattē thaumasta megethē phytōn trephetai, kai kata bythou tethēlen; hōn ta men elaias, ta de daphnas, ta Isidos trichas kalousin]. <i>But that part of the</i> Gedrosian <i>and</i> Troglodytic <i>region which reaches to the</i> ocean, <i>although it is wholly</i> [II, 563] <i>barren through dryness and lacks trees, yet in the neighbouring sea that washes it plants of unusual size grow and sprout from the depths; some of them they call olives, others laurels, others hairs of</i> Isis. I think <i>Plutarch</i> has this from <i>Agatharchides</i>, in whom some things are read, book 5, chapter 53, about olives in the sea and the hairs of <i>Isis</i>, but interpolated by <i>Photius</i>. The tree in the sea like a plane tree, of which <i>Theophrastus</i> and Pliny speak, is called in <i>Arabic</i> قرم [qurm] <i>Karam.</i> Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author]: القرم نبت كالدلب غلظا وبياضا ينبت في البحر جوف [al-qurm nabt ka-l-dulb ghilaẓan wa-bayāḍan yanbut fī al-baḥr jawf] <i>The</i> alkaram <i>grows like a plane tree, a large and white tree. It comes up in the middle of the sea</i> <sup>R1</sup>. Since it is clear from these passages that true trees live in the sea itself, and among them a tree most like the olive, which therefore not only Pliny but also the inhabitants of those places simply call an olive: why should not an olive, whose leaves never fall (whence the <i>Poet</i>,

‎— — <i>a branch of the ever-leafy olive</i>)

‎have kept its leaves under the waters for several months, or at least some one of them, which was plucked by the dove? All the more so, since it may be gathered that olive leaves long withstand water and rain from the fact that the <i>Ichthyophagi</i> cover their huts with them. <i>Strabo</i>, book 16, speaks of μάνδρας στεγαστὰς ἀπὸ φυλλάδος ἐλαίνης [mandras stegastas apo phyllados elainēs], <i>huts roofed with olive leaf.</i> And <i>Agatharchides</i>, book 5, ch. 17: Οἱ δὲ τὰς τῶν ἐλαιῶν κορυφὰς ἀλλήλαις συνάπτοντες εἶτα χρῶνται σκηνώμασι [Hoi de tas tōn elaiōn koryphas allēlais synaptontes eita chrōntai skēnōmasi]; <i>Others use the tops of olives, joined to one another, as tents.</i> And <i>Diodorus</i>, book 3, p. 109: Ἐλαίαι φύονται πάνυ πολλαὶ περὶ τοὺς τόπους τούτους, τὰ μὲν πρὸς τὴν ῥίζαν ἔχουσαι προσκλυζόμενα τῇ θαλάττη, πυκναὶ δὲ τοῖς φυλλώμασι [Elaiai phyontai pany pollai peri tous topous toutous, ta men pros tēn rhizan echousai prosklyzomena tē thalattē, pyknai de tois phyllōmasi], etc. ταύτας ἀλλήλαις συμπλέκοντες, καὶ συνεχῆ σκιὰν ποιοῦντες, ἰδιαζούσαις σκηναῖς ἐμβιοῦσιν [tautas allēlais symplekontes, kai synechē skian poiountes, idiazousais skēnais embiousin]. <i>Very many olives grow around those places, with their roots immersed in the sea and with very dense foliage. Weaving these together and making a continuous shade, they live in them privately as in tents.</i> For ἐλαίαι [elaiai], that is, <i>olives</i>, our editions wrongly have ἐλάται [elatai], <i>firs</i>, whose leaves are too narrow to make a continuous shade. <i>Diodorus</i> must therefore be corrected from <i>Agatharchides</i> and <i>Strabo</i>.

‎[II, 564] There is now no need to say how pleased <i>Noah</i> was to see the dove returning with an olive leaf in its mouth. From this he inferred two things. 1. What the <i>Prophet</i> observes: that the land had been relieved of the waters, that is, not only the highest mountains, whose summits had appeared long before (<i>Gen 8:5</i>), but also the level places, or at least the hills, in which olives most delight. <i>Columella</i>, book 6, chapter 6: <i>But it</i> (the olive) <i>loves neither low-lying places nor steep ones, but rather moderate slopes, such as we see in</i> Italy <i>among the Sabines, or in the whole province of</i> Baetica. <i>Palladius</i>, book 3, chapter 18: <i>In hot places it delights in a</i> northern <i>hill, in cold places in a southern one. It takes pleasure in middling slopes. It tolerates neither the lowest place nor a steep one, rather moderate slopes, such as the</i> Sabine <i>region or</i> Baetica (read <i>it rather loves moderate slopes</i>). <i>Florentinus</i>, book 9 of the <i>Geoponica</i>, in the chapter Περὶ ἀέρος ἐπιτηδείου ἐλαίας καὶ γῆς σχημάτιον [Peri aeros epitēdeiou elaias kai gēs schēmation], <i>On the air suitable for the olive and on the shapes of the ground:</i> Συναίρεται δὲ τῇ τοῦ ἀέρος ἐπιτηδειότητι καὶ τὰ τῆς γῆς σχήματα προσκλινῆ καὶ ὑψηλά. Αἱ γὰρ ἐν τοῖς τοιούτοις τόποις, διὰ τὸ ὑπὸ τῶν ἀνέμων καταψύχεσθαι· τὸ ὑπερβάλλον τοῦ ἡλίου πυρῶδες, κάλλιστον ἔλαιον ποιοῦσι. [Synairetai de tē tou aeros epitēdeiotēti kai ta tēs gēs schēmata prosklinē kai hypsēla. Hai gar en tois toioutois topois, dia to hypo tōn anemōn katapsychesthai; to hyperballon tou hēliou pyrōdes, kalliston elaion poiousi.] <i>Sloping and elevated shapes of ground also contribute to the suitability of the air. For those that are in such a situation, because the excessive heat of the sun is cooled by the winds, produce the finest oil. Perdicca</i>, in the <i>Themes of Jerusalem</i>, p. 71:

‎Ὄρος μικρὸν ἀνεστηκώς ἐστι τοῦ Ἐλαιῶνος, [Oros mikron anestēkōs esti tou Elaiōnos,] [col. 31]

‎<i>The small mount of</i> Olivet <i>rises up.</i> And a little later,

‎Πρὸς δέ γε τὸ μεσαίτατον μέρος τοῦ ὄρους τούτου Ὁ Ἐλαιῶν κατάκαρπος ὑπὲρ τὴν φύσιν ὄντως. [Pros de ge to mesaitaton meros tou orous toutou Ho Elaiōn katakarpos hyper tēn physin ontōs.]

‎<i>Toward the middle part of that mount the</i> olive grove <i>is fruitful wholly beyond nature.</i> A <i>small mount</i>, that is, a hill, in the middle of which olives grow. This is just what <i>Palladius</i> had said, that they <i>take pleasure in middling slopes.</i> Second, <i>Noah</i> learned from this that the earth had not been so devastated by the waters of the flood that some fruit-bearing trees did not still survive, on which men and beasts might feed after the flood. For from the olive a judgement could be made about the rest. Indeed, the fact that the dove had returned not at once, as before, but only toward evening, suggested that it had found places where it could rest. For it was not credible that it had flown the whole day. Yet in the evening it flew back to the ark, either because resting on the branches of trees did not seem comfortable enough, or for lack of food, or from longing for its mate and its young, if it had any, or finally because God so provided, that by its return it might refresh <i>Noah</i>'s anxious mind and rouse him to hope of an imminent deliverance. But when it was <i>sent out</i> a third time after seven days, it did not return to the ark any more, because then, as the waters <i>receded</i> more and more, it lacked neither food nor a nest in which to hide. From this it was perceived that the waters had ceased.

‎[II, 565] See the traces of this story in the <i>Chaldaica</i> of Abydenus, cited by <i>Eusebius</i>, book 9 of the <i>Praeparatio</i>, and by Cyril in the first book against <i>Julian</i>: Τρίτη δὲ ἡμέρᾳ ἔπειτα ὑὼν ἐκόπασε, μεθίει τῶν ὀρνίθων πείρην ποιούμενος, εἴπου γῆν ἴδοι, τοῦ ὕδατος ἐκδῦσαν. αἱ δὲ ἐκδεχομένου σφέας πελάγεος ἀχανέος, ἀπορέουσαι ὅπη καθορμίσονται, παρὰ τὸν Ξίσουθρον ὀπίσω κομίζονται, καὶ ἐπ᾽ αὐτῇσιν ἕτεραι, ὡς δὲ τῇσι τρίτησιν εὐτύχεεν· ἀφίκατο γὰρ δὴ πηλοῦ κατάπλεοι τοὺς ταρσοὺς, θεοί μιν ἐξ ἀνθρώπων ἀφανίζουσιν [Tritē de hēmera epeita hyōn ekopase, methiei tōn ornithōn peirēn poioumenos, eipou gēn idoi, tou hydatos ekdysan. hai de ekdechomenou spheas pelageos achaneos, aporeousai hopē kathormisontai, para ton Xisouthron opisō komizontai, kai ep᾽ autēsin heterai, hōs de tēsi tritēsin eutycheen; aphikato gar dē pēlou katapleoi tous tarsous, theoi min ex anthrōpōn aphanizousin], <i>On the third day after it had stopped raining, he</i> (<i>Xisuthrus</i>, as he calls <i>Noah</i>) <i>sent out some birds to make a trial, whether he might see land emerging from the water. But since the boundless sea everywhere received them, and they were uncertain where to land, they came back to him; and after these, others. When he succeeded with the third ones sent out, for they had returned with muddy feet, the gods removed him from among men.</i> He says <i>birds</i> in the plural, because <i>Noah</i> sent out a raven and a dove. And that they returned muddy, just as <i>Eutychius</i> also says of the dove, p. 40: رجعت رجلتها مبلولة بالماء [rajaʿat rijlatuhā mablūla bi-l-māʾ] <i>It returned with its feet wet with water.</i> And before him Josephus: ὑπανελθούσης δὲ πεπηλομένης [hypanelthousēs de pepēlomenēs], <i>but when it had returned muddy.</i> For in <i>Hebrew</i> we have ותבא אליו היונה, <i>and the dove came back to him.</i> And יון, which means <i>mud</i>, alludes to the name of the <i>dove</i>, יונה. Finally, what was said of the birds, or at least of the dove, that it flew away and did not return, Abydenus ineptly transferred to <i>Xisuthrus</i>, or <i>Noah</i>, as if he had then been removed from the midst. But the <i>Greeks</i> report this very thing in such a way that you would hardly find anything lacking in them, except that they substitute their own <i>Deucalion</i> for <i>Noah</i>. Plutarch, in the book <i>Whether Land or Sea Animals</i>, etc.: Οἱ μὲν οὖν μυθολόγοι τῷ Δευκαλίωνί φασι περιστερὰν ἐκ τῆς λάρνακος ἀφιεμένην δήλωμα γενέσθαι, χειμῶνος μὲν εἴσω πάλιν ἐνδυομένην, εὐδίας δὲ ἀποπτᾶσαν [Hoi men oun mythologoi tō Deukalioni phasi peristeran ek tēs larnakos aphiemenēn dēlōma genesthai, cheimōnos men eisō palin endyomenēn, eudias de apoptasan]. <i>The mythographers say that a dove, sent out of the ark, signalled a storm</i> to Deucalion <i>when it came back inside, and fair weather when it flew away.</i> The <i>Arab dream-interpreters</i> attribute to the ورشان [warashān] <i>vvarsen</i>, that is, the <i>wood pigeon</i>, what <i>Moses</i> attributes to the dove. For thus they say of the wood pigeon in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter الورشان [al-warashān] <i>Alvvarsen</i>: وهو في المنام رجل غريب يدل علي اخبار ورسل لانه اخبر توحا عليه [col. 32] السلام تنقص الما لما كان في السفينة [wa-huwa fī l-manām rajul gharīb yadullu ʿalā akhbār wa-rusul li-annahu akhbara Tūḥan ʿalayhi al-salām tanaqquṣ al-mā lammā kāna fī l-safīna] <i>Seen in sleep by a stranger, it signifies messengers and couriers, because it announced to</i> Noah, <i>peace be upon him, when he was in the ark, the decrease of the waters.</i> The <i>Arabs</i>, namely, prefer the wood pigeon to the dove because God is believed to have appeared to <i>Muhammad</i> in the form of a wood pigeon and to have inspired many things. <i>Ludovico di Varthema</i>, <i>Navigation</i>, book 1, ch. 18: <i>While we were on our way</i> (towards <i>Mecca</i>) <i>countless wood pigeons darkened the sky. They say that birds of this kind descend from that bird which spoke into the ear of</i> Muhammad <i>under the guise of the Holy Spirit.</i> See the rest there.

‎[II, 566] So much for the literal sense of the story of the dove which <i>Noah</i> sent out of the ark. As for the mystical sense, many hold that the olive leaf, or twig, was a symbol of the peace and covenant which God was shortly to make with <i>Noah</i>. Tertullian, <i>On Baptism</i>, book 8: <i>After the baptism (so to speak) of the world, the dove, herald of heaven's wrath appeased, announced peace to the earth, sent out of the ark and returning with the olive, a sign which is held out for peace among the nations too.</i> Jerome, Epist. 83 to <i>Oceanus</i>, ch. 3: <i>With a branch of refreshment and light it announces peace to the world.</i> And in the <i>Dialogue</i> against <i>Lucifer</i>: <i>The dove announces peace to the earth.</i> Chrysostom on <i>Matthew</i>, Homily 12: Κλάδον φέρον ἐλαίας, τὴν κοινὴν τῆς οἰκουμένης γαλήνην εὐηγγελίσατο [Kladon pheron elaias, tēn koinēn tēs oikoumenēs galēnēn euēngelisato]; <i>Bearing an olive branch, it proclaimed the common calm of the world.</i> <i>Amoenus</i> in the <i>Enchiridion of the Old and New Testament</i>:

‎<i>Messenger of the flood now abating, the dove brings back to the ark in its beak a branch of the green olive: for the raven, caught by gluttony among foul carcasses, had stayed; she brings back the new joys of peace granted.</i>

‎<i>Claudius Marius Victor</i> on <i>Genesis</i>, book 2:

‎— — — <i>sent out again, she brings back a small offering of the peace-bearing fruit of the olive.</i>

‎<i>Alcimus Avitus</i>, book 4:

‎<i>And seeing the peace-bearing branch of the green olive, she plucks it and carries it back in her gentle beak to those shut in.</i>

‎For the olive is a sign of peace. Virgil, book 2 of the <i>Georgics</i>:

‎— — <i>cultivate the olive, gentle to peace.</i>

‎And book 7 of the <i>Aeneid</i>:

‎<i>He orders a hundred envoys to go to the king's narrow walls, all veiled with branches of</i> Pallas.

‎And book 8:

‎<i>And in his hand he holds out a branch of the peace-bearing olive</i>,

‎And book 11:

‎<i>Veiled with olive branches, and begging for pardon.</i>

‎<i>Livy</i>, book 29: <i>Ten envoys</i> of the Locrians, <i>covered in squalor and rags</i>, <i>while the consuls were sitting in the comitium</i>, <i>holding out the suppliants' emblems, olive branches</i>, <i>as is the custom</i> of the Greeks, <i>prostrated themselves on the ground before the tribunal with tearful cries.</i> <i>Polybius</i>, book 3, on <i>Hannibal</i> crossing the <i>Alps</i>: Οἱ περὶ τὴν δίοδον οἰκοῦντες, συμφρονήσαντες ἐπὶ δόλῳ συνήντων αὐτῷ, θάλλους ἔχοντες, καὶ στεφάνους· [II, 567] τοῦτο γὰρ σχεδὸν πᾶσι τοῖς Βαρβάροις ἐπὶ σύνθημα φιλίας, καθάπερ τὸ κηρύκειον τοῖς Ἕλλησιν. [Hoi peri tēn diodon oikountes, symphronēsantes epi dolō synēntōn autō, thallous echontes, kai stephanous; touto gar schedon pasi tois Barbarois epi synthēma philias, kathaper to kērykeion tois Hellēsin.] <i>The inhabitants along the route, having secretly conspired, come to meet him with branches of green olive and with garlands. For almost everywhere among the barbarians this is a sign of peace and friendship, as the caduceus is among</i> the Greeks. Hence <i>Ovid</i>, <i>Fasti</i> book 1:

‎<i>With your tresses bound with</i> Actian <i>leaves,</i> <i>come, Peace, and gently visit all the world.</i>

‎<i>With Actian leaves</i>, that is, of the olive, from whose leaves a crown is woven for <i>Peace</i>. In the same way they hold that <i>Minerva made peace with</i> Neptune <i>crowned with this tree</i>, as <i>Tertullian</i> has it in <i>De corona militis</i>, chapter 13. So all interpret it. But why the olive's <i>leaves</i> are called <i>Actian</i> is not yet clear, and what the commentators offer does not satisfy me. I would therefore rather read <i>frondibus Actaeis</i>, that is, <i>Attic</i> leaves: [col. 33] because the olive is believed to have originated in <i>Attica</i>, which in <i>Greek</i> is called Ἀκτὴ [Aktē], whence the gentile adjective Ἀκταῖος [Aktaios]. <i>Stephanus</i>: Ἀκτὴ, οὕτως ἡ Ἀττικὴ ἐκαλεῖτο [Aktē, houtōs hē Attikē ekaleito]. And a little later: Τὸ ἐθνικὸν Ἀκταῖος καὶ Ἀκταῖα [To ethnikon Aktaios kai Aktaia]. Accordingly, just as <i>Actaean honey</i> is <i>Attic honey</i> in <i>Columella</i> on the garden,

‎Of Narycian <i>pitch</i>, <i>or the honey of</i> Actaean <i>Hymettus</i>:

‎so an <i>Actaean olive</i> is an <i>Attic olive.</i> <i>Nonnus, Dionysiaca</i> book 27, line 287:

‎Ἀκταίης τε γέραιρε φερέπτολιν ὄζον ἐλαίης, [Aktaiēs te geraire pheréptolin ozon elaiēs,]

‎<i>And honour the city-bearing branch of the</i> Actaean <i>olive.</i> <i>Ausonius</i>, in the <i>Mosella</i>, on the sheatfish:

‎<i>Whom, as if his back were smeared with</i> Actaean <i>olive oil,</i> <i>I take for a river-dwelling dolphin.</i> — —

‎Note in passing that in <i>Lucian's Tragopodagra</i> φύλλα ἀκτέας [phylla akteas] is something else, when he says:

‎Τίνες γὰρ οἵδε βάκτρα νωμᾶντες χεροῖν, Κάρηνα φύλλοις ἀκτέας καταστεφεῖς; [Tines gar hoide baktra nōmantes cheroin, Karēna phyllois akteas katastepheis?]

‎which the translator rendered badly:

‎<i>What crowd is this, who hold staves in their hands,</i> <i>their heads bound with garlands of</i> Actaean <i>leaf?</i>

‎For there ἀκτέα [aktea] is ἀκτὴ [aktē], that is, <i>sambucus</i> [elder]: the gouty are depicted as binding their heads with its leaves, because elder leaves ποδαγρικοῖς βοηθεῖ μετὰ στέατος ταυρείου, ἢ τραγείου [podagrikois boēthei meta steatos taureiou, ē trageiou]. So <i>Dioscorides</i>; and <i>Pliny</i> not very differently. And <i>Samonicus</i>, in the chapter <i>On driving away gout</i>:

‎<i>Likewise dwarf elder smeared with goat's tallow.</i> [II, 568] Of the olive I add only this: that even in <i>America</i> the custom had long prevailed that olive branches were held to be a sign of peace. <i>Columbus, Navigat.</i> ch. 103, on a certain <i>American</i> island not far from <i>Hispaniola</i>: <i>On our first arrival thirty young girls came to meet us, who,</i> etc. <i>carried olive branches in their hands, and when they approached the commander, they held out the branches to him as a sign of alliance and peace</i> <sup>R2</sup>.

‎At the baptism of Christ there are some of the <i>Fathers</i> who hold that a real dove descended. [col. 35] Thus <i>Tertullian</i>, in the book <i>On the Flesh of Christ</i>, chapter 3, writes of the Spirit descending upon <i>Christ</i>: <i>It was as truly a dove as it was also spirit; nor had it destroyed</i> <sup>a)</sup> <i>its own substance by assuming a foreign substance.</i> And <i>Augustine</i>, <i>On the Christian Combat</i>, ch. 22, comparing the body of <i>Christ</i> with this dove, says: <i>Nor do we say this in such a way as to say that the Lord</i> Jesus <i>alone had a true body, while the Holy Spirit appeared deceptively to the eyes of men; rather, we believe both those bodies to be true bodies. For just as it was not fitting that the Son of God should deceive men, so it was not becoming that the Holy Spirit should deceive men. But for almighty God, who made the whole creation out of nothing, it was not difficult to form a true body of a dove without the agency of other doves, just as it was not difficult for him to make a true body in the womb of</i> Mary <i>without male seed.</i> Yet the four <i>Evangelists</i> <sup>b)</sup> do not say that a dove descended upon <i>Christ</i>, but ὡσεὶ περιστερὰν [hōsei peristeran], <i>as a dove.</i> And <i>Luke</i> more explicitly says that the Holy Spirit descended σωματικῷ εἴδει ὡσεὶ περιστερὰν [sōmatikō eidei hōsei peristeran], <i>in bodily form as a dove.</i> There the <i>Syriac</i> has ܒܕܡܘܬܐ ܓܘܫܡܐ ܕܝܘܢܐ [בדמותא גושמא דיונא], <i>after the likeness of the body of a dove.</i> And the <i>Arabic</i>, شبه جسد حمامة [shibh jasad ḥamāma] <i>in the likeness of the body of a dove.</i> And the <i>Ethiopic</i>, በሥጋ፡ ከመ፡ ርእየተ፡ ርግብ፡ [baśəgā kama rəʾəyata rəgəb] <i>In a body as in the appearance of a dove.</i> From this one may conclude that, in the <i>Ethiopic translator's</i> mind, what appeared was a body, but not the body of a real dove. <i>St Augustine</i> is followed by <i>Thomas</i> in the <i>Summa</i>, part 3, question 39, article 7. Indeed <i>Sixtus of Siena</i>, <i>Biblioth.</i> book 6, annotation 13, writes that almost all the <i>Scholastic theologians</i> hold the same view. Most of the more recent writers, however, think otherwise, namely that the Spirit descended not in a real dove but in the appearance of a dove. Caring little about <i>Tertullian</i>, they evade <i>Augustine's</i> authority in two ways. Some, like <i>Pamelius</i>, deny that the book <i>On the Christian Combat</i> is really <i>Augustine's</i>. Others, like <i>Maldonatus</i>, accept it as genuine, but contend that <i>Augustine</i> asserts only that a true body appeared, not however a real dove. But neither party is convincing.

‎[II, 569] Not the first, since it is established that the book <i>On the Combat</i> is <i>Augustine's</i>, both from its style and from <i>Possidius' Indiculus</i> and from <i>Augustine</i> himself, who acknowledges that book as his own in book 2 of the <i>Retractations</i>, ch. 3. Not the second, because <i>Augustine</i> calls that dove not only a true body but also <i>a true body of a dove. It was not difficult for God</i>, he says, <i>to form a true body of a dove without the agency of other doves</i>. In his opinion this was necessary <i>so that the Holy Spirit should not deceive men, if that dove</i> [col. 36] <i>had not been what it seemed to be</i>. He holds, therefore, that this dove was as truly a dove as <i>Christ</i> was truly a man. Accordingly I would say of both, that is, of <i>Augustine</i> as well as of <i>Tertullian</i>, that in arguing against those who held that <i>Christ's</i> body was as imaginary as that dove was, they erred, as often happens, τῇ τῆς ἀνθόλκης ἀμετρίᾳ [tē tēs anthol kēs ametria]. For although it would have sufficed to assert the reality of <i>Christ's</i> body, they wanted even this φάσμα ὄρνιθος [phasma ornithos], as <i>Origen</i> rightly calls it, to be taken for a real dove. But four things are to be noted here. First, outside the heat of controversy the same writers define it otherwise. For in <i>Tertullian</i>, book <i>On Baptism</i>, ch. 8, we read that the Holy Spirit <i>descended upon the Lord in the figure of a dove</i>. And in <i>Augustine's</i> Letter 102 to <i>Evodius</i> this dove is said to have been <i>presented to the eyes in bodily appearance only, not expressed in the nature of a living animal</i>. And in <i>On the Trinity</i>, book 2, ch. 5: <i>Upon the Lord himself</i> (the Holy Spirit) <i>descended in bodily form, like a dove</i>. Second, other <i>Fathers</i> regarded this dove as imaginary. <i>Justin</i>, in the <i>Dialogue with Trypho the Jew</i>: Τοῦ ἐπελθόντος ἐν εἴδει περιστερᾶς πνεύματος [tou epelthontos en eidei peristeras pneumatos]. <i>Irenaeus</i>, book 3, ch. 19: <i>They said that the Spirit of God descended upon him like a dove. Origen</i> on <i>Luke</i>, Homily 27: <i>The Holy Spirit descended upon the Saviour in the appearance of a dove. Epiphanius</i> against the <i>Sabellians</i>, Heresy 62: Ἐν εἴδει περιστερᾶς σχηματίζεται [en eidei peristeras schēmatizetai]. And the translator: <i>He conformed himself to the likeness of a dove. Hilary</i> on <i>Matthew</i>, Canon 2: <i>The Holy Spirit is sent forth, and is recognized as visible in the appearance of a dove. Nonnus</i> in the Paraphrase of <i>St John</i> calls it Ἀντίτυπον μίμημα πελειάδος [antitypon mimēma peleiados]. <i>Juvencus</i>, <i>Evang. Hist</i>. book 1:

‎<i>And bearing bodily form, the Spirit descended from on high, imitating from the cloud an airy dove.</i>

‎<i>Sedulius</i>, <i>Carm</i>. book 2:

‎<i>At once the heavens opened, and the Holy Spirit in the appearance of a dove clothed</i> Christ <i>with honour</i>.

‎<i>Chrysostom</i> on <i>Matth</i>., Homil. 12: Ἦλθε τὸ πνεῦμα ἐν εἴδει περιστερᾶς [Ēlthe to pneuma en eidei peristeras]. <i>Isidore of Pelusium</i>, Epist. 106: Ἐν εἴδει περιστερᾶς τὸ θεῖον ἐπεφάνη πνεῦμα [En eidei peristeras to theion epephanē pneuma]. <i>Cyril</i> on <i>John</i>: Περιστερᾶς ἐν σχήματι [Peristeras en schēmati]. And: ἐν τῷ τῆς περιστερᾶς σχηματισμῷ [en tō tēs peristeras schēmatismō]. The author of the book <i>On the Wonders of Scripture</i>, under <i>Augustine</i>'s name, book 3, ch. 5: <i>“But although the Holy Spirit is said to have descended upon the Lord in bodily form, there is not the least doubt that he did not take the dove from among the birds, but from the air.”</i> The author of the book [II, 570] <i>On the Sacraments</i>, under <i>Ambrose</i>'s name, book 1, ch. 5: <i>“And behold! the Spirit descended as a dove: not a dove descended, but as it were a dove.”</i> And a little later: <i>“He descended from heaven not in the reality of a dove, but in the appearance of a dove.”</i> <i>Procopius of Gaza</i> on <i>Gen. XVIII</i>: <i>“The Holy Spirit came into our sight in the appearance of a dove; yet it was not a dove, because it did not have the nature that a dove has.”</i> Third, it must be noted that <i>Tertullian</i> errs very gravely. For he says things of the Holy Spirit that cannot be true unless one imagines that the Holy Spirit took a dove to himself in hypostatic union. <i>“The Spirit,”</i> he says, <i>“was as truly a dove as he was Spirit, and he had not destroyed his own substance by assuming a foreign one.”</i> From this another error arose, namely that the Holy Spirit is as far below <i>Christ</i> as a dove is below a man. So <i>Martial</i>, comparing <i>Stella</i>'s dove with <i>Catullus</i>'s sparrow, maintains that <i>Stella</i> is a better <i>poet</i> than <i>Catullus</i> by as much

‎<i>As a dove is greater than a sparrow.</i>

‎But <i>Chrysostom</i> refutes this error as follows in <i>Homily 12 on Matthew</i>: Ὁ μὲν ἐν τῇ ἡμετέρᾳ φύσει, τὸ δὲ ἐν ὄψει περιστερᾶς ἐφάνη· [Ho men en tē hēmetera physei, to de en opsei peristeras ephanē] Christ <i>appeared in our nature, but the Spirit in the form of a dove.</i> Again: Ὁ μὲν υἱὸς τοῦ Θεοῦ φύσιν ἀνθρώπου ἀνέλαβε· τὸ δὲ πνεῦμα οὐ φύσιν ἀνέλαβε περιστερᾶς. Διὰ τοῦτο καὶ ὁ Εὐαγγελιστὴς οὐκ εἶπεν, ὅτι ἐν φύσει περιστερᾶς, ἀλλ᾽ ἐν εἴδει περιστερᾶς· οὐκοῦν οὐδὲ μετὰ ταῦτα ὤφθη ἐν τούτῳ τῷ σχήματι, ἀλλὰ τότε μόνον. [Ho men huios tou Theou physin anthrōpou anelabe; to de pneuma ou physin anelabe peristeras. Dia touto kai ho Euangelistēs ouk eipen, hoti en physei peristeras, all' en eidei peristeras; oukoun oude meta tauta ōphthē en toutō tō schēmati, alla tote monon.] <i>The Son of God took on the nature of a man, but the Spirit did not take on the nature of a dove. Therefore the Evangelist did not say that he</i> (descended) <i>in the nature of a dove, but in the form of a dove.</i> [col. 37] <i>Nor did he appear in that form afterwards, but only then.</i> <i>Augustine</i>, however, who was wholly free of this error, believed only that a real dove, created by God for this purpose, was the symbol of the presence of the Holy Spirit. This is a much lighter error. For although this opinion cannot be reconciled with the words of the <i>Evangelists</i>, it differs from the former in that it contains nothing unworthy of the majesty of the Holy Spirit; for, without detriment to his dignity, he could be signified as well by a real dove as by the image of a dove. Fourth, it should be noted that <i>Augustine's</i> argument, by which he tries to prove that this was a real dove, has little force. <i>We believe,</i> he says, <i>that both those bodies</i>, namely those of <i>Christ</i> and of the Holy Spirit, <i>were real bodies. For just as it was not fitting that the Son of God should deceive men, so it was not proper that the Holy Spirit should deceive men.</i> I grant this. But I deny that anyone saw this form of a dove except <i>Christ</i> himself, <i>Matt 3:16</i> and <i>Mark 1:10</i>, and <i>John</i>, John 1:32 <i>Christ</i> was indeed baptized in the midst of the crowds, <i>Luke 3:21</i>, but no one has written that the dove appeared to them. And that it was seen by no one apart from <i>Christ</i> except <i>John</i> is plainly taught by <i>John 1:32</i>: <i>John bore witness to him, saying, I saw the Spirit descending from heaven like a dove, and he remained upon him.</i> For what need would there have been of the testimony of so great a [II, 571] man to prove what others too had seen, indeed all the crowds that then stood around <i>Christ</i> and <i>John</i>? Moreover, this form of a dove did not deceive <i>Christ</i>, who was free from error; nor <i>John</i>, who had been forewarned by God that his Spirit would descend in visible form and remain upon his Son, and so, when he saw this form of a dove descend and settle upon <i>Christ</i>, he knew for certain that this was taking place in a vision, as God had foretold. Accordingly he does not say that he saw a dove, but <i>as it were a dove.</i> Yet even supposing, without conceding it, that this φάσμα [phasma] of a dove was seen by all and was believed by most to have been a real dove, not even so would the Holy Spirit have deceived them. For he does not truly deceive who places an image before the eyes with the intention not of deceiving but of teaching. Such was this vision, in which the form of a dove was set before the eyes, not so that it should be believed to be a real dove, but so that through it the presence of the Holy Spirit, and what his powers are, might be signified. If this is held to be falsehood, the use of all visions will be abolished, in which one thing is shown to the eyes and another to the mind. And the Holy Spirit would then have deceived not only in the vision of the dove but also in that of the fiery tongues. For those tongues were not real tongues, nor was it real fire. Accordingly, just as here he is said to have descended ὡσεὶ περιστερὰ [hōsei peristera], so there the tongues are said to have been seen ὡσεὶ πυρὸς [hōsei pyros], <i>Acts 2:3.</i> And a little before, a sound is said to have been heard, not of wind, but as of wind, ὥσπερ φερομένης πνοῆς βιαίας [hōsper pheromenēs pnoēs biaias], so that we may know that all these things happened in a vision.

‎I cannot refrain from adding that the <i>Hebrews</i> too, in <i>Song 2:12</i>, understand the Holy Spirit by the name of the <i>turtledove</i>, a bird of the dove kind. Hence the words of the sacred text, <i>The voice of the turtledove is heard in our land,</i> are paraphrased by <i>the Chaldee</i> thus: וקול רוחא דקודשא דפורקנא דאמרית לאברהם אביכון כבר שמעתון <i>And the voice of the Holy Spirit, that is, the deliverance which I foretold to</i> Abraham, <i>your father</i>, Gen 15:14 16., <i>you have already heard.</i> <i>Origen</i> too refers these very words to the Holy Spirit, who, he holds, is signified by the turtledove when he reveals more hidden things, and by the dove when he teaches things better suited to the understanding of the common people. <i>The Holy Spirit,</i> he says, <i>when he speaks of great and more hidden mysteries, which many cannot grasp, is designated by the name of the</i> turtledove, <i>that is, of that bird which always dwells on mountain ridges and in the tops of trees. But in the valleys, and in those things which reach down to men, the dove is taken.</i> So he writes on the words of the <i>Canticle</i>: <i>The voice of the turtledove, etc.</i> [col. 38]
