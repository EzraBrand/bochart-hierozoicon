# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 11: The crane (vol. 2, pp. 614–631)

### ‎[II, 614] [col. 68] Chapter 11.

### ‎<i>On the Crane.</i>

‎Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] writes in book 4, chapter 1 of <i>De Avibus</i> that in the anatomy of the crane he observed something peculiar, namely that <i>the windpipe is arranged differently than in other birds. For, adhering to the breastbone,</i> he says, <i>it penetrates the flesh on both sides. And so it is no wonder that its voice is heard so far away. For no bird is as vocal as the crane.</i> Hence Hezekiah, in his song of thanksgiving after he recovered from illness, [col. 69] <i>Es. XXXVIII</i>, 14: כסיס עגור כן אצפצף, <i>like a sis</i> and <i>agor so did I chirp</i>, that is, <i>like a swallow and a crane</i>; of these birds the one has a plaintive voice, the other a most strained one. Between <i>sis</i> and <i>agor</i> the copulative <i>Vau</i> is lacking, as in <i>Hab 3:11.</i> שמש ירח עמד זבלה, <i>sun, moon</i> (that is, <i>sun and moon</i>) <i>stood still in their place.</i> And <i>1 Chr 2:1. Reuben, Simeon, Levi</i>, for <i>Reuben, and Simeon, and Levi.</i> Thus Hezekiah's meaning is this: that, afflicted by a grave illness, he not only moaned with a soft murmur like a swallow, but also wailed with a strained voice like a crane. So the Talmudists in tractate <i>Kiddusim</i>, ch. 2, fol. 44a: וצווח דל ככורכיא, <i>And Master</i> Lakish <i>cried out like a crane.</i> And Smyrnaeus [Quintus of Smyrna], in book 3, on the lament of the Nereids at the death of Achilles,

‎— — — — ταί δ᾽ ἐφέροντο Κλαγγηδὸν κραιπνῇσιν ἐειδόμεναι γεράνοισιν, Ὀσσόμεναι <sup>a)</sup> μέγα χεῖμα, — — [tai d᾽ epheronto klangēdon kraipnēsin eeidomenai geranoisin, ossomenai mega cheima]

‎<i>And they were borne along with clamour, like swift cranes that sense a great storm in advance.</i> And in book 13, on the destruction of Troy,

‎— — στοναχὴ δὲ δαϊκταμένων αἰζηῶν Ἔπλετο λευγαλέη, περὶ δ᾽ ἴαχε πάντα μέλαθρα [II, 615] Ἄσπετον· οἰμωγὴ δὲ πέλεν στονόεσσα γυναίκων Εἰδομένων γεράνοισιν, ὅτ᾽ αἰετὸν ἀθρήσωσιν. Ὑψόθεν αἴσσοντα δι᾽ αἰθέρος, οὐδ᾽ ἄρα τῇσι Θαρσαλέον στέρνοισι πέλει μένος, ἀλλὰ ἑ μοῦνον Μακρὸν ἀνατρύζουσι, φοβούμεναι ἱερὸν ὄρνιν. [stonachē de daiktamenōn aizēōn epleto leugaleē, peri d᾽ iache panta melathra aspeton; oimōgē de pelen stonoessa gynaikōn eidomenōn geranoisin, hot᾽ aieton athrēsōsin hypsothen aissonta di᾽ aitheros, oud᾽ ara tēsi tharsaleon sternoisi pelei menos, alla he mounon makron anatryzousi, phoboumenai hieron ornin.]

‎<i>And the groaning of the slain young men was pitiful, and all the houses resounded dreadfully. And most mournful was the wailing of the women, like cranes when they see an eagle swooping from on high through the air. Nor does any brave strength abide in their breast, but they only shriek loudly, dreading the sacred bird of</i> Jove. Homer too, comparing the war-cry of soldiers with the clamour of cranes, at the beginning of book 3 of the <i>Iliad</i>,

‎Τρῶες μὲν κλαγγῇ τ᾽ ἐνοπῇ τ᾽ ἴσαν ὄρνιθες ὥς, Ἠΰτε περ κλαγγὴ γεράνων πέλει οὐρανόθι πρό. [Trōes men klangē t᾽ enopē t᾽ isan ornithes hōs, ēute per klangē geranōn pelei ouranothi pro.]

‎<i>The</i> Trojans <i>went with clamour and shouting like birds, as is the clamour of cranes up to heaven.</i> Which Maro imitated in book 10 of the <i>Aeneid</i>,

‎— — <i>the</i> Dardanians <i>raise a shout to the stars from the walls, as beneath black clouds</i> Strymonian <i>cranes give signals and swim through the air with noise, and flee the</i> South Winds <i>with joyful clamour.</i>

‎And Martial, to signify that the old woman Philaenis was not only talkative but also loud and Stentorian, in book 9, <i>Epigram 30</i>:

‎<i>Alas, what a tongue is silent! A thousand auction-blocks could not outdo her, nor the crowd that loves</i> Serapis, <i>nor the curly-haired troop of the morning schoolmaster, nor the bank that resounds with the</i> Strymonian <i>flock.</i>

‎The following also pertain to the cry of cranes. Homer again, in the place cited above,

‎Κλαγγῇ ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων, [Klangē tai ge petontai ep᾽ Ōkeanoio rhoaōn,]

‎<i>With clamour they fly to the streams of</i> Ocean. Hesiod in the <i>Works</i>,

‎Φράζεσθαι δ᾽ εὖτ᾽ ἂν φωνὴν γεράνου ἐπακούσης, Ὑψόθεν ἐκ νεφέων ἐνιαύσια κεκλαγυίης, [Phrazesthai d᾽ eut᾽ an phōnēn geranou epakousēs, hypsothen ek nepheōn eniausia keklagyiēs,]

‎<i>Take heed when you hear the voice of the crane crying yearly from on high out of the clouds.</i> Lucretius, book 4, line 18:

‎<i>As the brief song of the swan is better than that clamour of cranes in the</i> south wind's <i>ethereal scattered clouds.</i>

‎[II, 616] Claudian, on the <i>Gildonic</i> war,

‎<i>With huge clamour the cranes leave their summer</i> Thrace, <i>when they exchange the</i> Strymon <i>for the warm</i> Nile.

‎Accordingly Eustathius calls the crane a φωνητικὴν [phōnētikēn], <i>vocal</i>, bird. And it should be noted that most languages imitate its voice in some way by the very name of the bird. Thus the Hebrew עגור <i>agor</i>, the Chaldee כורכיא <i>kurkeja</i>, the Arabic كركي [kurkī] <i>kurki</i> <sup>R1</sup> and غرنوق [ghurnūq] <i>gurnuk</i> <sup>R2</sup>, the Greek γέρανος [geranos], the Cambro-British <i>garan</i>, the German <i>cran</i>, the Roman <i>grus</i> are so many onomatopoeic words, ὀνοματοποιητικαί [onomatopoiētikai]. Hence Isidore: [col. 70] <i>Cranes took their name from their own voice. For they murmur with such a sound.</i> And <i>gruere</i> [to cry like a crane] is <i>to clamour in the manner of cranes.</i> Festus: <i>Cranes are said</i> gruere, <i>as swine</i> grunnire [grunt]. The little poem <i>De cantu Philomelae</i>:

‎<i>The crane cries, and in flocks the swans</i> drensant <i>near the rivers.</i>

‎An old <i>Glossary</i>: <i>Gruunt</i>, γερανίζουσι [geranizousi]. Meanwhile, lest anyone think that cranes always cry with a loud voice, Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] has this about the crane that keeps watch while the others sleep: والذي يحرس يهتف خافي كانه ينذر بانه جارس [wa-lladhī yaḥrusu yahtifu khāfī ka-annahu yundhiru bi-annahu jāris] <i>And the one on guard murmurs only in a low voice, as if it knew that it was on guard.</i> This itself might not unreasonably be referred to the passage of Hezekiah, as though he had joined this soft murmur of the crane with the twittering of the swallow.

‎[II, 617] Now we shall treat the migration of cranes, with reference to <i>Jer 8:7. The turtledove, and the swallow, and the crane have kept the time of their coming.</i> In general it is agreed that the crane not only migrates elsewhere, but also travels to the most remote places to escape the cold. <i>Symeon Seth</i>: Τὸ δὲ τοιοῦτον πτηνὸν διέρχεται τὴν οἰκουμένην ἅπασαν, μὴ μένον ἐν ἑνὶ κλίματι [To de toiouton ptēnon dierchetai tēn oikoumenēn hapasan, mē menon en heni klimati]; <i>This bird traverses the whole world, by no means staying in one region.</i> Aristotle, <i>History of Animals</i>, book 8, chapter 12: Ἐκτοπίζουσιν εἰς τὰ ἔσχατα ἐκ τῶν ἐσχάτων [Ektopizousin eis ta eschata ek tōn eschatōn]; <i>They</i> (the cranes) <i>migrate from the farthest regions to the farthest.</i> That is, as he himself explains, ἐκ τῶν Σκυθικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ [ek tōn Skythikōn eis ta helē ta anō tēs Aigyptou, hothen ho Neilos rhei], <i>from the</i> Scythian <i>shores to the marshes that lie above</i> Egypt, <i>whence the</i> Nile <i>flows</i>. He followed <i>Herodotus</i>, who, treating the sources of the <i>Nile</i> and the neighbouring <i>Ocean</i> in book 2, chapter 22, says: Γέρανοι δὲ φεύγουσαι τὸν χειμῶνα, τὸν ἐν τῇ Σκυθικῇ χώρῃ γενόμενον, φοιτῶσι εἰς χειμαδίην ἐς τοὺς τόπους τούτους [Geranoi de pheugousai ton cheimōna, ton en tē Skythikē chōrē genomenon, phoitōsi eis cheimadiēn es tous topous toutous]; <i>Cranes, fleeing the winter of the</i> Scythian <i>region, betake themselves to those places to spend the winter.</i> <i>Aelian</i> too, <i>History of Animals</i>, book 3, chapter 13: Εἰς Αἴγυπτόν τε, καὶ Λιβύην ἀπαίρουσι, καὶ Αἰθιόπιαν, ὥσπερ γὰρ οὖν γῆς περίοδον εἰδυῖαι, καὶ φύσεις ἀέρων, καὶ ὡρῶν διαφορὰς στέλλονται αἱ πᾶσαι [Eis Aigypton te, kai Libyēn apairousi, kai Aithiopian, hōsper gar oun gēs periodon eiduiai, kai physeis aerōn, kai hōrōn diaphoras stellontai hai pasai]; <i>As though they knew the circuit of the earth, the nature of the sky and the variation of the seasons, they depart for</i> Egypt, Libya <i>and</i> Ethiopia, <i>and all set out.</i> And <i>Homer</i> long before:

‎— — — ταί γε πέτονται ἐπ᾽ Ὠκεανοῖο ῥοάων [tai ge petontai ep᾽ Ōkeanoio rhoaōn],

‎<i>These fly to the streams of</i> Ocean, namely the <i>southern</i> Ocean, where the <i>Pygmies</i> are, as what follows shows. Hence <i>Lucullus</i> to <i>Pompey</i> in <i>Plutarch</i>: Εἶτά σοι δοκῶ ἐλάττονα τῶν γεράνων νοῦν ἔχειν, καὶ τῶν πελαργῶν, ὥς τε ταῖς ὥραις μὴ συμμεταβάλλειν τὰς διαίτας [Eita soi dokō elattona tōn geranōn noun echein, kai tōn pelargōn, hōs te tais hōrais mē symmetaballein tas diaitas]; <i>Do I seem to you to have less sense than cranes and storks, so as not to change my abode with the seasons of the year?</i>

‎The ancients believed that most of them are born in <i>Thrace</i>. <i>Aelian</i>, in the place cited above: Αἱ γέρανοι γίνονται μὲν ἐν Θράκῃ [Hai geranoi ginontai men en Thrakē]; <i>Cranes are born in</i> Thrace. And shortly after: Οὐκοῦν φιλοῦσι τὴν χώραν, ἐν ᾗ γεγόνασι [Oukoun philousi tēn chōran, en hē gegonasi]; <i>They therefore love the region in which they were born.</i> Hence they are called <i>birds of Thrace</i>, and <i>Strymonian</i> and <i>Bistonian</i>, from the <i>Strymon</i>, a river, and the <i>Biston</i>, a lake of <i>Thrace</i>, from which they are believed to set out. <i>Virgil</i>, <i>Aeneid</i>, book 10:

‎— — — <i>as beneath the high clouds</i> Strymonian <i>cranes give their signals.</i> — —

‎<i>Lucan</i>, book 3, line 199:

‎<i>The</i> Strymon <i>is deserted, which is wont to commit the</i> Bistonian <i>birds to the warm</i> Nile. — — — [II, 618] And book 5, line 711:

‎<i>So, when winter drives them out, the cranes leave the icy</i> Strymon, <i>to drink of you, O</i> Nile. — — — —

‎And book 7, line 832:

‎— — — <i>you who are wont to exchange</i> Thracian <i>winters for the</i> Nile. — — — —

‎<i>Martial</i>, cited just now:

‎<i>Nor the bank that resounds with the</i> Strymonian <i>flock.</i> [col. 71]

‎<i>Seneca</i>, <i>Oedipus</i>, Act 3:

‎<i>Not so many birds, fleeing the threats of the icy</i> Strymon, <i>exchange their winters, and, cleaving the sky, offset the</i> Arctic <i>snows with the warm</i> Nile.

‎<i>Claudian</i>, <i>On the Gildonic War</i>:

‎— — — — <i>they leave</i> Thrace <i>in summer, when they exchange the</i> Strymon <i>for the warm</i> Nile.

‎<i>Antipater of Sidon</i>, <i>Anthology</i>, book 3, chapter 16:

‎— — — — καὶ ἁρπάκτειραν ἐρύκων Σπέρματος ὑψιπετῆ Βιστονίαν γέρανον [kai harpakteiran erykōn Spermatos hypsipetē Bistonian geranon]· — —

‎<i>And warding off the high-flying</i> Bistonian <i>crane, plunderer of seed.</i> When about to leave <i>Thrace</i>, they are said to gather at the river <i>Hebrus</i>. <i>Aelian</i>, book 2, chapter 1: Ὅταν τὰ ἤθη τὰ τῶν Θρακῶν καὶ τοὺς κρυμοὺς ἀπολείπωσι τοὺς Θρακίους αἱ γέρανοι, ἀθροίζονται μὲν ἐς τὸν Ἕβρον [Hotan ta ēthē ta tōn Thrakōn kai tous krymous apoleipōsi tous Thrakious hai geranoi, athroizontai men es ton Hebron]; <i>When the cranes leave the abodes and the cold of the</i> Thracians, <i>they gather at the</i> Hebrus. <i>Tzetzes</i>, Chiliad 4, <i>Hist.</i> 120:

‎Ὅταν γοῦν πρὸς τὸν Αἴγυπτον μέλλουσιν ἀποτρέχειν, Ὡς πρὸς τὸν Ἕβρον ποταμὸν τὸν Θρακικὸν ἐλθοῦσαι [Hotan goun pros ton Aigypton mellousin apotrechein, Hōs pros ton Hebron potamon ton Thrakikon elthousai]·

‎<i>When they are about to fly off to</i> Egypt, <i>having come to the</i> Hebrus, <i>a river of</i> Thrace.

‎But the <i>Scythian</i> cranes take another route, namely across the <i>Pontus</i>, which they fly over. Hence <i>Virgil</i>, <i>Aeneid</i>, book 6:

‎— — — <i>as when many birds flock to land from the deep sea, when the cold season drives them across the</i> Pontus <i>and sends them to sunny lands.</i>

‎And <i>it is certain</i>, says <i>Pliny</i>, <i>that when they are about to fly across the</i> Pontus, <i>they first of all make for the narrows between the two promontories</i> Criu-metopon <i>and</i> Carambis. These are only 170,000 paces apart, as he likewise asserts in book 4, chapter 12. [II, 619] Others indeed judge differently, namely that these two promontories are three days' sailing apart, or, what is nearly the same, 2,500 stadia. <i>Dionysius Periegetes</i>, line 155:

‎Αἵτ' ἄμφω ξυνίασιν ἐναντίαι, οὐ μὲν ἐοῦσαι Ἐγγύθεν, ἀλλ᾽ ὅσον ὁλκὰς ἐπὶ τρίτον ἦμαρ ἀνύσσῃ [Hait' amphō xyniasin enantiai, ou men eousai Engythen, all᾽ hoson holkas epi triton ēmar anyssē].

‎These <i>Priscian</i> rendered thus:

‎<i>So they come together facing each other, across so great a gulf</i> <i>Separated, as far as a ship could cross in three days.</i> — — —

‎And <i>Ammianus</i>, book 22: <i>Not far from there rises</i> Carambis <i>etc.</i> <i>Opposite which lies</i> Criu-metopon, <i>a promontory of the Taurica, separated from it by two thousand five hundred stadia.</i> <i>Strabo</i> too, in book 2: Τὸ μὲν οὖν τῆς Εὐρώπης ἀκρωτήριον καλεῖται Κριοῦ μέτωπον, τὸ δὲ τῆς Ἀσίας Κάραμβις, διέχοντα ἀλλήλων περὶ δισχιλίους σαδίους, καὶ πεντακοσίους [To men oun tēs Eurōpēs akrōtērion kaleitai Kriou metōpon, to de tēs Asias Karambis, diechonta allēlōn peri dischilious sadious, kai pentakosious]· <i>The promontory of</i> Europe <i>is called</i> Criu-metopon (ram's forehead), <i>and that of</i> Asia Carambis, <i>distant from each other about</i> 2,500 <i>stadia.</i> These statements, however, are utterly false, and are openly refuted by <i>Strabo</i>'s own words in book 3: Διέσηκε δὲ ἡ Κάραμβις τῆς μὲν τῶν Χερσονησιτῶν πόλεως σταδίους δισχιλίους, καὶ πεντακοσίους, τοῦ δὲ Κριοῦ μετώπου πολὺ ἐλάττους τὸν ἀριθμόν [Diesēke de hē Karambis tēs men tōn Chersonēsitōn poleōs stadious dischilious, kai pentakosious, tou de Kriou metōpou poly elattous ton arithmon]· <i>Carambis is distant from the city of the</i> Chersonesites <i>by two thousand five hundred stadia, but from</i> Criu-metopon <i>by far less.</i> Quite rightly: for <i>Chersonesus</i> the city is one thing, and <i>Chersonesus</i> the region, in which that city lies, another. The city, which is in the <i>northern</i> part of the <i>Chersonese</i>, is indeed 2,500 stadia distant from <i>Carambis</i>. But on the opposite side of the <i>Chersonese</i>, <i>Criu-metopon</i> is much nearer to <i>Carambis</i>. Yet these have been confused: what some had written about the city of <i>Chersonesus</i>, that it is 2,500 stadia from <i>Carambis</i>, was taken as if said of the whole <i>Chersonese</i>, and of its most <i>southern</i> part, namely <i>Criu-metopon.</i> The emptiness of this invention is especially apparent from what <i>Strabo</i> adds in the same place: Συχνοὶ γοῦν τῶν διαπλευσάντων τὸν πορθμὸν, ἅμα φασὶν ἀμφοτέρας ἰδεῖν τὰς ἄκρας ἑκατέρωθεν [Sychnoi goun tōn diapleusantōn ton porthmon, hama phasin amphoteras idein tas akras hekaterōthen]· <i>Many of those who have sailed through this strait say that they saw both promontories at once,</i> that is, when placed in the middle of the passage; which would not [col. 72] happen if the strait were 2,500 stadia wide. For the sight of the human eye is too weak to reach 1,250 stadia, which make more than 52 hourly leagues.

‎However that may be, the ancients relate that both in crossing this strait and on the rest of their journey the remarkable ingenuity of cranes shines forth. <i>Pliny</i> asserts that when about to fly across the <i>Pontus</i> they <i>are steadied with ballast</i>, and that <i>when they have passed the middle, the pebbles are dropped from their feet, and when they have reached the mainland, the sand from their throat.</i> That is, only then do they disgorge not pebbles but sand too. <i>Solinus</i> puts this more explicitly: <i>Lest the force of the winds should resist them as they proceed to their destination, they swallow sand, and taking up little pebbles they</i> [II, 620] <i>ballast themselves to a moderate weight.</i> And shortly after: <i>When they know that they have come to the middle</i> (of the Pontus), <i>they free their feet of the load of stones. So sailors have reported, who have often been rained on by a shower of stones from this cause. The sand they do not disgorge until they are secure of their abode.</i> <i>Cassiodorus</i> also mentions these pebbles, in <i>Variae</i> book 4, <i>Letter</i> 47: <i>Cranes, when about to cross the sea, clasp small pebbles in their hooked feet, so that their lightness may not be carried off by too strong a wind, nor their native speed be weighed down by unequal burdens.</i> The scholiast on <i>Aristophanes'</i> <i>Birds</i>, and <i>Suidas</i> following him, think that these pebbles are carried and thrown down by cranes for an entirely different reason: namely, so that from the sound of the falling stone they may perceive πότερον ἐπὶ γῆς, ἢ ἐπὶ θαλάσσης φέρωνται· καὶ εἰ μὲν ἐπὶ θαλάσσης ἥκοι ὁ λίθος ἀνύουσι τὴν ὁδόν· εἰ δὲ ἐπὶ γῆς, ἀναπαύονται [poteron epi gēs, ē epi thalassēs pherōntai; kai ei men epi thalassēs hēkoi ho lithos anyousi tēn hodon; ei de epi gēs, anapauontai], <i>whether they are borne over land or over sea; if the stone falls into the sea, they continue on; but if onto land, they rest.</i> But <i>Maximus of Tyre</i>, Oration 40, asserts that cranes carry this stone in their mouth as ballast. Οὐ πρότερον, he says, ἀνίπταται, πρὶν ξυλλάβῃ λίθον τῷ στόματι, ἕρμα μὲν εἶναι αὐτῇ πρὸς τὴν πτῆσιν, φυλακὴν δὲ πρὸς σωτηρίαν [Ou proteron … anuptatai, prin xyllabē lithon tō stomati, herma men einai autē pros tēn ptēsin, phylakēn de pros sōtērian]· <i>It does not fly before it has taken up a stone in its beak, to be a support for its flight and a safeguard for its safety.</i> Nor otherwise <i>Nonnus</i>, <i>Dionysiaca</i> book 40, line 521:

‎Αἱ στομάτων ἔκτοσθεν ἀοσσητῆρα κελεύθου Λᾶαν ἐλαφρίζουσι καταχθέα, μήποτε κείνων Ἱπταμένων πτερὰ κοῦφα παρακλάγξειεν ἀήτης. [Hai stomatōn ektosthen aossētēra keleuthou / Laan elaphrizousi katachthea, mēpote keinōn / Hiptamenōn ptera koupha paraklangxeien aētēs.]

‎<i>Which carry in their mouth a heavy stone as a helper on their way, lest the wind should ever make the light wings of them as they fly go astray.</i> And <i>Philes</i> [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)], chapter 10:

‎Τίς ἕρμα ποιεῖ τῇσι γεράνοις τὰς λίθους, Ἃς ἐγκαθειργνύουσι τοῖς παρισθμίοις, Πρὸς τὰς ὑπὲρ γῆς ἐμβολὰς τῶν πνευμάτων; [Tis herma poiei tēsi geranois tas lithous, / Has enkatheirgnyousi tois paristhmiois, / Pros tas hyper gēs embolas tōn pneumatōn?]

‎<i>Who has made the cranes carry stones as ballast, which they hold in their throats against the onslaughts of the winds that are upon the earth?</i> They wrongly read ἀμβολάς [ambolas]. For this is taken from these words of <i>Aelian</i>: Λίθον δὲ ἑκάστη καταπιοῦσα, ὡς ἔχειν καὶ δεῖπνον, καὶ πρὸς τὰς ἐμβολὰς τῶν ἀνέμων ἕρμα, πειρῶνται τοῦ μετοικισμοῦ· [Lithon de hekastē katapiousa, hōs echein kai deipnon, kai pros tas embolas tōn anemōn herma, peirōntai tou metoikismou] <i>Each one, having swallowed a stone to serve both as a meal and as ballast against the onslaughts of the winds, sets out on its migration.</i> Others keep saying that this stone is a touchstone for gold. The same <i>Aelian</i>, book 3, chapter 13: Γέρανος δὲ λίθον ὅνπερ οὖν καταπίνει πετομένη ὑπὲρ τοῦ ἔχειν ἕρμα, χρυσοῦ βάσανός ἐστιν· [Geranos de lithon honper oun katapinei petomenē hyper tou echein herma, chrysou basanos estin] <i>The stone which the crane swallows as ballast when about to fly is a touchstone for gold.</i> Accordingly <i>Philes</i> calls them

‎Λίθους κριτικὰς τοῦ χρυσοῦ τοῖς ἐμπόροις [Lithous kritikas tou chrysou tois emporois], [II, 621] <i>stones that test gold for merchants.</i> But <i>Aristotle</i> refutes this fiction as follows, <i>History of Animals</i>, book 8, chapter 12: Τὸ δὲ περὶ τοῦ λίθου ψεῦδός ἐστι· λέγεται γὰρ ὡς ἔχουσιν ἕρμα λίθον, ὃς γίνεται χρήσιμος πρὸς τὰς τοῦ χρυσοῦ βασάνους, ὅταν ἐκπέσῃ. [To de peri tou lithou pseudos esti; legetai gar hōs echousin herma lithon, hos ginetai chrēsimos pros tas tou chrysou basanous, hotan ekpesē.] <i>What they tell about the stone is false: namely, that they carry a stone as ballast which, when it has fallen out, is useful for testing gold.</i> But if this is a fiction, perhaps what follows now will be truer.

‎There is among them a wonderful harmony and agreement, a wonderful concord of minds. Pliny: <i>When they set out, they agree together.</i> Solinus: <i>All share a common care for the weary, so that, if any have failed, they all gather together and lift up the exhausted ones,</i> [col. 73] <i>until their strength is recovered by rest.</i> Note here the verb <i>congruere</i> [to come together], taken from cranes: it is properly used of cranes, and from them transferred to other things. <i>Festus</i>: Congruere <i>is said from cranes, which do not separate from one another, whether they are flying or feeding.</i> So also <i>ingruere</i> means <i>to rush upon in a great host like cranes.</i> From this it was transferred to soldiers, to rain, to hail and to other things. And in <i>Solinus</i> we read <i>fluctus ingrui</i> [waves rushing in].

‎Not without reason does <i>Oppian</i> speak of

‎— — ὑψιπετῆ γεράνων χορὸν [hypsipetē geranōn choron],

‎<i>the high-flying chorus of cranes</i>; and <i>Antipater of Sidon</i>

‎— — ὑψιπετῆ Βιστόνιαν γέρανον [hypsipetē Bistonian geranon],

‎spoke of <i>the high-flying</i> Bistonian <i>crane</i>. And <i>Virgil</i>:

‎<i>The airy cranes have fled,</i> — — —

‎that is, as <i>Servius</i> explains, <i>flying on high.</i> And <i>Ausonius</i> in the <i>Monosyllables</i>:

‎<i>When</i> Ibycus <i>perished, the high-flying crane was the informer.</i>

‎And the crane says of itself in <i>Avienus</i>, Fable 15:

‎— — — <i>Lofty on my wing into the air</i> <i>I am borne, next to the stars and the gods.</i>

‎For cranes fly on high, attentive to every change of the air, so as not to be overwhelmed by a storm. <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>History of Animals</i>, book 9, ch. 10: Εἰς ὕψος πέτονται πρὸς τὸ καθορᾶν τὰ πόῤῥω, καὶ ἐὰν ἴδωσι νέφη, καὶ χειμέρια [Eis hypsos petontai pros to kathoran ta porrhō, kai ean idōsi nephē, kai cheimeria] (<i>Antigonus</i> has καὶ συστροφὴν [kai systrophēn]) καταπτᾶσαι ἡσυχάζουσιν· [kataptasai hēsychazousin] <i>They fly on high in order to see far ahead; and if they see clouds and stormy weather</i> (or <i>a whirlwind</i>), <i>they fly down and stay quiet.</i> Accordingly it is among the signs of a coming storm when a sailor in mid-sea sees γεράνους ὑποστρεφούσας, καὶ τὴν ἔμπαλιν πετομένας [geranous hypostrephousas, kai tēn empalin petomenas], <i>cranes turning back and flying in the opposite direction.</i> So <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i>, book 3, chapter 14. You may see similar statements in <i>Aratus</i>, <i>Virgil</i>, <i>Pliny</i>, <i>Theon</i> and <i>Avienus</i>. But [II, 622] cranes also <i>strive toward the greatest heights, so that from a loftier watchtower they may survey the lands they are heading for.</i> So <i>Solinus</i> and <i>Isidore</i>. They also seem to strive for the heights because the air is calmer there, as <i>Horus</i> attests in these words, book 2, ch. 93: Ὑψηλῶς πάνυ ἵπτάται (γέρανος) ἵνα θεάσηται τὰ νέφη, μὴ ἄρα χειμάζῃ, ἵνα ἐν ἡσυχίᾳ διαμένῃ· [Hypsēlōs pany hiptatai (geranos) hina theasētai ta nephē, mē ara cheimazē, hina en hēsychia diamenē] <i>The crane flies very high in order to observe the clouds, so that it may not be tossed by a storm but remain at rest.</i> Finally, <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] gives this fourth reason: فاذا طارت ترفعت في الهوا جدا كي لا يعرض لها شي من السباع الطير [fa-idhā ṭārat taraffaʿat fī al-hawā jiddan kay lā yaʿriḍ lahā shay min al-sibāʿ al-ṭayr] <i>And when it flies aloft, it rises very high, so that none of the birds of prey may meet it.</i>

‎In flight itself the flock has the form of a triangle, which, driven at its base by the winds, cuts the air with its apex. <i>Cicero</i>, book 2 <i>On the Nature of the Gods</i>: <i>"This too was observed by</i> Aristotle: <i>that cranes, when they cross the seas seeking warmer regions, form the shape of a triangle. With its apex the air in front of them is pushed aside. Then gradually, on either side, the course of the birds is borne up by their wings as by oars. And the base of the triangle which the cranes form is helped by the winds, as if from the stern."</i> <i>Plutarch</i>, in the book <i>Whether Land or Sea Animals</i>, etc.: Εἰς τρίγωνον συνάγουσαι σχίζουσι τῇ κορυφῇ τὸ πνεῦμα περιῤῥέον, ὥστε μὴ διασπᾶσθαι τὴν τάξιν· [Eis trigōnon synagousai schizousi tē koryphē to pneuma perirrheon, hōste mē diaspasthai tēn taxin] <i>"Gathered into a triangle, they cut with its apex the wind flowing around them, so that their line is not broken up."</i> Aelian, book 13, ch. 13: Τρίγωνον ὀξύγονον τὸ σχῆμα τῆς πτήσεως ἀποφαίνουσι, ἵνα ἐμπίπτουσαι τῷ ἀέρι διακόπτωσιν αὐτόν· [Trigōnon oxygonon to schēma tēs ptēseōs apophainousi, hina empiptousai tō aeri diakoptōsin auton] <i>"They shape their flight into an acute-angled triangle, so that, striking into the air, they may cut it more easily."</i> <i>Philes</i> also asserts that they fly

‎Εἰς ὀρμαθὸν τρίστοιχον ὀξυγωνίου, [Eis hormathon tristoichon oxygōniou]

‎<i>"in a three-sided formation with acute angles."</i> And <i>Tzetzes</i>:

‎Τρίγωνον ὀξυγώνιον ποιοῦνται τούτων πτῆσιν, [Trigōnon oxygōnion poiountai toutōn ptēsin] <sup>a)</sup> [col. 74] Ὡς τὸν ἀέρα τέμνωσιν οὕτως εὐμαρεστέρως, [Hōs ton aera temnōsin houtōs eumaresterōs]

‎<i>"They make their flight an acute-angled triangle, so that they may thus divide the air more easily."</i> Indeed, by this triangular figure they are thought to imitate certain letters, namely A, V, Δ, Λ, Υ. Hence <i>Jerome</i>, Epist. 4 to <i>Rusticus</i>: <i>"Cranes follow one leader in a lettered order."</i> So too <i>Isidore</i>. This sheds light on these lines of <i>Lucan</i> on cranes, book 5, line 712:

‎— — — <i>and in their first flight</i> <i>they form various figures, as chance directs;</i> <i>soon, when</i> Notus <i>strikes their outstretched wings from higher up,</i> [II, 623] <i>they are heedlessly mingled and massed into confused circles,</i> <i>and the letter, thrown into disorder, perishes as their feathers scatter.</i>

‎And <i>Martial</i> in the <i>Xenia</i>:

‎<i>You will throw the lines into disorder, and the letter will not fly whole,</i> <i>if you lose a single bird of</i> Palamedes.

‎And on the name of <i>Earinus</i>, book 9, <i>Epigram</i> 14:

‎<i>Which cranes, with writing wing, raise to the stars.</i>

‎And <i>Symphosius</i>, <i>Riddle</i> 36, where the crane says:

‎<i>I am a letter of the sky, written out by a flying wing.</i>

‎I would prefer <i>perscripta volante</i> [written out by (me) flying]. And <i>Claudian</i>, in <i>The Gildonic War</i>, where he speaks of cranes:

‎<i>In varied ranks a huge letter is woven across the clouds,</i> <i>and the air is inscribed with the marks of their wings.</i>

‎And <i>Cassiodorus</i>, <i>Variae</i> book 8, <i>Epistle</i> 12: <i>"For even today cranes, which form a fleet together, trace the shapes of the alphabet, nature instructing them."</i> And in <i>Philostratus' Heroicus</i>, <i>Ulysses</i>, seeing cranes flying in their usual manner, looked at <i>Palamedes</i> and said: Αἱ γέρανοι, ἔφη, μαρτύρονται τοὺς Ἀχαιοὺς, ὅτι αὗται γράμματα εὗρον, οὐχὶ σύ· [Hai geranoi, ephē, martyrontai tous Achaious, hoti hautai grammata heuron, ouchi sy] <i>"The cranes," he said, "testify</i> to the Greeks <i>that they, not you, invented letters."</i> I would read τοῖς Ἀχαιοῖς [tois Achaiois], not τοὺς Ἀχαιούς [tous Achaious]. And <i>Nazianzen</i>, Oration 34: Τίνος Παλαμήδους τακτικὰ, κινήματά τε, καὶ σχήματα γεράνων, ὡς πᾶσι, καὶ ταῦτα παιδεύματα, κινουμένων ἐν τάξει, καὶ μετὰ ποικίλης τῆς πτήσεως; [Tinos Palamēdous taktika, kinēmata te, kai schēmata geranōn, hōs pasi, kai tauta paideumata, kinoumenōn en taxei, kai meta poikilēs tēs ptēseōs] <i>"Of what</i> Palamedes <i>are the ordered movements, figures and precepts of the cranes, by which, as they say, they move in battle line with varied flight?"</i> The same birds, when an eagle is sighted, <sup>b)</sup> κολπωσάμεναι, μηνοειδὲς τὸ μέσον ἀποφήνασαι, ἀπειλοῦσιν ὡς ἀντιταξόμεναι, [kolpōsamenai, mēnoeides to meson apophēnasai, apeilousin hōs antitaxomenai] <i>"opening a hollow so that they imitate the appearance of the crescent moon, threaten as if about to resist"</i>; and the eagle retreats and checks its flight.

‎They have leaders at the head of the column and οὐραγοὺς [ouragous] at the rear, whom <i>the Philosopher</i> describes as ἐπισυρίττοντας ἐν τοῖς ἐσχάτοις, ὥστε ἀκούεσθαι τὴν φωνὴν [episyrittontas en tois eschatois, hōste akouesthai tēn phōnēn], <i>whistling among the hindmost, so that their voice can be heard.</i> Hence <i>Pliny: They choose a leader to follow. At the rear of the column they have birds posted to call out in turn and to keep the flock together with their voice.</i> And <i>Solinus</i>: <i>Confident of its course, it goes before the flocks and rebukes sluggishness in flight with its voice, which keeps the column together. When that voice has grown hoarse, another takes its place.</i> <i>Aelian</i> asserts that the older birds, experienced in the routes, are ἡγεμόνας τῆς πτήσεως [hēgemonas tēs ptēseōs], <i>leaders of the flight</i>, and that they οὐραγεῖν [ouragein], that is, <i>command the rear of the column</i>, while the younger ones are in the middle. [II, 624] Especially remarkable is the way they help one another in flight. Cicero: <i>They rest their necks and heads on the backs of those flying in front. Because the leader himself cannot do this, since he has nothing to lean on, he flies back so that he too may rest. His place is taken by the nearest of those that have been resting, and this alternation is kept up throughout the whole journey.</i> <i>Basil</i>, <i>Hexaëmeron</i>, Homily 8: Ἄλλό τε γὰρ ἄλλη τὴν ὁδηγίαν ἐκδέχεται, καὶ τακτόν τινα χρόνον προκαθηγησαμένη τῆς πτήσεως, εἰς τὸ κατόπιν περιελθοῦσα, τῇ μεθ᾽ ἑαυτὴν τὴν ἡγεμονίαν τῆς ὁδοῦ παραδίδωσι [Allo te gar allē tēn hodēgian ekdechetai, kai takton tina chronon prokathēgēsamenē tēs ptēseōs, eis to katopin perielthousa, tē meth᾽ heautēn tēn hēgemonian tēs hodou paradidōsi]. Hence <i>Ambrose</i>, <i>Hexaëmeron</i>, book 5, chapter 15: <i>One goes before the rest for a set time and, as it were, runs ahead of the standards; then it turns back and yields the office of leading the column to the one following.</i> <i>Cassiodorus</i>, book 9, <i>Letter</i> 2: <i>Their flight, too, is arranged by an equal alternation: the last becomes first, and the one that held first place does not refuse to be behind.</i> Damir the <i>Arab</i>: يقدمها واحد منها كالرايس لها وهي [yuqaddimuhā wāḥid minhā ka-l-rāyis lahā wa-hiya] [col. 75] تتبعه ويكون ذلك حينا ثم يخلفه اخر منها مقدما حتي يصير الذي كان مقدما موخرا [tatbaʿuhu wa-yakūnu dhālika ḥīnan thumma yakhlufuhu ākhar minhā muqaddaman ḥattā yaṣīra alladhī kāna muqaddaman muʾakhkharan] <i>One of them goes ahead, acting as leader, and the others follow, and this for a time. Then another from their number takes its place, until the one that had been first becomes last.</i>

‎The same alternation is observed in keeping watch. For when, tired from flying, they come down to the ground to rest and sleep, while the rest sleep, one or two, or a few out of the whole number, keep watch in turn over the entire flock. <i>Aristotle</i>, book 9, ch. 10: Ὅταν δὲ καθίζωνται, αἱ μὲν ἄλλαι, ὑπὸ τῇ πτέρυγι τὴν κεφαλὴν ἔχουσαι, καθεύδουσιν ἐπὶ ἑνὸς ποδὸς ἐναλλάξ, ὁ δὲ ἡγεμὼν γυμνὴν ἔχων τὴν κεφαλὴν προορᾷ, καὶ ὅταν αἴσθηταί τι, σημαίνει βοῶν [Hotan de kathizōntai, hai men allai, hypo tē pterygi tēn kephalēn echousai, katheudousin epi henos podos enallax, ho de hēgemōn gymnēn echōn tēn kephalēn proora, kai hotan aisthētai ti, sēmainei boōn], <i>When they settle, the others sleep with the head hidden under the wing, standing on one foot and then the other. But the leader</i> (that is, the watchman) <i>looks out with his head uncovered, and signals by his voice what he has perceived. Antigonus</i> follows the same in the <i>Mirabilia</i>, ch. 46, and so does <i>Alkazuinius</i> with the <i>Auctarium</i>: واذا ارادت النوم ادخل كل واحد راسه تحت جناحها لعلمها ان الجناح احمل للصدمة من الراس لما فيه من العين التي هي اشرف الاعضا والدماغ الذي هو ملاك البدن ونام كل واحد منها قايما علي احدي رجليه حتي لا يكون نومها ثقيل واما قايدها وحارسها فلا ينام ولا يدخل راسه في جناحه ولا يزال ينظر من جميع الجوانب فان احس باحد صاح باعلا صوته فاخبر اصحابه بالعدو [wa-idhā arādat al-nawm adkhala kull wāḥid raʾsahu taḥt janāḥihā li-ʿilmihā anna al-janāḥ aḥmal li-l-ṣadma min al-raʾs li-mā fīhi min al-ʿayn allatī hiya ashraf al-aʿḍā wa-l-dimāgh alladhī huwa milāk al-badan wa-nāma kull wāḥid minhā qāyiman ʿalā iḥdā rijlayhi ḥattā lā yakūn nawmuhā thaqīl wa-ammā qāyiduhā wa-ḥārisuhā fa-lā yanām wa-lā yudkhil raʾsahu fī janāḥihi wa-lā yazāl yanẓur min jamīʿ al-jawānib fa-in aḥassa bi-aḥad ṣāḥa bi-aʿlā ṣawtihi fa-akhbara aṣḥābahu bi-l-ʿaduww] <i>When they wish to sleep, they put their heads</i> [II, 625] <i>under their wings, because they know that the wings bear a blow better than the head, in which is the eye, the noblest of the parts, and the brain, on which the rest of the body depends. They sleep, moreover, standing on one foot, so that their sleep may not be heavy. But the one who is leader and guard abstains from sleep and does not hide his head under his wings. Rather, he continually looks out on every side. And if he perceives anyone, he cries out with a loud voice to signal to his companions that an enemy is near.</i> Others add two further points. The first is that cranes keep watch in turn. <i>Horus</i>, book 2, ch. 90: Αὗται γὰρ ἑαυτὰς φυλάττουσι γρηγοροῦσαι κατ᾽ ὄρδινον ἐν πάσῃ τῇ νυκτί [Hautai gar heautas phylattousi grēgorousai kat᾽ ordinon en pasē tē nykti]. <i>They guard themselves, keeping watch in order through the whole night.</i> Solinus: <i>They divide the night watches, so that every tenth one is sleepless.</i> Isidore: <i>At night they divide the watches, and keep the order of the vigils by turns.</i> Cassiodorus: <i>They watch in turn, and guard themselves by common caution.</i> Basil: Τοῦ καιροῦ τῆς φυλακῆς πληρομένου, ἡ μὲν βοήσασα πρὸς ὕπνον ἐτράπετο. ἡ δὲ τὴν διαδοχὴν ὑποδεξαμένη, ἧς ἔτυχεν ἀσφαλείας, [col. 76] ἀντέδωκεν ἐν τῷ μέρει [Tou kairou tēs phylakēs plēromenou, hē men boēsasa pros hypnon etrapeto. hē de tēn diadochēn hypodexamenē, hēs etychen asphaleias, antedōken en tō merei]. <i>Ambrose</i> expands this as follows: <i>When the time of the watch has been completed, having discharged its duty it settles down to sleep after first giving a cry, so as to wake the sleeper to whom it is to hand over its turn of duty. And that one willingly takes up its lot, and does not, as is our habit, renounce sleep unwillingly and sluggishly. Rather, it briskly shakes itself from its bed, performs its turn, and repays the favour it received with equal care and service.</i> And <i>Damir</i>: في طبعه الخوف والتحارس بالنوبة فاذا قضي نوبتة قام الذي كان نايما يحرس مكانه حتي تقضي كله ما يلزمها من الحراسة [fī ṭabʿihi al-khawf wa-l-taḥārus bi-l-nawba fa-idhā qaḍā nawbatahu qāma alladhī kāna nāyiman yaḥrus makānahu ḥattā taqḍī kulluhu mā yalzamuhā min al-ḥirāsa] <i>It is in its nature to be fearful and to keep guard by turns. And when one has completed its turn, one of those that were sleeping rises to keep watch in its place, until all have kept the watches incumbent on them.</i> The second is that the watchmen carry a stone in the foot, which by its fall wakes them when asleep or about to fall asleep. Pliny: <i>They keep watches at night, holding a pebble in the foot, which, loosened by sleep and falling, betrays their negligence.</i> Solinus: <i>The watchmen grasp small weights in their toes, which, if they should happen to fall, betray sleep.</i> Plutarch: Αἱ προφυλακὴν ἔχουσαι νυκτὸς, ἐπὶ θατέρου σκέλους ὀχοῦνται τὸ σῶμα, τῷ δ᾽ ἑτέρῳ ποδὶ λίθον περιλαβοῦσαι κρατοῦσι· συνέχει γὰρ ὁ τῆς ἀφῆς τόνος ἐν τῷ μὴ καθεύδειν πολὺν χρόνον. Ὅταν δὲ ἀνῶσιν, ἐκπεσὼν ὁ λίθος ταχὺ διήγειρε τὴν προεμένην [Hai prophylakēn echousai nyktos, epi thaterou skelous ochountai to sōma, tō d᾽ heterō podi lithon perilabousai kratousi; synechei gar ho tēs haphēs tonos en tō mē katheudein polyn chronon. Hotan de anōsin, ekpesōn ho lithos tachy diēgeire tēn proemenēn]. <i>Those that keep watch at night support the body on one of their legs, and hold a pebble grasped in the other foot. The effort of gripping holds off sleep for some time. But as soon as they relax, the falling stone at once wakes the negligent one.</i> Aelian [III, 13.]: Ἑστᾶσι μὲν ἀσκωλιάζουσαι, τῷ γε μὲν μετεώρῳ ποδὶ λίθον κατέχουσι τοῖς ὄνυξι μάλα ἐγκρατῶς τε καὶ εὐλαβῶς· ἵνα ἐάν ποτε λάθωσιν ἑαυτὰς εἰς ὕπνον ὑπολισθαίνουσαι, πεσὼν καὶ ὑποκτυπήσας ὁ λίθος, ἀποδαρθάνειν καταναγκάσῃ [Hestasi men askōliazousai, tō ge men meteōrō podi lithon katechousi tois onyxi mala enkratōs te kai eulabōs; hina ean pote lathōsin heautas eis hypnon hypolisthainousai, pesōn kai hypoktypēsas ho lithos, apodarthanein katanankasē]. <i>They rest on one foot only, and in the other, which is raised, they hold a pebble</i> [II, 626] <i>with their claws very firmly and with the greatest care, so that, if sleep should ever steal upon them, the falling pebble may wake them with its noise.</i> Philes and <i>Tzetzes</i> say the same. Accordingly, when <i>Aelian</i> says that cranes swallow pebbles ὡς ἔχειν καὶ δεῖπνον, καὶ πρὸς τὰς ἐμβολὰς τῶν ἀνέμων ἕρμα [hōs echein kai deipnon, kai pros tas embolas tōn anemōn herma], <i>so as to have them both for a meal and as ballast against the onslaught of the winds</i>, one should consider whether instead of δεῖπνον [deipnon] we ought to read δι᾽ ὕπνον [di᾽ hypnon], <i>on account of sleep.</i> For that pebbles should serve cranes as a meal is quite absurd. But perhaps <i>Aelian</i> meant that stones are swallowed by cranes not only to serve as ballast when they are about to fly across the <i>Pontus</i>, but also so that, once the sea has been crossed, they may be vomited up and serve to ward off sleep in the manner we have described.

‎The battles of the cranes with the <i>Pygmies</i> near the <i>southern Ocean</i> and the sources of the <i>Nile</i>, about which many writers since <i>Homer</i> have spun many tales, we gladly pass over. Yet the <i>Philosopher</i> asserts that in that fable at least this much is true, that very small men live in those places, <i>Hist.</i> book 8, ch. 12: Μεταβάλλουσιν, he says, αἱ γέρανοι ἐκ τῶν Σκυθικῶν εἰς τὰ ἕλη τὰ ἄνω τῆς Αἰγύπτου, ὅθεν ὁ Νεῖλος ῥεῖ. Ἔστι δὲ ὁ τόπος οὗτος, περὶ ὃν οἱ Πυγμαῖοι κατοικοῦσιν. Οὐ γάρ ἐστι τοῦτο μῦθος, ἀλλ᾽ ἔστι κατὰ τὴν ἀλήθειαν γένος μικρὸν μὲν, ὥσπερ λέγεται, καὶ αὐτοὶ, καὶ οἱ ἵπποι. Τρωγλοδύται δ᾽ εἰσὶ τὸν βίον. [Metaballousin hai geranoi ek tōn Skythikōn eis ta helē ta anō tēs Aigyptou, hothen ho Neilos rhei. Esti de ho topos houtos, peri hon hoi Pygmaioi katoikousin. Ou gar esti touto mythos, all᾽ esti kata tēn alētheian genos mikron men, hōsper legetai, kai autoi, kai hoi hippoi. Trōglodytai d᾽ eisi ton bion.] <i>From the</i> Scythian <i>regions the cranes migrate to the marshes that lie above</i> Egypt, <i>whence the</i> Nile <i>flows. Around this place the</i> Pygmies <i>live. For this is no fable; in truth, as they say, there is a small race there, both of men and of horses. They lead a Troglodyte life</i>, that is, they live in caves under the earth. Everyone knows that the true sources of the <i>Nile</i> were entirely unknown to the ancients. Here, therefore, by the name <i>Nile</i> is meant the <i>Astaboras</i>, which [col. 77] the ancients believed to be a branch of the <i>Nile</i>, as we have shown elsewhere. The <i>Astaboras</i> rises in places nearest to the <i>Avalites</i> gulf and the trading-post of <i>Mosylon</i>. That is why <i>Aethicus</i> places the sources of the <i>Nile</i> there: <i>And the river</i> Nile, he says, <i>which seems to emerge from the shore of the beginning of the</i> Red <i>Sea, in the place called the</i> Mossylon <i>trading-post.</i> And indeed <i>Nonnosus</i>, sailing about these places on his way from the <i>Homerites</i> to the <i>Axumites</i>, Ἐνέτυχέ τισι μορφὴν μὲν καὶ ἰδέαν ἔχουσιν ἀνθρωπίνην, βραχυτάτοις δὲ τὸ μέγεθος, καὶ μέλασι τὴν χρόαν· ὑπὸ δὲ τριχῶν δεδασυμμένοις διὰ παντὸς τοῦ σώματος· εἵποντο δὲ τοῖς ἀνδράσι καὶ γυναῖκες παραπλήσιαι, καὶ παιδάρια ἔτι βραχύτερα. [Enetyche tisi morphēn men kai idean echousin anthrōpinēn, brachytatois de to megethos, kai melasi tēn chroan; hypo de trichōn dedasymmenois dia pantos tou sōmatos; heiponto de tois andrasi kai gynaikes paraplēsiai, kai paidaria eti brachytera.] <i>came upon certain people, human indeed in form and shape, but very short in stature, black in colour, and hairy over the whole body. The men were followed by women of nearly the same size, and by children still shorter.</i> So <i>Photius</i> in the <i>Excerpts</i> from the history of <i>Nonnosus</i>, who lived under <i>Justinian</i>. Moreover, in <i>Hesychius</i> Νῶβαι [Nōbai] are Πυγμαῖοι [Pygmaioi]: the <i>Nobae</i>, that is, the <i>Nubae</i>, who live in those places. And <i>Strabo</i>, book 17, on the <i>Ethiopians</i> below <i>Egypt</i>: τά τε βοσκήματα αὐτοῖς ἐστι μικρὰ πρόβατα, καὶ αἶγες, καὶ βόες, καὶ κύνες μικροί· τραχεῖς δὲ καὶ μάχιμοι οἰκοῦντες μικροὶ ὄντες. Τάχα δὲ τοὺς Πυγμαίους ἀπὸ τῆς τούτων μικροφυΐας ἐπενόησαν καὶ ἀνέπλασαν· ἑωρακὼς [II, 627] μὲν γὰρ οὐδεὶς ἐξηγεῖται τῶν πίστεως ἀξίων ἀνδρῶν. [ta te boskēmata autois esti mikra probata, kai aiges, kai boes, kai kynes mikroi; tracheis de kai machimoi oikountes mikroi ontes. Tacha de tous Pygmaious apo tēs toutōn mikrophyias epenoēsan kai aneplasan; heōrakōs men gar oudeis exēgeitai tōn pisteōs axiōn andrōn.] <i>Their livestock are small sheep and goats, oxen too, and small dogs. The inhabitants themselves are also tiny, yet rough and warlike. And perhaps on account of their natural smallness</i> (the ancients of those parts) <i>invented the</i> Pygmies. <i>For no one worthy of credit has seen them.</i> That is, Pygmies such as they are imagined to be, three spans tall, such as not even <i>Homer</i> had described. But this invention has as its authors the most fabulous of writers, <i>Megasthenes</i> and <i>Daimachus</i>, of whom <i>Strabo</i> says in book 2: Ἐνεκαίνισαν δὲ καὶ τὴν Ὁμηρικὴν τῶν Πυγμαίων γερανομαχίαν τρισπιθάμους εἴποντες· [Enekainisan de kai tēn Homērikēn tōn Pygmaiōn geranomachian trispithamous eipontes;] <i>They embellished the</i> Homeric <i>battle of the</i> Pygmies <i>with the cranes, saying that they were three spans tall.</i> The <i>Arabs</i> too report the same on the word of a certain <i>little Greek</i>, who is said to have told it thus to <i>Jacob</i>, son of <i>Isaac</i>, <i>of Siraf</i>: ركبت في بحر الزنج والقتني الريح الي بعض الجزاير فوصلت منها الي مدينة اهلها ناس قاماتهم قدم نراع واكثرهم عور فاجتمع علي منهم جمع وانتهوا بي الي ملكهم فامر بحبسي فحبست في شبه قفص ثم رايتهم في بعض الايام يستعدون القتال وقالوا لنا عدو ياتينا في هذا الاوان فلم يلبث الي ان طلعت عليهم عصابة من الغرانيق وكان عورهم من نقرها اعينهم فاخذت عصي وشددت عليها وهربت واكرموني لذلك [rakibtu fī baḥr al-Zanj wa-alqatnī al-rīḥ ilā baʿḍ al-jazāyir fa-waṣaltu minhā ilā madīna ahluhā nās qāmātuhum qadm nrāʿ wa-aktharuhum ʿūr fa-ijtamaʿa ʿalayya minhum jamʿ wa-intahaw bī ilā malikihim fa-amara bi-ḥabsī fa-ḥubistu fī shibh qafaṣ thumma raʾaytuhum fī baʿḍ al-ayyām yastaʿiddūn al-qitāl wa-qālū lanā ʿaduww yaʾtīnā fī hādhā al-awān fa-lam yalbath ilā an ṭalaʿat ʿalayhim ʿiṣāba min al-gharānīq wa-kāna ʿawaruhum min naqrihā aʿyunahum fa-akhadhtu ʿaṣā wa-shadadtu ʿalayhā wa-harabat wa-akramūnī li-dhālik] <i>I was once sailing on the</i> Zanj <i>sea, and the wind drove me to a certain island. When I reached its town, I found the inhabitants to be a cubit tall, and most of them one-eyed. A crowd of them gathered around me and led me to</i> [col. 78] <i>their king. He ordered that I be held captive, and I was thrown into a kind of cage. When one day I saw them preparing for war, they said that an enemy was threatening and would soon fall upon them. Not long after, an army of cranes rose up against them. And they were one-eyed because the cranes had pecked out their eyes. But I took a stick and charged at them, and they flew off and fled. For this deed I was held in honour among them.</i>

‎<i>The Philosopher</i> (<i>Hist.</i> book 8, ch. 12) writes that cranes migrate from <i>Thrace</i> τοῦ Μαιμακτηριῶνος [tou Maimaktēriōnos], <i>in the month Maemacterion</i>; and <i>Aelian</i>, book 3, ch. 13, Φθινοπώρου ἤδη μεσοῦντος [Phthinopōrou ēdē mesountos], <i>in mid-autumn</i>. This strongly confirms what <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] maintained against <i>Gaza</i>, and <i>Samuel Petit</i> against <i>Petavius</i> in his <i>Eclogae chronologicae</i>, book 4: that among the <i>Attic</i> months <i>Maemacterion</i> is not the first but the second of the <i>autumn</i> months. <i>Aelian</i> defines the time of their return thus: Ὅταν ὑπεύδια ἄρξηται, καὶ εἰρηναῖα τοῦ ἀέρος ὑποστρέφουσιν ὀπίσω [Hotan hypeudia arxētai, kai eirēnaia tou aeros hypostrephousin opisō]; <i>when the fair weather begins and the calm of the air [II, 628] returns, they come back.</i> And <i>Maximus of Tyre</i>: Αἱ γέρανοι ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ὥραθέρους ἀφισάμεναι, οὐκ ἀνεχόμεναι τὸ θάλπος, τείνασαι πτέρυγας ὥσπερ ἱςία, φέρονται διὰ τοῦ ἀέρος εὐθὺ τῶν Σκυθῶν γῆς [Hai geranoi ex Aigyptou hōratherous aphisamenai, ouk anechomenai to thalpos, teinasai pterygas hōsper histia, pherontai dia tou aeros euthy tōn Skythōn gēs]; <i>The cranes, departing from</i> Egypt <i>in summer because they cannot endure the heat, spread their wings like sails and are carried straight through the air to the</i> Scythian <i>region.</i> Accordingly <i>Pliny</i> calls them <i>summer visitors</i>. I take this in such a way that under the name of <i>summer</i> I also understand <i>spring</i>. On these lines of <i>Theocritus, Idyll</i> 10,

‎Ἁ αἶξ τὸν κύτισον, ὁ λύκος τὰν αἶγα διώκει, Ἁ γέρανος τ᾽ ὤροτρον, — — — [Ha aix ton kytison, ho lykos tan aiga diōkei, Ha geranos t᾽ ōrotron]

‎<i>The goat follows the clover, the wolf the goat, the crane the plough</i>, the <i>Scholiast</i> says: Ἀρχομένου γὰρ σπόρου αἱ γέρανοι φαίνονται [Archomenou gar sporou hai geranoi phainontai], <i>For cranes appear at the beginning of sowing.</i> This I do not understand, since cranes do not arrive at sowing time but depart. For the migration of cranes falls in <i>autumn</i>, which is the customary time of sowing. <i>Virgil</i>, <i>Georgics</i> book 1:

‎<i>When Libra has made the hours of day and sleep equal, and now divides the globe in the middle between light and shade, work your bulls, men, sow barley in the fields.</i>

‎On these words <i>Servius</i> says: <i>He says that grain is to be sown in the</i> autumn <i>season.</i> So the <i>Greekling</i> who wrote these <i>Scholia</i> is talking nonsense. <i>Aristophanes</i> is therefore διαῤῥήδην [diarrhēdēn] opposed to him. For in the <i>Birds</i> he bids us

‎Σπείρειν μὲν ὅταν γέρανος κρώζουσ᾽ ἐς τὴν Λιβύην μεταχωρῇ, [Speirein men hotan geranos krōzous᾽ es tēn Libyēn metachōrē]

‎<i>Sow when the crane migrates, crying, to</i> Libya. The <i>Scholiast</i> notes that this is taken from <i>Hesiod's Works</i>, line 446:

‎Φράζεσθαι δ᾽ εὖ τ᾽ ἂν φωνὴν γεράνου ἐπακούσης, Ὑψόθεν ἐκ νεφέων ἐνιαύσια κεκληγυίης, Ἡτ᾽ ἀρότοιό τε σῆμα φέρει, καὶ χείματος ὥρην Δεικνύει ὀμβρηροῦ· — — — [Phrazesthai d᾽ eu t᾽ an phōnēn geranou epakousēs, Hypsothen ek nepheōn eniausia keklēgyiēs, Hēt᾽ arotoio te sēma pherei, kai cheimatos hōrēn Deiknyei ombrērou]

‎<i>Take heed when you hear the voice of the migrating crane, crying every year from on high in the clouds, which gives the signal for ploughing and shows the season of rainy winter.</i>

‎From what has been said about the migration of cranes it is clear that the <i>Prophet</i> not without reason sets them forth as an example of natural prudence. <i>The swallow</i>, he says, <i>and the crane have kept the time of their coming; but my people has not known the judgment of the Lord.</i> Accordingly <i>the Philosopher</i> (<i>Hist.</i> book 9, ch. 10) begins his account of the migration of cranes thus: Φρόνιμα δὲ πολλὰ καὶ περὶ τὰς γεράνους δοκεῖ συμβαίνειν [Phronima de polla kai peri tas geranous dokei symbainein]; <i>Many things also seem to be done prudently by cranes.</i> And <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 3, chapter 14, maintains that men learned τὸ κυβερνητικὸν [to kybernētikon], <i>the art of steering</i>, from them. And <i>Cassiodorus</i> holds that <i>Mercury</i>, [II, 629] <i>Philostratus</i> that <i>Palamedes</i>, had cranes as teachers. Accordingly among the <i>Egyptians</i>, as <i>Horus</i> reports, a watchful crane signified a cautious man, and a flying crane ἄνθρωπον εἰδότα τὰ μετέωρα [anthrōpon eidota ta meteōra], <i>a man skilled in lofty matters</i>. [col. 79] And among the <i>Arabs</i> one who is very cautious is called, in a common proverb, احرس من الكركي [aḥras min al-kurkī], <i>more cautious than a crane</i>.

‎Nor should anyone wonder that the crane is among the clean birds rather than the stork, a bird very like it, since the stork feeds on serpents, the crane on crops and seeds. An old author in <i>Suidas</i>: Γέρανοι γεωργοῦ κατενέμοντο χώραν ἐσπαρμένην νεωστὶ πυρίνῳ σίτῳ [Geranoi geōrgou katenemonto chōran esparmenēn neōsti pyrinō sitō], <i>Cranes were grazing on a farmer's field newly sown with wheat.</i> <i>Aelian</i>, book 2, ch. 1: Σπείραντας δὲ τοὺς Αἰγυπτίους καταλαμβάνουσι, καὶ τράπεζαν, ὡς ἂν εἴποις, ἄφθονον τὴν ἐν ταῖς ἀρούραις εὑρόντες, εἶτα ἄκλητοι ξενίων μεταλαγχάνουσιν [Speirantas de tous Aigyptious katalambanousi, kai trapezan, hōs an eipois, aphthonon tēn en tais arourais heurontes, eita aklētoi xeniōn metalanchanousin]; <i>They come upon the</i> Egyptians <i>as they are sowing, and finding in the fields a table, so to speak, abounding in food, they enjoy, uninvited, the rights of hospitality.</i> Hence <i>Mela</i> writes that the <i>Pygmies</i> fought against the cranes for their sown crops; and <i>Antipater of Sidon</i>, as cited above, in the Epitaph of a Fowler:

‎Ὁ πρὶν ἐγὼ καὶ ψῆρα καὶ ἁρπάκτειραν ἐρύκων Σπέρματος ὑψιπετῆ Βιστόνιαν γέρανον. [Ho prin egō kai psēra kai harpakteiran erykōn Spermatos hypsipetē Bistonian geranon]

‎<i>I, who formerly kept the starling and the high-flying</i> Bistonian <i>crane away from the seed</i> (that is, <i>from the grains</i>). Cranes, then, will have been clean by the definition of <i>Aristeas</i>, who counts as clean τῶν πτηνῶν τὰ πυροῖς καὶ ὀσπρίοις χρώμενα πρὸς τὴν τροφὴν [tōn ptēnōn ta pyrois kai ospriois chrōmena pros tēn trophēn], <i>those birds which are nourished on wheat and pulse.</i> What is more, among the ancient <i>Greeks</i> cranes were not only eaten but even counted among the delicacies, especially around <i>Thessaly</i>, where the wealthier had on their estates their own γερανοβοτίας [geranobotias] or γερανοβοσίας [geranobosias], that is, <i>crane pastures</i>, which <i>Pollux</i> mentions, following <i>Plato</i> in the <i>Statesman</i>. And in the <i>Balearic</i> islands the <i>vipio</i>, as they called the smaller crane, was held in honour at table. So Pliny, book 10, ch. 49. The same author wonders why <i>Cornelius Nepos</i>, writing under <i>Augustus</i>, said that <i>storks were preferred to cranes</i>, <i>since this bird is now</i>, he says, <i>sought after among the very first, while no one would want to touch the other.</i> Hence Horace, in the dinner of <i>Nasidienus</i>, at which <i>Maecenas</i> was present, book 2, <i>Satire</i> 8:

‎— — — — <i>then followed</i> <i>slaves carrying on a great</i> platter <i>the carved limbs of a crane, sprinkled with much salt, not without meal.</i>

‎And Ovid, treating of the frugality of the ancients, <i>Fasti</i> book 6:

‎<i>Nor did</i> Latium <i>know the bird that rich</i> Ionia <i>supplies,</i> <i>nor the one that delights in</i> Pygmy <i>blood.</i> [II, 630] That is, the francolin and the crane were not yet among the delicacies of the <i>Romans</i>. And <i>Statius</i> too, in <i>Hercules Vindex</i>, censures thus the gluttons of his own time, whose palate was more learned than was fitting:

‎<i>Ah, wretches! who delight to know how the bird of the</i> Phasis <i>differs from the wintering crane of</i> Rhodope. — —

‎So much, then, for the word עגור <i>agor</i>, over which I see that the ancient translators stumbled. In <i>Isaiah</i>, for the words כסוס עגור, <i>like a swallow</i> and <i>agor</i>, that is, <i>a crane</i>, the <i>Greek</i> Elders have only ὡς χελιδὼν [hōs chelidōn], and the <i>Arabic translator</i> مثل سنونوة [mithl sunūnwa] <i>like a swallow</i>, the following word being omitted altogether; and Jerome, <i>Like a young swallow</i>; Aquila, ὡς ἵππος ἀγώρ [hōs hippos agōr]; and <i>Theodotion</i>, ὡς χελιδὼν ἀγὼρ [hōs chelidōn agōr], because they were content to transcribe an unknown word. But the <i>Syriac</i> has ܐܝܟ ܣܢܘܢܝܬܐ ܕܡܨܦܪܐ [איך סנוניתא דמצפרא], <i>like a swallow that chatters</i>; and the Chaldee כסוסיא דאחיד, <i>like a swallow that has been caught</i>; with which <i>Symmachus</i>'s version agrees, ὡς χελιδὼν ἐγκλειθεῖσα [hōs chelidōn enkleitheisa], <i>like a swallow shut in</i>; as if they had read סגור <i>sagur</i> instead of עגור <i>agor</i>. But that עגור <i>agor</i> is to be read is shown by <i>Aquila</i>, <i>Symmachus</i>, all the <i>Hebrews</i>, and the comparison of this passage with <i>Ierem. VIII,</i> 7. There again the same words סיס <i>sis</i> and עגור <i>agor</i> are joined, and indeed so joined that it is clear that עגור <i>agor</i> is the name of a bird, like תור <i>thor</i> and סיס <i>sis</i>, which precede it. Jerome renders it <i>stork</i>, and the <i>Greeks</i> ἀγροῦ στρουθία [agrou strouthia]. But ἀγροῦ [agrou] arose from ἀγὼρ [agōr], and στρουθία [strouthia] is a gloss that crept from the margin into the text. The Chaldee and the <i>Syriac</i> very well explain it as ܟܘܪܟܝܐ [כורכיא] <i>kurkeja</i>, that is, <i>crane</i>, although not in its proper order, as [col. 80] we noted above <sup>R3</sup>.

‎Although עגור <i>agor</i> is not used today for the <i>crane</i> among the <i>Arabs</i>, it may nevertheless once have been so used, and may derive from the fact that by the verb عجر [ʿajara] <i>agara</i> they mean <i>that someone returns home by right of return</i>, or <i>flees away</i>, which is most characteristic of cranes. For it is well known that out of love of their homeland they return every year to <i>Scythia</i> and <i>Thrace</i>, where they were born, and indeed τὴν ἑαυτῶν ἕκαστον καλιὰν ἀναγνωρίζειν, ὡς τὴν οἰκίαν ἀνθρώπους [tēn heautōn hekaston kalian anagnōrizein, hōs tēn oikian anthrōpous], <i>that each recognizes its own nest, as men do their house</i>: which Aelian sets out at length in book 3, ch. 13 and 23. And perhaps from the same source it came about that the royal couriers were called by the <i>Syrians</i> and <i>Persians</i> Ἄγγαροι [Angaroi], <i>aggari</i> or <i>angari</i>. For the angari were believed to rival cranes in speed of running. Φασί τινες θᾶττον τῶν γεράνων ταύτην τὴν πορείαν ἀνύττειν [Phasi tines thatton tōn geranōn tautēn tēn poreian anyttein] (says <i>Xenophon</i> in book 8 of the <i>Cyropaedia</i>): <i>Some report that this journey is accomplished faster than the flight of cranes.</i> [II, 631] That the running of the angari is treated there will be clear to anyone who reads the passage. Among the <i>Cypriots</i>, however, <i>agor</i> was a bird very different from the crane, namely the <i>eagle</i>, if we believe these words of <i>Hesychius</i>: Ἀγώρ· ἀετὸς, Κύπριοι [Agōr: aetos, Kyprioi]. But perhaps it had been written Ἀγώρ· γερανός [Agōr: geranos]; and, the first part of the word γερανὸς [geranos] having been omitted because of its similar ending to the preceding word, ἀετός [aetos] was made out of ἀνὸς [anos] <sup>R4</sup>.

‎R4 *(p. 631)* But I can scarcely believe that Bochart has established anything certain by this whole very learned discussion. Let us weigh the force of his arguments. One will look in vain in this entire chapter for any explicit statement of them. At the very outset he assumes that עגור is the <i>crane</i>, but he is completely silent about the reasons that led him to it. Yet, so far as one can gather from the course of the whole discussion, he seems to have reasoned thus: "From the two passages of Isaiah and Jeremiah it is clear that עגור is a <i>migratory bird</i> and one <i>using a strained voice</i>; and we find both of these in the <i>crane</i>; therefore עגור is the <i>crane</i>." Nothing would be more certain than this, if those two features were peculiar to <i>cranes</i>. As it is, since there are many migratory birds that use a strained voice, you see how unsafe this argument is unless weightier ones are added. But Bochart is wholly without these, so that there is not even one of the ancients on whose authority he can safely rely. For the Syriac and the Chaldee put <i>crane</i> where the Hebrew text has סוס, but <i>swallow</i> in place of עגור; unless you say that the words have been transposed in both translators, which Bochart assumes without any adequate reason, merely in favour of his preconceived opinion. Equally weak is the other argument for the <i>crane</i>, drawn from the fact that the Arabic عجر [ʿjr] means <i>he returned</i>; for on that basis <i>all migratory birds</i> whatsoever could have been so named. But what chiefly restrains me from taking עגור as the name of the <i>crane</i> is this: that in Isaiah <i>chirping</i> (צפצף) is attributed to the birds סוס and עגור. — VELTHUSEN (in <i>Symbolae Kilonienses</i> vol. 2, p. 273—390) labours hard to show that the Hebrew עגור is the same word as نغر [nughar], the Arabic name of the <i>nightingale</i>, with an interchange of ن and ע (whose related sound in pronunciation once approached <i>ng</i>, like the French <i>n</i> in <i>mon</i>, <i>ton</i>), and of ג and غ, examples of whose interchange Schultens gives in the <i>Clavis Dialectorum</i> p. 199. He further argues that the <i>nightingale</i> was in the minds both of the Alexandrians, when they put στρουθια [strouthia], <i>sparrows</i>, in Jeremiah, that bird being of their kind; and of the Chaldee and the Syriac, who in their Jeremiah by כורכיא (from the verb כָּרַךְ <i>to cover</i>) would indicate <i>a bird that hides itself amid thick foliage in mourning</i>, i.e. the <i>nightingale</i>, and by that word sought at the same time to express the same notion of <i>hiding</i> in the verb עגר that can be derived from the Arabic عجر [ʿjr], in Conjugation 5 <i>he covered himself.</i> How contrived and far-fetched all this is, anyone will easily notice for himself. — EVERARD SCHEIDIUS, in his <i>Dissertatio ad Canticum Hiskiae</i> p. 86 ff., taking our עגור in Isaiah as a passive participle of the verb עגר, which, on the testimony of Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] and Firuzabadi, has the notion of <i>twisting and bending</i>, considers it an epithet of the <i>swallow</i>, which, struck with fear, <i>wheels</i> this way and that and repeatedly turns aside in its course. — Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791)] too, in the <i>Clavis</i> to Isaiah, took the word for a <i>participle</i>; but since he there regards סוס as the name of the <i>horse</i> (cf. note 4 to the preceding Chapter), he interprets עגור as <i>disturbed</i>, <i>anxious</i>, calling to his aid the Syriac ܥܓܘܪܐ [עגורא] and also the Arabic عجر [ʿjr], <i>to turn aside in the way</i>, <i>to wheel</i>, so that the poet, tossed by the heat of fever, compares himself with an <i>exhausted horse</i>, panting from the depths of its chest and uttering a <i>shrill and sharp</i> sound, which is expressed by the verb [II, 632] צפצף. — To me עגור seems to be the name of a <i>bird</i> in Isaiah too, chiefly for this reason: that in Jer 8:7 the same words סיס and עגור are joined, and joined in such a way that it is evident that עגור is the name of a <i>bird</i>, like תור and סיס, which precede. But which species of bird is meant, since the other dialects are silent and the ancients disagree remarkably, no one today can say.
