# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 7: Chiryonim or dibyonim (2 Kgs 6:25) is a kind of chickpea, not dove's dung (vol. 2, pp. 572–589)

### ‎[II, 572] Chapter VII.

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> חריונים, <i>or</i> דביונים, <i>2 Reg. VI, 25, is a kind of chickpea, not dove's dung, as has hitherto been believed.</i>

‎It is related in <i>2 Reg. VI, 25</i> that in the time of the famine at <i>Samaria</i> רובע הקב חריונים, <i>the fourth part of a cab of dove's dung</i>, was sold for five pieces of silver. Understand by these silver coins half-ounce pieces, each worth about thirty-six <i>Tours</i> sous, so that the total came to roughly nine livres of our money. Further, all the <i>Hebrews</i> with complete agreement report that the cab held only twenty-four eggs. So the fourth part of a cab would be a log, that is, a measure of six eggs. I shall not undertake to prove these things, lest I seem to do what has already been done. I shall only try to answer a most difficult question, which no one has solved so far: why so tiny a measure of so worthless a thing, namely dove's dung, was valued so highly, and for what use it was bought.

‎I. There are those who say that the <i>Samaritans</i>, pressed by hunger, used this dung as food. Among them is <i>Eutychius, patriarch of Alexandria</i>, in his <i>Annals</i>, where he speaks of the famine of <i>Samaria</i>, p. 213: واكلوا زبل الحمام [wa-akalū zibl al-ḥamām] <i>And they ate dove's dung.</i> In the same way <i>Rabshakeh</i>, about to besiege <i>Jerusalem</i>, threatened that he would make the inhabitants eat their own dung and drink their own urine, <i>2 Reg. XVIII,</i> 27, and <i>Es. XXXVI,</i> 12. But these words of <i>Rabshakeh</i> are nothing but the empty taunt of a boastful <i>Thraso</i>, by which he meant that the people of <i>Jerusalem</i> would be oppressed by the utmost want of all things. And the <i>Hebrews</i> rightly interpret the [col. 39] saying על דרך גוזמא, <i>by way of hyperbole</i>; for it is a thing without precedent that anyone should, like swine, have swallowed the dung of others, let alone his own. Nature utterly recoils from so monstrous a food. Or if, when famine struck, necessity bound with iron spikes drove someone to it, he would still do it in vain, because nothing remains in excrement that can be digested. This is far more true of dove's dung than of human dung, because it is much hotter. This is taught both by experience itself and by the books of the physicians and of all who have written on manuring. <i>Varro, De re rust.</i> book 1, ch. 38: <i>Cassius writes that the best dung</i> (for manuring fields) <i>is that of birds</i>, etc. <i>Of these, that of doves is the best, because it is the hottest.</i> <i>Columella</i> book 2, ch. 15: <i>There are, then, three chief kinds of dung: that from birds, that from men, and that from cattle. Of birds' dung the first is held to be that which is cleared out of dovecotes.</i> <i>Pliny</i> book 17, ch. 9: <i>Marcus Varro gives first place to the dung of thrushes from aviaries. Columella puts that from dovecotes next,</i> [II, 573] <i>then that from henhouses. Some prefer that from dovecotes.</i> <i>Palladius</i> book 2, ch. 33: <i>That of doves</i> (dung) <i>is the most fiery, and that of other birds is quite useful.</i> <i>Quintilius</i> book 2 of the <i>Geopon.</i>: Ἔστι δὲ κόπρος μὲν καλὴ ἡ τῶν πτηνῶν ἁπάντων, πλὴν τῶν χηνῶν, καὶ τῶν ἐνύδρων ὀρνέων, διὰ τὴν ὑγρότητα [esti de kopros men kalē hē tōn ptēnōn hapantōn, plēn tōn chēnōn, kai tōn enydrōn orneōn, dia tēn hygrotēta], etc. Πολὺ δὲ καλλίων ἡ τῶν περιστερῶν, πολὺ τὸ θερμὸν ἔχουσα [poly de kalliōn hē tōn peristerōn, poly to thermon echousa]· <i>The dung of all birds is good, except that of geese and water-birds, because of its moisture,</i> etc. <i>but that of doves is far superior, because it is the most fiery.</i> <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] book 2 of the <i>Canon,</i> in the chapter زبل [zibl], or <i>On dung</i>: زبل الحمام اسخان الازبال المستعمل [zibl al-ḥamām askhan al-azbāl al-mustaʿmal] <i>Dove's dung is the hottest of all those that have any use.</i> The cause is clear from <i>Aristotle</i>, who lists the dove first among those that have θερμὴν κοιλίαν [thermēn koilian], <i>a hotter stomach</i>, <i>De part. anim.</i> book 2, ch. 7. Accordingly some of the moderns write that the dove, like the ostrich, swallows and digests small stones. And the <i>Arabic</i> name of the dove, حمام [ḥamām] <i>himam</i>, is formed <i>from heat</i>. These things, I think, show abundantly that nothing remains in dove's dung that a human being could digest <sup>R1</sup>.

‎[II, 574] II. Driven from this position, <i>R. Jonah</i> [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim] and <i>R. David</i> devise something else, namely that the <i>Israelites</i> used the dung in place of wood, since during the siege of <i>Samaria</i> they could not go out to gather firewood. This is a little less absurd, since dove's dung can serve in place of wood. For <i>Galen</i> reports as an established fact that dove's dung catches fire so easily that somewhere in <i>Mysia</i> a neighbouring house burned down because dove's dung had ignited spontaneously from the sun's rays. Near that house, namely, κόπρος ἀπέκειτο περιστερῶν, ἤδη σεσηπυῖα, καὶ τεθερμαμένη, καὶ ἀτμὸν ἀναπέμπουσα, καὶ ἁπτομένοις ἱκανῶς θερμή. ταύτης δὲ πλησίον ἦν, ὡς καὶ ψαύειν ἤδη θυρὶς ἔχουσα ξύλα νεωστὶ καταληλιμμένα ῥητίνῃ πολλῇ, θέρους οὖν μέσου λαῦρος ἥλιος προσβαλὼν ἐξῆψε τὴν ῥητίνην τε, καὶ τὰ ξύλα [kopros apekeito peristerōn, ēdē sesēpuia, kai tethermamenē, kai atmon anapempousa, kai haptomenois hikanōs thermē. tautēs de plēsion ēn, hōs kai psauein ēdē thyris echousa xyla neōsti katalēlimmena rhētinē pollē, therous oun mesou lauros hēlios prosbalōn exēpse tēn rhētinēn te, kai ta xyla], <i>there lay dove's dung, already putrid and hot</i>, <i>giving off steam</i>, <i>and quite hot to the touch</i>, <i>and so close to that house</i>, <i>that it touched a window</i>, <i>whose wood had recently been smeared with much resin: so in midsummer the blazing sun, striking from above, set the wood and the resin alight.</i> So <i>Galen</i>, book 3 <i>De temperam.</i> Similar is what one reads in the same author, book 1 <i>De different. febr.</i> and in the book <i>De morborum causis</i>: that the dung of wild doves catches fire more slowly but holds it longer. So no one should think this material wholly unfit for feeding a fire. But truly, who would believe that in the city of <i>Samaria</i>, which, being the royal seat, we may fairly suppose was very far removed from rustic ways, there was so much dove's dung that fires could be built from it? Or that there was anyone who would not rather go without fire for a time than buy so tiny a quantity of a substance most foul-smelling in the flames at so great a price? [col. 40] Moreover, these things do not hang together: that there was a great famine in <i>Samaria</i> because there was nothing to feed a fire with except dove's dung, bought at a high price.

‎III. The third opinion is that of those who hold that the <i>Israelites</i> bought this dung in order to manure better the fields enclosed within the city, and so provide against famine in the coming year. As if it were very likely that in a most populous city there was so much farmland; or that those who lacked the means to live from day to day had the means to sow; or that the citizens of <i>Samaria</i> were so anxious about the future when they had nothing for the present; or so fastidious in choosing dung that, when other dung, only a little less useful, was available in great abundance, they bought dove's dung at so high a price.

‎IV. The fourth opinion, equally absurd, is that of <i>Josephus</i>: that dove's dung served them ἀντὶ ἁλῶν [anti halōn], <i>in place of salt.</i> For although I grant that it does not lack salt, yet if they had to use salt from excrement, everyone knows that there is [II, 575] much more salt in urine, and that it is extracted more easily and with less disgust. So there would have been no need to buy at so high a price what everyone had at home. Besides, what would be the point of that salt in so great a famine? If one cared to play the rhetorician, I know that much could be gathered in praise of salt, and that after bread hardly anything is more useful for life: without it a great part of <i>foods</i> is flat and tasteless, <i>can that which is unsavoury be eaten without salt?</i> Job 6:6. Thus, under the Law no sacrifice was without salt, <i>Lev 2:13</i> and <i>Mark 9:49.</i> And even among the <i>heathen</i>, on the authority of <i>Pliny</i>, no rites were performed without salted meal. So too there is hardly any table of some refinement that lacks salt. But salt also seems to have been the food of the poor. For people of slender means used πρὸς ἅλα δειπνεῖν, καὶ κύμινον [pros hala deipnein, kai kyminon], <i>to dine on salt and cumin</i>, as can be gathered from <i>Plutarch</i>. Hence <i>Diogenes</i>, in <i>Laertius</i> book 6, answered <i>Craterus</i> when he invited him: Ἀλλὰ βούλομαι ἐν Ἀθήναις ἅλα λείχειν, ἢ παρὰ Κρατερῷ τῆς πολυτελοῦς τραπέζης ἀπολαύειν [Alla boulomai en Athēnais hala leichein, ē para Kraterō tēs polytelous trapezēs apolauein]· <i>But I would rather lick salt</i> at Athens <i>than enjoy a sumptuous table at the house of</i> Craterus. And <i>Persius</i>, <i>Satire</i> 5,

‎<i>You will live content to bore with your finger into a salt-cellar tasted again and again, if you strive to live with</i> Jove.

‎that is, you will be extremely poor if you are pious. And in <i>Horace</i>, book 1, <i>Satire</i> 3, <i>Tigellius</i>, of whom <i>Seneca</i> speaks in Epistle 126, in order to seem a frugal man, says: I care nothing for the luxuries of the rich,

‎— — — <i>let me only have a three-legged table, and a shell of pure salt.</i> — — — —

‎Varro <i>also reports that the ancients used</i> (salt) <i>as a relish: for they ate salt with bread and cheese, as the proverb shows.</i> So <i>Pliny</i>, book 31, ch. 7, where shortly before the same author says: <i>It is also involved in honours and in military service: hence salaries are so called, with great authority among the ancients.</i> Likewise, Ancus Martius, <i>the king, gave the people six thousand modii of salt as a largess.</i> The kings of <i>Assyria</i> also gave salt as pay. Accordingly, in <i>Ezra 4:14</i> the governors of <i>Samaria</i> write this to King <i>Artaxerxes</i>: <i>Because</i> די־מלח היכלא מלחנא, <i>we are salted with the salt of the palace.</i> That is the literal rendering; that is, as <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] rightly puts it, <i>we receive a salary from the king's palace.</i> But no one praises salt more fully than <i>Plutarch</i>, <i>Sympos.</i> book 5, <i>Probl.</i> 10, where, on the question why <i>Homer</i> <sup>a)</sup> calls salt divine, he asserts among other things that salt is τῶν ἄλλων ὄψων ὄψον εἶναι, καὶ ἥδυσμα [tōn allōn opsōn opson einai, kai hēdysma], <i>the relish and seasoning of all other relishes.</i> Likewise, Θρίγκωμα τῆς τροφῆς γινόμενον εἰς τὸ σῶμα, καὶ παρέχον ἐναρμοσίαν αὐτῇ προς τὴν ὄρεξιν [thrinkōma tēs trophēs ginomenon eis to sōma, kai parechon enarmosian autē pros tēn orexin], <i>that which becomes a safeguard of food in the body, making it agreeable and suited to our palate.</i> Hence in <i>Sirach. XXXIX, 31.</i> certain things are praised as [II, 576] ἀρχὴ πάσης χρείας εἰς ζωὴν ἀνθρώπου [archē pasēs chreias eis zōēn anthrōpou], <i>the beginning</i> (that is, <i>the chief</i>) <i>of all things necessary for life.</i> And among these salt comes before all food. But whatever may be said of salt, this much is certain: salt is nothing but [col. 41] a seasoning, and seasonings are not usually bought at a great price in a severe famine, when food is lacking. For hunger itself is the best seasoning, and one does not then seek what stimulates the palate, but what satisfies.

‎V. The fifth opinion is that of the <i>Talmudists</i>. To understand it, the reader must first be told that there is this <i>Talmudic</i> axiom in the tractate <i>Megilla</i>, ch. 3: כל המקראות הכתובין בתורה לגנאי קורין אותן לשבח, <i>All words written indecently in the Law are read decently.</i> Therefore, wherever words occur in Scripture that are less decent in common usage, the teachers of the <i>Hebrews</i>, out of regard for decency, wanted them read otherwise than they are written. So in place of the word found in the text they substitute in the margin another, more decent one, which it is still the custom today to read in its place in the synagogues. Hence for שגל<sup>b)</sup>, <i>to have intercourse</i>, they read שכב, <i>to lie with</i>; for עפולים<sup>c)</sup>, <i>diseases of the anus</i>, טחורים, <i>haemorrhoids</i>; for טחוראות<sup>d)</sup>, <i>latrines</i>, properly <i>places of dung</i>, מוצאות, <i>privies</i>; for חורים and שינים<sup>e)</sup>, <i>dung</i> and <i>urine</i>, צואה and מימי רגלים, <i>excrement</i> and <i>water of the feet.</i> In the same way, in this passage, for חריונים, <i>dove's dung</i>, they want דביונים to be read, <i>the flux of doves</i>, a word compounded of דב, <i>flux</i>, and יונים, <i>doves.</i> But the <i>Talmudists</i> interpret דביונים differently, as if it were compounded of the <i>Chaldee</i> pronoun ד, which corresponds to the <i>Hebrew</i> אשר, <i>who, which, that,</i> the preposition ב, <i>in</i>, and the noun יונים, <i>doves.</i> For them, then, דביונים means <i>that which</i> (supply: <i>is</i> or <i>was</i>) <i>in the doves.</i> For they hold that at <i>Samaria</i> doves had been trained to go out foraging in the fields, so to speak; and that when they returned stuffed and swollen, their owners shook out their crops, extracted the grain and sold it at a high price. So, in their view, the words of the sacred text run thus: The famine in <i>Samaria</i> was so great <i>that a quarter of a kab</i> of the grains <i>that were in the doves</i>, and were extracted from their crops, <i>sold for five shekels.</i> Hence <i>Comestor</i> in the <i>Historia Scholastica</i>, book 4 <i>of Kings</i>, ch. 10: <i>Some relate that by the word</i> dung <i>is meant the little bladder of the dove, in which were found grains brought from outside.</i> I do not think anything more absurd than this fiction could have been devised. Not that I deny that doves, through love of their young, can be so trained that, released from a besieged city, they return home; for we have shown in the immediately preceding chapter that ancient and more recent people often used such messengers. But who would believe that at <i>Samaria</i> there were so many such docile doves? Or that in fields laid waste far and wide by the enemy they found crops everywhere at hand? Or that, returning with full crops, [II, 577] they so easily disgorged whatever they had swallowed in the field? And that by such arts even one kab of grain could have been gathered in the whole time of the siege? Or, if God had sustained this people for some time in so miraculous a way, that Scripture would have passed over so unheard-of a thing so lightly? But we argue in vain, since the word חריונים, which is in the text, can in no way be drawn to this sense. Indeed, hardly, or not even hardly, can the marginal word דביונים be rendered <i>that which is in the doves</i>, unless we suppose that a <i>Chaldee</i> pronoun was prefixed to a <i>Hebrew</i> noun, and so a hybrid, half-barbarous word was formed.

‎VI. <i>Junius</i>, thinking he could avoid these <i>Symplegades</i> by a new interpretation, rendered this passage as follows: <i>There arose a great famine in Samaria,</i> etc. <i>so that the fourth part</i>, <i>the belly itself</i>, <i>which is in the hollow of doves</i>, <i>was of five shekels of silver.</i> But at first reading it is immediately apparent that this exceedingly learned man would not have rendered it thus, almost, I would say, without any sense, had he not felt himself to be in great straits. For what would <i>the fourth part</i>, <i>the belly itself of doves</i> be? And what point was there in specifying that the belly is in the hollow? Although the hollow is rather in the belly than the belly in the hollow. [col. 42] Again, how could it have entered the mind of that man, who was most perspicacious, that the belly of doves was sold separately? Nor can what is found in his version, <i>the fourth part</i>, <i>the belly itself</i>, <i>which is in the hollow of doves</i>, be extracted by any torture from the <i>Hebrew</i> words רובע הקב חריונים. For if רבע, <i>the fourth part</i>, and הקב, <i>the belly</i>, as the distinguished <i>Junius</i> renders it, were in apposition, each noun would have the <i>He hajedia</i>, or article, prefixed, as one would say in <i>Greek</i> τὸ τέταρτον, ἡ κοιλία [to tetarton, hē koilia]. I add that in this version קב <i>kab</i> is wrongly confused with קבה <i>keba</i>. Of these, קבה <i>keba</i> means <i>belly</i> or <i>crop</i>; but קב <i>kab</i> is something quite different, namely κάβος [kabos], <i>cabus</i>, a kind of measure. So it is explained among the <i>Hebrews</i> by the <i>Aruch</i> [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)], <i>Elias</i> and <i>Aquinas</i> [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)] in their <i>Lexicons</i>, by <i>Mardochaeus</i> in his <i>Concordance</i>, by <i>Alphes</i> in the tractate <i>On Passover</i>, ch. 5, fol. 176, and by the <i>Gloss on the Talmud</i> passim. Hence the compound תרקב <i>tharkab</i> means to some <i>two cabs</i>, as if contracted from תרי קבין <i>there kabin</i>, <i>two cabs</i>; to others, more correctly, <i>three cabs</i>, as if it were תרי וקב, <i>two and a cab</i>, one more. So <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] in various places of the <i>Talmud</i>. And κάβος [kabos] is also a measure in <i>Hesychius</i>, <i>Philoxenus</i>, the <i>Suda</i>, the <i>Etymologicum</i>, <i>Phavorinus</i> and others. Finally, none of the ancients renders חריונים otherwise than as <i>dove's dung</i>. Let <i>Josephus</i> and the <i>Greek</i>, <i>Chaldee</i>, <i>Syriac</i>, <i>Arabic</i> and <i>Latin</i> translators be consulted, and, among the <i>Hebrews</i>, <i>Iarchius</i>, <i>Kimchi</i> and <i>R. Levi</i> on this passage. Among them a remarkable agreement will be found, which rarely happens in the interpretation of an obscure passage. Indeed, of the more recent writers no one dissents except <i>Junius</i> and <i>Fuller</i>, about whom now a few words.

‎VII. He (in his <i>Sacred Miscellanies</i>, book 6, ch. 11) understands both דביונים and חריונים (the former of which is written in the margin, the latter in the text) as <i>the crop of doves</i>, [II, 578] <i>their belly and entrails</i>. As if they were called דביונים, that is, <i>the flux of doves</i>, because those who cut up and gut doves let these flow out, or slip down to the ground. And חריונים, that is, κοιλία [koilia], or <i>the belly of doves</i>. For he tries to prove that חריונים are <i>the belly and entrails of doves</i> as follows: "<i>The Hebrew</i> חור first means <i>white</i>; 2. <i>a hole</i>, from the brightness of the light that is usually seen through it; 3. τρώγλην [trōglēn] <i>a cavity</i>, which is a kind of hole; 4. <i>the belly</i>, in Greek κοιλίαν [koilian], which is a notable cavity of the human body; whence <i>alvus</i> [belly] is named as if <i>alveus</i> [hollow], κοιλία ἀπὸ τοῦ κοίλου [koilia apo tou koilou]; 5. <i>the excrement enclosed in the belly</i>, by a metonymy of the contents for the container. Since we may choose the most suitable of these senses of the word, why should we not think that <i>the belly and entrails of doves</i> are meant here, which, though they are usually thrown away, were in a time of most severe famine not only kept, but even sold off and disposed of at a great price?" This is <i>Fuller</i>'s opinion, in which (if I may say so with all respect to a most careful writer) I seem to find not a few things that have not even the appearance of truth. Thus, that <i>the entrails of doves</i> should be called דביונים <i>in Hebrew</i> has never occurred to anyone even in a dream. Rather, all explain it as <i>excrement</i>, so named from flowing, just as <i>in Greek</i> ῥεῦμα γαστρὸς [rheuma gastros] and διάῤῥοια [diarrhoia], and <i>in Latin alvi profluvium</i> [flux of the belly], are said of this kind of flux. And in <i>Celsus</i> the <i>febres fluentes</i> [flowing fevers], in Greek ῥοώδεις πυρετοὶ [rhoōdeis pyretoi], are those which loosen the bowels. Again, in what the same <i>Fuller</i> asserts, that the word חור means 1. <i>white</i>, 2. <i>a hole</i>, 3. <i>a cavity</i>, 4. <i>the belly, crop, entrails</i>, 5. <i>excrement</i>, most is fabricated. חור for <i>white</i> is not so much <i>Hebrew</i> as <i>Chaldee</i> and <i>Arabic</i>. But for <i>a hole</i> or <i>a cavity</i> it is <i>Hebrew</i>, and from a different root. For the former is from חור <i>chavar, to grow white</i>; the latter from the geminate חרר <i>charar, to perforate</i>. Accordingly you may read in the <i>Talmud</i>, tractate <i>Succa</i>, fol. 20b, חור שחררוהו מים, <i>a hole which water has bored</i>. And for the same <i>Talmudists</i> חריר is <i>the eye of a needle</i>, as ܚܪܘܪܐ [חרורא] is for the <i>Syrians</i> in <i>Matt 19:24.</i> Further, that this חור also means <i>belly</i>, or <i>crop</i>, or <i>entrails</i>, [col. 43] is a mere fabrication, whose author is not cited, nor indeed can be cited, since no one has ever used it so. Finally, חר, or rather חרי, or חרא, for <i>excrement</i>, is an altogether different word, whose root is neither חור nor חרר, but חרה or חרא. Hence the <i>Talmudists</i> consistently write חרי or חריא. And <i>in Arabic</i> خري [kharī] <i>charia</i>, with <i>hamza</i> at the end, means <i>to void excrement</i>, خرء [khurʾ] <i>excrement</i>, مخرأة [makhraʾa] <i>latrine</i>. Indeed the <i>Hebrews</i> too, when they write in full, insert <i>Aleph</i>, as in <i>Isa 36:12.</i> לאכול את חראיהם, <i>to eat their own dung</i>. And <i>2 Reg. X, 27. They destroyed the house</i> of Baal <i>and made it</i> למחראות, <i>into latrines</i>. Nor is the <i>Aleph</i> there adventitious, as they think, but radical, and to be supplied <i>where</i> it is missing, as is clear from Arabic usage. These points observed, it appears that the eminent <i>Fuller</i> did not know <i>the etymology of the word</i> or its true spelling, and indiscriminately took three words of different origin, which led him astray. For since whatever he adduces rests solely [II, 579] on the etymologies of words, if these etymologies are false, whatever is built upon them must also be false. And truly most futile is what he ascribes to the sacred author, that a cab of the bellies and entrails of doves was sold in <i>Samaria</i> for five shekels. For it has never been heard of that the crop and entrails of doves were sold separately. I admit that one reads this of hens, and that <i>in Arabic</i> تبودك [tabūdak] <i>thabudach</i> is the name for <i>one who sells the entrails of a hen</i>, namely the heart, gizzard, etc. <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] explains it as من يبيع ما في بطون الدجاج من القلب والقانصة [man yabīʿu mā fī buṭūn al-dajāj min al-qalb wa-l-qāniṣa] But of doves' entrails nothing of the kind occurs anywhere, so far as I know. Nor is it fitting that doves' intestines should have been measured by cabs. For the cab is a measure not of entrails but of grains, like the seah, ephah and cor. Six cabs make a seah, three seahs an ephah, ten ephahs a cor, the largest dry measure. Accordingly in the <i>Talmud</i> and in the <i>Aruch</i> you read everywhere קב חטין, <i>a cab of wheat</i>. And it is defined by <i>Elias</i> as מדת היובש, <i>a measure of dry things</i>; by <i>Pomarius</i> as מרת של חטום, <i>a grain measure</i>; by <i>Aquinas</i> as מדת למדות דבר היבש, <i>a measure for measuring a dry thing</i>. So in <i>Pollux</i>, Κάβασον δὲ ἐκάλουν τὸν περὶ τὴν τροφὴν ἄπληστον, etc. Κάβος γὰρ σίτου μέτρον [Kabason de ekaloun ton peri tēn trophēn aplēston, etc. Kabos gar sitou metron]. <i>They called cabasus one insatiable for food</i>, etc. <i>For the cab is a measure of grain.</i> And in <i>Hesychius</i>, Κάβαισος· ἄπληστος· κάβος γὰρ μέτρον σιτικόν [Kabaisos; aplēstos; kabos gar metron sitikon]; <i>Cabasus, insatiable; for the cab is a grain measure.</i> From this correct <i>Suidas</i>, who has κάβαιστος [kabaistos] for κάβαισος [kabaisos]. And the <i>Etymologicum</i>, which has μετασιτικὸν [metasitikon], a word of no meaning, for μέτρον σιτικὸν [metron sitikon]. But in <i>Hesychius</i> what follows a little below is corrupt, Κάβος· μέτρον σιτικὸν, καὶ οἰνικὸν [Kabos; metron sitikon, kai oinikon], <i>Cab, a measure of grain and of wine.</i> For that the cab is a measure of wine, or of liquids, you will read nowhere else. So I emend thus: Κάβος· μέτρον σιτικὸν, καὶ κάδος οἰνικόν [Kabos; metron sitikon, kai kados oinikon]; <i>The cab is a measure of grain, and the cadus of wine.</i> He compares two words close in sound as well as in meaning. For both mean <i>a measure</i>, but the latter of <i>wine</i>, the former of <i>grain</i>. Indeed, that the cadus is a measure of wine, as the cab is of grain, there is no one who does not know. Thus <i>Herodotus</i>, book 3, chapter 20, speaks of Φοινικηΐου οἴνου κάδον [Phoinikēiou oinou kadon], <i>a cadus of palm wine</i>. And in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 11, <i>Anacreon</i>,

‎Οἴνου δ᾽ ἐξέπιον κάδον, [Oinou d' exepion kadon,]

‎<i>I drained a cadus of wine.</i> And in the same place <i>Hedylus</i> in an <i>Epigram</i>,

‎Ἀλλὰ κάδοις Χίου με κατάβρεχε, [Alla kadois Chiou me katabreche,]

‎Here the translator ridiculously has <i>But water by pouring wine from casks</i>, instead of <i>But drench me with casks of</i> Chian <i>wine</i> [col. 44]: as in <i>Tibullus</i>, book 2, Elegy 1:

‎— — — <i>loosen the bonds of the</i> Chian <i>cask.</i>

‎And <i>Horace</i>, book 3, <i>Ode</i> 19:

‎[II, 580] <i>At what price shall we buy a cask</i> of Chian? — — —

‎And like <i>cabus</i>, so <i>cadus</i> is of <i>Hebrew</i> origin. Just as קב <i>kab</i> is <i>cabus</i>, so כד <i>cad</i> is <i>cadus</i>, although the <i>Hebrew</i> כד has a wider range. For <i>Moses</i>, <i>Solomon</i> and <i>Elijah</i> use it for a <i>water jar</i>. See <i>Gen. XXIV</i>, 14. <i>Eccles. XII</i>, 8. and 1 <i>Reg.</i> <i>XVIII</i>, 34.

‎Having thus weighed the opinions of others, it now remains for us to put forward our own as well. To establish it, we must first lay down as most certain that by dove's dung some kind of food is meant. Otherwise there would be no coherence in this sentence: <i>There was a great famine</i> in Samaria: <i>for an ass's head was sold for eighty pieces of silver, and the fourth part of a cab of dove's dung for five pieces of silver</i>. Further, since it has been proved that dove's dung, as these words are commonly understood, is in no way a food, something else must necessarily be meant by them. When I consulted the <i>Arabs</i> on this matter, I found that two things are customarily marked with this epithet among them. One of them is what is called by another name كوز كندم [kūz kandum] <i>kuz kendem</i>, or جوز جندم [jawz jandum] <i>giauz gendem</i>, that is, a nut named after a place. For a nut is called كوز [kūz] <i>kuz</i> in <i>Persian</i> and جوز [jawz] <i>giauz</i> in <i>Arabic</i>. And <i>Kendem</i> or <i>Gendem</i> is the same word, for the <i>Persian Caph</i> there sounds as <i>G</i> because of the three dots placed over it. Of this nut <i>Avicenna</i>, book 2 of the <i>Canon</i>, says: كوز كندم الماهية هو خفيف كالاشنة طيني وبالرقة يسمونه حرا الحمام وببغداد يسمي جوز جندم الاختيار اجوده المربري والذفني ضغيف [kūz kandum al-māhiyya huwa khafīf ka-l-ushna ṭīnī wa-bi-l-Raqqa yusammūnahu ḥurā al-ḥamām wa-bi-Baghdād yusammā jawz jandum al-ikhtiyār ajwaduhu al-mrbrī wa-l-dhfnī ḍghīf] Kuzkendem, <i>what is it? It is a light thing, like tree moss, clay-like. In the city of Raqqa</i> (on the <i>Euphrates</i> below <i>Samosata</i> in <i>Mesopotamia</i>) <i>they call it dove's dung. And in Baghdad giauz Gendem. Selection: the best is the Barbaric, but the Daphnensian is weak.</i> See the rest there. The same author elsewhere: جوز جندم الطبع قال بولس له قوة مبردة مطفية مجففة قليلا [jawz jandum al-ṭabʿ qāla Būlus lahu quwwa mubarrida muṭfiya mujaffifa qalīlan] <i>Giauz</i>, or the nut <i>Gendem. Its nature. Paul said that it has the power of cooling, quenching and drying somewhat.</i> But the definition is omitted there; in the <i>Latin</i> version it reads thus: <i>What is the nut Kendem? It is a granular earth like a chickpea, white, inclining to yellow, which is exported from Barca and from Corascena, from which wine is made with honey.</i> If this definition is true, it does not help us at all. For it is a kind of earth, and no kind of earth is edible.

‎But there is also another kind of dove's dung among the <i>Arabs</i>, entirely different from this one: namely the very thing commonly called <i>usnen.</i> For it is established that this is called by another name <i>sparrows' dung</i>, or <i>doves' dung</i>. <i>Avicenna</i>, book 2: اشنان [ushnān] [II, 581] الماهية هي انواع الطغها الابيض ويسمي خرو العصافير [al-māhiyya hiya anwāʿ al-ṭghhā al-abyaḍ wa-yusammā khurūʾ al-ʿaṣāfīr] <i>What is</i> usnen<i>? It has several species, of which the finest is white, and it is called sparrows' dung.</i> Sylvaticus: <i>Sparrows' dung, usnen.</i> Giggeius [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)]: [col. 45] الاشنان [al-ushnān] <i>Alusnen, a cleansing powder, which is called dove's dung.</i> The same elsewhere: الحرض [al-ḥuruḍ] <i>Alhord, usnen, or dove's dung.</i> Likewise: خرو العصافير [khurūʾ al-ʿaṣāfīr] <i>dove's dung, a certain cleansing powder.</i> Likewise: الوشنان [al-wushnān] <i>dove's dung, a kind of medicinal powder.</i> But even so the matter is not settled, since it is not well enough known what <i>usnen</i> properly is. Most hold that it is the same as القلي [al-qilī] <i>alkali</i>, from which a very well-known salt is named. The Pandectarius: <i>The salt</i> alkali <i>is made in this way: Take of the herb otherwise called usnen</i>, etc. The same: <i>Antifilos</i> (read <i>anthyllion</i>) <i>or antili</i> (read <i>anthyllis</i>) in Greek, in Arabic <i>usnen</i>, in Latin <i>salsola,</i> <i>is a herb from which alkali is made.</i> And elsewhere: <i>Usnen</i>, etc. <i>is a herb whose leaves are like those of stonecrop or of the lentil, having a salty taste, from which salt is made.</i> <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] against <i>Cardano</i>, Exercitation 104, Section 18: <i>The Arabs call Chali</i> (better <i>kali</i>) <i>the plant which we call Soda. It is a shrub in the province of Narbonne. By grinding and pressing it they produce what they call salicornum, others soda, others alumen catini, from the shape of the basin into which the juice drips</i>, etc. <i>In Serapion [=Serapion, Liber de simplicibus medicinis (the Arabic-Latin pharmacopoeia)] it is called usnen.</i> Giggeius: القلي [al-qilī] <i>alkali</i>, etc. <i>or usnen.</i> Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)]: اشنان [ushnān] <i>Usnen</i>, <i>the herb</i> alkali, <i>and the ashes made from it, the best and in great quantity in</i> الحوران [al-Ḥawrān] <i>Auranitis, a region of Syria.</i> Likewise: بلس [bils] <i>bils</i>, the same as اشنان [ushnān] <i>usnen.</i> <i>Ashes of alkali.</i> And that <i>Alcanzius</i>, or <i>Alchindus</i>, a most celebrated <i>Arab</i> physician and almost a contemporary of <i>Avicenna</i>, held this opinion, you may gather from the fact that in the chapter <i>On usnen</i> he describes, almost word for word, everything that <i>Avicenna</i> had said about <i>kali</i>, as though <i>usnen</i> and <i>kali</i> did not differ at all. This is partly true, partly false. قلي [qilī] <i>kali</i>, for the <i>Arabs</i> as for the <i>Hebrews</i>, means <i>roasted</i> or <i>fried</i>. So <i>usnen</i>, roasted so that from its ash a kind of lye or soap is made, is rightly called <i>kali.</i> And by this name it is also known to the <i>Hebrews</i>, who write קליא <i>kalia</i>, in the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>Erubim</i>, ch. 3, fol. 28b. In <i>Pomarius</i> קליא is defined as עשב שנשרף ומשימין אפרו בבורית, <i>A kind of herb whose ash, when it is burnt, is put into soap.</i> And in the <i>Aruch</i>: עשב שורפין ונותנין אותו בבורית ורוחצין ידיהם, <i>A herb which they burn and put into soap, in order to wash their hands with it.</i> The <i>Arabs</i> say the very same of their اشنان [ushnān] <i>usnen</i>, or قلي [qilī] <sup>R2</sup>. Thus the verb تاشن [taʾashshana], formed from the noun اشنان [ushnān] <i>usnen</i>, is rendered by the distinguished <i>Golius</i>: <i>He cleansed his hands with ashes of alkali, or with their lye.</i> [II, 582] And <i>Alcamus</i>: غسل يده فيه [ghasala yadahu fīhi] <i>He washed his hand in it,</i> because it is useful للجرب والحكك [li-l-jarab wa-l-ḥakak] <i>for scabies and itching</i>, as is stated in the same place. But as for the view of <i>Sylvaticus, Scaliger, Salmasius</i> on <i>Solinus</i>, and other more recent writers, that this <i>usuen</i> or <i>kali</i> is our <i>soda</i>, or <i>solida</i> <sup>R3</sup>, from which they make glass <sup>R4</sup>, I would not grant it, except for some single species. For the most learned of the <i>Arabs</i> teach that <i>usnen</i>, or <i>kali</i>, is a species in the genus of the <i>chickpea</i> <sup>R5</sup>, which has nothing in common with our <i>soda</i> or <i>solida</i>. <i>Abuchanipha</i>: اجناس الاشنان كثيرة وكلها من الحمص والاشنان الحرض الذي يغسل به الثياب [ajnās al-ushnān kathīra wa-kulluhā min al-ḥimmaṣ wa-l-ushnān al-ḥuruḍ alladhī yughsalu bihi al-thiyāb] <i>The kinds of</i> usnen <i>are many,</i> [col. 46] <i>all of which are from the chickpeas</i> (if the word may be pardoned), <i>and</i> usnen <i>is</i> alhord, <i>with which garments are washed.</i> [II, 583] In agreement with this is <i>Serapion</i>, chapter 257: Usnen <i>is a very hot herb, whose species are from</i> amoch (read <i>from amos</i>, من حمص [min ḥimmaṣ] <i>from chickpeas</i>) <i>and</i> usnen <i>is</i> haron (read <i>hord</i> حرض [ḥuruḍ]) <i>and it is that with which cloths are washed.</i> Again elsewhere <i>Abuchanipha</i>: القلي يتخذ من الجمص واجوده ما يتخذ من الحرض وهو قلي الصباغين وساير ذلك الزجاجين [al-qilī yuttakhadhu min al-jmṣ wa-ajwaduhu mā yuttakhadhu min al-ḥuruḍ wa-huwa qilī al-ṣabbāghīn wa-sāʾir dhālika al-zajjājīn] Alkali <i>is taken from the chickpea; but the best is that taken from</i> alhord, <i>which is the</i> kali <i>of the dyers, as the rest is that of the glassmakers.</i> Albacra: الاشنان نبات لا ورق له وله اعضان دقاق شبه العقر وهي رخصة كثيرة الما وتعظم حتي يكون له خشب غليظ يتوقد به وناره حر جدا ورايحته ودخانه كريهة وطعمة الي الملوحة وهو من الحمص [al-ushnān nabāt lā waraq lahu wa-lahu aʿḍān diqāq shibh al-ʿqr wa-hiya rakhṣa kathīrat al-māʾ wa-taʿẓumu ḥattā yakūna lahu khashab ghalīẓ yutawaqqadu bihi wa-nāruhu ḥarr jiddan wa-rāʾiḥatuhu wa-dukhānuhu karīha wa-ṭaʿmuhu ilā al-mulūḥa wa-huwa min al-ḥimmaṣ] Usnen <i>is a plant without leaves, having slender branches like a root, tender, full of moisture, which grow until there is dense wood in them, with which fire is kindled, which is very hot. Its smell and smoke are abominable, its taste salty. And it is in the genus of chickpeas.</i> Alcamus: القلي شي يتخذ من حريق الحمص [al-qilī shayʾ yuttakhadhu min ḥarīq al-ḥimmaṣ] Alkali, <i>a thing obtained from the burning of chickpeas.</i> <i>Abenbitar</i> [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], from <i>Abuchanipha</i>, in the chapter قاقلي [qāqulī] <i>kakeli,</i>: هو من الحمص والناس ياكولونه مع اللبن وهو مثل الاشنان الا ان القلام اعظم منه وورقه شبيه بورق الحرف [huwa min al-ḥimmaṣ wa-l-nās yaʾkulūnahu maʿa al-laban wa-huwa mithl al-ushnān illā anna al-qlām aʿẓam minhu wa-waraquhu shabīh bi-waraq al-ḥurf] <i>It is a species of chickpea, which is eaten with milk, and it is like</i> usnen, <i>except that it has a larger stalk, and a leaf like the leaf of cress.</i> Giggeius: الغولان [al-ghawlān] Algaulano, <i>a kind of chickpea similar to</i> alusnen <sup>R6</sup>. What of the fact that in <i>2 Sam 17:28</i> the <i>Hebrew</i> קלי <i>kali</i> is rendered by <i>Jerome</i> <i>fried chickpea?</i> This will seem novel to those who know that the <i>Hebrew kali</i> does not mean <i>chickpea</i> but <i>roasted grain, Lev 23:14. Ruth 2:14 1 Sam 17:17</i> and <i>XXV, 18.</i> etc. Yet <i>Jerome</i>'s version is doubtless correct. For there the <i>Ammonites</i> and <i>Gileadites</i> are said to have brought to <i>David</i>, a fugitive in the desert, <i>wheat, and barley, and flour,</i> וקלי, <i>and</i> kali, <i>and beans, and lentils,</i> וקלי, <i>and</i> kali. So in the same verse the noun <i>kali</i> is repeated twice. The first, joined to wheat, barley and flour, means <i>roasted grain.</i> But the second, which follows beans and lentils, denotes some roasted or fried legume, namely <i>fried chickpea.</i> For the chickpea was customarily fried or roasted. Thus in <i>Aristophanes</i>' play <i>Peace</i>, a countryman is described

‎᾿Ανθρακίζων τὲρεβίνθου [Anthrakizōn terebinthou] [col. 47] [II, 584] <i>roasting chickpea over the coals.</i> And on the second page of the <i>Ecclesiazusae</i> the <i>Scholiast</i> has: Ὑποπίνοντες ἔκοπτον φρυκτοὺς ἐρεβίνθους [Hypopinontes ekopton phryktous erebinthous], <i>Having drunk rather freely, they pounded roasted chickpea.</i> The <i>Roman</i> writers, too, mention <i>roasted chickpea</i> more than once. <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Bacchides</i>, Act 4, Scene 5:

‎<i>I will roast him as thoroughly as chickpea is roasted.</i>

‎<i>Horace</i> in the <i>Art of Poetry</i>:

‎<i>Nor if the buyer of roasted chickpea or of nuts approves of something.</i>

‎<i>Cassian, Collat.</i> 8, ch. 1: <i>He brought in a basket holding roasted chickpea, which they call</i> trogalia. Τρωγάλια [Trōgalia], that is, <i>sweetmeats.</i> And even today the <i>Arabs</i> call <i>roasted chickpea</i> قلي [qly] <i>kali</i>, as we have just shown <sup>R7</sup>. Accordingly all the <i>Hebrews</i>, on the passage of <i>Samuel</i> we mentioned, recognize two kinds of <i>kali</i>: the former made from grain, the latter מקטניות, <i>from pulses</i>, or ממיני קטניות, <i>from kinds of pulses.</i> So <i>Kimchi</i>, <i>R. Levi</i> and <i>R. Esaias</i> on that passage. And <i>R. Selomo</i> describes the latter <i>kali</i> thus: וקלי ממינים של קטניות מיבישין אותו בתנור כשהן לחין והם מתוקין לעולם וטוחנין אותן ועושין מהם מאכל שקורין שתית, Kali <i>are kinds of pulses which, while still fresh, are dried in an oven; they always remain sweet, and they are ground, and from them is made a food which they call</i> sethith. This is taken from the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>On Idolatry</i>, ch. 2, fol. 38a, where we read that <i>Barzillai the Gileadite</i> sent <i>David</i> חרי מיני שתיתאה, <i>two kinds of sethitha</i>, that is, two kinds of <i>kali</i>. And there the <i>Gloss</i> says: שתיתא תבשיל העשוי מקמח של קליות שייבשין בתנור ויש שעושין אותו מקמח עדשין ולפי שהן מתוקין יותר מדאי נותנים שם חומץ, Sethita <i>is a dish made from the flour of</i> kali <i>dried in an oven. There are also some who make it from lentil flour; and because these are excessively sweet</i> (that is, <i>insipid), vinegar is poured on them.</i> It is true that שתיתא <i>sethitha</i> is said to be made from lentils, for in tractate <i>Gittin</i>, ch. 7, fol. 70a, the <i>Talmud</i> text itself mentions שתיתא דטלפחי, <i>sethitha of lentils.</i> But what is added about vinegar I think is a σφάλμα [sphalma] of the <i>Glossator</i>, who had read somewhere that this dish is made <i>from lentil</i> וחמצא, <i>and chickpea</i>, and understood <i>vinegar</i> instead of <i>chickpea</i>, because <i>in Chaldee</i> חמצא chimtza, <i>chickpea</i>, and חמצא chumtsa, <i>vinegar</i>, are written with the same letters. However that may be, we have amply proved that <i>usnen</i>, or <i>kali</i>, is chickpea or a kind of chickpea. And we have shown why it is called קלי <i>kali</i>: namely because it is roasted or parched. But اشنان [ushnān] <i>usnen</i>, or rather وشنان [wshnān] <i>vvsnen</i>, as many write it, [II, 585] comes from the verb وشن [wshn], which, of land, a camel, etc., means <i>to swell</i> and <i>to stand out.</i> Hence the chickpea is rightly called <i>usnen</i>, because of the uneven angles with which it swells and stands out. For it is not round like the pea, but angular and top-shaped <sup>R8</sup>. But why it should be called <i>sparrows' dung</i> or <i>dove's dung</i> is entirely hidden from me. I only know that it is so called. Nor is there any doubt that the nut <i>Kendem</i>, or <i>Gendem</i>, is called <i>dove's dung</i> for this very reason: that with its grains or little stones it imitates the shape of the chickpea. For it is <i>a granular earth, like chickpea</i>, as the old translator of <i>Avicenna</i> has it, in the place cited above.

‎So when we read in the Sacred text that in the famine of <i>Samaria</i> a quarter of a cab of dove's dung sold for five pieces of silver, why should we not understand the chickpea, or a kind of chickpea? Certainly everything will then fit nicely. 1. First, that the chickpea was very well known in <i>Judaea</i> is shown, besides several places of the <i>Talmud</i>, by the Sacred History in the passage cited above from 2 <i>Sam 17:28</i>, where the <i>Gileadites</i> and <i>Ammonites</i> bring <i>David</i>, among other things, קלי <i>kali</i>, that is, <i>roasted chickpea</i>. Accordingly חמצה, that is, <i>Emesa</i>, [col. 48] a famous city on the border of <i>Judaea</i>, which had its own kings, seems to have been named from the chickpea. <i>Belon</i> [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], in book 2, chapter 53 of his <i>Singularia</i>, observes that in the <i>Egyptian</i> city of <i>Cairo</i> there are many shops in which nothing is sold but roasted chickpea, which is bought in great quantity by the pilgrims who set out from there every year for <i>Mecca</i>. And in chapter 99 he reports the same of <i>Damascus</i>: <i>In Damascus</i>, he says, <i>there are many shops in which all are occupied with roasting chickpeas. They call them</i> Erevithia (ἐρεβίνθια [erebinthia]) <i>by a Greek name. Roasted and dried in bronze pans, they are very convenient for those who undertake a long journey.</i> 2. Further, the cab is a measure of legumes as well as of grain. For legumes, just like grain, have their seeds, which the <i>Arabs</i> designate by the common name حبوب [ḥubūb]. 3. That some cheap kind of food is signified by <i>dove's dung</i> is clear from these words of the Sacred text: <i>An ass's head was sold for eighty shekels, and a quarter of a cab of dove's dung for five shekels.</i> For there <i>dove's dung</i> is compared with <i>the ass's head</i>: the latter was for sale at eighty shekels, a quarter of a cab of the former at five shekels. So a whole cab, which is the measure of a day's food, cost twenty shekels, and four cabs eighty shekels, the price at which an ass's head was then sold. Therefore, whatever you understand there by <i>dove's dung</i>, it must plainly have been a very cheap food, since four cabs of it, that is, as much as suffices a sober man for four days' sustenance, were valued no higher than one ass's head. Now among foods in common use hardly anything is cheaper than the chickpea. Accordingly the chickpea is the food only of [II, 586] the frugal or the poorer sort. Thus in <i>Aristophanes' Peace</i>, as noted above, a certain countryman wishes to be with his companions,

‎Ἀνθρακίζων τοὐρεβίνθου, Τήν τε φηγὸν ἐμπυρεύων· [Anthrakizōn tourebinthou, Tēn te phēgon empyreuōn]

‎<i>Toasting the chickpea over the coals, and roasting the beech-nut at the fire.</i> In <i>Alexis the Comic poet</i>, in <i>Athenaeus</i> book 2, an old woman lamenting her poverty lists, among the common foods by which she barely sustains her wretched life, ἐρέβινθον [erebinthon] and λάθυρον [lathyron], that is, <i>chickpea</i> and <i>chickling</i>. <i>Horace</i>, book 1, <i>Satire</i> 6, to show that he is content with little and that a meagre diet suffices him:

‎— — — <i>thence I take myself home</i> <i>to a dish of leeks and chickpeas and fritters.</i>

‎And in the <i>De Arte</i> he thus contrasts the rich with those who live on roasted chickpeas and nuts:

‎<i>For those who have a horse, a father and property take offence,</i> <i>nor, if the buyer of roasted chickpeas and nuts approves something,</i> <i>do they receive it with fair minds or bestow a crown.</i>

‎Hence that line of <i>Martial</i>, book 5, Epigram 79:

‎<i>Both hot chickpea and warm lupin,</i> <i>it is a small little dinner, who can deny it?</i>

‎And book 1, Epigram 104, on the miser <i>Scaevola</i>, who cheated his own appetite:

‎<i>And the thick dregs of reddish</i> Veientan <i>are drunk:</i> <i>warm chickpea costs an as, and an as buys</i> Venus.

‎And Epigram 42 of the same book:

‎— — — — — — <i>he who sells soaked chickpea</i> <i>to the idle crowd.</i> — —

‎That is, he who peddles the cheapest foods, among them soaked chickpea, to the common people on holiday, at leisure for the Circus and the stage. To the same effect is the fact that at <i>Rome</i>, at the shows, those canvassing for office, to win the favour of the populace, scattered chickpeas along with beans and lupins among the gifts thrown to the crowd. To this allude these words of <i>Horace</i>, book 2, <i>Satire</i> 3:

‎<i>May you squander your goods on chickpeas, beans and lupins,</i> <i>so that you may strut broad in the</i> Circus <i>and stand in bronze.</i>

‎And these words of <i>Persius</i>, <i>Satire</i> 5.

‎— — — — <i>pour out chickpeas generously to the brawling people, so that the old men may remember our sunny</i> Floralia. — — — [II, 587] Thus in the Scetic desert of <i>Egypt</i>, where the monks professed a remarkable frugality, [col. 49] roasted chickpeas served them as food. <i>Cassian</i>, in the place cited above, on <i>Abbot Serenus</i>: <i>He brought in a basket containing roasted chickpeas, which they call</i> trogalia, <i>of which we took only five grains each.</i> Hence <i>Tertullian</i> uses <i>cicercula</i> [chickling] for <i>a thing of very little value</i>, when he writes thus against <i>Marcion</i>, book 1, chapter 11: <i>The God of</i> Marcion <i>ought to have produced at least one chickling, so that he might be proclaimed a new</i> Triptolemus. Among the <i>Greeks</i> too, ἐρεβίνθινος [erebinthinos], that is, <i>cicernus</i> [of chickpea], is taken for <i>worthless</i>. <i>Hesychius</i>: Ἐρεβίνθινος Διόνυσος· οὐδενὸς ἄξιος, παρά τινα παροιμίαν ἐρεβίνθινος ζωμός [Erebinthinos Dionysos; oudenos axios, para tina paroimian erebinthinos zōmos]; <i>Chickpea Bacchus</i> (chickpea wine), <i>that is, of no value; in a certain proverb chickpea broth is so called.</i> Likewise <i>Suidas</i>: Ἐρεβίνθινος Διόνυσος· ἐπὶ τῶν μηδενὸς ἀξίων [Erebinthinos Dionysos; epi tōn mēdenos axiōn]. <i>Chickpea Bacchus, of things of no value.</i> <i>Plutarch</i>, in the <i>Rom. Quaest.</i>, chapter 95, observes that τὸν λάθυρον, καὶ τὸν ἐρέβινθον [ton lathyron, kai ton erebinthon], <i>the chickling and the chickpea</i>, were forbidden and detested foods for some, but for a jocular reason: namely ὡς παρωνύμους τοῦ Ἐρέβους, καὶ τῆς Λήθης [hōs parōnymous tou Erebous, kai tēs Lēthēs], <i>as alluding to Erebus and Lethe</i> (the river of the underworld). For as ἐρέβινθος [erebinthos] alludes to <i>Erebus</i>, so λάθυρος [lathyros] to <i>Lethe</i>. He adds that pulses were very much used πρὸς τὰ περίδειπνα, καὶ τὰς προκλήσεις τῶν νεκρῶν [pros ta perideipna, kai tas prosklēseis tōn nekrōn], <i>at funeral meals and for the summoning of the dead.</i> But this pertains rather to the bean or the lentil. For on the bean <i>Pliny</i>, book 18, ch. 12: <i>The</i> Pythagorean <i>opinion</i> (on the bean) <i>condemned it, as others have reported, because the souls of the dead are in it. For this reason it is certainly used in rites for deceased parents.</i> Varro <i>also reports that for these reasons the</i> Flamen <i>does not eat it, since mournful letters are also found on its flower.</i> And Festus: <i>It is not permitted to the</i> Flamen Dialis <i>either to touch or to name the bean, because it is thought to pertain to the dead. For it is thrown to the ghosts at the</i> Lemuria, <i>and used in sacrifices to deceased parents, and the letters of mourning seem to appear on its flower.</i> Similar is the fact that the ancients counted the lentil among τὰ πένθιμα [ta penthima], <i>mournful things</i>, καὶ προτίθενται τοῖς νέκυσιν [kai protithentai tois nekysin], <i>and set it before the dead</i>, as <i>Plutarch</i> notes in the <i>Crassus</i> and <i>Appian</i> in the <i>Parthica</i>. That the <i>Jews</i> were not entirely free of this superstition is attested by these words of <i>Jerome</i> to <i>Paula</i> on the death of <i>Blaesilla</i>: <i>And to this day the</i> Jews, <i>weeping, with bare feet, rolled in the dust, lie on sackcloth; and, so that nothing may be lacking to superstition, by a most empty rite of the</i> Pharisees <i>they take lentils as their first food, showing, no doubt, for what kind of dish they lost the birthright.</i> But concerning the chickpea I have found only in <i>Pliny</i> that religion uses it at night vigils. If, however, along with the bean and the lentil, the chickpea and the chickling also belonged to funeral meals, as <i>Plutarch</i> seems to hint, then, since the reasons adduced have little to do with the matter, I would believe the ancients had nothing else in view than that a time of mourning befits thin and frugal foods, such as all pulses are, which fill the body but nourish it little. Accordingly the chief of <i>Nebuchadnezzar's eunuchs</i> feared that if <i>Daniel</i> and his companions ate pulses, their faces would look sadder and leaner than those of the other youths whom the king kept in his retinue. See <i>Dan 1:8</i> etc. It should be noted [II, 588] that there, in verses 12 and 16, pulses are not called קטניות, as elsewhere by the <i>Hebrews</i>, but זרעים, or זרענים. This word is more general. For זרעים or זרענים are any seeds whatever. Accordingly the <i>Greek</i> version, which is that of <i>Theodotion</i>, has σπέρματα [spermata]. And <i>Maimonides</i> [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] defines what זרעונים, <i>seeds</i>, are in the treatise <i>On Mixed Kinds</i>, book 1, ch. 8: הזרעונים נחלקין לשלושה חלקים האחד מהם הוא הנקרא תבואה והיא חמשה מינין החטים והכוסמין והשעורין ושבולת שועל והשיפון והשני מהן הוא הנקרא קטנית והן כל זרעים הנאכל לאדם חוץ מן התבואה כגון הפול והאפונים והדוחן והאורז והשומשמין והפרגין והספיר וכיוצא בהן והשלישי מהן הוא הנקרא זרעוני גנה והן שאר זרעונין שאינן ראויין למאכל אדם והפרי [col. 50] של אותר הזרע מאכל אדם כגון זרע הבצלין והשומין וזרע החציר והקצח וזרע לפת וכיוצא בהן, <i>Seeds are divided into three kinds. The first is of those called produce</i> (grain crops, σιτώδη [sitōdē]). <i>Of these there are five kinds</i>: <i>wheat</i>, <i>spelt</i>, <i>barley</i>, <i>fox's ear</i> (oats, whose ear resembles a fox's tail), <i>rye. The second is of those they call pulses, that is, all seeds eaten by man</i>, <i>such as beans</i>, <i>peas</i>, <i>millet</i>, <i>rice</i>, <i>sesame</i>, <i>poppy</i>, <i>chickling and the like. The third is of those called garden seeds, which are themselves not fit for eating, but what grows from them is eaten by man, such as onions</i>, <i>garlic</i>, <i>leeks</i>, <i>nigella</i>, <i>turnips and the like.</i> Yet in <i>Daniel</i> זרעי is explained as ὄσπρια [ospria], <i>pulses</i>, by <i>Josephus</i> and the <i>Greek Scholiast</i>, <i>Tertullian</i>, <i>Jerome</i>, <i>R. Selomo</i>, <i>Saadias Gaon</i>, <i>Aben Ezra</i> [=Abraham ibn Ezra], <i>Joseph</i> son of <i>Jachia</i>, etc. And they could just as well be called זרעים, that is, <i>seeds</i>, in <i>Hebrew</i> as ὄσπρια [ospria] in <i>Greek</i>. For they are called ὄσπρια [ospria] for σπόρια [sporia], ἀπὸ τῆς σπορᾶς [apo tēs sporas], <i>from sowing.</i> So this <i>Greek</i> name corresponds exactly to the <i>Hebrew</i>. But in <i>Greek</i> they are also called χεδροπὰ [chedropa], as if χειρόδροπα [cheirodropa], just as in <i>Latin</i> <i>legumina</i> [pulses], because they are gathered (<i>leguntur</i>) by hand. Referring to this, <i>Nicander</i>, <i>Theriaca</i>, verse 752, says.

‎Χειροδροπὰ δ' ἵνα φῶτες ἄνευ δρεπάνοιο λέγονται Ὄσπρια, χεδροπά τ' ἄλλα, μεσοχλόου ἐντὸς ἀρούρης· [Cheirodropa d' hina phōtes aneu drepanoio legontai / Ospria, chedropa t' alla, mesochlou entos arourēs]

‎<i>Where men gather by hand, without a sickle, pulses and other things of that kind, in the half-green field.</i> Once these points are noted, it will at once be clear what <i>Josephus</i> means when he says that dove's dung served the <i>Samaritans</i> in place of salt. <i>Theophrastus</i> (<i>De causis plantarum</i>, book 4, chapter 9), inquiring into the reasons why the chickpea ripens sooner than other pulses, gives this one among others: Ἡ ἅλμη πρὸς τὴν τελείωσιν συμβάλλεται [Hē halmē pros tēn teleiōsin symballetai], <i>The saltiness</i> of the chickpea <i>contributes to</i> that quick <i>ripening.</i> And in book 5, chapter 11: Ὁ ἐρέβινθος ἀπόλλυται σφακελίας, ὅταν ἀνθοῦσιν ὕδωρ ἐπιγινόμενον ἐπικλύσῃ τὴν ἄλμην· τὸ γὰρ ἔφαλμα οἷον σφακελίζειν ἐστί [Ho erebinthos apollytai sphakelias, hotan anthousin hydōr epiginomenon epiklysē tēn halmēn; to gar ephalma hoion sphakelizein esti]. <i>The chickpea perishes of blight when rain washes off its saltiness while it is in flower: for the loss of its saltiness is to it a kind of blight.</i> <i>Cato</i> too says of the chickpea, in <i>De re rustica</i>, chapter 37: <i>If you sow chickpea in crumbly soil, since it is pulled up, it is salty and harmful.</i> And <i>Columella</i>, book 2, chapter 14, says that Tremellius [=Immanuel Tremellius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] states that <i>the soil is harmed</i> most [II, 589] <i>by the poison of chickpea and flax: the one because it is of a salty nature, the other because it is of a hot nature.</i> And <i>Pliny</i>, book 18, chapter 12: <i>It is the nature of the chickpea to grow with saltiness; therefore it burns the soil.</i> And in book 17: <i>A defect common to all crops is blight, the chickpea's too, when the rain, washing off its saltiness, makes it sweeter.</i> <i>Galen</i>, in book 6 of <i>De simplic.</i>, calls this <i>bitterness</i>, when he says of chickpeas: Μετέχουσι δέ τι καὶ τῆς πικρότητος [Metechousi de ti kai tēs pikrotētos]; <i>They also have something of bitterness.</i> And of the wild chickpea: Δριμύτερός τ᾽ ἐστι, καὶ πικρότερος [Drimyteros t' esti, kai pikroteros], <i>It is sharper and more bitter.</i> But he put <i>bitter</i> for <i>salty</i>, as when the <i>Poet</i> says of the sea,

‎<i>let bitter</i> Doris <i>not mingle her wave.</i>

‎Accordingly, as we have noted, the <i>Arabs</i> extract from a certain kind of chickpea that very famous salt which is therefore called <i>sal alkali</i>, that is, <i>salt of burnt chickpea.</i> So, on the passage with which we are dealing, when the older <i>Hebrews</i> had remarked that it was dove's dung, from which salt is extracted, this was misunderstood by <i>Josephus</i>, who wrote that at <i>Samaria</i> dove's dung was used ἀντὶ ἁλῶν [anti halōn], <i>in place of salt.</i> Hence <i>Theodoret</i> on 4 <i>Kings</i>, <i>Question</i> 21: Ἡ τῆς πολιορκίας ἀνάγκη, καὶ τοῦ λίμου τὸ μέγεθος ἠνάγκασεν αὐτοὺς ἀντὶ ἁλῶν κεχρῆσθαι τῷ κόπρῳ [Hē tēs poliorkias anankē, kai tou limou to megethos ēnankasen autous anti halōn kechrēsthai tō koprō]. <i>The necessity of the siege and the severity of the famine compelled them to use dung in place of salt</i> <sup>R9</sup>.

‎R9 *(p. 589)* It hardly seems necessary to warn the reader that all this has been argued in vain, since the thing which the Arabs call <i>dove's dung</i> is not of the <i>chickpea</i> kind (on which, as on a foundation, Bochart's whole argument rests), but a species of <i>saline plant</i>, as we have amply proved in note 5. Of lesser weight is what Otho Verbrugge said in support of Bochart's opinion in <i>Observatt. Philologg. de nominum Hebraeorum plurali numero</i>, Groningen 1730, in duodecimo, p. 7–8. He himself thinks that חרייונים means <i>small loaves</i>, from a singular חרייון, which he takes to be a diminutive of חרי, a kind of <i>bread</i> among the Chaldeans and Talmudists. This opinion will hardly win anyone's approval, since what is said of the חרייונים in the sacred text, that they were measured by <i>kabs</i>, in no way suits loaves. Much more absurd is what Hiller put forward in <i>Hierophyt.</i> vol. II, ch. 22, sect. 3, p. 129: that חריינים are <i>dove's holes</i>, that is, the entrails of doves, which, being perforated, draw nourishment and growth out of the body. All these things weighed, it is best to retain that interpretation of this much-vexed word which Bochart listed in first place at the beginning of this chapter, but rejected there; yet undeservedly, as we have shown in <i>note 1</i>.
