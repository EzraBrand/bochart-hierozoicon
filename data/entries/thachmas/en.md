# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 15: Thachmas is the male ostrich (vol. 2, pp. 830–838)

### ‎[II, 830] [col. 231] Chapter 15.

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> תחמס <i>thachmas is the male ostrich.</i>

‎Just as among the <i>Greeks</i> ἅρπη [harpē], ἁρπετὸς [harpetos], ἁρπυία [harpyia] are names of birds of prey, so called <i>from seizing</i> [col. 232], so among the unclean birds forbidden in the Law, תחמס <i>thachmas</i> is believed to be one of the birds of prey, because חמס <i>chamas</i>, from which <i>thachmas</i> comes, means <i>to seize</i> and <i>to do violence.</i> This is roughly the view of the more recent <i>Hebrews.</i> <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim]: נקרא כן שחומס שאר העופות וגוזל אותם, <i>It is so called because it does violence to the other birds and seizes them.</i> Aquinas [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: תחמס שם עוף דורס, <i>Thachmas, the name of a bird of prey.</i> Pomarius [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)]: עוף דורסים פלקוני בלעז או כדומה, <i>A bird of the raptor kind, falcon in the vernacular, or another similar to it.</i> Accordingly <i>Arias</i> [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] renders it <i>hawk.</i> Among the ancients, <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] and the <i>Jerusalem</i> translator seemed to support this view; they have here חטפיתא <i>chataphitha</i>, or חטופיתא <i>chatupphitha</i>, which most <i>lexicographers</i> hold to be the name of a bird of prey, because חטף means <i>to seize.</i> So <i>Kimchi</i> confirms from the <i>Jerusalem</i> Paraphrase what he had said, that תחמס <i>thachmas</i> is a <i>bird of prey.</i> <i>The Elders</i> render it γλαῦκα [glauka], and <i>Jerome</i> <i>little owl.</i> <i>Fuller</i> [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)] strongly approves this version in <i>Miscellanea</i> book 6, chapter 9, and tries to confirm it from the etymology of the word, since it is characteristic of the little owl both to do violence and to suffer it, as the other birds vie in flying at it. Perhaps also in <i>Onkelos</i> [=Targum Onkelos] ציצא <i>tsitsa</i> is the <i>little owl</i>, as in <i>Greek</i> τυτώ [tytō]. <i>Hesychius</i>: Τυτώ· ἡ γλαύξ [Tytō: hē glaux]. From ציצא <i>tsitsa</i> came τυτώ [tytō], as τύρος [tyros] from צור <i>tsor</i>, and τάνις [tanis] from צען <i>tsoan.</i> Or ציצא <i>tsitsa</i> and τυτὼ [tytō] are onomatopoeic words, ὀνοματοποιητικὰ [onomatopoiētika], which [II, 831] seem to imitate the hooting of the little owl. To this belongs the joke of <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Menaechmi</i>, Act 4.

‎<i>M. Who is this</i> Menaechmus? <i>P. YOU, I say. M. I? P. YOU,</i> etc. <i>M. Did I give it? P. YOU there, I say. Shall I have a little owl brought, to keep saying TUTU at you? For we are already done for.</i>

‎Therefore I would also rather explain the <i>Arabs'</i> طوط [ṭwṭ] taut as <i>little owl</i> than, with <i>Giggeius</i> [=Antonio Giggei, Thesaurus linguae Arabicae (Milan 1632)], as <i>bat</i> <sup>R1</sup>. Here I deliberately pass over those who render תחמס as <i>harpe</i>, or <i>harpy</i>, or <i>cymindis</i>, or <i>nycticorax</i>: both because they are not of much weight, and because, as far as I know, they offer no reasons for their interpretation except this one alone, namely that the name of the bird תחמס <i>thachmas</i> is derived <i>from robbery.</i>

‎But this was the τὸ πρῶτον ψεῦδος [to prōton pseudos] that led most interpreters astray: they thought that חמס <i>chamas</i> and חטף <i>chataph</i> were wholly synonymous. I admit they are of neighbouring meaning, but I flatly deny that they mean the same. Properly חטף <i>chataph</i> is <i>to seize</i>; חמס <i>chamas</i>, <i>to act unjustly.</i> For you would not correctly render עד חמס <sup>a)</sup>, so frequent in the sacred text, as <i>witness of robbery</i>, but <i>witness of iniquity</i>, that is, <i>unjust</i> and <i>false.</i> Likewise חמסו תורה <sup>b)</sup> you should not render <i>they have seized the Law</i>, but <i>they have acted unjustly against the Law.</i> And elsewhere: <i>Against the orphan and the widow</i> אל תחמסו <sup>c)</sup>, <i>do not act unjustly.</i> Again: <i>He who offends against wisdom</i> חומס נפשו <sup>d)</sup> <i>acts unjustly against himself.</i> So in the <i>Psalms</i> חמס is everywhere joined as a synonym with עמל, עולה, תוך, רע and רשע. And the <i>Septuagint</i> never renders חמס as ἁρπαγὴν [harpagēn], but as ἀδίκημα [adikēma], ἀδικίαν [adikian], ἀθεσίαν [athesian], ἀνομίαν [anomian], ὄνειδος [oneidos], ὕβριν [hybrin]. And indeed, wherever חמס means <i>to seize</i> or <i>to do violence</i>, it is so taken only by consequence [col. 233], because extorting something by force against law and right is the conduct of unjust men. Therefore there is no need for תחמס to be a <i>bird of prey</i>, in Hebrew עוף דורס, since it has been proved that the word חמס properly signifies <i>what is impious and unjust</i>, even in one free of the crime of rapacity. So whatever bird is said to be תחמס <i>thachmas</i> will answer well enough to the name, if it is proved to be unjust and impious, [II, 832] even if it is not counted among the birds of prey. Moreover, no bird has so bad a name for impiety as the ostrich. The others take care to brood their eggs and rear their young; <i>this one leaves her eggs on the ground, to be warmed in the dust, and does not consider that a foot may crush them and a beast of the field trample them. She is harsh toward her young, as if they were not hers</i>; Job 39:17–18. 19. Accordingly <i>Jeremiah</i>, <i>Lam 4:3</i>, takes an example of similar cruelty from the ostrich, as we have already touched on in passing and will explain more fully in expounding the words of <i>Job.</i>

‎Further, by this name the <i>Arabs</i> call the ostrich ظليم [ẓalīm] <i>dtalim</i>, that is, <i>impious</i>, <i>unjust</i>. This name of the ostrich, though less frequent than نعام [naʿām] <i>naam</i>, discussed above, nevertheless occurs in <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)], book 2 of the <i>Canon</i>, and in <i>Abenbitar</i> [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], who treat in alphabetical order <i>Of the animals, plants, stones, minerals</i>, etc. <i>that pertain to medicine.</i> And in <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], besides the chapter <i>On</i> النعام [al-naʿām] the <i>naam</i>, in which he explains the nature of the ostrich more fully, there is also another chapter <i>On</i> الظليم [al-ẓalīm] the <i>dtalim</i>, which begins thus: الظليم ذكر النعام وكنيته ابو البيض وابو ثلاثين وابو الصحاري وجمعه ظلمان [al-ẓalīm dhakar al-naʿām wa-kunyatuhu abū al-bayḍ wa-abū thalāthīn wa-abū al-ṣaḥārī wa-jamʿuhu ẓulmān] Dtalim <i>is the male of the ostrich, whose surnames are: father of eggs, and father of thirty</i> (supply <i>eggs</i>) <i>and father of the deserts. The plural is</i> dtulman. The same word is mentioned by <i>Ibnocholcan</i>, in whom عار الظليم [ʿāra al-ẓalīm] <i>arra dtalim</i>, that is, <i>the male ostrich cried out.</i> It is mentioned also by <i>Nasrius</i> (a writer on the interpretation of dreams, ὀνειροκριτικὸς [oneirokritikos], whom I read in manuscript at <i>Oxford</i>), book 3, ch. 98, sect. 21. And by the <i>Arabic Pandectarius</i>: Talim, <i>that is, the male struzo</i>. It also occurs everywhere in the <i>Lexicon</i> of <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī], as these few examples out of many more may show: الظليم الذكر من النعام [al-ẓalīm al-dhakar min al-naʿām] Dtalim, <i>the male, of the ostrich.</i> يرفا الظليم النافر [yarfā al-ẓalīm al-nāfir] Iarpha, <i>that is, the dtalim</i> (or <i>male ostrich</i>) <i>in flight.</i> عمود من الظليم رجلاه [ʿamūd min al-ẓalīm rijlāhu] <i>The columns of the</i> dtalim, <i>that is, its feet.</i> اسقف الاعوج العنق من الظلمان [asqaf al-aʿwaj al-ʿunuq min al-ẓulmān] Ascaph, <i>one with a twisted neck, among the dtulman</i>, or <i>male ostriches.</i> They often fall into this ailment because, when they flee across the sands against the wind, they turn their heads away. الاصنع الظليم متقشر الساق [al-aṣnaʿ al-ẓalīm mutaqashshir al-sāq] <i>Asna, a dtalim</i> (or <i>male ostrich</i>) <i>with skinned legs</i>, namely when it flees headlong through rough places. الحاضب الظليم اغتلم فاخمرت ساقاه [al-ḥāḍib al-ẓalīm ightalama fa-ikhmarrat sāqāhu] <i>Alhadib, a dtalim</i> (or <i>male ostrich</i>), <i>when it is in rut and its legs turn red.</i> This happens to it every year, [col. 234] at the time when the dates begin to redden. جفلت الظليم حركته وطردته [jaffaltu al-ẓalīm ḥarraktuhu wa-ṭaradtuhu] <i>He roused</i> the <i>dtalim, and drove it on</i>, as hunters do. I could [II, 833] bring forward ten times as many of this kind. But I fear I would weary the reader. And these show more than enough that ظليم [ẓalīm] <i>dtalim</i> is the ostrich. Although this name is properly said of the male, it nevertheless seems to be given also to the female by catachresis, καταχρηστικῶς [katachrēstikōs]. For thus <i>Alcamus</i>: توحوح الظليم فوق البيض رومها واظهر ولوعها بها [tawaḥwaḥa al-ẓalīm fawqa al-bayḍ rawmuhā wa-aẓhara walūʿahā bihā] <i>The dtalim</i> (or <i>ostrich</i>) <i>thevvachvvacha over the eggs</i>, that is, <i>loved them intensely, and showed how desperately it doted on them.</i> And الزاجل [al-zājil] <i>zagil</i> is for him ما يسيل من دبر الظليم ايام تحضينها بيضها [mā yasīlu min dubr al-ẓalīm ayyām taḥḍīnihā bayḍahā] <i>what flows from the rear of the dtalim while it is brooding its eggs.</i>

‎I note this because the hitherto unidentified <i>Hebrew</i> תחמס <i>thachmas</i> corresponds exactly to the <i>Arabic</i> name ظليم [ẓalīm] <i>dtalim</i>. For as ظليم [ẓalīm] <i>dtalim</i> comes from ظلم [ẓalama] <i>dtalima</i>, which means <i>to be unjust</i> and <i>to oppress</i>, so תחמס <i>thachmas</i> comes from the verb חמס <i>chamas</i>, which has the same meaning as the <i>Arabic</i> ظلم [ẓalama] <i>dtalima.</i> Accordingly, in the <i>Pentateuch</i>, nearly every time חמס occurs in the sacred text, ظلم [ẓulm] occurs in the <i>Arabic</i> paraphrasts. Thus in <i>Gen 6:11</i> the Hebrew reads: <i>The earth was full of</i> חמס, <i>iniquity</i>; the <i>Erpenianus</i> [=the Arabic version edited by Thomas Erpenius (Pentateuch 1622; New Testament 1616)] has بالظلم [bi-l-ẓulm]. So too in verse 13, where the other <i>Arabic</i> version, published in <i>Paris</i>, also has تملات ظلما [tamallaʾat ẓulman]. And in chapter 16, verse 5, where the <i>Hebrew</i> has חמסי עליך, <i>My wrong be upon you,</i> that is, the wrong done to me will fall back upon you, both <i>Arabic</i> versions have ظلمي عليك [ẓulmī ʿalayka]. And in <i>Gen 49:5</i>, for the Hebrew כלי חמס, <i>instruments of iniquity,</i> both render آلة الظلم [ālat al-ẓulm]. Likewise in <i>Exod 23:1</i> and <i>Deut 19:16</i>, עד חמס, <i>a witness of iniquity,</i> is in both شاهد ظلم [shāhid ẓulm]. Examples of this kind may also be seen in the other books of Scripture. Thus <i>Ps 7:17. Let his iniquity come down upon his own pate.</i> Hebrew חמסו, <i>Arabic</i> ظلمه [ẓulmuhu]. <i>Ps 11:5. He who loves iniquity.</i> Hebrew חמס, Arabic الظلم [al-ẓulm]. <i>Ps 25:19. They hate me</i> שנאת חמס, <i>with a hatred of iniquity:</i> Arabic ظلما [ẓulman], <i>unjustly.</i> See also <i>Ps 27:12</i> and <i>LVIII, 2.</i> and <i>LXXII, 14.</i> and <i>LXXIII, 6.</i> and <i>CXL, 2. 5. 12.</i> So also in the <i>Prophets.</i> Thus <i>Isa 60:18. There shall no more be heard</i> חמס, <i>iniquity in your land:</i> Arabic ظلم [ẓulm]. <i>Ezek 45:9. Remove</i> חמס, <i>iniquity:</i> Arabic الظلم [al-ẓulm]. <i>Amos 3:10. Who store up</i> חמס, <i>iniquity:</i> Arabic ظلما [ẓulman]. <i>Jonah 3:8. Let everyone turn from his evil way,</i> ומן החמס, <i>and from the iniquity that is in his hands:</i> Arabic ومن الظلم [wa-min al-ẓulm]. <i>Hab 1:2. I cry out to you</i> חמס, <i>suffering oppression</i> (that is, <i>violence</i>), as <i>Jerome</i> has it: Arabic مظلوم [maẓlūm], <i>oppressed.</i> Since, then, the <i>Arabic</i> ظلم [ẓulm] is exactly the same as the <i>Hebrew</i> חמס, [col. 235] [II, 834] as so many examples have shown, a highly probable conjecture follows: that ظليم [ẓalīm] is also the same as תחמס, and תחמס the same as ظليم [ẓalīm]; that is, that both words denote the <i>ostrich</i>, and specifically the <i>male.</i> How probable, and I might almost say necessary, this reasoning is, all who understand the genius of these languages know. It is entirely certain, therefore, that as in <i>Moses</i> בת היענה is the <i>female ostrich,</i> so the next word, התחמס, is the <i>male ostrich.</i> Nor is it anything new for the sexes of animals to be distinguished in this way. Thus <i>Avicenna</i> and <i>Abenbitar</i>, as we have noted, treat نعام [naʿām] <i>naam</i> and ظليم [ẓalīm] <i>dtalim</i>, that is, the <i>female</i> and the <i>male ostrich</i>, in separate chapters. And in <i>Moses</i> himself, Gen 32:15, we read that <i>Jacob</i> offered <i>Esau</i> she-goats and he-goats, ewes and rams. And in <i>Gen. XLV</i>, <i>23.</i>, <i>Joseph</i> sends his father ten male asses and as many she-asses. But, you will say, why is the sex distinguished in the ostrich rather than in the eagle, the raven, etc.? The reason is ready to hand. Since נשר comprises the whole genus of eagles, and עורב the whole genus of ravens, it was enough for <i>Moses</i> to prohibit נשר and עורב. But the <i>Hebrews</i> have no generic name for the ostrich. For תחמס denotes only the males, and בת יענה only the females, as does רננים in <i>Job.</i> Therefore, lest by prohibiting one of these he should seem to permit the other, he had to prohibit both expressly <sup>R2</sup>.

‎Meanwhile, lest you be surprised that the interpreters went different ways in explaining this word, here is another similar example in the word חטפיתא, by which the <i>Chaldeans</i> render תחמס! For all the <i>lexicographers</i>, relying on the etymology of the word, hold that it signifies a <i>bird of prey</i>. For חטף means <i>to seize</i>. Therefore חטפיתא, plainly derived from it, was believed to be a bird of the class τῶν γαμψωνύχων [tōn gampsōnychōn]; this is a very grave error. For the <i>Chaldeans'</i> חטפיתא <i>chatphitha</i>, or חטופיתא <i>chatophitha</i>, is the very same as the <i>Arabs'</i> خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i>, since in both the so-called radical letters are the same. Accordingly, in this very passage, for the <i>Chaldee</i> חטפיתא the <i>Arabs</i> have خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i> in three different paraphrases, of which two are printed and the third is in manuscript. Further, that the <i>Arabic</i> خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i>, whose plural is خطاطيف [khaṭāṭīf] <i>chatatiph</i>, is the <i>swallow</i> is clear from <i>Avicenna</i> and <i>Abenbitar</i>. They cite the passage of <i>Dioscorides</i> on the swallow near the end of his second book, Χελιδόνος νεοσσοὺς τοὺς ἐκ τῆς πρώτης νεοσσοποιΐας αὐξομένης τῆς Σελήνης ἀνατεμὼν, εὑρήσεις ἐν τῇ γαστρὶ λίθους [Chelidonos neossous tous ek tēs prōtēs neossopoiias auxomenēs tēs Selēnēs anatemōn, heurēseis en tē gastri lithous], etc., in <i>Arabic</i> thus, in the chapter خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>cottaph</i>: قال ديسقوريدوس [II, 835] في الثاني اذا اخذ فرخه في زيادة القمر وكان اول ما افرخ وشق وجد فيه حصايان [qāla Dīsqūrīdūs fī al-thānī idhā ukhidha farkhuhu fī ziyādat al-qamar wa-kāna awwal mā afrakha wa-shuqqa wujida fīhi ḥaṣāyān] <i>Dioscorides said in the second book: if its chick is taken while the moon is waxing, and is from the first brood, and is cut open, stones are found in it,</i> etc. Or واخذ من الحصا الموجودة في جوفه حصاتان [wa-ukhidha min al-ḥaṣā al-mawjūda fī jawfihi ḥaṣātān] <i>And let two stones be taken from the stones that are found in its belly,</i> etc., as <i>Abenbitar</i> has it. See the rest there, for there is no time to copy out more. Moreover <i>Avicenna</i>, in book 2, in the chapter خاليدونيون [khālīdūniyūn] Χελιδόνιον [Chelidonion], that is, <i>swallow-wort</i>, has among other things the following: اذا عمي فرخ لخطاطيف حملت اليه الام هذه النبات فزيده [col. 236] بصرا وكذلك سمي الخطافي [idhā ʿamiya farkh li-khaṭāṭīf ḥamalat ilayhi al-umm hādhihi al-nabāt fa-zīduhu baṣaran wa-kadhālika summiya al-khuṭṭāfī] <i>When a chick of the</i> chatatiph (<i>swallows</i>) <i>is blinded, the mother brings it this plant, which increases its sight; hence it is called</i> chottaphi (<i>swallow-wort</i>). And <i>Abenbitar</i>, in the chapter عروق الصباغين [ʿurūq al-ṣabbāghīn] <i>dyers' roots</i>, says that it is otherwise called بقلة الخطاطيف [baqlat al-khaṭāṭīf] <i>herb of the chatatiph,</i> or <i>of the swallows.</i> In the same place both chapters of <i>Dioscorides</i> on the greater and lesser celandine are given in <i>Arabic</i>. And his <i>Greek</i> words Ἐάν τις τυφλωθῇ τῶν τῆς χελιδόνος νεοσσῶν [Ean tis typhlōthē tōn tēs chelidonos neossōn] are rendered thus: اذا عمي فرخ من افراخ الخطاف [idhā ʿamiya farkh min afrākh al-khuṭṭāf] <i>When some chick among the chicks of the</i> alchottaph <i>is blinded</i>. The <i>aspis chelidonias</i> too is called خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i> by <i>Avicenna</i>, book 4 of the Canon, <i>Fen</i> 6, tract. 3, ch. 24. There is also the <i>swallow fish</i>, which <i>Abuchalid Andalusius</i>, or <i>the Spaniard</i>, describes under the name <i>chottaph</i> thus: الخطاف سمكة ببحر سبتة لها جناحان علي ظهرها اسودان وبها تخرج من الما وتطير الي الشجر ثم تعود الي البحر [al-khuṭṭāf samaka bi-baḥr Sabta lahā janāḥān ʿalī ẓahrihā aswadān wa-bihā takhruju min al-mā wa-taṭīru ilā al-shajar thumma taʿūdu ilā al-baḥr] <i>The chottaph</i> (or <i>swallow</i>) <i>is a fish in the sea of Ceuta</i> (near the Strait of <i>Gades</i>), <i>furnished with two black wings on its back, with which it comes out of the sea and flies over the trees, and then returns to the sea.</i> But nothing proves that خطاف [khuṭṭāf] is the swallow so much as its ample descriptions in <i>Damir</i> and <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt], from which we quoted some passages in the first book <i>On Birds</i>.

‎But what, you will say, does the swallow have in common with birds of prey, that it should take its name <i>from seizing</i>? To me it makes no difference: whatever the reason it is so called, it is nevertheless certain that it is so called. But the origin of the name is also clear enough, since the swallow is carnivorous and lives only by seizing prey. Hence <i>Isidore</i>, <i>Origins</i>, book 12, chapter 7: <i>The swallow is so called because it does not take its food sitting, but</i> SEIZES <i>its food in the air and eats it.</i> And <i>the Philosopher</i> more than once calls it σαρκοφάγον [sarkophagon]. Thus in <i>History of Animals</i>, book 6, chapter 5: Ἡ δὲ χελιδὼν δὶς νεοττεύει μόνον [II, 836] τῶν σαρκοφάγων [Hē de chelidōn dis neotteuei monon tōn sarkophagōn]; <i>The swallow alone of carnivorous animals nests twice.</i> And in book 8, chapter 3: Εἰσὶ δὲ καὶ τῶν μὴ γαμψωνύχων ἔνιοι σαρκοφάγοι, οἷον ἡ χελιδών [Eisi de kai tōn mē gampsōnychōn enioi sarkophagoi, hoion hē chelidōn]. Pliny rendered this in book 10, chapter 24: <i>The swallow is the only bird that feeds on flesh, among those which do not have hooked talons.</i> And <i>Plutarch</i>, <i>Symposiacs</i>, book 8, <i>Problem</i> 7, comparing these two symbols of <i>Pythagoras</i>, Χελιδόνας οἰκίᾳ μὴ δέχεσθαι, μηδὲ γαμψωνύχων οἴκοι τρέφειν [Chelidonas oikia mē dechesthai, mēde gampsōnychōn oikoi trephein], <i>Swallows are not to be admitted into the house, nor birds with hooked talons to be reared in the home</i>, asserts that they rest on the same reason: Σαρκοφάγος γάρ ἐστι (ἡ χελιδὼν) καὶ μάλιστα τοὺς τέττιγας ἱεροὺς καὶ μουσικοὺς ὄντας ἀποκτίννυσι, καὶ σιτεῖται [Sarkophagos gar esti (hē chelidōn) kai malista tous tettigas hierous kai mousikous ontas apoktinnysi, kai siteitai]; <i>For the swallow is carnivorous</i>, he says, <i>and above all kills and devours cicadas, which are sacred and musical.</i> Moreover, Pliny observes of the swallow, in the place cited above, that the speed of its winding flight is so swift that for this reason it is not exposed to the attacks of other birds. Perhaps those who named it <i>from seizing</i> had this in view. For it is well known that in almost all languages verbs of <i>seizing</i> also refer to speed. Thus in <i>Hebrew</i> Kimchi and <i>Mardochaeus</i> explain חטיפה, <i>seizure</i>, as לקיחה במהירות, <i>a snatching with speed.</i> And the <i>Talmudists</i> in <i>Moed</i> [col. 237] <i>Katan</i> ch. 3, fol. 28a: מת פתאום זו היא מיתא חטופא, <i>If someone dies suddenly, this is a snatched death</i>, that is, <i>headlong</i> and <i>swift.</i> Like that saying of Plutarch in the <i>Consolation to Apollonius</i>: Ἐὰν δὲ ταχέως ἀποθάνῃ, ὀδύρονται λέγοντες, Ἀνηρπάσθη [Ean de tacheōs apothanē, odyrontai legontes, Anērpasthē]. <i>If he dies suddenly, they lament, saying, He has been snatched away.</i> Thus you will see ἁρπάγδην [harpagdēn], ἁρπακτικῶς [harpaktikōs], ἁρπαλέως [harpaleōs], ἁρπαλιμῶς [harpalimōs], and, with <i>Kappa</i> prefixed, καρπαλιμῶς [karpalimōs] used everywhere not of <i>plunder</i> but of <i>speed</i>, like Latin <i>rapide</i> [rapidly] and <i>raptim</i> [hurriedly]. Hesychius: Καρπάλιμον· ταχύ [Karpalimon: tachy]. Likewise: Ἁρπυία· ἁρπαγὴ, ἢ διὰ τάχους [Harpyia: harpagē, ē dia tachous]. The <i>Etymologicum</i>: Ἅρπασος· ποταμὸς Καρίας, διὰ τὸ ὀξύτατον τοῦ ῥοῦ [Harpasos: potamos Karias, dia to oxytaton tou rhou]. Virgil, <i>Aeneid</i> book 5:

‎— — — <i>and suddenly, at the signal heard,</i> <i>they seize the course.</i> — —

‎Where <i>corripiunt</i> [they seize], says <i>Servius</i>, <i>means they go hurriedly.</i> Persius, <i>Satire</i> 5:

‎— — <i>nothing prevents you from seizing</i> the Aegean <i>with a huge ship.</i> — — —

‎Which <i>Casaubon</i> explains as <i>you swiftly cleave.</i> <i>Lucan</i>, book 5, line 403:

‎<i>Thence he seizes his course, and the fields which the sluggish</i> Apulian <i>has abandoned by the rake and given over to idle grass,</i> <i>swifter than the flames of heaven and the tigress with young,</i> <i>he runs across.</i> — — —

‎And a little later, [II, 837] <i>It seemed shameful to the leader, at a time for seizing war,</i> <i>to be stuck in sluggish delays.</i> — —

‎And book 6, line 372:

‎— — — <i>and with seized (rushing) flood</i> the Apidanus, <i>and the</i> Enipeus, <i>never swift unless mingled.</i>

‎Pliny in the Panegyric: <i>When you led the legions, or rather</i> (<i>so great was your speed</i>) <i>swept them along</i>. A twin to which is that line of <i>Lucan</i>, the poet, on <i>Caesar</i>, book 1:

‎— — — <i>the tireless leader</i> <i>sweeps his columns on.</i> — — —

‎Martial to <i>Priscus</i>, book 12, <i>Epigram</i> 14:

‎<i>Use, I warn you, the rushing post-horse more sparingly.</i>

‎Accordingly Damir, in the chapter <i>On the horse</i>, derives the <i>Arabic</i> name of the <i>horse</i>, فرس [faras] <i>pharas</i>, from seizing, thus: لفظها مشتق من الافتراس كانها تفرس الارض بسرعة مشيتها [lafẓuhā mushtaqq min al-iftirās kaʾannahā tafrisu al-arḍ bi-surʿat mishyatihā] <i>Its name is derived from seizing, as though it tore up the earth by the speed of its gait.</i> And the verb خطف [khaṭafa] <i>chatapha</i> signifies first <i>to seize</i>, then <i>to be swift</i>. From this, then, the <i>swallow</i> could be called خطاف [khuṭṭāf] <i>chottaph</i>, as among camels the <i>dromedary</i> is called خيطف [khayṭaf] <i>chaitaph</i>, and a <i>headlong gait</i> حيطفي [ḥayṭafī] <i>chaitapha</i>, and <i>lightning</i> خاطف [khāṭif] <i>chatiph</i>, <i>because it dazzles the eyes</i>, they say, <i>by the rapidity of its motion.</i> Therefore those who take خطاف <i>chottaph</i>, or, what is the same, חטפיתא <i>chatphitha</i> and חטופיתא <i>chatophitha</i>, for a <i>bird of prey</i> of the class τῶν γαμψωνύχων [tōn gampsōnychōn] are as mistaken as those who so take תחמס, since חטפיתא means the <i>swallow</i>, and תחמס the <i>ostrich</i>, as we have tried to prove. Next will follow the exposition of the words with which the ostrich is described in <i>Job</i>: words that are excellent, but, as we think, not yet sufficiently understood, and therefore to be explained here.
