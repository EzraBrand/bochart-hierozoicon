# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 9: Daja is the black vulture (vol. 2, pp. 782–786)

### ‎[II, 782] [col. 195] CHAPTER IX.

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> דיה daja <i>is the black vulture.</i>

‎After איה <i>aja</i> comes דיה <i>daja</i>, Deut 14:13. It is omitted in <i>Leviticus</i>, either by the <i>Prophet</i> himself, for a reason we do not know, or by scribes after the <i>Prophet</i> because of the τὸ ὁμοιόπτωτον [to homoioptōton] of the two words האיה <i>haaja</i> and הדיה <i>hadaja</i>; I think the latter is nearer the truth. What דיה <i>daja</i> is, is not sufficiently known. <i>Jerome</i>, the <i>Arabs</i> <sup>R1</sup>, and many of the more recent writers interpret it as the <i>kite</i>, as though it were [II, 783] the same as דאה <i>daa,</i> Lev 11:14 <sup>R2</sup>. This seems scarcely tenable, because, as we have proved, דאה <i>daa</i> and ראה <i>raa</i> are the same. Moreover, ראה <i>raa</i> and דיה <i>daja</i> cannot be the same, since in the same verse they are forbidden to the <i>Jews</i> as different animals. For the sacred text reads: <i>These are the ones of which you shall not eat: the eagle,</i> etc. והראה ואת האיה והדיה, <i>and the</i> raa, <i>and the</i> aja, <i>and the</i> daja. Here, since ראה <i>raa</i> is the same as דאה <i>daa</i> in <i>Leviticus</i>, namely the <i>kite</i>, דיה <i>daja</i> is certainly something else: namely the <i>vulture,</i> as I at least think. So render <i>R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)],</i> [col. 196] <i>Schindler</i>, and the <i>English</i> and <i>Italian</i> versions; also <i>Fuller</i> [=Nicholas Fuller, Miscellanea theologica / sacra (1612–22)], <i>Miscellanea</i> book 6, ch. 4. This rendering is strongly confirmed by <i>Es. XXXIV,</i> 15., on the desolation of <i>Idumaea</i>: <i>There the</i> דיות dajoth <i>have gathered together.</i> For דיות <i>dajoth,</i> the plural of דיה <i>daja,</i> cannot be <i>kites</i>, which are not gregarious birds and never gather or flock together <sup>R3</sup>. But they can be <i>vultures</i>, of which Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] says, p. 785: <i>Vultures alone among the birds with hooked talons live in flocks, so that sometimes fifty appear in a single flock,</i> as Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)] <i>writes that he observed</i> [II, 784] in Egypt. Hence <i>the Philosopher</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 6, chapter 5: Πολλοὶ ἐξαίφνης φαίνονται ἀκολουθοῦντες τοῖς στρατεύμασι [polloi exaiphnēs phainontai akolouthountes tois strateumasi]; <i>Many suddenly appear, following armies.</i> And in book 9, chapter 11, he writes likewise, φαίνεσθαι ταχὺ πολλοὺς [phainesthai tachy pollous], <i>that many suddenly appear.</i> <i>Basil</i> too, in the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, Homily 8: Ἴδοις ἂν μυρίας ἀγέλας γυπῶν τοῖς στρατοπέδοις παρεπομένας [Idois an myrias agelas gypōn tois stratopedois parepomenas]; <i>You may see countless flocks of vultures following armies.</i> And <i>Ambrose</i>, imitating him, in the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, book 5, chapter 23: <i>When opposing lines renew a lamentable war among themselves, the aforesaid birds follow in a great column.</i> Pietro Bembo [=Pietro Bembo, Historia Veneta (1551)], <i>History of Venice</i>, book 5: <i>It is established that in those days when war against</i> Bajazet <i>was being prepared in</i> Apulia, <i>ravens and vultures fought one another in the air with such force and in such hosts that twelve carts were filled with their carcasses.</i> I cannot here omit the twelve vultures seen by <i>Romulus</i> when he founded the city of <i>Rome</i>, from which <i>Vettius</i> inferred that the <i>Roman</i> empire would end after as many centuries had passed. <i>Censorinus</i>, following <i>Varro</i>, wrote of this in chapter 14, about the thousandth year from the founding of the city. <i>How many centuries</i>, he says, <i>are owed to the city of</i> Rome <i>it is not for me to say. But I will not keep silent about what I have read in</i> Varro, <i>who in book 18 of the</i> Antiquities <i>says that there was at</i> Rome <i>one</i> Vettius, <i>not undistinguished in augury for his talent, equal to any learned man in debate; and that he had heard him say that if things were as the historians reported about the auguries of</i> Romulus <i>at the founding of the city and the twelve vultures, then, since the Roman people had passed 120 years unharmed, it would reach a thousand two hundred.</i> Nor did the prophecy lack fulfilment. For shortly after those twelve centuries had passed, when the strength of the state had been transferred from the <i>West</i> to the <i>East</i>, the <i>Roman</i> dignity was exhausted and the <i>Western</i> empire came to an end in <i>Augustulus</i>. For he laid down the purple out of fear of the <i>Heruli</i>, who under their leader <i>Odoacer</i> had burst into <i>Italy</i>, as <i>Paul the Deacon</i> writes in book 16. Likewise, when <i>Caesar</i> was leading his army down into the <i>Campus Martius</i>, six vultures appeared to him. Then, when he mounted the rostra after being made <i>consul</i>, six vultures were again seen and gave a sign, as though he were to found a new city under the auspices of <i>Romulus</i>. So <i>Julius Obsequens</i> reports. And in <i>Suetonius</i>, twelve vultures showed themselves to <i>Octavius Augustus</i>, as to <i>Romulus</i>, when he was taking the auspices in his first consulship. <i>Dio</i> reports this somewhat differently: Πρώτῃ τῶν ἀρχαιρεσιῶν ἐς τὸ πεδίον τὸ Ἄρειον εἰσελθὼν, γύπας ἓξ· καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα δημηγορῶν τι πρὸς τοὺς στρατειώτας, ἄλλους δώδεκα εἶδε [Prōtē tōn archairesiōn es to pedion to Areion eiselthōn, gypas hex; kai meta tauta dēmēgorōn ti pros tous strateiōtas, allous dōdeka eide]. <i>On the first day of the elections, having entered the</i> Campus Martius, <i>he saw six vultures; and shortly afterwards, while addressing the soldiers, twelve others.</i> From these it can at least be inferred that vultures often flock together, which is not written of other birds of prey. In vain would you object what we read in <i>Matt 24:28</i> and <i>Luke 17:37</i>, that eagles too gather at a carcass. For there it is not a question of true eagles but of percnopteri, or gypaeeti, which are degenerate eagles of vulturine appearance, born, [II, 785] as it seems, from the mating of an eagle with a vulture, as we showed above<sup>a)</sup>. However that may be, whoever would have <i>kites</i> understood in the words of <i>Isaiah</i> where he writes that the דיות <i>dajoth</i> gather or flock together, will have to show, either from experience or from reliable writers, that kites sometimes flock together. This is as foreign to the disposition of kites as it is suited to the nature of vultures. Therefore the דיות <i>dajoth</i> are certainly <i>vultures</i>, not <i>kites</i>. [col. 197] Yet I would not think that every kind of vulture is contained under this name, but only the black ones. 1. Because in <i>Deut 14:13 17.</i> <i>Moses</i> distinguishes דיה <i>daja</i> and רחם <i>racham</i> as different animals; and that the latter signifies <i>a kind of vulture largely white</i> will be shown below. 2. Because דיה <i>daja</i> and דיו <i>dejo</i>, <i>ink</i>, are cognates. Not to mention that the <i>Syrians</i> and <i>Persians</i> call a <i>demon</i> ܕܝܘܐ [דיוא] <i>dajava</i>, or <i>deva</i>, from the appearance of a black colour, in which it chiefly shows itself to men, to strike greater terror into those who see it. Hence a certain species of <i>savin</i> is called in <i>Persian</i> ديودار [dīvdār] <i>diudar</i>, that is, <i>demon's tree</i>. So also <i>that kind of vulture which is black</i> is called דיה <i>daja</i> from its colour. And <i>Elias</i> [=Elijah Levita (1469–1549), author of the Aramaic lexicon Meturgeman and of Tishbi] rightly says: <i>The bird that is black</i> כדיו, <i>like ink, is called</i> דיה Daja. Nor without reason does <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] refer דיה and דיו to the same root. Moreover, much can be read about the black vulture in the ancients. Thus <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 6, says that <i>among vultures the black ones prevail</i>. And among the <i>poets</i>, one of the gravest punishments of the underworld is to be gnawed by a black vulture. Hence <i>Juvenal</i>, <i>Satire</i> 13,

‎— — — <i>nor the rock, or the punishment</i> <i>of the black vulture</i>. — — — — — —

‎And <i>Seneca</i> in the <i>Thyestes</i>, of <i>Tityus</i>:

‎<i>With his dug-out entrails he feeds the black birds.</i>

‎<i>Black birds</i>, that is, <i>black vultures</i>. <i>Physicians</i>, too, prefer to use the black vulture in their remedies. Thus <i>Samonicus</i>, in chapter 14, would have applied, to soothe pain of the eyes,

‎— — — — <i>the gall of the black vulture,</i> <i>with which blades of celandine have been mixed.</i>

‎And in chapter 34, πρὸς εὐτοκίαν [pros eutokian] [for an easy delivery]:

‎— — <i>they place beneath the dung of the black vulture,</i> <i>so that she may scarcely feel the birth that presses close.</i> [II, 786] And in chapter 58, for the falling sickness:

‎<i>The gall of the black vulture is of help with old</i> Bacchus.

‎Others, however, read <i>ampli</i> [large]; and <i>Pliny</i> makes an exception for remedies against serpents, in which, he writes in book 29, chapter 4, black vultures are less effective. <i>Among birds</i> (he says) <i>the vulture is foremost as a help against serpents. It has also been noted that the black ones have less power.</i> That those <i>black vultures</i> are properly called αἰγυπιοὺς [aigypious], that they are of both sexes, unlike other vultures, and that they stand between the eagle kind and the vulture kind, <i>Aelian</i> reports in book 2, chapter 46, in these words: Τοὺς δὲ αἰγυπιοὺς (λέγουσιν) ἐν μεθορίῳ γυπῶν ὄντας καὶ αἰετῶν, εἶναι καὶ ἄῤῥενας, καὶ τὴν χρόαν πεφυκέναι μέλανας [tous de aigypious (legousin) en methoriō gypōn ontas kai aietōn, einai kai arrhenas, kai tēn chroan pephykenai melanas]. And <i>Philes</i> [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)] hints that <i>aegypii</i> are so called for <i>Aegyptii</i> [Egyptians], when he writes in chapter 2: εἶναι δὲ καὶ τοὺς μέλανας ὡς Αἰγυπτίους [einai de kai tous melanas hōs Aigyptious], <i>that they too are black, like</i> Egyptians<sup>R4</sup>. But αἰγυπιὸς [aigypios] is one thing, Αἰγύπτιος [Aigyptios] another. The former comes from γυπός [gypos]; the latter is formed from <i>Cophti.</i>

**Notes**

‎R1 *(p. 782)* To these Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer] (<i>in den Fragen an eine Gesellschaft von Gelehrten, die nach Arabien reisen</i>, p. 305. 306.) adds the Syriac and the Alexandrian translators, about whom, however, we must look a little more carefully. That the Syriac translator puts his ܕܝܬܐ [דיתא] for דיה might indeed seem doubtful, since for the <i>six</i> bird names that are read in the Hebrew text of Deut 14:12–13. he gives only these <i>two</i>: ܢܨܐ ܘܕܝܬܐ [נצא ודיתא]. It is nevertheless clear from the fact that the same translator has that same ܕܝܬܐ [דיתא] for the Hebrew דיות in Isa 34:15. That the <i>kite</i> is meant by that word is believed on the authority of the Arabic translator, since in Job 28:7 he renders the Syriac ܕܝܬܐ [דיתא] by his حداة [ḥidāh] (which Bochart proved in Chapter 7 to be the <i>kite</i>; whether rightly, I do not dare to decide). [II, 783] But the matter is much more uncertain with the Alexandrians. For the <i>three</i> bird names found in the Hebrew of Deut 14:15 they give only these two: τον γυπα και τον ἰκτινον [ton gypa kai ton iktinon]; whether the Hebrew דיה is expressed by either of them, and if so by which, no one will say with certainty. In Isaiah (XXXIV, 15.), moreover, they translate that same Hebrew word as ἐλάφους [elaphous].

‎R2 *(p. 783)* To these Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde] has recently been added, in <i>den Vermischten Sammlungen</i>, fasc. 3, ch. 3, p. 75 ff., relying chiefly on the authority of the Alexandrian translator; how much that is worth you will understand from what was said in the preceding note. Nor does that most learned man seem to have weighed sufficiently what Bochart adds immediately afterwards about the order of the Hebrew words in both passages, Lev 11:14 and Deuter. XIV, 13.

‎R3 *(p. 783)* But Oedmann, p. 81, asserts the contrary, producing Belon as witness, who writes that he saw great flocks of <i>kites</i>, and so dense that the crowd of birds in flight darkened the rays of the sun. But, with all respect to that most learned man, he seems to have made poor use of Belon's words. For in the passage Oedmann cites, Belon is speaking of kites changing their abode as winter approaches, and then, like absolutely all migratory birds, flying in great swarms. Here are Belon's own words (<i>de avibus</i> book 2, ch. 28): <i>Once, when we were on the shore of the Euxine Sea, in the part where Thrace lies, at about the end of April, on a very lofty hill next to the column that stands at the mouth of the Bosporus, where a fowler was spreading a net to catch sparrowhawks flying in from the right-hand stretch of the sea, we observed kites arriving there in flocks on a straight course, in such numbers and so dense a column that it seemed a marvel to us. — But when summer comes on, they return to Europe to escape the excessive heat of those regions.</i> But there is yet another thing that prevents us from taking דיות as <i>kites</i>. It is clear from Isa 34:15 that the דיות inhabit desolate places and wildernesses, whereas <i>kites</i> shun the wilderness and seek out cities and places frequented by men, as Aldrovandi [=Ulisse Aldrovandi, Ornithologia (1599–1603)] reports from his own observation and the testimony of many others, <i>Ornithol.</i> vol. 1, book 5, ch. 15, p. 401. Belon too, loc. cit., writes that in Egypt kites are so tame that they fly right up to the windows of houses.

‎a) *(p. 785)* <i>Chapter 2, p. 175.</i>

‎R4 *(p. 786)* Schneider, on Aelian 2, 46, suspects that the Αἰγυπιον [Aigypion] is the same bird that Belon (<i>de avibus</i> book 2, ch. 23) saw frequently in Egypt and called the black vulture, although it is not yet sufficiently established whether it is the same as Linnaeus' <i>percnopterus</i>. The same Schneider, on the <i>Reliqua</i> of Frederick II, p. 14, observes that the <i>vultur ater</i> [dark vulture] of the ancients is the Emperor's <i>Galeranus niger</i> (p. 28), the <i>Avoltoio nero</i> of Cetti [=Francesco Cetti, Storia naturale di Sardegna (1774–77)] in the <i>Histor. Nat. Sardin.</i> vol. 2, p. 9 ff., German trans. In the newest edition of the Linnaean System, vol. 1, part 1, p. 248, no. 9, this species of vulture appears under the heading <i>Vultur niger, with dusky primaries and tail feathers, the feathers of the feet black.</i> Yet Gmelin [=J. F. Gmelin, 13th edition of Linnaeus, Systema naturae (1788–93)] too doubts <i>whether it is a mere variety of the percnopterus</i> (which is the Hebrew and Arabic רחם). — But by what argument Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791) and Clavis über den Jesaias (1793)], in the <i>Clavis in Iesaj.</i> p. 234, was moved to take the דיות as <i>white vultures</i>, I for my part cannot grasp.
