# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 2: The eagle's wings, flight, nest, sight and food (vol. 2, pp. 750–758)

### ‎[II, 750] [col. 169] Chapter II.

### ‎<i>The eagle's wings, flight, nest, sight and food.</i>

‎Scripture commends the eagle's wings for their size, <i>Ezechielis XVII, 3. A great eagle with great wings, long-winged</i>, etc. And there the <i>Greeks</i> have Ὁ ἀετὸς ὁ μέγας, ὁ μεγάλου πτέρυγος, ὁ μακρὸς τῇ ἐκτάσει [Ho aetos ho megas, ho megalou pterygos, ho makros tē ektasei]. [col. 170] For the eagle both has great wings and spreads them as widely as possible in flight. Hence the <i>poets</i> call it τανύπτερος [tanypteros], as if one were to say <i>with outstretched wings</i>. <i>Hesiod, Theogony</i> line 523:

‎Καὶ οἱ ἐπ᾽ αἰετὸν ὦρσε τανύπτερον, [Kai hoi ep᾽ aieton ōrse tanypteron,]

‎<i>And he roused against him an eagle with outstretched wings.</i> And <i>Pindar, Pyth.</i> 5:

‎Τανύπτερος ἐν ὄρνιξιν αἰετὸς, [Tanypteros en ornixin aietos,]

‎<i>The eagle, among birds the one with the most outstretched wings.</i> This the greatest of poets describes thus in the last book of the <i>Iliad</i>, line 317:

‎Ὅσση δ᾽ ὑψορόφοιο θύρη θαλάμοιο τέτυκται Ἀνέρος ἀφνειοῖο εὐκληῒς ἀραρυῖα, Τόσσ᾽ ἄρα τοῦ ἑκάτερθεν ἔσαν πτερά. [Hossē d᾽ hypsorophoio thyrē thalamoio tetyktai Aneros aphneioio euklēis araryia, Toss᾽ ara tou hekaterthen esan ptera.]

‎<i>As great as is the door of a rich man's lofty chamber, well bolted and skilfully wrought, so great were its wings on either side.</i> And in <i>Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], p. 206, <i>Georg Fabricius</i> mentions an eagle's nest found in <i>the year</i> 1500 between <i>Meissen</i> and <i>Bresa</i>, towns of <i>Germany</i>, with three chicks in it, the wings of one of which, stretched out crosswise, measured seven ells.

‎With these wings the eagle is borne along most swiftly and with the greatest force, especially when it swoops on its prey. Hence <i>Apuleius</i>, <i>Florida</i> book 1, says the eagle <i>hurls itself down from above upon its prey like a thunderbolt.</i> And in <i>Cicero</i>, <i>On Divination</i> book 2, when a runner reported to an interpreter that he had dreamed he had become an eagle, the latter said: <i>You have won, for no bird flies more vehemently than that one.</i> Accordingly the ancients relate that the serpent, otherwise a bold and fearless animal, is put to flight by the mere rushing sound of the eagle's wings. <i>Aelian, Hist.</i> book 2, ch. 26: Καὶ τόν γε τῶν πτερῶν αὐτοῦ ῥοῖζον, καὶ τὸ τῶν θηρίων ἀτρεπτότατον, ὅμως κατέπτηξεν ὁ δράκων ἀκούσας μόνον καὶ παραχρῆμα κατέδυ, καὶ ἀσμένως ἠφανίσθη [Kai ton ge tōn pterōn autou rhoizon, kai to tōn thēriōn atreptotaton, homōs kateptēxen ho drakōn akousas monon kai parachrēma katedy, kai asmenōs ēphanisthē]. <i>Philes</i> [=Manuel Philes, De animalium proprietate (14th c.)], ch. 1:

‎Τούτου δὲ καὶ τὸν ῥοῖζον ἀκούσας δράκων, Ἔπτηξέ τε παραχρῆμα, καὶ συνεστάλη, Καὶ τοὺς μυχοὺς ὑπῆλθε τοὺς ἀποκρύφους. [Toutou de kai ton rhoizon akousas drakōn, Eptēxe te parachrēma, kai synestalē, Kai tous mychous hypēlthe tous apokryphous.]

‎Hence <i>Festus</i> holds that the wind <i>aquilo</i> [north wind] is so called <i>from its most vehement flight, like that of an eagle.</i> And most think that the eagle itself is called ἀετός [aetos] in <i>Greek</i> from ἀΐσσειν [aïssein], [II, 751] that is, <i>to rush with force</i> and <i>to swoop</i>. We have derived it from עיט <i>ajit</i>, the name by which the <i>Hebrews</i> call a <i>bird of prey</i>. But it comes to much the same thing, for עיט is formed from העיט, that is, from <i>flying at</i> and <i>swooping</i>. All this serves to show that it is not without reason that Scripture so often compares the speed and force of those who rush into battle with eagles, and indeed with eagles swooping on their prey. Thus <i>Deut 28:49. The Lord will raise against thee a nation from afar, from the farthest ends of the earth,</i> כאשר ידאה הנשר, <i>as the eagle flies.</i> The Greeks: ὡσεὶ ὅρμημα ἀετοῦ [hōsei hormēma aetou]; and Jerome: <i>like an eagle flying with force.</i> So in <i>Iliad</i> 21, line 252, <i>Achilles</i>, rushing upon the <i>Trojans</i>, is described by the <i>Poet</i> as αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou oimat᾽ echōn], <i>having the onrush of an eagle.</i> For to the <i>Scholiast</i> οἴματα [oimata] are ὁρμήματα [hormēmata]; to <i>Hesychius</i>, Οἴματα· ἀετοῦ ὁρμήματα [Oimata: aetou hormēmata]. Likewise the same <i>poet</i> of <i>Hector</i>, <i>Iliad</i> 22, line 308:

‎Οἴμησεν δὲ ἀλεὶς, ὥς τ᾽ αἰετὸς ὑψιπετήεις, [Oimēsen de aleis, hōs t᾽ aietos hypsipetēeis,]

‎<i>Gathering himself, he swooped with force, like a high-flying eagle.</i> Wrongly, therefore, did <i>Philetas</i> want to read αἰετοῦ ὄμματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou ommat᾽ echōn], <i>having the eyes of an eagle</i>, instead of αἰετοῦ οἴματ᾽ ἔχων [aietou oimat᾽ echōn]. For the subject here is not the eagle's eyes but the force of its swoop. To the same point belongs <i>David</i>'s statement that <i>Saul</i> and <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan], most warlike men, were swifter than eagles, <i>2 Sam 1:23.</i> And what <i>Jeremiah</i> writes of the <i>Chaldeans</i>, <i>IV, 13. Their horses are swifter than eagles.</i> And of their king, <i>Ier. XLVIII, 40. He shall fly as an eagle, and shall spread his wings over</i> Moab. And again <i>Ier. XLIX, 22. He shall come up as an eagle, and fly, and spread his wings over</i> Bozrah. And <i>Lam 4:19. Our persecutors are swifter than the eagles of heaven.</i>

‎Job uses the same simile, but in a different context, <i>Job 9:26. They have passed by</i> [col. 171] (my days) <i>like ships</i> אבה <i>ebe, as the eagle flies to its food.</i> In these words, to say this in passing, I suspect with <i>Selomo</i> and others that <i>Ebe</i> is the name of a river, because even today there are two rivers named ابى [Abī] <i>Ebe</i> or <i>Ebbe</i> in those regions: one of them is near <i>Kufa</i>, the other in the district of <i>Babylonia</i> called <i>Wasit</i>, which is the <i>Mesene</i> of the <i>Greeks</i>. See the <i>Lexicon</i> of the distinguished <i>Golius</i> [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)], p. 2. Something else could also be adduced. In <i>Arabic</i> ابى [abā] <i>aba</i> is a verb meaning <i>to gird oneself for a journey</i>. So אניות אבה, <i>ships ebe</i>, could be called swift ships, girded for a journey, or <i>actuariae</i> [light vessels], if you prefer, which are the more nimble <sup>R1</sup>. Thus the spans of our swiftly running life are compared both with ships of this kind and with the birds we have mentioned, that [II, 752] is, with eagles hastening to their food. This is not only because the speed is equal in both cases, but also because nothing remains of time once it has slipped away, just as the eagle in the air and the ship in the sea leave no trace of themselves, as <i>Solomon</i> observes, <i>Prov 30:19.</i> Hence in <i>Wis 5:10–11. 12.</i> the author of the book, whoever he is, elegantly uses both similes to teach that we, scarcely born, at once so fail that nothing is left of the life we have lived.

‎Now another point must be observed in the flight of eagles: it is not only swift but also sustained. For the eagle does not tire quickly, but, when the situation demands, flies for a very long time and travels to quite distant places. Hence the <i>poets</i> imagine that two eagles, sent out by <i>Jupiter</i>, one from the farthest east and the other from the farthest west, did not cease from flight until they met at <i>Delphi</i>, or on neighbouring <i>Parnassus</i>. See <i>Pindar, Pyth. 4</i>, and the <i>Scholiast</i> there, <i>Strabo</i>, book 9, <i>Plutarch, De Orac.</i>, and <i>Claudian's Prologue</i> to the <i>Panegyric of Manlius Theodorus</i>. Of the same kind is the fiction of the <i>Arabs</i> in <i>Damiri</i>: وهو اشد الطير طيرانا واقوي جناحا حتي انه ليطير ما بين المشرق والمغرب في يوم واحد [wa-huwa ashadd al-ṭayr ṭayarānan wa-aqwā janāḥan ḥattā annahu la-yaṭīru mā bayna al-mashriq wa-al-maghrib fī yawm wāḥid] <i>It is of all birds the most vigorous in flight and has the strongest wings, so that it flies across all that lies between east and west in a single day.</i> Accordingly Scripture applies <i>eagle's wings</i> to those who are carried far away. Thus in <i>Rev 12:14</i> the woman, who is the church, is given two wings of a great eagle, with which to fly into the desert out of the serpent's sight. And in <i>Ps 139:9</i> the <i>Syriac</i> translator has: <i>If I raise my wings</i> ܐܝܟ ܕܢܫܪܐ [איך דנשרא], <i>like an eagle</i>, <i>and dwell in the uttermost sea.</i> And the <i>Arabic</i>: <i>If I were lifted up on two wings</i> كالنسر [ka-al-nasr] <i>like an eagle, so as to come to the ends of the sea.</i> The <i>Ethiopic</i> also: <i>If I take my wings</i> ከመ [kama] ፡ ንስር [nəsər] ፡ <i>like an eagle</i>, etc. Yet the <i>Hebrew</i> does not have כנפי נשר, <i>wings of an eagle</i>, but כנפי שחר, <i>wings of the dawn</i>. This is followed by the <i>Greeks</i>, the Chaldee, the Vulgate translator, Jerome and all the <i>Hebrews</i>. Here also belongs the statement in <i>Proverb. XXIII, 5.</i> that fleeting riches <i>take on the wings of an eagle, with which they fly off into heaven</i>, that is, they slip away very swiftly and are removed far from you, so that they are no longer in your possession. Hence in Lucian's <i>Timon</i> <i>Plutus</i>, the god of riches, says that he limps and advances only slowly when he is sent by <i>Jupiter</i> to someone to increase his wealth. Ὁπόταν δὲ ἀπαλλάττεσθαι δέῃ [Hopotan de apallattesthai deē], he says, πτηνὸν ὄψει πολὺ τῶν ὀρνέων ὠκύτερον [ptēnon opsei poly tōn orneōn ōkyteron]· <i>But when I must depart, you will see me winged, far swifter than birds.</i> So, since God says in <i>Exod 19:4</i> that he carried the <i>Israelites</i>, as they went out of <i>Egypt</i>, as on eagles' wings, the <i>Jews</i> infer from this that they covered 120 miles in one hour from <i>Rameses to Succoth</i>. So R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] on <i>Exod 12:37</i>: מרעמסס סכתה מאה ועשרים מיל היו ובאו שם לפי שעה שנ״ ואשא אתכם על כנפי [col. 172] [II, 753] נשרים, <i>From</i> Rameses to Succoth <i>is a journey of one hundred and twenty miles, which they covered in the space of one hour, as it is said: “And I will carry them on eagles' wings”</i>.

‎Nor should this be omitted: there is no bird that rises higher than the eagle <sup>R2</sup>. Hence in the oracle given concerning the <i>Athenians</i>,

‎Αἰετὸς ἐν νεφέλῃσι γενήσεαι, — — [Aietos en nephelēsi genēseai, — —]

‎<i>You will be an eagle in the clouds</i>: the Scholiast on <i>Aristophanes</i>, <i>Birds</i>, says this means that they τοσοῦτον αὐξηθήσεσθαι, ὅσον αἰετὸς τῶν ἄλλων ὀρνέων ἐν ταῖς νεφέλαις προὔχει [tosouton auxēthēsesthai, hoson aietos tōn allōn orneōn en tais nephelais prouchei], <i>will be increased as much as the eagle in the clouds stands out above the other birds.</i> <i>Aben Ezra</i> [=Abraham ibn Ezra] too, on <i>Job 29:30</i>: אין עוף שיגבה יותר ממנו, <i>There is no bird that rises higher.</i> And <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], on <i>Jer 49:16</i>, asserts that it flies גבוה מכל העופות, <i>higher than all birds.</i> And <i>Apuleius</i>, <i>Florida</i> book 1: <i>For the eagle, when it has raised itself most high, up to the clouds, borne on its wings, beyond that whole space where it rains and snows, beyond which summit there is no place for thunderbolt or lightning, in the very floor, so to speak, of the aether and the topmost height of winter.</i> And <i>Horus</i>, book 2, ch. 53: ὑψηλότερον πάντων τῶν πετεινῶν ἵπταται [hypsēloteron pantōn tōn peteinōn hiptatai], <i>it flies highest of all birds.</i> Hence the <i>poets</i> call it ὑψιπετὴς [hypsipetēs] <i>and</i> ὑψιπετήεις [hypsipetēeis] <sup>a)</sup>, <i>high-flying.</i> Indeed, for this very reason, that it flies on high, if we believe <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>Histor.</i> book 9, ch. 32, θεῖον οἱ ἄνθρωποί φασιν εἶναι μόνον τῶν ὀρνέων [theion hoi anthrōpoi phasin einai monon tōn orneōn], <i>men say it alone among birds is divine.</i> At least from this arose the fables of the eagle in the bosom of <i>Jupiter</i>, and as the minister of his thunderbolts; and that some dreamed its feathers were scorched by the elemental fire, others by the rays of the nearby sun. Not to mention the eagle that used to be released from the pyre of the <i>Caesars</i>, as though to carry the emperor's soul to heaven. For so it was believed. <i>Dio</i>, book 56, on the <i>funeral of Augustus</i>: Καὶ ἡ πύρα μὲν ἀναλίσκετο· ἀετὸς δέ τις ἐξ αὐτῆς ἀφεθεὶς ἀπίπτατο, ὃ καὶ δὲ τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ ἐς τὸν οὐρανὸν ἀναφέρων [Kai hē pyra men anaKisketo; aetos de tis ex autēs aphetheis apeptato, ho kai de tēn psychēn autou es ton ouranon anapherōn]. <i>And the pyre was consumed, and from it flew an eagle, bearing his soul to heaven.</i> <i>Herodian</i>, book 4: Ἀετὸς ἀφίεται σὺν τῷ πυρὶ ἀνελευσόμενος εἰς τὸν αἰθέρα, ὃς φέρειν ἀπὸ γῆς ἐς οὐρανὸν τὴν τοῦ βασιλέως ψυχὴν πιστεύεται ὑπὸ Ῥωμαίων [Aetos aphietai syn tō pyri aneleusomenos eis ton aithera, hos pherein apo gēs es ouranon tēn tou basileōs psychēn pisteuetai hypo Rhōmaiōn]: <i>Together with the</i> (kindled) <i>fire an eagle is released to ascend into the aether, which the</i> Romans <i>believe carries the emperor's soul from earth to heaven.</i> Accordingly on ancient coins one may see on one side the emperor's portrait, on the other an eagle on which the emperor sits, with this inscription: CONSECRATIO S. C. And if a king or prince dreams that he is carried by an eagle, this portends death for them, writes <i>Artemidorus</i>, book 2,

‎[II, 754] ch. 20, because there was an old custom πλάσσειν τε, καὶ γράφειν ἐπὶ ἀετῶν ὀχουμένους [plassein te, kai graphein epi aetōn ochoumenous], <i>to sculpt and paint them seated on eagles.</i> What of the fact that from Scripture itself it seems one can gather that the eagle flies right up to heaven and has its nest among the stars? For so <i>Solomon</i>, Proverb. XXIII, 5.: <i>Like an eagle it will fly to heaven.</i> And <i>Obadiah</i>, vers. 4., addressing the <i>Edomites</i>, proud of the height of their strongholds: <i>Though you exalt yourself like the eagle, and though you set your nest among the stars, thence I will bring you down, says the Lord.</i> But even boys know that <i>heaven</i> is taken by the <i>Hebrews</i> for the <i>air</i>. And the <i>nest set among the stars</i> is said with the same hyperbole with which the king of <i>Babylon</i> boasts, <i>Isa 14:13. I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God.</i> Not unlike this is the boast of <i>Xerxes</i> in <i>Herodotus</i>, book 7, ch. 10: Γῆν τε τὴν Περσίδα ἀποδέξομεν τῷ Διὸς αἰθέρι ὁμουρέουσαν [Gēn te tēn Persida apodexomen tō Dios aitheri homoureousan], <i>We shall make the</i> Persian <i>land border on the aether of</i> Jupiter. That is, just as exalting one's throne above the stars and making one's land border on the aether of <i>Jupiter</i> means raising it to the highest summit of loftiness, so one who nests in the loftiest places is said <i>to set his nest among the stars</i>. That the eagle does this, besides <i>Obadiah</i>, <i>Jeremiah</i> also teaches in a passage parallel to this, addressed to the same <i>Edomites</i>, Ier. XLIX, 16.: <i>O you who dwell in the clefts of the rock, who</i> [col. 173] <i>hold the summit of the hill. Though you should make your nest as high as the eagle, thence I would bring you down.</i> And there the <i>Chaldee</i> paraphrast: <i>You are like the eagle, which lives</i> בשני כיפא, <i>on the crags of the rock: its dwelling is</i> בתקוף רומא, <i>in a lofty stronghold.</i>

‎To these must be added the famous passage on the eagle in <i>Job 39:27</i> var. <i>30.</i>, in which God addresses his servant thus: <i>Will the eagle rise up at your command, and set her nest on high? She dwells on the rock, and on the sheer crag</i> as <i>in a citadel she abides. From there she searches out food, her eyes gazing far off; and her chicks suck up blood; and where the slain are, there is she.</i> Since all of this pertains to the nature of the eagle, it must be expounded carefully. <i>Will the eagle</i>, he says, <i>rise up at your command?</i> namely in lofty flight. Man did not give the eagle this nature, that she should rise up to the very clouds; nor can you steer her in flight and bend her to your will, since at times she is so high in the air that she wholly escapes the sight of men. <i>At your command,</i> etc. <i>will she set her nest on high</i> in places that are often impassable and inaccessible to us? This the following words explain thus: <i>She dwells on the rock, and on the sheer crag</i> as <i>in a citadel she abides.</i> The <i>Septuagint</i>: Ἐπ᾽ ἐξοχῇ πέτρας, καὶ ἀποκρύφῳ [Ep᾽ exochē petras, kai apokryphō]. Had they not rather written καὶ ἀποκρήμνῳ [kai apokrēmnō]? So <i>Jerome</i>: <i>She remains on the rocks, she lingers on sheer flints and on inaccessible cliffs.</i> Not very differently <i>the Philosopher</i>, on the nests of eagles, <i>Histor.</i> book 9, ch. 32: Ποιοῦνται δὲ αὐτὰς οὐκ ἐν πεδινοῖς τόποις, ἀλλ᾽ ἐν ὑψηλοῖς μάλιστα μὲν, καὶ ἐν πέτραις ἀποκρήμνοις· [Poiountai de autas ouk en pedinois topois, all᾽ en hypsēlois malista men, kai en petrais apokrēmnois] <i>They make them not in level places but in lofty ones, and chiefly on sheer cliffs.</i> And <i>Pliny</i>, book 10, ch. 3: <i>They nest on rocks.</i> And <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], in the chapter <i>On</i> النسر [al-nasr] or the <i>Eagle</i>: تبيض في الاماكن [tabīḍu fī al-amākin] [II, 755] العالية [al-ʿāliya] <i>She lays her eggs in lofty places</i> <sup>R3</sup>. And in the chapter <i>On</i> العقاب [al-ʿuqāb] <i>alokab,</i> or the <i>Melanaeetus</i>: ولا تقعد الا علي الاماكن المرتفعة [wa-lā taqʿudu illā ʿalī al-amākin al-murtafiʿa] <i>She dwells only in elevated places.</i> As <i>Pliny</i> says of the same bird: <i>It keeps to the mountains.</i> And <i>Albertus</i> of the herodius, the name by which barbarous writers call the larger eagle: <i>It is always found sitting and nesting on sheer flints.</i>

‎There are, moreover, two reasons why she betakes herself to these steep places, which the sacred writer touches on here in passing. The first, that they may serve her as מצודה, that is, as ὀχυρώματος [ochyrōmatos], or a <i>stronghold</i>, as <i>Aquila</i> rightly rendered it; and that the chicks, which she is forced to leave in the nest in order to provide food for them, may be safe by the very nature of the place. For such lofty crags are not open to just anyone. To this pertains what the <i>Arabs</i> relate in <i>Damir</i>: قيل لبشار بن يزد الاعمي الشاعر لو خيرك الله أن تكون حيوانا ماذا كنت تختار قال العقاب لانها تلبث حيث لا يبلغها سبع ولا ذواريع وتحيد عنها سباع الطير [qīla li-Bashshār ibn Yazd al-aʿmī al-shāʿir law khayyaraka Allāh an takūna ḥayawānan mādhā kunta takhtār qāla al-ʿuqāb li-annahā talbathu ḥaythu lā yablughuhā sabʿ wa-lā dhawārīʿ wa-taḥīdu ʿanhā sibāʿ al-ṭayr] Besar, <i>son</i> of Iazid, <i>the blind poet, when asked what he would choose if God gave him the option of becoming any animal, answered:</i> Alokab (that is, that he would choose the <i>melanaeetus</i>), <i>because it dwells in places inaccessible to all wild beasts and animals, and even the birds of prey flee from meeting it.</i>

‎The second reason why the eagle prefers to dwell in a lofty place is expressed thus in <i>Job</i>: [col. 174] <i>From there she searches out food, her eyes gazing far off.</i> Nor did this escape <i>the Philosopher</i>, who says of the eagle in the place cited above: Ὑψοῦ δὲ πέτεται, ὅπως ἐπὶ πλεῖστον τόπον καθορᾷ [Hypsou de petetai, hopōs epi pleiston topon kathora], <i>She flies high, so as to look down upon more places.</i> And Apuleius: <i>When therefore the eagle has borne herself up there,</i> etc. <i>looking down upon everything from there,</i> etc. <i>she gazes around and seeks where above all she should swoop down from on high upon her prey.</i> These things agree with the words of <i>Job</i>: <i>From there she searches out food, her eyes looking down from afar.</i> <i>Searches out</i> is in Hebrew חפר, a verb which properly means <i>to dig</i>, but here <i>to search out with the eyes.</i> The same kind of metaphor is found in the verb <i>rimari</i> [to search out]. For properly <i>rimari</i> is <i>rimas agere</i> [to make cracks], and almost the same as <i>fodere</i> [to dig]. Hence <i>the Poet</i> in book 3 of the <i>Georgics</i>:

‎<i>So with difficulty they scratch the earth with hoes, and with their very nails dig in the grain.</i> — — — [II, 756] Nor is it added in vain that the eagle gazes far off with her eyes. For no animal is more sharp-sighted than the eagle. <i>Homer, Iliad</i> ρ΄ [17], line 674:

‎— — — ὥς τ᾽ αἰετὸς, ὅν ῥά τέ φασιν Ὀξύτατον δέρκεσθαι ὑπουρανίων πετεηνῶν. [hōs t᾽ aietos, hon rha te phasin Oxytaton derkesthai hypouraniōn peteēnōn]

‎<i>Like the eagle, which they say sees most keenly of the birds of the air.</i> Where the <i>Scholiast</i> says: Αἰετός· ὄρνις δορατικὸς (an legendum διορατικὸς) καὶ ὀξὺς ἐν τῷ ὁρᾷν, καὶ ἵπτασθαι [Aietos· ornis doratikos (or should we read dioratikos) kai oxys en tō horan, kai hiptasthai]. <i>Aelian</i> likewise, <i>Hist.</i> book 1, ch. 42: Αἰετὸς δὲ ὀρνίθων ὀξυωπήστερος [Aietos de ornithōn oxyōpēsteros]. <i>Horace</i>, Serm. 1, <i>Sat.</i> 3:

‎— — — — <i>you see as sharply</i> <i>as either the eagle or the</i> Epidaurian <i>serpent</i>.

‎Seneca, <i>De Beneficiis</i> book 2, ch. 29: <i>They complain that dogs surpass us in keenness of scent, and eagles in sharpness of sight.</i> Apuleius, <i>Florida</i> book 1: <i>If they were stronger in the judgement of the eyes than of the mind, then surely wisdom would have to be conceded to the eagle.</i> Isidore, <i>Orig.</i> book 12, ch. 7: <i>It is said to have such power of vision that, when it is borne over the seas on motionless wing and is not visible to human eyes, it sees little fish swimming from so great a height.</i> <i>Sidonius</i>, book 7, <i>Letter</i> 14: <i>But perhaps they prefer the power of seeing: as though the sight of eagles were not more eminent.</i> <i>Philes</i>, ch. 1:

‎Πλὴν ὀξυδερκής ἐστιν ὁ πτηνοκράτωρ, Καὶ μέχρι σητὸς ἐκ νεφῶν πάντα βλέπει· [Plēn oxyderkēs estin ho ptēnokratōr, Kai mechri sētos ek nephōn panta blepei]

‎<i>But the prince of birds is sharp-sighted</i> (that is, the eagle), <i>and from the clouds sees everything, down to a moth.</i> Apostolius, Adage 62: Ὀξύτατα ὁρᾷ ἐκ πολλοῦ τοῦ αἰθέρος [Oxytata hora ek pollou tou aitheros]. And among the <i>Arabs, Damir</i>, though not without hyperbole: وهو حاد البصر يري الجيفة من اربع ماية فرسخ [wa-huwa ḥādd al-baṣar yarī al-jīfa min arbaʿ māya farsakh] <i>It is of the keenest sight, so that it sees a carcass from four hundred parasangs.</i> To this many refer the name <i>aquila</i>, as if it were said <i>from the sharpness</i> [<i>acumen</i>] <i>of sight.</i> Accordingly it alone of animals is said to gaze at the sun with unblinking eyes, and not to regard its chicks as genuine unless they endure the light of the sun.

‎There follows in <i>Job</i>: <i>And its chicks suck up blood</i>, namely from the prey which the mother carries to the nest. <i>They suck up</i> is in Hebrew יעלעו, by metathesis for ילועו. Since this is without parallel, some interpreters have stumbled over this word. <i>The Greeks</i> have φύρονται ἐν αἵματι [phyrontai en haimati], as if they had read יתגללו בדם, as in <i>2 Sam 20:12</i> πεφυρμένος ἐν τῷ αἵματι [pephyrmenos en tō haimati] stands for the <i>Hebrew</i> מתגלל בדם. This is plainly foreign to the sense. Nor do I understand what ἐστυμισμένοι [estymismenoi] in <i>Aquila</i> means. Be that as it may, <i>Jerome</i>, <i>the Chaldee</i>, the <i>Syriac</i>, the <i>Arabic</i> and most of the <i>Hebrews</i> render it very well, and according to the mind of the sacred writer, <i>they lick</i> or <i>suck up blood.</i> You will say that the eagle is πάμπαν ἄποτον [pampan apoton], <i>wholly without drink.</i> For so <i>the Philosopher</i> asserts, <i>History</i> book 8, ch. 3 and 18,

‎[II, 757] where he charges <i>Herodotus</i> with ignorance, because in the siege of <i>Nineveh</i> he makes an eagle, presiding over an augury, drink. In the <i>Assyrian History</i>, that is, which is no longer extant; for in those works of <i>Herodotus</i> that are extant nothing of the kind is found. Hence <i>Aelian</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 2, ch. 26: Οὐδέποτε αἰετὸς οὔτε πηγῆς δεῖται, οὔτε γλίχεται κονίστρας, ἀλλὰ δί- [col. 175] ψους ἀμείνων ἐστί· [Oudepote aietos oute pēgēs deitai, oute glichetai konistras, alla dipsous ameinōn esti] <i>The eagle never needs a spring, nor does it seek to roll in the dust, but it is superior to thirst.</i> Likewise: Ὑπερφρονῶν δὲ καὶ τῶν ὑδάτων, καὶ τῆς ἀναπαύσεως· [Hyperphronōn de kai tōn hydatōn, kai tēs anapauseōs] <i>And it scorns both waters and rest.</i> And <i>Gregory of Nyssa</i> in the book <i>On the Soul</i>: Πολλὰ τῶν ζώων ἐστὶ μὴ πίνοντα, ὡς ἐπί τινων ἀετῶν ἱστόρηται· [Polla tōn zōōn esti mē pinonta, hōs epi tinōn aetōn historētai] <i>There are many animals that abstain from drink, as is related of certain eagles.</i> <i>Pisides</i> in <i>Suidas</i>:

‎Ἀλλ᾽ εἰσι δίψης καὶ κρύους ἀνώτεροι, [All' eisi dipsēs kai kryous anōteroi] <i>And they know not the force of thirst and cold.</i>

‎And <i>Suidas</i> himself, under Γαμψώνυχος [Gampsōnychos], has this: Οἷός ἐστιν ἀετὸς, ἱέραξ, κεγχρηΐς, ἄλλα τε τὰ μὴ πίνοντα· [Hoios estin aetos, hierax, kenchrēis, alla te ta mē pinonta] <i>Such as the eagle, the hawk, the kestrel, and other animals that do not drink.</i> And <i>Apostolius</i>, Adage 62: Οὐ γὰρ ἁλίσκεται ἐν νεφέλαις ὁ ἀετὸς, καὶ δίψους ἀμείνων ἐστί. [Ou gar haliksetai en nephelais ho aetos, kai dipsous ameinōn esti] <i>For the eagle is not caught in the clouds, nor is it overcome by thirst.</i> But there is nothing contrary to these in <i>Job</i>. For he does not write that eagles drink water, but that they <i>suck up blood</i>; which the very authors who deny the eagle drink themselves write. Thus, on the authority of <i>Oppian</i> in the <i>Ixeutica</i>, cited by <i>Gesner</i>: <i>The blood of the animals they have caught suffices them, but water they do not taste at all.</i> As <i>Horapollo</i> says of the hawk: Ὕδωρ οὐ πίνει τὸ καθόλου, ἀλλ᾽ αἷμα· [Hydōr ou pinei to katholou, all' haima] <i>It does not drink water at all, but blood.</i> And <i>Demetrius</i> in the <i>Hieracosophion</i>: Πρῶτον μὲν τὸν φάρυγγα τοῦ ἁλόντος ὄρνιθος τέμνε, καὶ δίδου πίειν τῷ ἱέρακι θερμοῦ ἀναβλύζοντος αἵματος· [Prōton men ton pharynga tou halontos ornithos temne, kai didou piein tō hieraki thermou anablyzontos haimatos] <i>First cut the throat of the captured bird, and give the hawk the warm, gushing blood to drink.</i> And <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 10, ch. 14: Σαρκῶν δὲ ἥδεται βορᾷ, καὶ πίνει αἷμα, καὶ τὰ νεόττια ἐκτρέφει· [Sarkōn de hēdetai bora, kai pinei haima, kai ta neottia ektrephei] <i>It delights in eating flesh, and drinks blood, and with these it rears its chicks.</i> <i>Hesychius</i> also, of the vulture, under Τόργιον [Torgion]: Τόργος· εἶδος γυπὸς αἱματοῤῥόφους· [Torgos; eidos gypos haimatorrophous] Torgos, <i>a kind of vulture that sucks blood.</i>

‎One thing about the eagle remains in <i>Job</i>: <i>Where the slain are, there is she.</i> <i>The slain</i>, that is, the bodies <i>of the slain</i> or <i>of the killed.</i> Accordingly, the words of <i>Christ</i> in <i>Matt 24:28</i> and <i>Luke 17:37</i> plainly allude to this passage: <i>Wheresoever the carcass is, there the eagles are gathered together.</i> The <i>Arabs</i> assert the same. <i>Damir</i>, above, on the eagle: <i>It sees a carcass from four hundred parasangs.</i> Again: واذا وقع علي جيفة وعليها عقبان تاخرت ولم تاكل ما دام تاكل منها [wa-idhā waqaʿa ʿalī jīfa wa-ʿalayhā ʿiqbān taʾakhkharat wa-lam taʾkul mā dām taʾkul minhā] <i>And when it comes upon a carcass on which there are ossifrages, it withdraws and does not eat their leavings.</i> Again: اذا وقع علي جيفة وامتلا منها [II, 758] لم يستطع الطيران حتي يثب وثبات يرفع بها نفسها طبقة بعد طبقة في الهوا [idhā waqaʿa ʿalī jīfa wa-imtalā minhā lam yastaṭiʿ al-ṭayarān ḥattā yathib wathabāt yarfaʿ bihā nafsahā ṭabaqa baʿd ṭabaqa fī al-hawā] <i>And when it comes upon a carcass and has filled itself with it, it cannot fly until, advancing by leaps, it has raised itself into the air by degrees.</i> But this properly belongs to the percnopterus, or gypaeetus, a degenerate kind of eagle of vulturine appearance, <i>which alone of the eagles carries off dead bodies</i>, as <i>Pliny</i> <sup>c)</sup> has it from <i>Aristotle</i> <sup>b)</sup>. That the percnos, which is also the morphnos, differs greatly from the percnopterus is clear from the same <i>Pliny</i>. For in him the percnos, or morphnos, is the eagle of the third kind, and the percnopterus of the fourth. And the descriptions of the two differ considerably from each other. But <i>Suidas</i>, not knowing this, attributed to the percnos, or morphnos (which is the same), what is proper to the percnopterus. His words are: Μόρφνος· εἶδος ἀετοῦ· μόνοι δὲ οὗτοι τῶν ἀετῶν οὐ κυνηγητοῦσιν, ἀλλὰ νεκροῖς σώμασι τρέφονται· [Morphnos; eidos aetou; monoi de houtoi tōn aetōn ou kynēgētousin, alla nekrois sōmasi trephontai] Morphnos, <i>a species of eagle; of the eagles these alone do not hunt, but feed on carcasses.</i> [col. 176] By a like error <i>Theophylact</i> took the eagles of which <i>Christ</i> speaks for vultures. Ὥσπερ, he says, ἐπὶ νεκρὸν σῶμα συνάγονται ὀξέως οἱ ἀετοὶ, οἱ γῦπες δηλαδή· [Hōsper epi nekron sōma synagontai oxeōs hoi aetoi, hoi gypes dēladē] <i>As the eagles, that is, the vultures, gather at once to a carcass.</i> And <i>Tzetzes</i>, <i>Chil.</i> 5, ch. 9, denies that the eagle kind touches carcasses in any way; his words are:

‎Λέοντες θῆρες, σὺν αὐτοῖς καὶ ἀετῶν τὸ γένος, Νεκρῶν οὐκ ἀπογεύονται τὸ σύνολον σωμάτων· [Leontes thēres, syn autois kai aetōn to genos, Nekrōn ouk apogeuontai to synolon sōmatōn]

‎<i>Lions, the beasts, and with them the eagle kind, do not taste dead bodies at all.</i> Unless exceptions are added to so general a statement, its truth will not stand, as what has already been adduced amply proves. These are further confirmed by the words of <i>Solomon</i>, Proverb. XXX, 17: <i>The eye that mocks his father and despises the instruction of his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it.</i> For rebellion against parents was then a capital crime, as is clear from <i>Exod 21:15 17. Lev 20:9</i> and <i>Deuter.</i> <i>XXI, 18.</i> And the bodies of the condemned remained unburied, exposed to wild beasts and carnivorous birds. Nor is it without reason that <i>Solomon</i> mentions the eye, since in the whole body no part is so exposed to the attacks of birds of prey. Hence <i>Epictetus</i>, explaining the sense of the old adage Κρεῖττον εἶναι ἐς κόρακας ἀπελθεῖν, ἢ ἐς κόλακας· [Kreitton einai es korakas apelthein, ē es kolakas] <i>It is better to go to the ravens than to flatterers</i>, says: Οἱ μὲν κόρακες τῶν τελευτηκότων τοὺς ὀφθαλμοὺς λυμαίνονται, ὅταν αὐτῶν οὐδέν ἐστι χρεία· οἱ δὲ κόλακες τῶν ζώντων τὰς ψυχὰς διαφθείρουσι, καὶ τὰ τούτων ὄμματα τυφλώττουσι· [Hoi men korakes tōn teleutēkotōn tous ophthalmous lymainontai, hotan autōn ouden esti chreia; hoi de kolakes tōn zōntōn tas psychas diaphtheirousi, kai ta toutōn ommata typhlōttousi] <i>Ravens ravage the eyes of the dead only when they are of no further use; but flatterers corrupt the souls of the living and blind their eyes</i> <sup>R4</sup>.
