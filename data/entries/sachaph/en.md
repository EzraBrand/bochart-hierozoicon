# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 18: Sachaph is the gull, or the cepphus (vol. 3, pp. 1–5)

### ‎[III, 1] Chapter XVIII. [col. 263]

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> שחף sachaph <i>is the gull or the cepphus,</i> <i>whence</i> κεπφωθεὶς [kepphōtheis] <i>Prov. VII,</i> 22.

‎As the ostrich is a huge bird, so it has detained us long. Now in what follows we shall settle matters more briefly. And first presents itself שחף <i>sachaph</i>, entirely omitted by the <i>Syrian</i>; rendered by the <i>Chaldeans</i> שחפא <i>sachpha</i>; by the <i>Arabs</i> سَاف [sāf] <i>saph;</i> by the <i>Samaritan</i> ࠔࠅࠐࠀ [שופא] <i>sopha:</i> words equally unknown. So no help comes from here. The <i>Greeks</i>, <i>Jerome</i>, <i>Munster</i> and <i>Castalio</i> [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)] explain it as the <i>gull</i>; Junius [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] as the <i>aesalon</i> [merlin], as if on the authority of <i>R. Judah</i>; Diodati as <i>la folica</i>, that is, the <i>fulica</i> [coot], in the <i>Italian</i> version, but the same man in the <i>French</i> as <i>la movette</i>, that is, the <i>gull</i>; the Genevans and the English as the <i>cuckoo</i>; Arias [=Benito Arias Montano, the interlinear Latin of the Antwerp Polyglot] as the <i>phthisic bird</i>, as if the name were taken from <i>phthisis</i>. For most of the <i>Hebrews</i> observe that this bird's name שחף <i>sachaph</i> alludes to שחפת <i>sachepheth</i>, a kind of disease, <i>Lev 26:16</i> and <i>Deut. XXVIII,</i> 22, whether because the bird itself is subject to this disease, or because it produces it in those who eat its flesh. But it is not well enough known either what kind of disease שחפת <i>sachepheth</i> is. <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] defines it: חולי שמשחפת את הבשר אנפו״ליש בלע״ז דומה לנפוח שהוקלה כפיחהו ומראית פניו זעיפה, <i>a disease that inflates the flesh;</i> in French <i>you would call them blisters; one who suffers from it is like a man who puffs his cheek slightly, and has the look of an angry man.</i> And <i>David de Pomis</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587), a Hebrew-Latin-Italian dictionary]: הוא חולי אשר יוליד אבעבועות כמו תפוחים לפי רש״י, <i>It is a disease producing pustules like apples, as R. Selomo Jarchi [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac), called Jarchi by the Christian Hebraists] says.</i> Yet <i>R. Selomo</i> says nothing about apples. But instead of דומה לנפוח, <i>Pomarius</i> [=David de Pomis, Ṣemaḥ David (Venice 1587)] seems to have read in <i>R. Selomo</i> דומה לתפוח, <i>like an apple</i>: a reading which what follows plainly refutes. However that may be, <i>R. Jonah</i> [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim] defines this same disease very differently, as these words of <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] in his <i>Lexicon</i> show: פירשו רבי יונה בל׳שון ערבי סחא״ף והוא החולי שהגוף מתנונה בו, R. Jonah <i>explains it as the disease called in</i> Arabic sochaph, <i>through which the body becomes</i> מתנונה <i>mathnuna,</i> that is, מתחלשת ומתכחשת ומתדלדלת, <i>weakened, and emaciated, and wasted.</i> For so the word מתנונה <i>mathnuna</i> is rendered in the <i>Aruch</i> [=the ʿArukh of Nathan ben Jehiel of Rome], taken from the <i>Talmudic</i> tractate <i>Sota</i>, ch. 4, fol. 26a

‎[III, 2] Hence the same <i>Pomarius</i>, who above wrote אבל לפי קמחי הוא חולי שיוליד דקות הגוף ואבודו, <i>But according to</i> Kimchi <i>it is a disease producing wasting of the body and its destruction.</i> <i>And Aquinas</i> [=Philippe d'Aquin, Dictionarium hebraeo-chaldaeo-talmudico-rabbinicum (Paris 1629)]: חולי מה שגורם כיחוש ודלות לגוף עד שמאבדו והוא מן קדחא, <i>It is a disease which causes leanness and wasting of the body, until it consumes it entirely; and this comes from fever.</i> Everyone can see that these words describe phthisis, or consumption. And indeed the Arabic سحاف [suḥāf] <i>sochaph</i> in <i>Giauhari</i> [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ] [col. 264] is the same as سل [sill] <i>sil.</i> That this word signifies <i>phthisis</i> is taught, among others, by <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] in the treatise <i>On Phthisis</i>, book 3, <i>Fen</i> 10, tract. 4, ch. 18. Moreover, the <i>Hebrew</i> שחפת <i>sachepheth</i> and the <i>Arabic</i> سحاف [suḥāf] <i>sochaph</i> have the same radicals, except that the <i>Arabs</i>, as is their custom, change the Hebrew <i>Shin</i> into <i>Sin</i>. So the two words certainly signify the same disease. Accordingly, in <i>Moses</i>, for שחפת the <i>Arabs</i> have السل [al-sill] <i>phthisis</i>, or حمي السل [ḥummā al-sill], <i>phthisical fever.</i> I also suspect that the <i>Greeks</i> rendered it ἀτροφίαν [atrophian], which is among the species of phthisis, although our copies have shown ἀπορίαν [aporian] for many centuries now. For so <i>Jerome</i> read, and accordingly he interprets שחפת as poverty. However that may be, that שחפת is not ἀπορίαν [aporian], <i>poverty</i>, is clear from the fact that in both passages of the Law it is counted among the kinds of diseases. Therefore שחפת is a disease, and indeed the one we have named: namely <i>phthisis</i>, or <i>consumption.</i> This is confirmed from the <i>Talmudists</i>, among whom שחוף means <i>consumptive</i>, as the distinguished <i>Buxtorf</i> [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] proves from <i>Sota</i> fol. 26b Moreover, among other <i>Hebrew</i> writers שחף in <i>Kal</i> is <i>to waste away</i>, in <i>Niphal</i> <i>to become consumptive</i>, in <i>Piel</i> <i>to cause to waste away</i>, as the same author affirms. It is therefore probable that שחף <i>sachaph</i> is a bird named from wasting, and that <i>Jerome</i>, following the <i>Greeks</i>, rightly rendered it <i>larus</i> [gull] (that is, French <i>movette</i>). Of this bird <i>Belon</i> [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], book 3, chapter 13, says: <i>No bird is so light in proportion to its bulk. For though it is bigger than a hen, it has not as much flesh as a quail.</i> This is found above all in the cepphus, a very light species of gull, which has a great deal of plumage but very little flesh. The <i>Scholiast on Aristophanes</i>, on the <i>Peace</i>: Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος, etc. Ἐκ δὲ τῆς θαλάττης ζῆν αὐτὸν λέγουσιν, ἐσθίοντα τὸν ἀφρόν· εἶναι δὲ αὐτὸν πολὺν μὲν ἐν τοῖς πτεροῖς, ὀλίγον δὲ ἐν τοῖς κρέασι [Euēthes zōon ho kepphos, etc. Ek de tēs thalattēs zēn auton legousin, esthionta ton aphron; einai de auton polyn men en tois pterois, oligon de en tois kreasi]. <i>The cepphus is a stupid animal</i>, etc. <i>They say that it lives from the sea, eating the foam, and that it has much plumage but little flesh.</i> Hence <i>Hesychius</i>: Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου κουφοτάτου, περὶ τὴν θάλασσαν διατρίβοντος, ὃ εὐχερῶς ὑπὸ ἀνέμου μετάγεται [Kepphos; eidos orneou kouphotatou, peri tēn thalassan diatribontos, ho eucherōs hypo anemou metagetai]. <i>Cepphus: a species of very light bird, accustomed to spend its time about the sea, which is easily carried off by the wind.</i> And <i>Suidas</i>: Κέπφος· εἶδος ὀρνέου ὁ λεγόμενος λάρος· ἔστι δὲ κοῦφον, καὶ ἐπιπλέον τοῖς κύμασιν [Kepphos; eidos orneou ho legomenos laros; esti de kouphon, kai epipleon tois kymasin]; <i>Cepphus: a species of bird, which is also called larus; it is light, and floats on the waves.</i> His statement that it is called by another name <i>larus</i> comes from the <i>Scholiast on Aristophanes</i>, on the <i>Plutus</i>, whose words on the cepphus are: Καλεῖται δὲ κοινῶς λάρος [Kaleitai de koinōs laros]. <i>It is commonly called larus.</i> Accordingly they are joined as synonyms in the proverb Κέπφος λάρος [Kepphos laros] in <i>Erasmus</i> [=Desiderius Erasmus, Adagia] and others. And in <i>Hesychius</i>, Κὴξ [Kēx] for <i>Apion</i> is ὁ λάρος [ho laros], but others render it κέπφον [kephon], [III, 3] and others again ἐφυϊάν [ephyian]; read αἰθυϊὰν [aithyian], that is, <i>diver.</i> Yet you may gather that the gull and the cepphus differ from these words of <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>History</i>, book 8, chapter 3: Περὶ δὲ τὴν θάλατταν καὶ ἀλκύων, καὶ κήρυλος, etc. Ἔτι δὲ ὁ λάρος ὁ λευκὸς, καὶ κέπφος, καὶ χαράδριος [Peri de tēn thalattan kai alkyōn, kai kērylos, etc. Eti de ho laros ho leukos, kai kepphos, kai charadrios]; <i>But about the sea there are also the alcyon and the cerylus,</i> etc. <i>Also the white gull, and the cepphus, and the charadrius.</i> The cepphus and the gull, then, are not entirely the same, but very similar birds. Hence the <i>Scholiast on Nicander</i>, on the <i>Alexipharmaca</i>: Ὁ δὲ κέπφος θαλάσσιόν ἐστι ὄρνεον παραπλήσιον λάρῳ [Ho de kepphos thalassion esti orneon paraplēsion larō]. <i>Gesner</i> [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium] thinks they are birds of the same genus, or that the cepphus is a species of gull. However the matter stands, the name שחף suits both the gull and the cepphus exceedingly well, since both birds are described as almost devoid of flesh, as if consumed by wasting <sup>R1</sup>.

‎The verb κεπφοῦσθαι [kepphousthai], formed from cepphus, means not only <i>to be carried away</i> and <i>to be lightly driven about by every wind like a cepphus,</i> but also <i>to be easily deceived</i> and <i>lured with bait.</i> So in <i>Prov. VII,</i> 22, of the heedless young man whom the adulteress has charmed with her enticements: Ὁ δὲ ἐπηκολούθησεν αὐτῇ κεπφωθείς [Ho de epēkolouthēsen autē kepphōtheis]; <i>But he follows her, like a cepphus, easily driven,</i> or <i>easily deceived.</i> Hesychius: Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρθεὶς, ἀπατηθείς [Kepphōtheis; epartheis, apatētheis]; [col. 265] <i>Carried away, deceived.</i> And again, after Κέφαλον [Kephalon]: Κεπφωθεὶς, καταγελασθείς· εἴρηται δὲ ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων [Kepphōtheis, katagelastheis; eirētai de epi tōn alogou dikēn anoētōs synepagomenōn]; <i>Mocked: it is said of those who, like a brute beast, are foolishly led along.</i> Suidas: Κεπφωθείς· ἐπαρθεὶς, ἐρεθισθεὶς, ὀξέως ἐλαυνόμενος [Kepphōtheis; epartheis, erethistheis, oxeōs elaunomenos]; <i>Carried away, stirred up, swiftly driven.</i> These are various interpretations of the same passage. But ἀπατηθεὶς [apatētheis] pleases me most. 1. Because in the passage we have cited κεπφωθεὶς [kepphōtheis] stands for the <i>Hebrew</i> פתאים, for which the <i>Greeks</i> read פתאים, that is, <i>simple ones,</i> εὐαπάτητοι [euapatētoi]. For it comes from the verb פתה, which they usually render ἀπατᾷν [apatan]. 2. Because the cepphus is a bird easily deceived, which boys, by offering it foam, draw to themselves without any trouble and catch by laying hands on it. Hence κέπφος [kepphos] is commonly used by the <i>Greeks</i> for a <i>fool.</i> Thus the proverb Κέπφος λάρος [Kepphos laros] strikes at the senseless man who is deceived by desire for anything and is caught. And in <i>Aristophanes,</i> in the <i>Peace,</i>

‎Καὶ κέπφοι τρήρωνες ἀλωπεκίδεσσι πέπεισθε [Kai kepphoi trērōnes alōpekidessi pepeisthe], [III, 4] <i>And you cepphi and doves</i> (that is, <i>fools and simpletons</i>) <i>trust little foxes.</i> And again in the <i>Plutus,</i> Ὦ κέπφε [Ō kepphe], <i>O cepphus,</i> that is, <i>O fool.</i> Accordingly, on the former passage the <i>Scholiast</i> says: Εὔηθες ζῶον ὁ κέπφος [Euēthes zōon ho kepphos], <i>The cepphus is a foolish animal.</i> And on the latter: Ὄρνεον γὰρ ἄφρον, ὅπερ φιλεῖ ἀφρὸν θαλάττιον ἐσθίειν. Τοῦτο βουλόμενοι οἱ τῶν ἁλιέων παῖδες χειρώσασθαι, ῥίπτουσι τὸ πρῶτον πόῤῥωθεν ἀφρὸν, εἶτα ἐγγύτατα, εἶτα φέρουσιν τοῦτο ἐν ταῖς χερσὶν, καὶ οὕτω κατὰ μικρὸν ἀπατῶντες, εὐχερῶς ἀγρεύουσι τοῦτο ἐμπεσὸν εἰς τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῶν· εἴληπται οὖν εἰς παροιμίαν ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλογίστων ἀνδρῶν, καὶ ἀνοήτων [Orneon gar aphron, hoper philei aphron thalattion esthiein. Touto boulomenoi hoi tōn halieōn paides cheirōsasthai, rhiptousi to prōton porrhōthen aphron, eita engytata, eita pherousin touto en tais chersin, kai houtō kata mikron apatōntes, eucherōs agreuousi touto empeson eis tas cheiras autōn; eilēptai oun eis paroimian epi tōn alogistōn andrōn, kai anoētōn]. <i>For it is a foolish bird, which delights in eating sea foam. So when the fishermen's sons wish to catch it, they first throw foam from a distance, then nearer and nearer, afterwards they carry it in their hands, and so, luring it little by little, they easily catch it as it falls into their hands. Hence it has become a proverb for senseless and witless men.</i> The older writers had reported this very thing about this bird. So the <i>Philosopher,</i> <i>History of Animals,</i> book 9, chapter 35: Οἱ δὲ κέπφοι ἁλίσκονται τῷ ἀφρῷ, κόπτουσι γὰρ αὐτόν· διὸ προσραίνοντες θηρεύουσιν [Hoi de kepphoi haliskontai tō aphrō, koptousi gar auton; dio prosrainontes thēreuousin]. <i>The cepphi are caught with foam, for they go after it; therefore they hunt them by throwing foam at them.</i> And <i>Nicander,</i> <i>Alexipharmaca,</i> verse 165:

‎Ἀφρὸν ἐπεγκεράσοιο θοοῦ δορπήϊα κέπφου. Τῷ γὰρ δὴ ζωήν τε σαοῖ, καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖ. Εὖτε δόλοις νήχοντα κακοφθόρα τέκν᾽ ἁλιήων Οἰωνὸν χραίνωσιν. ὁ δ᾽ ἐς χέρας ἔμπεσε παίδων, Θηρεύων ἀφροῖο νέην κλύδα λευκαίνουσαν. [Aphron epenkerasoio thoou dorpēia kepphou. Tō gar dē zōēn te saoi, kai potmon epispei. Eute dolois nēchonta kakophthora tekn᾽ haliēōn Oiōnon chrainōsin. ho d᾽ es cheras empese paidōn, Thēreuōn aphroio neēn klyda leukainousan.]

‎<i>Mix in foam, the food of the swift cepphus. For by it the bird both preserves its life and brings on its doom, when the destructive sons of fishermen craftily spatter the swimming bird with it. And it falls into the boys' hands, chasing the fresh white spray of foam.</i> See more in the <i>Scholiast on Nicander, Hesychius, Suidas, the Etymologicum</i> <sup>R2</sup> and others, whom I have no time to copy out now. Nor is there need, since they merely repeat what has already been said. I will only note that in these words of <i>Hesychius</i> and <i>Suidas,</i> Κεπφωθεὶς· ἐπαρθεὶς [Kepphōtheis; epartheis], one should read ἐπαχθεὶς [epachtheis] instead of ἐπαρθεὶς [epartheis]. For on Hesychius's own authority Κεπφωθεὶς [Kepphōtheis] is said ἐπὶ τῶν ἀλόγου δίκην ἀνοήτως συνεπαγομένων [epi tōn alogou dikēn anoētōs synepagomenōn], of those who are foolishly led along like a brute beast.

**Notes**

‎R1 *(p. 3)* That the <i>cepphus</i> is a bird of the Linnaean genus <i>Procellaria</i> (<i>der Sturmvoegel</i> [the storm-birds]) was shown by J. G. SCHNEIDER in the Leipzig Repository of Natural History (<i>Leipziger Magazin zur Naturgeschichte und Oeconomie</i>) for the year 1786, p. 501 ff. But whether the שחף of the Hebrews is the same bird I would neither affirm nor deny, since apart from the name nothing about this animal occurs in the Hebrew text, and Bochart's conjecture rests on no other foundation than the etymology of the word שחף. We have already seen quite often that, where the question concerns the names of animals, nothing is more uncertain than this way of reasoning.

‎R2 *(p. 4)* And in OPPIAN, Ἰξευτικ. [Ixeutik.] book 2, ch. 10, p. 189, ed. Schneider.
