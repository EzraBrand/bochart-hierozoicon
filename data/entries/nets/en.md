# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 19: The hawk, nets in Hebrew (vol. 3, pp. 5–14)

### ‎[III, 5] [col. 265] Chapter 19.

### ‎<i>On the hawk, which is</i> נץ nets <i>to the Hebrews.</i>

‎Next comes נץ <i>nets</i>, that is, the <i>hawk</i>. For so all the translators render it, in complete [col. 266] agreement. And among the <i>Syrians</i> the very same bird is ܢܨܐ [נצא] <i>natsa</i> or <i>nitsa</i>; among the <i>Chaldeans</i> too, as נצצא <i>netsatsa</i>; and among the <i>Samaritans</i> ࠍࠑࠑࠄ [נצצה] <i>netsatsah.</i> The name is formed <i>from flight</i>. For in <i>Hebrew</i> נצה natsa means <i>to fly</i>. And in <i>Arabic</i> نض [naḍ] means <i>to keep moving the wings while flying</i>. Moreover, that this bird excels above all in flight is attested in more than one place by the most renowned of poets. Thus <i>Iliad</i> 15, line 237, where <i>Apollo</i> descends from <i>Ida</i>,

‎— — — ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς [irēki eoikōs] Ὠκέϊ, φασσοφόνῳ, ὅς τ᾽ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν [ōkei, phassophonō, hos t᾽ ōkistos peteēnōn],

‎<i>Like a swift hawk, the killer of wood pigeons, which is the swiftest of birds.</i> And <i>Odyssey</i> 13, line 86, where the ship of the <i>Phaeacians</i> that carried <i>Ulysses</i> to <i>Ithaca</i> is described,

‎— — — οὐδὲ μὲν ἴρηξ [oude men irēx] Κίρκος ὁμαρτήσειεν ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν [kirkos homartēseien elaphrotatos peteēnōn],

‎<i>Not even the circus hawk could keep pace with it, though the fleetest of birds.</i> And <i>Odyssey</i> 15, line 526:

‎Κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος [kirkos, Apollōnos tachys angelos], —

‎<i>The circus,</i> Apollo's <i>swift messenger.</i> Accordingly, in <i>Iliad</i> 13, line 819, it is foretold to <i>Hector</i> that he will one day wish

‎Θάσσονας ἰρήκων ἔμεναι καλλίτριχας ἵππους [thassonas irēkōn emenai kallitrichas hippous],

‎<i>That his fair-maned horses were swifter than hawks.</i> And not Apollo alone, but <i>Thetis</i> <sup>a)</sup> and <i>Neptune</i> too are said to move ἴρηξ ὡς [irēx hōs], <i>like a hawk</i>; or ὥς τ᾽ ἴρηξ ὠκύπτερος [hōs t᾽ irēx ōkypteros], <i>like a hawk with swift wings.</i> And in <i>Hesiod's</i> fable,

‎Ὡς ἔφατ᾽ ὠκυπετὴς ἴρηξ, τανυσίπτερος ὄρνις [hōs ephat᾽ ōkypetēs irēx, tanysipteros ornis],

‎<i>So spoke the swift hawk, the bird with outstretched wings.</i> And in <i>Aristophanes</i>, in the <i>Birds</i>, the <i>Sycophant</i> says,

‎Ἀλλὰ πτέρου με ταχέσι καὶ κούφοις πτεροῖς Ἱέρακος, ἢ κερχνῇδος [alla pterou me tachesi kai kouphois pterois hierakos, ē kerchnēdos]. — — —

‎<i>But fit me with the swift and light wings of a hawk, or of a cerchneis</i>: this too is a species of hawk; the <i>Romans</i> call it <i>tinnunculus</i> [kestrel]. But no one describes the flight of the hawk [III, 6] either more fully or more elegantly than the illustrious <i>Thuanus</i>, who, among the species of hawks, depicted the <i>triorches</i>, or <i>buteo</i> [buzzard], in flight thus in book 1 of <i>De re accipitraria</i>:

‎<i>See how, not in the manner of others, with whirring wings he is borne aloft and wheels in frequent circles in the air; but advancing in straight flight he strides through the void, soaring, and climbs to the stars of the great heaven. Then, when he has raised his head among the lofty clouds, he is swept down in a headlong plunge, rushes and rages in his fury, and strikes from on high the prey lying beneath his eyes.</i>

‎And in book 2 the same author says this of the hawk in general:

‎<i>You would scarcely believe in how short a time, flying, he overcomes long distances between places, how many seas he traverses.</i> — —

‎And further on, of the hawk of King <i>Francis</i>, which in one short night flew from the castle of <i>Villers</i> [col. 267] to <i>London</i>, and, recognized by its marks, was sent back to its master:

‎— — — <i>he flees through the breezes, the faithless truant, and with rapid wings through the air hastens faster than a missile, swifter than the winged</i> Eurus; <i>and while the sun bathes his locks in the</i> Tartessian <i>sea, and again tinges mortals with his</i> Eastern <i>lamp, he is borne over the Ocean</i> and the British Thames, <i>and at last, weary, alights upon the citadel of</i> London.

‎Rightly, then, among the <i>Egyptians</i> the hawk was the symbol of the winds. <i>Horus</i>, book 2, chapter 14: Τὴν ἀνατολὴν ἱέραξ ἐςὶ μετεώρου θέων ἀνέμους σημαίνει [tēn anatolēn hierax es[t]i meteōrou theōn anemous sēmainei]; <i>A hawk, rising aloft toward the east, signifies the winds.</i> The same was signified by ἱέραξ διατεταμένος τὰς πτέρυγας [hierax diatetamenos tas pterygas], <i>a hawk with outstretched wings</i>, as the same author adds. The <i>Tentyrites</i>, however, see it otherwise: comparing hawks with fire, μαρτύριον ἐπάγουσιν ἀπότομον αὐτῶν [martyrion epagousin apotomon autōn], <i>they adduce as evidence their headlong speed.</i> Nor are there lacking those who hold that for this very reason hawks were consecrated to the <i>Sun</i>. <i>Eustathius</i> on <i>Odyssey</i> 15: Ἱέραξ ἱεροῦται Ἡλίῳ Ἀπόλλωνι διά τε τὸ ὀξὺ τῆς κινήσεως· ὠκυπετὴς γὰρ ὁ ἱέραξ, καὶ ὁ Ἥλιος ὀξυκίνητος, καὶ διὰ τὴν ἐξ ἐτυμολογίας συγγένειαν. καὶ γὰρ ἵεται ῥᾷον ὁ Ἥλιος. ἐντεῦθεν δὲ καὶ ὁ ἱέραξ ὠνόμασαι [hierax hieroutai Hēliō Apollōni dia te to oxy tēs kinēseōs; ōkypetēs gar ho hierax, kai ho Hēlios oxykinētos, kai dia tēn ex etymologias syngeneian. kai gar hietai rhaon ho Hēlios. enteuthen de kai ho hierax ōnomasai]. <i>The hawk is sacred</i> to Apollo the Sun <i>because of the swiftness of its motion: for the hawk is fleet, as the Sun is of swift motion; and because of the kinship of etymology.</i> For the Sun <i>goes easily. Hence the hawk has its name.</i> To this etymology, however, I prefer Virgil's, in book 11 of the <i>Aeneid</i>: [III, 7] <i>As easily as the hawk, the sacred bird, from a high rock.</i>

‎Where the <i>sacer ales</i> [sacred bird] is ἱέραξ [hierax]; as in <i>Demetrius</i>, in the <i>Preface</i> to the <i>Hieracosophium</i>: Τῷ τῶν ἱερῶν ὀρνίθων γένει πρὸς σωτηρίαν τινὰ πορίσασθαι μηχανὴν [Tō tōn hierōn ornithōn genei pros sōtērian tina porisasthai mēchanēn], <i>"to find some art for the preservation of the race of sacred birds."</i> And again: Ἐσπούδασά τινα χρήσιμον θεραπείαν πρὸς σωτηρίαν τῷ ἱερῷ τῶν ὀρνίθων γένει καταλιπεῖν [Espoudasa tina chrēsimon therapeian pros sōtērian tō hierō tōn ornithōn genei katalipein], <i>"I have endeavoured to leave to the sacred race of birds some useful treatment for its preservation."</i> In these words it is plain that by <i>sacred birds</i> and <i>the race of sacred birds</i> τοὺς ἱέρακας [tous hierakas], that is, <i>hawks</i>, are meant. <i>"And why the hawk</i> is <i>so called</i> in Greek," says <i>Servius</i>, <i>"is not without a reason, which is known to those versed in sacred rites."</i> I would think this arose from the superstition of the <i>Egyptians</i>, for whom among the sacred animals none was so sacred as the ibis and the hawk. Thus, if anyone had killed some other animal unwillingly, he was fined at the discretion of the priests. Ὃς δ' ἂν ἶβιν, ἢ ἴρηκα ἀποκτένῃ, ἤν τε ἑκὼν, ἤν τε ἀέκων, τεθνάναι ἀνάγκη [Hos d' an ibin, ē irēka apoktenē, ēn te hekōn, ēn te aekōn, tethnanai anankē]; <i>"But whoever had killed an ibis or a hawk, whether willingly or unwillingly, had to die."</i> So it stands in the <i>Euterpe</i> of <i>Herodotus</i> [ch. 65].

‎Indeed, even today they count the <i>sacer</i> among the species of hawks or falcons. But, to say what the matter really is, the word is not <i>Latin</i> but <i>Arabic</i>: صقر [ṣaqr] or سقر [saqr] or زقر [zaqr], as some write it with <i>Sin</i> and with <i>Ze</i>, although it is more often written with <i>Sad</i>, from the verb صقر [ṣaqara] <i>sakara</i>, which means <i>to see sharply</i>, just like the <i>Chaldee</i> סכר <i>sekar.</i> The <i>Kamus</i>: الصاقر حديد البصر [al-ṣāqir ḥadīd al-baṣar] Sakir, <i>"one who is sharp-sighted."</i> Thus الصقر [al-ṣaqr], the noun derived from it (French <i>sacre</i>), first signifies كل طاير يصيد [kull ṭāyir yaṣīd], <i>every bird used in fowling.</i> Secondly, one of the four species of birds of prey. These are namely صقر [ṣaqr] <i>sacer</i>, شاهين [shāhīn] <i>sahin</i>, عقاب [ʿuqāb] <i>okab</i>, and بازي [bāzī] <i>bazi.</i> And the صقر [ṣaqr] <i>sacer</i> is subdivided into three further species: namely the صقر [ṣaqr] <i>sacer</i> properly so called, the كونج [kawnaj] <i>chauneg</i>, and the يويو [yūyū] [col. 268] <i>juju.</i> To describe each of these here would be long and tedious. Therefore we shall treat only of the <i>sacer</i> properly so called. It is otherwise called اجدل [ajdal] agdal, <i>robust</i>; اكدر [akdar] achdar, <i>dusky</i>; and قماطي [qumāṭī] kumati, <i>greedy for flesh</i>; and it is described by <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and others thus: هو من الجوارح بمنزلة البغال من الدواب لانه اصبر علي الشدة واحمل الغليظ الاذي واحسن القاء واشد اقدام علي جملة من الكركي وغيره ومزاجه ابرد من ساير ما تقدم ذكره من الحوارح وارطب وبهذا السبب يضرا علي الغزال والارنب وهو اهدي من البازي نفسا واسرع بالناس انسا واكثر منة [III, 8] منه قنعا ولبرد مزاجه لا يشرب ماء ولو اقام دهرا وكذلك يوصف بالبخر ونتي الفم وله كفان في يده كالسبع لانه يكف بهما علي ما اخذ اي يجمع [huwa min al-jawāriḥ bi-manzilat al-bighāl min al-dawābb li-annahu aṣbar ʿalā al-shidda wa-aḥmal al-ghalīẓ al-adhā wa-aḥsan ilqāʾ wa-ashadd iqdām ʿalā jumla min al-kurkī wa-ghayrihi wa-mizājuhu abrad min sāyir mā taqaddama dhikruhu min al-ḥawāriḥ wa-arṭab wa-bi-hādhā al-sabab yuḍrā ʿalā al-ghazāl wa-al-arnab wa-huwa ahdā min al-bāzī nafsan wa-asraʿ bi-al-nās unsan wa-akthar mna minhu qanaʿan wa-li-bard mizājihi lā yashrab māʾ wa-law aqāma dahran wa-kadhālika yūṣaf bi-al-bakhar wa-ntī al-fam wa-lahu kaffān fī yadihi ka-al-sabuʿ li-annahu yakuff bihimā ʿalā mā akhadha ay yajmaʿ], <i>"It stands to the other birds of prey as the mule to the other beasts of burden. For it is stronger, more able to bear heavier injuries, better in attack, and firmer of foot against every kind of crane and birds of that sort. And it is of a colder and also moister temperament than the other birds of prey treated above. Therefore it is trained for hunting gazelles and hares."</i> And than the bazi, <i>or hawk, it is more docile, is tamed by men more quickly, and is more easily satisfied. Because of the coldness of its temperament it never drinks water, however long it remains</i> (without drink). <i>Hence it is described as having a fetid and foul-smelling mouth. In its foot it has palms like wild beasts, with which it pounces on what it catches and holds it."</i> <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] writes that no hunting by birds of prey is so marvellous as when two <i>sacres</i>, flying at a gazelle's eyes or beating it with their wings, detain it until it falls into the hunters' hands. He also marvels that so small a bird لشجاعة خلقها الله تعالي فيه [li-shajāʿa khalaqahā Allāh taʿālā fīhi], <i>"because of the boldness which God Most High has put in it,"</i> dares to leap upon a crane, which is of so much greater bulk. The first originator of this kind of fowling among the <i>Arabs</i> was <i>Alharits, son of Muawiya</i>. So much in passing about the <i>sacer</i> <sup>R1</sup>.

‎<i>Callimachus</i> reckons six kinds of hawks, <i>Aristotle</i> ten, <i>Pliny</i> sixteen. But by size they are reduced to two, of which the <i>Philosopher</i> speaks in book 8, chapter 3 of the <i>History</i>: Ἱέρακες ἄμφω, ὅτε φαβοτύπος, καὶ ὁ σπιζίας, διαφέρουσι τὸ μέγεθος πολὺ ἀλλήλων [Hierakes amphō, hote phabotypos, kai ho spizias, diapherousi to megethos poly allēlōn]; <i>"Both hawks, namely the dove-hawk and the finch-hawk, differ much from each other in size."</i> The former more recent writers call <i>astur</i> (French <i>autour</i>); the latter <i>nisus</i> and <i>sparverius</i>. <i>Iulius Firmicus</i> indeed mentions <i>asturs</i> [col. 269] in book 5, chapter 7. But the name <i>nisus</i> seems taken from the <i>Hebrew</i> נץ <i>nets</i>, or from the <i>Chaldee</i> נצה <i>netsa.</i> To the same is referred the metamorphosis of <i>Nisus</i>, king of the <i>Megarians</i>, although the <i>poets</i> feign that he was changed not into a hawk but into a sea eagle. <i>Ovid</i>, in book 8 of the <i>Metamorphoses</i>:

‎<i>And now, made a bird, a haliaeetus with tawny wings.</i>

‎<i>Virgil</i>, or whoever else it was, in the <i>Ciris:</i> [III, 9] <i>And made her a winged haliaeetus, to dwell upon the earth.</i>

‎But among the species of hawks, or falcons, the <i>lanius</i> or <i>lanarius</i> [lanner] is so similar to the haliaeetus that they are distinguished only by their different diet. <i>Thuanus</i>, in his description of the <i>haliaeetus</i> in book 1 of <i>De re accipitraria</i>:

‎<i>This one has the same form as the lanner, and between the two</i> <i>There lies an obscure difference, by which each may be called</i> <i>Marine or lanner. Indeed, even the skilled often err,</i> <i>Their eyes deceived by the ambiguous look;</i> <i>And, did each not delight in a different diet,</i> <i>A most shameful error would often convict</i> <i>The masters of the art.</i> — — — —

‎So they could have been confused by the ancients and called by the same names because of the similarity of their form.

‎Of the hawk God speaks thus in <i>Iob. XXXIX,</i> 26. [var. 29.]: <i>Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom, spreading its wings toward the</i> south? <i>Grows feathers</i> is in Hebrew יאבר. So <i>Jerome</i> renders it, and so also the <i>Genevan translators</i>, because יאבר, the future <i>Hiphil</i>, seems to be formed from the noun אבר or אברה, which means <i>feather</i>. Thus in <i>Ierem. LI,</i> 11, הברו חחצים is rendered by some <i>polish the arrows</i>, as if from ברר, as in <i>Es. XLIX,</i> 2, לחץ ברור, <i>into a polished arrow</i>. But for <i>Joseph Kimchi</i> it is πτεροῦτε τὰ βέλη [pteroute ta belē], <i>fit wings to the arrows</i> (French <i>empennès les flesches</i>), as if it were the imperative of האביר: not an unsuitable sense, since in <i>Homer</i> too you read πτερόεντες ὀϊστοὶ [pteroentes oistoi], and in <i>Silius</i>,

‎— <i>to have filled the quiver with feathered arrows.</i>

‎Further, in the hawk <i>to grow feathers</i> means <i>to be renewed by casting off the old feathers</i>, as is said of the eagle. On this see <i>Demetrius</i> in the <i>Hieracosophion</i>, in the chapters entitled Πρὸς τὸ συνεργῆσαι τὴν ἀποβολὴν τῶν πτερῶν [Pros to synergēsai tēn apobolēn tōn pterōn], <i>By what remedy the feathers may be quickly cast</i>; and Πρὸς τὸ πτεροφυῆσαι τὸν ἱέρακα τάχιστα [Pros to pterophyēsai ton hieraka tachista], <i>That the hawk's feathers may be quickly renewed</i>; and Πρὸς λειποπτεροῦντα ἱέρακα [Pros leipopterounta hieraka], <i>For a hawk that lacks feathers</i>; and Πρὸς τροφὴν πτερῶν [Pros trophēn pterōn], <i>For the nourishment of the plumage</i>; and Εἰς τύφλωσιν πτερῶν [Eis typhlōsin pterōn], <i>For the blinding of the feathers</i>. Although one may gather from these that human skill can do something here, so that new feathers quickly grow in a hawk in place of those lost, yet God said to <i>Job</i> not without reason, <i>Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom</i> (or <i>skill</i>)? For means and remedies can do nothing without God, and the whole power of medicine depends on him. Not to mention that in <i>Job</i>'s time this art of hawking was not yet in use. These words also fit together very well: <i>Does the hawk grow feathers by your wisdom, its wings spread toward the</i> south? For <i>Gregory</i> rightly says on this passage: <i>Domestic hawks, that they may grow feathers better, require moist and warm places. But wild hawks are accustomed,</i> [III, 10] <i>when the</i> south wind <i>blows, to spread their wings, so that their limbs may be warmed by the mildness of the wind to loosen the old feathers. When there is no wind, they spread their wings against the rays of the sun and beat them, and so make themselves a warm air; and thus, having caught</i> (read <i>having sought</i>) <i>the warmth, with their pores opened either the old feathers drop out or new ones grow.</i> Not very differently <i>Petrus de Crescentiis</i> in the book <i>On Animals</i>: <i>Hawks moult every year. They should be placed in</i> March <i>or</i> April <i>in a large</i> gabia <i>(cage), made expressly for this purpose, exposed to the sun in a warm place, or next to walls facing the southern quarter, etc.</i>

‎Yet some render <i>Job</i>'s words, <i>Will the hawk fly by your wisdom, with wings spread toward the</i> south? So <i>Aben Ezra</i> [=Abraham ibn Ezra], <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] and <i>R. Levi</i>. [col. 270] In their view the words concern flight, not the shedding and renewal of feathers, so that the sense is: "Is it to be credited to your providence that, when winter comes on, the hawk flies away to the <i>southern</i> regions of the world", כי יבקש מקום חם, <i>to seek a warm place</i>, as <i>Aben Ezra</i> has it here? For most species of hawks are counted among migratory birds: the <i>Philosopher</i> excepts only the buzzard <sup>b)</sup>, <i>Pliny</i> only the aesalon <sup>c)</sup>, which they report are always to be seen. Indeed, <i>of the hawk kind</i> (says <i>Budaeus</i> in his <i>Philologia</i>) <i>are those they call high-rangers, which, sometimes neglecting their prey, when the sky has cleared, delighting mostly in basking toward the south, are wont to soar quite freely into the clouds and to be carried for a long time out of men's sight, like truants.</i> In which these words are to be noted, <i>delighting mostly in basking toward the south</i>, as agreeing with <i>Job</i>'s words under the second interpretation, <i>The hawk flies with wings spread toward the</i> south. So this interpretation can be admitted as well as the former.

‎But what do the <i>Greeks</i> mean by their version, Ἐκ δὲ τῆς σῆς ἐπιστήμης ἕστηκεν ἱέραξ, ἀναπετάσας τὰς πτέρυγας ἀκίνητος, καθορῶν τὰ πρὸς νότον; [Ek de tēs sēs epistēmēs hestēken hierax, anapetasas tas pterygas akinētos, kathorōn ta pros noton?] <i>Does the hawk stand by your knowledge, spreading its wings motionless, looking toward the south?</i> Did they read יעמד, <i>stands</i>, in place of יאבר, and add ἀκίνητος καθορῶν [akinētos kathorōn] on their own, to make the sense clearer? However that may be, I think they had in view the tradition that the hawk, like the eagle, gazes at the sun with unblinking eyes and is in no way harmed by it. Aelian, book 10, chapter 14: Ὁρῶσι γὰρ ἱέρακες ὀρνίθων μόνοι ἀεὶ ἐν ταῖς ἀκτῖσι τοῦ ἡλίου ῥαδίως, καὶ ἀβασανίστως βλέποντες, καὶ δυσωπούμενοι ἥκιστα, πορείαν τε τὴν ἀνωτάτω ἴασι, καὶ αὐτοὺς ἡ θεία φλὸξ λυπεῖ οὐδέν· [Horōsi gar hierakes orni­thōn monoi aei en tais aktisi tou hēliou rhadiōs, kai abasanistōs blepontes, kai dysōpoumenoi hēkista, poreian te tēn anōtatō iasi, kai autous hē theia phlox lypei ouden] <i>Hawks alone among birds, gazing steadily at the rays of the sun easily and without effort, and unaffected in their sight, strive upward to the heights in such a way that the divine flame in no</i> [III, 11] <i>way harms them.</i> And for this very reason the <i>Egyptians</i> held the hawk to be sacred to <i>Horus</i>, that is, to the <i>Sun</i> or <i>Apollo</i>.

‎It is laughable that R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] understands here by the name of <i>hawk</i> מלאך דומה לרמות נץ יעמיד קושי הריח דרומית בפרישית כנפיו שלא תחריב את העולם, <i>an angel resembling a hawk, who by spreading his wings checks the force of the</i> south <i>wind, lest it lay waste the whole world.</i> This, I say, is laughable, since it is plain that in these chapters of <i>Job</i> everything is to be taken literally. Nor does Scripture present angels under the figure of <i>hawks</i>, but under that of <i>eagles</i>. It must be admitted, however, that it belongs to the office of angels to check the force of the winds. Alluding to this, <i>John</i> in <i>Rev 7:1</i> relates that he <i>saw four angels at the four corners of the earth</i>, κρατοῦντας τοὺς τέσσαρας ἀνέμους τῆς γῆς [kratountas tous tessaras anemous tēs gēs], <i>holding the four winds of the earth, that the wind should not blow upon the earth</i>, etc. Indeed, the <i>Apostle</i> calls <i>Satan</i> τὸν ἄρχοντα τῆς ἐξουσίας τοῦ ἀέρος [ton archonta tēs exousias tou aeros], <i>Eph. II, 2.</i> A specimen of this power he displayed in <i>Job 1:19.</i>

‎On this passage Chrysostom asks why God made no mention of oxen, sheep and other such animals, ἀλλὰ τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι [alla tōn achrēstōn, kai eikē dokountōn einai], <i>but of useless things, which seem to exist to no purpose</i>: as if the hawk were a bird of no use, created by the supreme Maker to no purpose, especially when compared with domestic livestock. But quite to the contrary, <i>Demetrius</i>, in the <i>preface</i> to the <i>Hieracosophion</i>, marvelling that so many had written about horses, oxen, dogs, mules and songbirds, says: μόνον τῆς τῶν ἱεράκων ἀνατροφῆς, καὶ θεραπείας, οὐκ οἶδ᾽ ὅ, τι παθόντες, κατεμέλησαν, καὶ ταῦτα οὐκ ἔλαττον εἰς τέρψιν καὶ χρείαν τῷ ἀνθρώπων γένει, τοῖς προῤῥηθεῖσι ζώοις συνηνεγκαμένοις, τούτοις [monon tēs tōn hierakōn anatrophēs, kai therapeias, ouk oid' ho, ti pathontes, katemelēsan, kai tauta ouk elatton eis terpsin kai chreian tō anthrōpōn genei, tois prorrhētheisi zōois synēnenkamenois, toutois], <i>only the rearing and medical care of hawks they have neglected, I know not how, especially since these contribute no less to the delight and use of the human race than the animals we have mentioned.</i> If you say that in Chrysostom's age the art of fowling with hawks was unknown, and that this is why he judged hawks and the other birds of prey useless, much will seem open to objection. In Ctesias, a very ancient writer, in [col. 271] Photius's <i>Excerpts</i>, the <i>Indians</i> λαγώους, καὶ ἀλώπεκας θηρεύουσιν, οὐ τοῖς κυσὶν, ἀλλὰ κόραξι, καὶ ἰκτῖσι, καὶ κορώναις, καὶ ἀετοῖς [lagōous, kai alōpekas thēreuousin, ou tois kysin, alla koraxi, kai iktisi, kai korōnais, kai aetois], <i>hunt hares and foxes, not with dogs, but with ravens, kites, crows and eagles.</i> Aelian explains this more fully from the same Ctesias in the <i>History</i>, book 4, chapter 26. And in the <i>Mirabilia</i> Aristotle teaches by what art, around <i>Amphipolis</i> in <i>Thrace</i>, ἐξιόντες οἱ παῖδες ἐκ τῶν κωμῶν, καὶ τῶν ἐγγὺς χωρίων, ἐπὶ θήραν τῶν ὀρνιθαρίων, συνθηρεύειν παραλαμβάνουσι τοὺς ἱέρακας [exiontes hoi paides ek tōn kōmōn, kai tōn engys chōriōn, epi thēran tōn ornitharion, synthēreuein paralambanousi tous hierakas], <i>boys going out from the villages and neighbouring fields to catch small birds take hawks as partners in the fowling.</i> <i>Antigonus</i> relates the same in <i>History</i> 34. Pliny too, book 10, chapters 8 and 43, mentions a certain <i>Craterus</i>, <i>surnamed Monoceros, [III, 12] in the region of Erizena</i> (or rather <i>Troezena</i>) in Asia, <i>who hunted with the help of ravens; for he carried them into the woods perched on his crests and shoulders, and they tracked and drove the game, the habit having been brought so far that even wild ones accompanied him as he went out.</i> And Martial in the <i>Apophoreta</i>, book 14, <i>Epigram</i> 216, has this about the <i>hawk</i>:

‎<i>He was a plunderer of birds; now he is the fowler's servant: he still deceives them, and grieves that the birds he catches are not for himself.</i>

‎With these the reader may compare what Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium], in his <i>Birds</i>, p. 24, reports that he read in the <i>Greek</i> paraphrase of Oppian's <i>Ixeutica</i>: that small birds, in fear of a hawk brought near by the fowler, hide themselves under the foliage of trees and, since they do not dare to fly away, are easily caught. And this passage of <i>Apuleius</i> in the first <i>Apology</i>: <i>"Remember, however, that it would be as ridiculous an argument to say that obscene sea creatures were sought for love affairs as to say that a sea comb was sought for combing hair, or a hawk-fish for catching flying creatures."</i> Finally, nothing is more explicit than these words of <i>Julius Firmicus</i>, who lived under <i>Constantine</i>, in book 5, chapter 7 of the <i>Mathesis</i>, concerning those whose horoscope has <i>Venus</i> in <i>Aquarius</i>: <i>"They will be eager to keep hawks, falcons, goshawks, eagles and birds of this kind, and horses for hunting."</i> And in the following chapter: <i>"If</i> Mercury <i>is found in</i> Virgo, <i>whoever have him so will be strong, industrious, shrewd, breeders of horses, of hawks, of falcons, and of the other birds that belong to fowling."</i> Accordingly, since in the age of Chrysostom, who flourished only at the end of the fourth century, fowling with hawks seems long to have been in wide use, we may well wonder why he placed hawks in the class τῶν ἀχρήστων, καὶ εἰκῆ δοκούντων εἶναι [tōn achrēstōn, kai eikē dokountōn einai]. It could indeed be answered that the great man, being a very strict guardian and rigid attendant of virtue, could have regarded such fowling as superfluous and counted it among the things on which time is uselessly spent. But, as I think more true, most of these passages are little to the point and do not sufficiently establish the antiquity of fowling with hawks. For what is adduced from Ctesias about the <i>Indians</i>, from the <i>Philosopher</i> about the <i>Thracians</i>, and from Pliny about <i>Craterus</i> are rudiments of the art, and these received only among barbarians, not yet among the <i>Greeks</i>. And in <i>Martial</i> and Oppian the hawk deceives or frightens birds, but does not yet catch them. And in <i>Apuleius</i> it is sought for catching birds: not that it may catch them itself, but that it may assist those who catch them. Only <i>Julius Firmicus</i> therefore remains; and since he lived and wrote in an age from which so many records survive, I cannot wonder enough that in that age no one else mentioned so noble a kind of fowling, one notable and memorable for its very novelty. Moreover, the name <i>astures</i> [goshawks], which you read there, occurs nowhere else among the ancients; and therefore these passages are highly suspect to me, and in them the scribes seem to have added something of their own. It is certain, however, that this fowling flourished not long after Chrysostom. For <i>Prosper</i>, who follows immediately after him, says this in <i>On the Contemplative</i> [III, 13] <i>Life</i>, book 3, chapter 17: <i>"They use hawks and fattened dogs for hunting."</i> And <i>Sidonius</i> in the <i>Panegyric</i> to <i>Avitus</i>, his father-in-law, emperor [col. 272] and consul in the year 456:

‎<i>What of his zeal for birds, which nature makes rapacious against a crowd almost of their own kin? Who more skilfully than he has taught them to join varied combats through the clouds! With a bird he conquers birds, and with swift beating of wings through the air no taloned fighter wages war better for anyone than for him.</i>

‎And to <i>Herodicus</i>, the son of this <i>Avitus</i>, in Letter 3 of book 3: <i>"Ball, dice-box, hawk, dog, horse, bow were your play."</i> And to <i>Industrius</i>, Letter 9 of book 4, in commendation of the illustrious <i>Vectius</i>: <i>"Those are lighter matters, that he of whom we speak is second to none in training, watching and carrying about horses, dogs and hawks."</i> Thereafter, as luxury grew, since in <i>Gaul</i> the clergy too kept dogs and hawks, many councils throughout the sixth century forbade it: as in the year 506 the Council of <i>Agde</i>, canon 55: <i>"Let it not be lawful for bishops, presbyters and deacons to keep dogs for hunting, or hawks."</i> This same is repeated in the year 517 in canon 4 of the Council of <i>Epaone</i>, and in the year 585 in canon 13 of the second Council of <i>Mâcon</i>: <i>"Where there is the constant singing of God, it is a monstrous thing and a mark of disgrace for dogs or hawks to dwell there."</i> Therefore <i>Leander</i> and <i>Pancirolla</i>, who attribute the invention of this fowling to the emperor <i>Frederick Barbarossa</i>, and <i>Pandolfo Collenuccio</i>, who attributes it to the emperor <i>Henry VI</i>, are gravely mistaken.
