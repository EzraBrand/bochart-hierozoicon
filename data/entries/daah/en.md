# Part 2, Book 2, chapter 7: The kite is better called daa, from its flight, as in Leviticus, than raa, from its sight, as in Deuteronomy (vol. 2, pp. 777–778)

### ‎[II, 777] CHAPTER 7. [col. 191]

### ‎<i>That the kite is named from flying,</i> דאה daa, <i>as in Leviticus,</i> <i>rather than from sight,</i> ראה raa, <i>as in Deuteronomy.</i>

‎Next comes דאה <i>daa</i>, or ראה <i>raa</i>. For it is not certain which way it should be written, since the same bird is called דאה in <i>Leviticus</i> and ראה <i>raa</i> in <i>Deuteronomy</i>; of these, one is taken <i>from flying</i>, the other <i>from sight</i>. For דאה <i>daa</i> is the verb <i>to fly</i>, and ראה <i>raa</i> <i>to see.</i> The letters <i>Daleth</i> and <i>Resh</i>, being very similar, are often confused by scribes. Thus דודנים <i>Dodanim</i>, Gen 10:4 and רודנים <i>Rodanim</i>, 1 Chr 1:7 are the same; so are ריפת <i>Riphath</i>, Gen 10:3 and דיפת <i>Diphath</i>, 1 Chr 1:6; הדר <i>Hadar</i>, Gen 36:39 and הדד <i>Hadad</i>, 1 Chr 1:46; הדד־עזר <i>Hadad-ezer,</i> 2 Sam 8:3 and הדר־עזר <i>Hadar-ezer,</i> 1 Chr 18:3; ההרודי <i>Harodites</i>, 2 Sam 23:25 and ההרורי <i>Harorites</i>, 1 Chr 11:27; חמדן <i>Hamdan,</i> Gen 36:26 and חמרן <i>Hamran</i>, 1 Chr 1:39 (var. 41.); דעואל <i>Daguel</i>, Num 1:14 and VII, 42. and רעואל <i>Raguel</i>, Num 2:14. And of God it is said in <i>2 Sam 22:11.</i> וירא vajera, <i>and he appeared</i>, instead of וידא vajeda, <i>and he flew,</i> Ps 18:11. In exactly the same way the same bird is called דאה <i>daa</i> in one place and ראה <i>raa</i> in another. But if that bird is truly the <i>kite</i>, as most of the ancient and more recent interpreters hold, among them <i>Jerome</i>, the <i>Arabs</i>, <i>Munster</i>, <i>Castalio [=Sebastian Castellio, Latin Bible (1551)],</i> <i>Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible], <i>Diodati</i>, <i>Buxtorf</i> [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum], <i>Schindler,</i> etc., I would rather have it written דאה <i>daa</i> than ראה <i>raa</i>, with the name derived <i>from flying</i> rather than <i>from sight.</i> For while writers on natural history observe nothing peculiar in the kite's sight, in its flight it has this first distinctive feature: with its wings almost motionless it hovers in the air longer than any other bird. <i>Munster</i>: רחף, <i>It moved, namely as the kite moves in the air without moving its wings,</i> etc. Budaeus: <i>We have watched kites for a whole hour or more catching prey without beating their wings,</i> etc. <i>This the Italian fowlers call</i> tendere, the French planare. <i>For by raising the wing a little they take the movement of the air into the rest of the body as well, and are thus held up.</i> Moreover, there is scarcely any bird that flies higher than the kite. <i>Martial</i>, book 9, <i>Epigram</i> 55:

‎<i>Hence the rapacious kite flies almost to the highest stars.</i>

‎Belon [=Pierre Belon, Observations (1553)], <i>De Avibus</i> book 2, ch. 26: <i>When a hawk is let loose at a kite, both are carried up into the highest air, so that they escape human sight.</i> Budaeus in his Philologia: <i>Neither herons nor kites, though hidden within the clouds, can escape human hands.</i> Finally, the kite does not easily tire in flying. Hence <i>Persius</i> on <i>Vectidius</i>, Satire 4:

‎— <i>he ploughs at</i> Cures <i>more than a kite could fly over.</i>

‎Where <i>Cornutus</i> comments: <i>As is commonly said in the proverb,</i> "As far as kites do not fly", <i>because kites fly a great deal.</i> And <i>Juvenal</i>, Satire 9:

‎[II, 778] — — <i>for whom are you keeping so many mountains, so many</i> Apulian <i>estates, so many kites wearied within your pastures?</i>

‎And there the old <i>Scholiast</i> says: <i>The poet wishes to show the size of the estates, how widely they extend in fields, so that not even kites can fly across them.</i> This, then, suits the nature of the kite [col. 192] better: that it was called דאה <i>daa</i> <i>from the excellence of its flight</i> rather than ראה <i>raa</i> <i>from its sight</i>. You may object these words of <i>Apuleius</i>, <i>Metamorphoses</i> book 6: <i>Yet I could not escape the kite's eyes of the crafty little old woman</i>, in which remarkable keenness of sight seems to be attributed to the kite. I shall reply, 1. that the same is attributed to all birds of prey; 2. that this passage of <i>Apuleius</i> is unique. This the illustrious commentator <i>Philippus Beroaldus</i> acknowledges in these words: <i>No writer, so far as I know, celebrates the eyes of the kite as excelling in keenness of sight.</i> I add that in the <i>Samaritan</i> copy, both in <i>Deuteronomy</i> and in <i>Leviticus</i>, ࠃࠀࠄ [דאה] is read with <i>Daleth</i>, nowhere ࠓࠀࠄ [ראה] with <i>Resh</i>. And from the <i>Hebrew</i> דאה <i>daa</i> the <i>Arabic</i> حدا [ḥdā] <i>hida</i> seems to have been formed, with a guttural prefixed, as in the <i>Hebrew</i> name of the <i>Tigris</i>, חדקל <i>Hiddekel</i>, formed from the <i>Chaldee</i> דגלה <i>Digla</i>. At least this is certain: among the <i>Arabs</i> the <i>kite</i> is called حداة [ḥidāh] <i>hida</i> <sup>R1</sup>. This is clear from <i>Abenbitar</i> [=Ibn al-Bayṭār], in the chapter قاطاننقي [qāṭānanqī] <i>Catananke</i>, in which these <i>Greek</i> words of <i>Dioscorides</i> on the dried seed of the herb <i>catananke</i>, Ἰκτίνου ὄνυξιν ὁμοιοῦται νεκροῦ [Iktinou onyxin homoioutai nekrou], <i>It resembles the claws of a dead kite</i>, are rendered thus in the <i>Arabic</i> paraphrase: وكان شكلها شبيها شكل مخالب الحداة الميتة [wa-kāna shakluhā shabīhan shakla makhālib al-ḥidāh al-mayyita] <i>And its shape is similar to the shape of the claws of a dead</i> hida. <i>Hida</i>, that is, <i>kite</i>. To say nothing of <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān], who in the chapter <i>On</i> حداة [ḥidāh] <i>hida</i> attributes to it whatever the <i>Philosopher</i> has about the kite, <i>Hist.</i> book 6, ch. 6. Nor of <i>Andrea Bellunensis</i>, whose words are: <i>Alhada is a bird which is called the kite.</i> Correct, except that <i>alhida</i> should be written, not <i>alhada</i>. For الحداة [al-ḥadāh] <i>alhada</i> with <i>Fatha</i> is a <i>double axe</i>, as <i>Damir</i> and <i>Alcamus</i> [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author] note; but <i>alhida</i> with <i>Kasra</i> is the name of the bird. There are some who interpret both דאה <i>daa</i> and ראה <i>raa</i> quite differently; but I pass over their versions because they do not seem in any way probable <sup>R2</sup>.

**Notes**

‎R1 *(p. 778)* Forsskål [=Peter Forsskål, Descriptiones animalium (1775)], p. 1, describes a <i>falcon with yellow cere and feet, ash-grey above, rust-coloured below, the wings dusky above, the tail forked, banded with dusky brown, of the length of the body</i>. He renders the Arabic name of this bird in Latin letters as Haddai; perhaps the man from whose mouth Forsskål took down this name pronounced our حِداة [ḥidāh], plural حِداء [ḥidāʾ], with that sound. In an observation Forsskål adds the following: "Is it <i>Falco Milvus</i> Linn.? But it is neither wholly rust-coloured, nor is the head whitish. Is it <i>Falco forficatus</i>? But below it is not whitish, but rust-coloured." Hence in the newest edition of the Linnaean System, vol. 1, part 1, p. 261, this falcon forms a distinct species under the heading <i>Aegyptius</i>.

‎R2 *(p. 778)* Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis (1717–91), orientalist: Supplementa ad lexica hebraica; Fragen an eine Gesellschaft gelehrter Männer] has set out conjectures on the guesses of the ancients about this word in <i>den Fragen an eine Gesellschaft von Gelehrten, die nach Arabien reisen</i>, p. 303 ff.
