# Book 4, chapter 8: Why the altars of the idols are said to be ὡς χελῶναι, like tortoises, in the Greek version of Hosea (vol. 2, pp. 520–523)

### ‎[II, 520] [col. 1090] Chapter VIII.

### ‎<i>Why the altars of the idols are said to be</i> ὡς χελῶναι [hōs chelōnai], like tortoises, <i>in the Greek version of Hos 12:12. And on the</i> chelonis <i>of Holofernes, Jdt 14:13.</i>

‎No word is <i>better known to the Hebrews</i> than גל <i>gal</i>, for <i>a heap of stones</i>. From it <i>Galaad</i>, a very famous mountain in the land of <i>Canaan</i>, took its name, Gen 31:48. So too <i>Gallim</i>, a town near <i>Accaron</i>, on which see <i>1 Sam 25:44. Isa 10:30</i> [col. 1091] and <i>Eusebius</i> and <i>Jerome</i> in the <i>Places</i>. When <i>Achan</i> had been stoned because he had taken from the devoted things, a <i>great</i> גל <i>gal</i> <i>of stones</i> (that is, a <i>heap</i>) was piled over him as a memorial of the deed, <i>Josh 7:26.</i> Likewise over the corpse of the rebel <i>Absalom</i>, <i>2 Sam 18:17.</i> Houses, too, and cities that are demolished are said to be reduced לגל, <i>to a gal</i> (that is, a <i>heap</i>), <i>Isa 25:2</i> or לגלים, <i>to gallim</i> (that is, <i>heaps</i>), Job 15:28 and <i>Jer 9:11</i> and <i>LI, 37.</i> And to become גלים נצים <i>gallim nitsim</i> (that is, <i>desolate heaps</i>), 2 <i>Reg. XIX, 25.</i> There is therefore nothing obscure in these words of <i>Hos 12:11</i> (var. <i>12.</i>)<i>: Their altars are</i> כגלים, <i>like gallim</i> (or <i>heaps of stones</i>) <i>on the furrows of the field.</i> The <i>Prophet</i> is speaking of the altars which the idolaters of <i>Gilgal</i> had built to their gods not only in the city but also in the neighbouring fields; and he compares them with the heaps of stones customarily piled up by those who clear stony fields. The <i>Greek</i> translators, however, for whom גל <i>gal</i> is elsewhere βουνὸς [bounos], a <i>mound</i>, χῶμα [chōma] an <i>embankment</i>, σωρὸς [sōros] a <i>heap</i>, συναγωγὴ λίθων [synagōgē lithōn] a <i>collection of stones</i>, render the plural גלים <i>gallim</i> in this passage of <i>Hosea</i> quite differently, namely as χελώνας [chelōnas], <i>tortoises</i>. Τὰ θυσιαστήρια αὐτῶν [Ta thysiastēria autōn] (they say), ὡς χελῶναι ἐπὶ χέρσον ἀγροῦ [hōs chelōnai epi cherson agrou], <i>Their altars are like tortoises on the waste of the field.</i> And on these words [II, 521] <i>Theodoret</i> observes that the tortoise, when swimming in the water, escapes the hands of fishermen more easily, whereas when walking on land it is πρόχειρος, καὶ εὐάλωτως [procheiros, kai eualōtōs], ready to hand and easily caught; so it would come about that the altars of the <i>Gilgalites</i> would easily be taken and overthrown by their enemies. <i>Jerome</i> preferred to apply what is said of the altars to the heretics, who, like tortoises, are slow of step and move about in one place, etc. Wiser than these, perhaps, will seem <i>Cyril</i>, for whom χελῶναι [chelōnai] in this passage are not animals but αἱ τῶν χωμάτων ἐγέρσεις [hai tōn chōmatōn egerseis], <i>the raising of embankments</i>; just as for <i>Drusius</i> too <i>tortoises</i> signify γεωλόφους [geōlophous], <i>mounds of earth.</i> And I would gladly agree with them if any example of a similar expression were given. But since none is adduced, I have two conjectures. The first is that instead of ὡς χελῶναι [hōs chelōnai] we should read ὡς κολῶναι [hōs kolōnai], a word often used for <i>mounds</i>. As in the <i>Iliad</i>, β΄ [book 2], line 811.

‎Ἔστι δέ τις προπάροιθε πόλεως αἰπεῖα κολώνη, [Esti de tis propároithe poleōs aipeia kolōnē,]

‎<i>Now there is near the city a certain lofty mound.</i> And in <i>Apollonius' Argonautica</i>, book 1, line 1114.

‎Φαίνετο δ᾽ ἠερόεν στόμα Βοσπόρου, ἠδὲ κολῶναι Μύσιαι. — — — — — — [Phaineto d' ēeroen stoma Bosporou, ēde kolōnai Mysiai.]

‎<i>And the misty mouth</i> of the Bosphorus <i>and the ridges</i> of Mysia <i>appeared.</i> Prose writers also use it: for example Polemo in Athenaeus, book 13, and another in the Suda; and Pausanias in the <i>Laconica</i>, where he speaks of Colona, a place in Sparta. In Hesychius κολώνη [kolōnē] is τόπος ὑψηλὸς, καὶ ἐπανάστημα γῆς βουνοειδὲς [topos hypsēlos, kai epanastēma gēs bounoeides], <i>a lofty place, and an elevation of the ground in the manner of a mound.</i> And in the scholiasts on Homer, τόπος εἰς ὕψος ἀνήκων, καὶ γεώλεφος ἐξοχὴ [topos eis hypsos anēkōn, kai geōlephos exochē], <i>a place rising to a height, and a hilly prominence.</i> The other conjecture is that גלים <i>gallim</i> is rendered here as χελώνας [chelōnas], or <i>tortoises</i>, following the usage of the Syriac language, in which ܓܠܐ [גלא] <i>gala</i> is <i>tortoise.</i> Accordingly the Syriac here has ܐܝܟ ܓܠܐ [איך גלא], that is, <i>like tortoises</i>, according to Gabriel Sionita [=translator of al-Idrīsī's geography into Latin (Geographia Nubiensis, Paris 1619)]. And in the Syriac Lexicon of Giovanni Battista Ferrari, page 107, ܓܠܐ [גלא] <i>gala</i> is rendered <i>tortoise.</i> Yet in the <i>Thesaurus</i> of Thomas of Novara, page 241, the Syriac words ܫܠܘܦܝܬܐ [שלופיתא] <i>seluphita</i> and ܥܓܠܐ [עגלא] <i>gala</i> are distinguished in such a way that the former signifies the <i>tortoise</i> in general, the latter only the <i>male tortoise.</i> This I think is more correct, since the Arabs call the same animal غيلم [ghaylam] <i>gailam</i>, surely in allusion to this word. So testify Giauhari [=al-Jawharī, al-Ṣiḥāḥ], Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author], Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] and others. And the name is formed <i>from lust.</i> For غلم [ghalam] <i>galam</i> means <i>lust</i>, and the verb غلم [ghalima] <i>galima</i> means <i>to burn with lust.</i> That the <i>male tortoise</i> is rightly called غيلم [ghaylam] [col. 1092] <i>gailam</i> may be shown by these words of Aelian, <i>Historia</i>, book 15, chapter 19: Χερσαία χελώνη ζῶον λαγνίστατον· ἀλλὰ ὅγε ἄῤῥην. ὁμιλεῖ δὲ ἡ θήλεια ἄκουσα [Chersaia chelōnē zōon lagnistaton; alla hoge arrhēn. homilei de hē thēleia akousa]. <i>The land tortoise is a most lustful animal; that is, its male: for the female mates only unwillingly.</i> And Damostratus writes that she shrinks from mating for this reason: because, when she has been turned on her back by the male, she cannot right herself on account of the excessive weight of her shell, [II, 522] and after mating lies open as prey to the eagle and to other animals. But I think what others write is more correct, that in the very act of mating she suffers severe pain, because the male's member is hard and spiny. I pass over the fable of Damostratus, not omitted by Damir, about a certain unknown herb which the male holds in his mouth and so draws the resisting female to himself. Meanwhile, although not only for the Greeks but also for the Syriac and the Arabic translators גלים <i>gallim</i> in Hosea are <i>tortoises</i>, I nevertheless see no reason why we should depart from the received rendering of that passage. Everyone sees that the altars of the idols, met everywhere in the fields, are rightly compared with those heaps of stones we have mentioned, both on account of their cheapness and on account of their frequency. Not to mention that to those viewing them from afar their appearance is not dissimilar. But whatever is adduced to establish a likeness between these altars and tortoises is forced and far-fetched. Hosea's words can also be taken as spoken prophetically, as if he foretold that the altars of the people of Gilgal would shortly be כגלים, like <i>heaps of stones that are in the fields</i>, that is, would shortly be demolished. Nor should the allusion of the word <i>Galgal</i> to גלים <i>gallim</i> be neglected, so that Galgal and its altars are said to be destined to become like <i>gallim</i>, or <i>heaps of stones.</i> Just as in Zeph 2:4: <i>Aza</i> (or <i>Gaza</i>) <i>shall be</i> עזובה (azuba) <i>forsaken. And Accaron</i> תעקר (theaker) <i>shall be uprooted.</i> As in the Pseudo-Sibyllines,

‎Καὶ Ῥώμη ῥύμη ἔσεται, καὶ Δῆλος ἄδηλος· [Kai Rhōmē rhymē esetai, kai Dēlos adēlos;]

‎and in the history of Susanna σχίσαι [schisai] and πρίσαι [prisai] allude to the names of the trees σχίνον [schinon] and πρίνον [prinon].

‎When we read that <i>Bagoas</i> found the trunk of <i>Holofernes</i> thrown down ἐπὶ τῆς χετλωνίδος [epi tēs chetlōnidos], <i>Jdt 14:13</i>, others rightly observe that in place of χετλωνίδος [chetlōnidos], a meaningless word, we should read χελωνίδος [chelōnidos], as <i>Hesychius</i> and <i>Suidas</i> read it, and that χελωνίς [chelōnis] is so called ἀπὸ τῆς χελώνης [apo tēs chelōnēs], <i>from the tortoise.</i> But what exactly a χελωνὶς [chelōnis] is, is not sufficiently clear. <i>The Vulgate translator</i> renders it <i>cortina</i> [curtain]. Most of the more recent interpreters render it <i>stratum</i> [couch] or <i>lectus</i> [bed]; they are easily refuted. For <i>Judith</i> had cut off <i>Holofernes</i>' head, καὶ ἐπεκύλισε τὸ σῶμα αὐτοῦ ἀπὸ τῆς στρωμνῆς [kai epekylise to sōma autou apo tēs strōmnēs], <i>and had rolled his body off the couch</i>, ch. 13, v. 10. Therefore the χελωνὶς [chelōnis], in which <i>Bagoas</i> found this body ἐῤῥιμμένον [erhrimmenon], <i>thrown down</i>, is something different from the couch and the bed. <i>Hesychius</i>, under χελωνίδος [chelōnidos], explains it as οὐδὸς τῆς θύρας τῆς σκηνῆς [oudos tēs thyras tēs skēnēs], <i>the threshold of the door of the tent.</i> So <i>Suidas</i> too must be corrected from him, since he writes ὅδου [hodou] for οὐδοῦ [oudou]. This is hardly likely, both because the name does not fit, and because <i>Holofernes</i>' body was rolled from the couch into the chelonis. The <i>chelonis</i>, then, was not near the threshold of the door, but in the innermost part of the tent, where the commander's bed itself also stood. Not to detain the reader any longer: to me the χελωνὶς [chelōnis] is קבה <i>kubba.</i> The word occurs once in Scripture and is not well understood, namely <i>Num 25:8</i>, where, while <i>Zimri</i> and <i>Kozbi</i> were fornicating, <i>Phinehas</i> went אל הקבה, <i>into the kubba</i>, and pierced them both through. Different people explain this differently. But among the <i>Arabs</i> قبة [qubba] <i>kubba</i>, or <i>kobba</i>, and with the article القبة [al-qubba] <i>alkobba</i>, is <i>a chamber of vaulted work, by which a bed is enclosed.</i> The Spaniards call it <i>alcoba</i> [II, 523] and <i>alcova</i>, a word which among us too has a similar meaning. The root قب [qabba] <i>kabba</i> in the second conjugation means <i>to vault</i>, or <i>to build vaulted work.</i> So, just as the musical instrument χέλυς [chelys], or <i>testudo</i> [tortoise], is called in Arabic العود [al-ʿūd] <i>alaud</i>, whence the Spanish <i>laud</i> or <i>laut</i>, and our <i>lut</i>, so القبة [al-qubba] <i>alkobba</i>, [col. 1093] or the <i>chamber</i> we have described, is called in Arabic by another name ام العود [umm al-ʿūd] <i>ommolaudi</i>, that is, <i>mother of the tortoise</i>, because it is of vaulted, tortoise-shaped work. Among the <i>Arabs</i> the <i>bridal chamber</i> is in such an القبة [al-qubba] <i>alkobba</i>. And leading the bride there is called بنا [banā] <i>bana</i> and زف [zaff] <i>zappha</i>. So <i>Holofernes</i>' bedchamber, when he was about to lie with <i>Judith</i>, as he thought, was in an <i>alcobba</i> of this kind; and why it is called χελωνὶς [chelōnis] in Greek is now clear to anyone. Perhaps the χελωνὶς [chelōnis] was also named from its material. For in former times tortoise shell was much used in the beds and bedsteads of the great. This luxury, first adopted in the <i>East</i>, finally reached the <i>Romans</i>. The date is noted by <i>Pliny</i>, book 9, chapter 11: <i>To cut tortoise shells into plates</i>, he says, <i>and to veneer beds and sideboards with them</i>, <i>was introduced by</i> Carbilius Pollio. And book 16, [col. 1094] chapter 43: <i>Tortoise shell is cut for this purpose. And recently, in the principate of</i> Nero, <i>it was devised by monstrous ingenuity that it should destroy itself with paints and sell for more by imitating wood. Thus high prices are sought for beds.</i> Hence <i>Martial</i>, book 12, <i>Epigram</i> 67:

‎<i>Beds jewelled with the finest tortoise shell gleam.</i>

‎And <i>Juvenal</i>, Satire 11:

‎<i>What kind of tortoise swam in the waves of Ocean, to make a splendid and noble headrest for the</i> Trojan-born.

‎Where the <i>Scholiast</i> says: <i>That he might have a tortoise-shell bed. For the ancients adorned beds with the tortoise shell of Ocean.</i> <i>Apuleius</i> too, not far from the end of book 10: <i>A bed shining with</i> Indian <i>tortoise shell, swelling with a heap of feathers</i>, etc. But this is more than enough on a matter of no great importance. So here we end, and now pass on to the birds.
