# Part 2, Book 1, chapter 2: The dove's feathers, neck, eyes, moaning and flight (vol. 2, pp. 533–544)

### ‎[II, 533] Chapter 2.

### ‎<i>On the dove's feathers, neck, eyes, moaning and flight.</i>

‎I am pleased to begin the description of the dove with its plumage and feathers, whose beauty [col. 9] the sacred poet commends thus, <i>Ps 68:14: The wings of the dove covered with silver, and its feathers with the paleness of gold.</i> <i>Wings</i> and <i>feathers</i> stand here by συνεκδοχὴν [synekdochēn] for the whole body. <i>Covered with silver</i>, that is, supremely white like silver, and bright with a certain splendour. For silver is called in <i>Greek</i> ἀργύριον [argyrion], from τὸ ἀργὸν, τὸ λευκόν [to argon, to leukon, "the bright, the white"]; and things that are most brilliantly white are called <i>silver</i>. Hence in the <i>poets</i> ἀργυρόπεζα Θέτις [argyropeza Thetis], that is, λευκόπους [leukopous]. So it is in <i>Hesychius</i>, who likewise explains ἀργυροδίνην κρήνην [argyrodinēn krēnēn] as λευκὰ ῥεύματα ἔχουσαν [leuka rheumata echousan]; and in the eighth book of the <i>Aeneid</i>,

‎— — — — <i>the silver goose,</i>

‎is, on the testimony of <i>Servius</i>, <i>an epithet of colour</i>. The raven too was once a silver bird, when it equalled doves in whiteness. <i>Ovid, Metam.</i> book 2, Fable 7:

‎<i>For this was once a silver bird with snowy feathers,</i> <i>so that it equalled doves wholly without blemish.</i> [II, 534] For among doves some are extremely white <sup>R1</sup>. <i>Varro, On Agriculture</i> book 3, ch. 7: <i>The other kind of doves is the tamer, which, content with household food, is usually fed within the threshold of the door. This kind is mostly white in colour.</i> That is, at some times and in some places, not always nor everywhere. For in <i>Greece</i> white doves were seen late, namely when the forces of <i>Xerxes</i> were routed at Mount <i>Athos</i>. This is expressly reported in the <i>Persica</i> by <i>Charon of Lampsacus</i>, who lived at that time and wrote before <i>Herodotus.</i> <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9: Χάρων δ᾽ ὁ Λαμψακηνὸς, ἐν τοῖς Περσικοῖς περὶ Μαρδονίου ἱστορῶν, καὶ τοῦ διαφθαρέντος στρατοῦ Περσικοῦ περὶ τὸν Ἄθω, γράφει ταῦτα· „Καὶ λευκαὶ περιστεραὶ τότε πρῶτον εἰς „Ἕλληνας ἐφάνησαν, πρότερον οὐ γιγνόμεναι“ [Charōn d᾽ ho Lampsakēnos, en tois Persikois peri Mardoniou historōn, kai tou diaphtharentos stratou Persikou peri ton Athō, graphei tauta: "Kai leukai peristerai tote prōton eis Hellēnas ephanēsan, proteron ou gignomenai"]. <i>Charon of Lampsacus, in the</i> Persica, <i>treating of</i> Mardonius <i>and of the</i> Persian <i>army routed at Mount</i> Athos, <i>writes this: "And white doves were then first seen among the</i> Greeks, <i>there having been none before</i>", namely in <i>Greece</i>, not <i>anywhere</i>, as the translator there wrongly has it. <i>Aelian</i> in the <i>Varia</i> book 1, ch. 15: Χάρων δὲ ὁ Λαμψακηνὸς περὶ τὸν Ἄθω φανῆναι περιστερὰς λευκὰς λέγει, ὅτε ἐνταῦθα ἀπώλοντο αἱ τῶν Περσῶν τριέρεις παρακάμπτουσαι τὸν Ἄθω [Charōn de ho Lampsakēnos peri ton Athō phanēnai peristeras leukas legei, hote entautha apōlonto hai tōn Persōn triereis parakamptousai ton Athō]; <i>But</i> Charon of Lampsacus <i>asserts that white doves were seen around</i> Athos <i>when the triremes of the</i> Persians <i>perished there while sailing past</i> Athos. There follows in the <i>Psalm</i> ואברותיה בירקרק חרוץ, <i>And its feathers with the paleness of gold</i>; supply <i>covered</i>, which must be repeated from the preceding hemistich. <i>Paleness of gold</i>, that is, <i>gold of paleness</i>, or <i>pale gold.</i> Similar is <i>Hab 2:6.</i> עב טיט, <i>thickness of mud</i>, for <i>mud of thickness</i>, or <i>thick mud.</i> התואר במקום המתואר, the predicate for the subject, as <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] has it here. <i>Gold</i> is called חרוץ <i>charuts</i>, that is, <i>dug out of the earth</i>, here and in <i>Zech 9:3</i> and frequently in <i>Solomon's Proverbs</i> <sup>R2</sup>. From this comes the <i>Greek</i> χρυσός [chrysos]. And the <i>paleness of gold is</i> ירקרק, with doubled letters, so that the meaning is intensified. Accordingly from this very word comes the <i>Ethiopic</i> ወርቅ፡ [warəq] <i>vvark</i> for <i>gold.</i> The Greek translators rightly call the <i>paleness of gold</i> χλωρότητα χρυσίου [chlōrotēta chrysiou]. For this is the true colour of gold. Hence <i>Diogenes</i>, asked Διὰ τί τὸ χρυσίον χλωρόν ἐστιν; [Dia ti to chrysion chlōron estin?] <i>Why</i> [II, 535] <i>is gold pale?</i>, answered Ὅτι πολλοὺς ἔχει τοὺς ἐπιβουλεύοντας [Hoti pollous echei tous epibouleuontas], <i>Because it has many plotting against it.</i> So it is in <i>Laertius</i>; and <i>Catullus</i> in the wedding of <i>Peleus and Thetis</i>,

‎<i>O how often she grew paler than the sallow hue of gold!</i>

‎And again, to <i>Juventius</i>, [col. 10]

‎<i>A guest paler than a gilded statue.</i>

‎And <i>Statius</i> to <i>Maximus Junius</i> [=Franciscus Junius, of the Tremellius-Junius Latin Bible] in the fourth book of the <i>Silvae</i>,

‎<i>When will the</i> Dalmatian <i>mountains send you back to sweet</i> Latium, <i>where, having seen</i> Dis, <i>the pale miner returns, of the same colour as the gold he has dug up?</i>

‎And <i>Lucian</i> in the <i>Cock</i>, to a greedy rich man: Οἴμωζε, καὶ ἀγρυπνεῖ, καὶ ὅμοιος γίγνου τὸ χρῶμα τῷ χρυσῷ, προσετηκὼς αὐτῷ [oimōze, kai agrypnei, kai homoios gignou to chrōma tō chrysō, prosetēkōs autō]; <i>Lament and keep watch, and, wasting away over your gold, become like it in colour.</i> Now, if you ask why this golden colour is attributed to doves, <i>Kimchi</i> answers: שיש בכנפי היונה נוצה שהמצהבת כזהב, <i>In the wings of doves there is plumage yellow like gold</i>: namely in the wings of the younger birds, after they have left the nest. The <i>Talmudists</i> observe the same of turtledoves, and say that from this one learns when they are fit or unfit for sacrifice. For since the Law commands that of doves only the young, and of turtledoves only the adults, be offered, they hold that the former become unfit for sacrifice, and the latter fit, precisely when the feathers begin to turn yellow like gold. The words are in the tractate <i>Cholin</i>, chapter 1, folio 22b: מאימתי התורים כשרים משיצהיבו מאימתי בני יונה פסולים משיצהיבו <i>When are turtledoves</i> (for offering) <i>fit? From the time they begin to turn yellow like gold. When are young doves</i> (for offering) <i>unfit? From the time they begin to turn yellow like gold.</i> And on the word משיצהיבו the <i>Gloss</i> has: שיהא כנפי גופן גדולים ואדומים ומזהיבים כזהב, <i>because the feathers of the body become large and red, and yellow like gold.</i> And somewhat earlier: תחילה הציהב כשמתחילין לצהב כנפים שמסביבות צוארן <i>The beginning of the yellowing is when the feathers they have around the neck begin to turn yellow like gold.</i> So also <i>Maimonides</i> [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah] in <i>Issure Mizbeach</i> ch. 3, §2: והתורים כשרים משיזהבו <i>Turtledoves are fit for offering from the time they have a golden colour.</i> Where the <i>Gloss</i> is the same as in the <i>Talmud</i> above. But the necks of doves too, when facing the sun, shine with a golden colour. <i>The Philosopher</i>, in the book <i>On Colours</i>: Οἱ τῶν περιστερῶν τράχηλοι, καὶ τῶν ὑδάτων οἱ σταλαγμοὶ φαίνονται χρυσοειδεῖς, τοῦ φωτὸς ἀνακλωμένου [Hoi tōn peristerōn trachēloi, kai tōn hydatōn hoi stalagmoi phainontai chrysoeideis, tou phōtos anaklōmenou]. <i>The necks of doves and drops of water appear to be of a golden colour when the light is reflected.</i> Others write only that they gleam with various colours. <i>Lucretius</i>, book 2, line 800:

‎<i>As the plumage of doves appears in the sun,</i> <i>which, set about the nape, crowns the neck:</i> [II, 536] <i>for at times it comes to be red with bright fire-bronze,</i> <i>at times, by a certain way of seeing, it seems</i> <i>to mingle green emeralds with sky-blue.</i>

‎<i>Philo</i>, in the book <i>On Drunkenness</i>: Τὸν αὐχένα τῆς περιστερᾶς ἐν ἡλιακαῖς αὐγαῖς οὐ κατενόησας μυρίας χρωμάτων ἀλλάττοντα ἰδέας; ἢ οὐχὶ φοινικοῦν, καὶ κυανοῦν, πυροπόν τε, καὶ ἀνθρακοειδὲς, ἔτι τε ὠχρὸν, καὶ ἐρυθρὸν, καὶ ἄλλα παντόδαπα ἴσχει χρώματα, ὧν οὐδὲ τὰς κλήσεις ῥάδιον ἀπομνημονεῦσαι [Ton auchena tēs peristeras en hēliakais augais ou katenoēsas myrias chrōmatōn allattonta ideas? ē ouchi phoinikoun, kai kyanoun, pyropon te, kai anthrakoeides, eti te ōchron, kai erythron, kai alla pantodapa ischei chrōmata, hōn oude tas klēseis rhadion apomnēmoneusai]. <i>Have you never seen a dove's neck in the sun's rays varied into countless colours? Does it not have crimson, and sky-blue, and the colours of fire-bronze and of carbuncle, and pale, and red, and colours of every other kind, whose mere names it would not be easy to recount?</i> This <i>Eustathius</i> copied almost word for word in the <i>Hexaemeron</i>, page 29. <i>Nero</i>, in <i>Seneca</i>, <i>Natural Questions</i> book 1, ch. 5:

‎<i>The necks of</i> Cytherea's <i>dove shine as it moves.</i>

‎<i>Pliny</i>, book 10, chapter 26: <i>Doves have a certain sense even of glory. You would think they know their own colours and the ordered variety of them.</i> And book 37, chapter 5: <i>The Carchedonian</i>, etc. <i>uncertain in colour, and like the feathers of the green in peacocks' tails and on the necks of doves.</i> <i>Ausonius</i>, on ducks, <i>Epistle</i> 3:

‎<i>Whom iridescent plumage has painted with varied colour,</i> <i>rivals of doves in their necks.</i>

‎But these colours are only apparent. Thus in <i>Martianus</i>, book 2, [col. 11] <i>Arcesilas</i>, gazing at the neck of doves and moved by this uncertainty of variation, began to build up further his doctrine of ἀκαταληψία [akatalēpsia]. And <i>Carneades</i>, to prove that our senses are uncertain, brought forward the necks of doves, which change colour at every turn. So <i>Jerome</i> attests in his letter to <i>Pammachius</i> against the errors of <i>John of Jerusalem</i>. But <i>Oppian</i>, in book 1 of the <i>Cynegetica</i>, taught that the young even in the egg can be marked with various colours, and real ones at that: namely if, when the females are about to mate, there are set before them

‎— — — — — δαίδαλα πολλὰ Εἵματα πορφύρεα· ταὶ δὲ κλιδὸν ὄσσε βαλοῦσαι, Θυμὸν ἰαινόμεναι τίκτουσ᾽ ἁλιπόρφυρα τέκνα, [— — — — — daidala polla heimata porphyrea; tai de klidon osse balousai, thymon iainomenai tiktous' haliporphyra tekna,]

‎<i>many richly wrought purple garments: and they, casting sidelong glances at them and delighted in mind, bring forth purple young.</i> I cite this the more gladly because by a similar art <i>Jacob</i> obtained parti-coloured sheep for himself.

‎<i>The eyes of doves</i> are attributed to the bride in <i>Song 1:15</i> and <i>IV, 1.</i>, and to the bridegroom as well in <i>Song 5:12</i>; that is, eyes that are pure, lovely, simple, and finally chaste. This last is what the <i>Hebrews</i> chiefly understand here, because the dove looks only to its mate and does not [II, 537] cast its eyes on any other. <i>Rabbi Selomo</i>: משמכרת את בן זוגה אונה מניחתו שיזדווג לאחר, <i>After she has been betrothed to her mate, she does not delight in being joined to another.</i> And <i>Aben Ezra</i> [=Abraham ibn Ezra]: מנהג בני יונה שלא להתחבר אלא עם זוגה שלה, <i>Doves are not accustomed to associate with any other than their own mate.</i> <i>Aelian</i> teaches the same of the wood pigeon in <i>Hist.</i> book 3, ch. 44, and then says: Ἐὰν δὲ ἐποφθαλμιάσωσιν ἑτέροις, περιέρχονται αὐτοὺς οἱ λοιποὶ, καὶ τὸν μὲν ἄῤῥενα οἱ ὁμογενεῖς διασπῶσιν, αἱ θήλειαι δὲ τὸν θῆλυν [Ean de epophthalmiasōsin heterois, perierchontai autous hoi loipoi, kai ton men arrena hoi homogeneis diaspōsin, hai thēleiai de ton thēlyn]; <i>But if they cast lustful eyes on others, the rest surround them, and the males tear the male to pieces, the females the female.</i> Note here that the verb ἐποφθαλμιᾶν [epophthalmian] is used of a lustful look, because <i>the eyes are the leaders in love</i>, and ἐκ τοῦ ὁρᾷν γίνεται ἐρᾷν [ek tou horan ginetai eran]. Hence <i>John</i> <sup>a)</sup> speaks of Ἡ ἐπιθυμία τῶν ὀφθαλμῶν [Hē epithymia tōn ophthalmōn], and <i>Peter</i> <sup>b)</sup> of ὀφθαλμοὺς ἔχοντες μεστοὺς μοιχαλίδος [ophthalmous echontes mestous moichalidos]. <i>David</i> <sup>c)</sup>, having learned this to his own cost, earnestly asked God to turn his eyes away so that they should not behold vanity. And <i>Job</i> <sup>d)</sup> made a covenant with them that they should not look upon a virgin. The interpreters inquire anxiously what these words refer to in <i>Song 5:12</i>: <i>His eyes</i> (the bridegroom's) <i>are like</i> (the eyes) <i>of doves by the streams of waters, washed in milk, dwelling by the fullness.</i> I think the sacred writer had only this in view: that doves have livelier eyes by the waters, because they prefer to live near them. Accordingly <i>Varro</i> (<i>De re rustica</i>, book 3, chapter 7), in his description of the dovecote, τῆς περιστεροφείου [tēs peristeropheiou], says: <i>There should be pure water flowing in, from which they can both drink and bathe; for these birds are very clean.</i> And they are described not only as by the waters but also as by the fullness (supply "of the waters", as Scripture repeatedly speaks <sup>e)</sup> of the fullness of the sea), that is, by places where waters gush forth and come up close to the top of the banks, so that they are easy to drink from. The same doves are said to be <i>washed in milk</i>, that is, very white, as if things washed in milk became white, as things washed in ink become black. Compare <i>Job. IX, 30.</i>: <i>If I wash myself with snow water, and cleanse my hands with soap.</i> Far-fetched, and found nowhere else so far as I know, is the view of <i>Gregory</i> on the <i>Canticle</i>, chapter 5, that doves sit beside streams <i>so that they may see in the water the shadow of birds flying overhead, and by throwing themselves upon it may thus escape the talons of birds of prey.</i> But this seems to have been taken in part from the story of a certain bird which in <i>Arabic</i> is called خاطف ظله [khāṭif ẓillihi] <i>chatipho-dhillihi</i>, as if to say <i>snatcher of its own shadow</i>, because اذا راي ظله في الما اقبل اليه ليخطفه [idhā rāy ẓillahu fī al-mā aqbala ilayhi li-yakhṭafahu], <i>when it sees its shadow in the water, it approaches it in order to snatch it.</i> So <i>Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī, which Bochart cites like an author].</i> In <i>Damir</i> [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] it is called otherwise: [col. 12] قرلا [qirlā] <i>koralla</i>, a <i>Persian</i> word; رفراف [rafrāf] <i>riphraph</i>, from the fluttering of its wings; and [II, 538] ملاعب ظله [mulāʿib ẓillihi] <i>mulaibo dhillihi, playing with its shadow</i>. It is asserted to be في جنس العصافير [fī jins al-ʿaṣāfīr] <i>of the kind of sparrows</i>, or <i>small birds.</i> It is therefore not the jackdaw, which I once thought it was, because <i>Tzetzes</i> says of jackdaws in <i>Chiliad</i> 4, Hist. 119:

‎— εἴπερ χέεις ἔλαιον ἔν τινι λεκανίσκῃ, Πρὸς τὴν σκιὰν τὴν ἑαυτῶν τοὺς κολοιοὺς κρατήσῃς. Ναρκίσσους ἄλλους Λάκωνας ὄντας τῶν φιλοσκίων. [— eiper cheeis elaion en tini lekaniskē, Pros tēn skian tēn heautōn tous koloious kratēsēs. Narkissous allous Lakōnas ontas tōn philoskiōn.]

‎<i>If you pour oil into a small basin, you will hold the jackdaws fast at their own shadow, as if they were</i> Laconian Narcissi, <i>of those who love their own shadow.</i> Note in passing that <i>Narcissus</i> was not a <i>Laconian</i> but a <i>Boeotian</i>, as <i>Ovid</i>, <i>Metam.</i> book 3, Fab. 5, <i>Conon</i>, Hist. 24, <i>Strabo</i>, book 9, <i>Hyginus</i>, Fab. 271, <i>Pausanias</i> in the <i>Boeotica</i>, <i>Claudian</i>, book 2 <i>de raptu</i>, and countless others attest. Yet <i>Tzetzes</i> makes him a <i>Laconian</i> both here and in <i>Chil.</i> 1, Hist. 9, as if he were a countryman of <i>Hyacinthus</i>, with whom he compares him in <i>Hist.</i> 11. This μνημονικὸν σφάλμα [mnēmonikon sphalma] is not to be tolerated in a man who, in that very passage, boasts thus:

‎Οὐδὲ γὰρ μνημονέστερον τοῦ Τζέτζου Θεὸς ἄλλον Ἄνδρα τῶν πρίν τε, καὶ τῶν νῦν ἐξέφηνεν ἐν βίῳ. [Oude gar mnēmonesteron tou Tzetzou Theos allon Andra tōn prin te, kai tōn nyn exephēnen en biō.]

‎<i>For God has not brought into life any other man more mindful</i> than Tzetzes, <i>of those who have been or who now are.</i> And shortly before he had said that he knew all books. But, to return to the point from which we digressed, <i>the Hebrews</i> render the sacred words, which we refer to doves as to the eyes of doves, thus: <i>His eyes are like doves'</i> (eyes) <i>by the courses of waters, washed with milk, set in fullness</i>, that is, filling their place like a gem its setting. This is what in <i>Hebrew</i> is called מלואת אבן, <i>fullness</i> or <i>filling of stone</i>, Exod 28:17. So <i>Aben Ezra</i> and <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)], who holds that the eyes are described as לא בולטות יותר מדאי ולא שיקעות אלא יושבות מלאת גומא שלהם, <i>neither more prominent than is fitting, nor sunken, but so placed that they fill their socket.</i> And the recent interpreters follow this. But in this interpretation I find some things lacking. First, that in this way a milky colour seems to be attributed to the eyes, whose principal part, that is, the pupil, is black, or blue, etc. Second, that although stones enclosed in a setting are called in <i>Hebrew</i> אבני מלואים, <i>stones of fillings</i>, that is, which fill the setting, and the setting of a stone in a bezel is called מלואת אבן, <i>filling of stone</i>, yet a stone set in a bezel is nowhere said to be יושב על מלאת, <i>remaining at fullness.</i> Nor indeed can this be said. For who would say that money remains or stands at the fullness of a bag, or wine at the fullness of a cask, when the bag is full of money, or the cask full of wine <sup>R3</sup>?

‎[II, 539] In the voice of the dove there is something plaintive and moaning. Hence doves are said to moan. The author of the <i>Philomela</i>:

‎<i>And the chaste turtledove and the dove moan.</i>

‎<i>Virgil, Eclogue</i> 1: <i>Yet meanwhile neither will the hoarse wood-pigeons, your care,</i> <i>nor the turtledove cease to moan from the airy elm.</i>

‎<i>Martial</i>, book 3, <i>Epigr.</i> 57:

‎<i>Here moans the wood-pigeon, there the wax-coloured turtledove.</i>

‎<i>Gregory</i> on <i>Job</i>, book 32, ch. 4: <i>We know that the young of doves, or turtledoves,</i> <i>have moaning instead of song.</i> And on the <i>Song</i>, ch. 1: <i>The dove in its love moans</i> <i>instead of singing.</i> For the <i>Greeks</i> too this same thing is στένειν [stenein]. <i>Theocritus, Idyll</i> 7, verse 141:

‎Ἄειδον κόρυδοι, καὶ ἀκανθίδες, ἔστενε τρυγὼν, [Aeidon korydoi, kai akanthides, estene trygōn,]

‎<i>The larks and goldfinches were singing, the turtledove was moaning.</i> Hence <i>Chrysostom</i>, in the Homily <i>On the Turtledove</i>: Ἡ τρυγὼν ἡ φιλέρημος, ὅτε ἐν ὕλαις συγκοιταζομένη καλεῖ τὸν ὁμόζυγον, γοεραῖς κολακείαις τὰς κοιλάδας δονήσασα· [Hē trygōn hē philerēmos, hote en hylais synkoitazomenē kalei ton homozygon, goerais kolakeiais tas koilada donēsasa;] <i>The solitary turtledove, when</i> <i>lying in the woods it calls its mate, stirring the valleys with mournful blandishments.</i> [col. 13] Others say that turtledoves τρύζειν [tryzein] and doves γογγύζειν [gongyzein]. <i>Pollux</i>, book 5, chapter 13, §. 2, which is entitled Ὄρνεων φωναὶ [Orneōn phōnai], <i>Voices of Birds</i>: Εἴποις ἂν τρυγόνας τρύζειν, περιστερὰς γογγύζειν [Eipois an trygonas tryzein, peristeras gongyzein]. Γογγύζειν [Gongyzein] means <i>to murmur.</i> Hence you may find <i>murmura</i> [murmurs] for the moaning of doves: as when the emperor <i>Gallienus</i>, in <i>Trebellius</i>, says of the bride and groom in an epithalamium,

‎— — — <i>let not doves surpass your murmurs,</i> <i>nor ivy your arms, nor shells your kisses.</i>

‎But the <i>Hebrew</i> has הגה, which is rightly rendered by the verb <i>to moan</i>, since it everywhere refers to mourning. So in the song of King <i>Hezekiah</i> about his illness, <i>Isa 38:14</i> [II, 540] אהגה כיונה, <i>I moaned like a dove.</i> And <i>Isa 59:11</i>, where the afflicted condition of the <i>Jews</i> is treated: <i>We all growl like bears,</i> וכיונים הגה נהגה, <i>and like doves we moan with moaning,</i> that is, with heavy and continuous moaning <sup>R4</sup>. But המה too, which properly means <i>to roar</i>, applies to the moaning of doves, when it is said in <i>Ezek 7:16.</i> כיוני הגאות כלם הומות איש בעונו, <i>like doves of the valleys they all roared</i> (that is, <i>moaned</i>), <i>each for his own sin.</i> For כיוני הגיאות, <i>like doves of the valleys</i>, the <i>Septuagint</i> read כיונה הגות. Hence arose that rendering ὡς περιστεραὶ μελετητικαὶ [hōs peristerai meletētikai], <i>like moaning doves.</i> For with them μελετᾷν [meletan], said of doves, means <i>to moan</i>. Accordingly <i>Theodoret</i> on this passage: Ὀδυρόμενοι γὰρ καὶ θρηνοῦντες περιστεραῖς μελετώσαις ἀπεικαθήσονται· στένουσι γὰρ ἔοικε τῶν περιστερῶν ἡ φωνή [Odyromenoi gar kai thrēnountes peristerais meletōsais apeikasthēsontai; stenousi gar eoike tōn peristerōn hē phōnē]. This must certainly be rendered thus: <i>For those who lament and mourn will be compared to moaning doves</i> (not <i>to doves on high</i>, as the translator has it here). <i>For the voice of doves is like that of people moaning.</i> The voice of the dove is also taken for <i>mournful moaning</i> in <i>Nahum II,</i> 7., where the queen of <i>Nineveh</i>, as she is led into captivity, is accompanied by maidservants moaning, <i>as with the voice of doves, and beating their breasts.</i> The <i>Hebrew</i> has מנהגות כקול יונים, <i>leading away as with the voice of doves.</i> <i>Leading away</i> here, as they hold, is the same as <i>accompanying</i>, and <i>moaning</i> must be supplied. For <i>to lead away</i> is put for <i>to lead away and to moan</i>: one for two. So in <i>Prov 25:22</i>, <i>You will take coals of fire upon his head</i>, for <i>you will take and put</i>; and <i>Ps 68:19</i>, <i>You received gifts for men</i>, for <i>you received and gave</i>, or <i>you received things to give.</i> Accordingly <i>Paul</i> renders it <i>he gave gifts to men</i>, Ephes. IV, 8. What if for מנהגות, <i>leading away</i>, one were to read מהגות, <i>moaning</i>, with one small letter less? The sense would thus be plainer; yet I would not dare to change anything, since it is established that the most ancient translators read מנהגות <sup>R5</sup>.

‎Scripture praises the flight of the dove more than once. Thus <i>Isa 60:8</i>: <i>Who are these that fly together like clouds, and like doves to their windows?</i> The Rabbis refer this [II, 541] to the <i>Israelites</i>, who were to return from the <i>Babylonian</i> captivity with the greatest eagerness. Hence the <i>Chaldee</i> paraphrast: <i>Who are these who come openly like swift clouds without delay? The exiles</i> of Israel, <i>who are gathered and return to their land,</i> כיונין דתיבין לגו שובכיהן, <i>like doves that return to their dovecotes.</i> On these words <i>Kimchi</i>: יותר יעופו מהרה בשובם אל קניהם ממה שיעופו בצאתם מהם מפני שהם ממהרות לשוב אל גוזליהם שהניחו להאכילם מאשר בפיהם, <i>They fly faster when they return to their nests than when they go out from them, because they hasten to return to the young they have left, to offer them the food which they have in their mouth.</i> A similar passage is <i>Hos 11:11</i>: <i>They will tremble like a little bird out of</i> Egypt, <i>and like a dove from the land of</i> Assyria. <i>They will tremble</i>, יחרדו, that is, <i>they will hurry up in agitation.</i> So <i>1 Sam. XVI,</i> 4., when <i>Samuel</i> came to <i>Bethlehem</i>, ויחרדו, <i>And the elders of the city trembled</i> (that is, <i>hurried in agitation</i>) <i>to meet him.</i> And in this same <i>prophet</i>, Ose. III, 5.: <i>They will tremble to the Lord and to his goodness</i>, that is, <i>they will hurry up in agitation.</i> Aben Ezra: [col. 14] ישובו במהרה במרוצא פתע פתאום, <i>They will return hastily, at a run, quickly, swiftly.</i> Yet in the sacred text there is not חרדו but its synonym פחדו. Indeed, verbs of <i>trembling</i> sometimes relate to haste. <i>Virgil</i>, in the ninth book of the <i>Aeneid</i>:

‎<i>Do not tremble</i>, Teucrians, <i>to defend my ships.</i>

‎that is, <i>do not hurry.</i> So <i>Servius</i> and <i>Nonius Marcellus.</i> And <i>Livy</i>, book 23: <i>And soon, while each hurries</i> [<i>trepidat</i>] <i>running about to his own duties at the first signals.</i> <i>Trepidat</i>, that is, <i>festinat</i> [hurries]. So certainly in <i>Hosea</i>: <i>They will tremble like a dove</i> (that is, <i>they will flee with the utmost haste</i>) <i>from</i> Assyria <sup>R6</sup>. For the flight of the dove is extremely swift. Hence it is that in <i>Psal. LV</i>, 7. <i>David</i> wishes for the wings of a dove, to withdraw by swift flight from imminent danger. <i>Who</i> (he says) <i>will give me a wing like a dove's, with which I might fly away to safety? Behold, I would wander as far away as possible, to stay in the wilderness. And I would hasten my escape, swifter than the most rapid wind and whirlwind.</i> Just as the Chorus in <i>Sophocles</i>' <i>Oedipus at Colonus</i>:

‎Εἴτ᾽, ἀελλαία ταχύῤ- [Eit', aellaia tachyr-] <i>Would that, like a dove of swift</i> ῥωστος πελειὰς, [rhōstos peleias,] <i>whirlwind onrush,</i> Αἰθερίας νεφέλας [Aitherias nephelas] <i>I might pass through</i> Κύρσαί με, [Kyrsai me,] etc. <i>the ethereal clouds,</i> etc.

‎<i>Gregory of Nazianzus</i>, in Oration 17, says that <i>David</i> longed for the wings of a dove ὡς κουφοτέρας, καὶ ταχυτέρας [hōs kouphoteras, kai tachyteras], <i>as being lighter and swifter.</i> And <i>Jansenius</i> [=Cornelius Jansen of Ghent (d. 1576), commentary on Wisdom] in his Paraphrase: <i>O that wings were given me such as the dove has, which of all birds is the swiftest in flight; so that, as by its most rapid flight, and by that alone, it frees itself from</i> [II, 542] <i>its enemy the hawk,</i> etc. <i>Pliny</i> too, book 10, chapter 36, says that doves <i>in free flight</i> are <i>much swifter</i> than the hawk. And <i>Phaedrus</i>, book 1, <i>Fable 31</i>:

‎<i>The doves, when they had often fled the kite,</i> <i>And by the swiftness of their wings escaped death.</i>

‎And <i>Euripides</i>, of the <i>Bacchae</i> hastening to the slaughter of <i>Pentheus</i>:

‎Ἤιξαν πελέιας ὠκύτητ᾽ οὐκ ἥσσονες, [Ēixan peleias ōkytēt᾽ ouk hēssones,]

‎<i>They rushed on, not inferior to doves in swiftness.</i> Accordingly <i>Virgil</i>, <i>Aeneid</i> book 5, compares the ship of <i>Mnestheus</i>, which

‎<i>With the swift stroke of oars, and the winds called up,</i> <i>Makes for the sloping seas, and runs down the open main,</i>

‎to a dove in flight, at the moment when

‎<i>It is borne flying into the fields, and gliding through the still air</i> <i>Skims its liquid path, and does not move its swift wings.</i>

‎And, so that the <i>Argo</i> may be believed to have been a very swift ship, the <i>poets</i> describe it as quick ὅσον πελειάδος [hoson peleiados], <i>like a dove.</i> And they invent the story that a dove was sent by the <i>Argonauts</i> between the <i>Symplegades</i>, and that when it had got through, they concluded from this that the <i>Argo</i> too would get through, as <i>Eustathius</i> teaches on <i>Odyssey</i> μ´. The <i>Arabs</i> and <i>Hebrews</i> agree with this. Thus <i>Damir</i>, after teaching that الشهرمان والشاهين [al-shahramān wa-l-shāhīn], the <i>saharman</i> and the <i>sahin</i>, species of hawk, are most hostile to the dove, says: وهو اطير منهما ومن ساير الطير كلها [wa-huwa aṭyar minhumā wa-min sāyir al-ṭayr kullihā] <i>And it surpasses them in flight, and all birds whatsoever.</i> And on the passage of the <i>Psalm</i> that we have cited, <i>R. Simon, R. Iodan, R. Jonah [=Jonah ibn Janāḥ, Sefer ha-Shorashim],</i> and <i>R. Kimchi</i>, to the question why <i>David</i> prefers the wings of the dove to the wings of other birds, answer thus: כל העופות בשעה שיגעים הם נסים על גבי הסלע או על גבי האילן ונתפסין אבל יונה בשעה שיגעה היא קובצת אחת מאגפיה ופורחת באחת והיא נצולת ואינה נתפסת, <i>All other birds, when they are tired from flying, flee onto a rock or onto a tree, and are caught; but the dove, when it is tired, folds one of its wings and flies with the other, and so escapes and is not caught.</i> And <i>Aben Ezra</i>: והזכיר יונה [col. 15] שהיא עם בני אדם ועמה ישגרו המלכים ספריהם, <i>He mentions the dove because it lives with men, and kings send letters by it.</i> Indeed, in the twelfth century after <i>Christ</i>, when <i>Aben Ezra</i> wrote, this was much practised. <i>Paulus [=H. E. G. Paulus, Clavis über die Psalmen (1791)] Aemilius</i>, <i>On the Deeds of the Franks</i>, book 4, where he speaks of the governor of <i>Hasarchum</i>, a town of <i>Syria</i>: <i>His envoys</i> (sent to <i>Baldwin</i> to beg for help) <i>tied a letter to the tail of a dove which they had brought with them from home when they set out on their embassy, and sent it back as agreed. Thus informed, the governor defended himself excellently</i> [II, 543]. And where he speaks of <i>Jerusalem</i> besieged by the <i>Christians</i>: <i>It happened that a dove flying over the camp</i> of the Latins, <i>descending as a hawk pressed upon it, was caught by our men. Under its wings was found a letter written in Arabic, both in words and in letters.</i> And in book 5: <i>While they were encamped before the walls</i> (of <i>Tyre</i>), <i>some authors say that a dove flew up. When our men, taught by experience, recognized it as a letter-carrier, so great a shout was raised by the land and naval forces</i> of the Latins <i>that the bird, as if driven by a whirlwind of the disturbed air, fell to the ground. The letter taken from it, written in barbarian characters to</i> the Tyrians, <i>said that help would soon come, that they should be of good courage and endure the siege a few days. Our men, on the other hand, had another letter written in the hand and language of those barbarians whom they held in their power</i> etc. <i>and sent the bird back with the forged and deceitful letter, telling the besieged to take counsel for themselves according to their present need,</i> etc. <i>that no hope of help near at hand could be held out,</i> etc. <i>The Tyrians yielded to the trick.</i> <i>Marmol</i>, in his <i>Africa</i>, book 2, chapter 31, refers this to the year 1123. On the same matter see <i>Jacques de Vitry</i>, <i>Histor. Orient.</i> book 1; <i>Arnold of Lübeck</i> in the <i>History of the Slavs</i>; <i>Vincent</i> in the <i>Doctrinale</i> book 16, ch. 154; and <i>Wildebrand of Oldenburg</i> in the <i>Itinerarium Hierosolymitanum</i>, where he wrongly ascribes to the people of <i>Tripoli</i> what others attribute to the <i>Tyrians</i>. But older histories too offer us examples of the same art. For, to pass over fables, such as what the <i>Samaritans</i> write in chapter 20 of their spurious book of <i>Joshua</i>, that after defeating the five kings he informed <i>Eleazar</i> of this victory by a letter tied to the wings of birds, and in chapter 34 that he sent a letter to <i>Nebichus</i> by the service of a dove; and what we read in <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i>, book 6, chapter 7, that <i>Mares</i>, king of <i>Egypt</i>, used a crow to carry letters wherever he wished: to pass over these, I say, since they are plainly fabulous, why should we not believe what is found in <i>Aelian</i>'s <i>Varia</i>, book 9, chapter 2, that <i>Taurosthenes</i>, the Ὀλυμπιονίκην [Olympionikēn], announced his victory to his father in <i>Aegina</i> from <i>Olympia</i> on the same day by a dove as messenger? They say, namely, περιστερὰν τὸν Ταυροσθένην ἐπάγεσθαι ἀπολιποῦσαν τοὺς ἑαυτῆς νεοσσοὺς ὑγροὺς ἔτι, καὶ ἀπτῆνας, νικήσαντα, καὶ ἀφεῖναι τὴν πελειάδα, προσάψαντα πορφύραν αὐτῇ· τὴν δὲ ἐπειγομένην πρὸς τοὺς νεοττοὺς, ἀπαυθημερῆσαι ἐκ Πίσσης εἰς Αἴγιναν· [peristeran ton Taurosthenēn epagesthai apolipousan tous heautēs neossous hygrous eti, kai aptēnas, nikēsanta, kai apheinai tēn peleiada, prosapsanta porphyran autē; tēn de epeigomenēn pros tous neottous, apauthēmerēsai ek Pissēs eis Aiginan] that Taurosthenes <i>took with him a dove taken away from its chicks, still tender and unfledged; and when he had won, he sent it back with a piece of purple tied to it, and it, hastening with all speed to its chicks, flew on the same day</i> from Pisa to Aegina. <i>Pliny</i> and <i>Frontinus</i> report that something similar happened at the siege of <i>Mutina</i>, although they hardly agree with each other. For the latter, in book 3 of the <i>Stratagems</i>, writes that <i>Hirtius</i> sent letters, tied with a bristle to a dove's neck, to <i>Decimus Brutus</i>, besieged in <i>Mutina</i> by <i>Antony</i>; the former, in book 10, chapter 37, says that doves were messengers in great affairs, <i>with letters attached to their feet, at the siege of</i> Mutina, <i>when</i> D. Brutus <i>sent them to the consuls' camp</i>. But in the same siege [II, 544] the same <i>Brutus</i> could have both received and sent back letters by the service of doves. You may often see the <i>poets</i> allude to this skill of doves. Among them <i>Anacreon</i>, in Ode 9, attributes the following to his dove:

‎Ἐγὼ δ᾽ Ἀνακρέοντι [Egō d᾽ Anakreonti] <i>And I, for Anacreon,</i> Διακονῶ τοσαῦτα, [Diakonō tosauta,] <i>serve in such matters,</i> Καὶ νῦν οἵας ἐκείνου [Kai nyn hoias ekeinou] <i>And now see</i> [col. 16] Ἐπιστολὰς κομίζω. [Epistolas komizō.] <i>what letters of his I carry.</i>

‎<i>Pherecrates</i> in the <i>Painters</i>, in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9:

‎Ἀπόπεμψον ἀγγέλλοντα, τὸν περιστερὸν, [Apopempson angellonta, ton peristeron,]

‎<i>Send back the dove as messenger.</i> <i>Martial</i>, on a dove that flew down into the lap of <i>Aretulla</i>, book 8, <i>Epigram</i> 32:

‎<i>Perhaps this bird has come to you from the</i> Sardinian <i>shores of the exile,</i> <i>a messenger that your brother will return.</i>

‎To these I cannot help adding these few lines from the most charming <i>Elegy</i> of the late <i>Heinsius</i> on the doves of <i>Leiden</i>, book 3, <i>Elegy</i> 10:

‎<i>Here he sends the sacred bird down from the anxious city,</i> <i>to carry back his directions; she flies.</i> <i>And soon, rejoicing to see her home and sweet nest again,</i> <i>she glided back through the midst of the air.</i> <i>She carries in her wings the letters and orders of</i> Boisot, <i>and has now completed the sweet work of her service.</i>

‎This art, which had nearly died out in our fathers' time, the <i>Dutch</i> revived. So too did the <i>Turks</i>: <i>Linschoten</i>, in his description of <i>Ormuz</i>, chapter 6, writes that their emperor learns within a short time by means of doves what is happening in the most remote parts of his empire. Accordingly, in the book <i>On Animals</i>, which <i>Calonymus</i> translated from the <i>Arabic</i> of <i>Abulsaphe</i> into <i>Hebrew</i> in the year of <i>Christ</i> 1316, it is said of the dove: היונה המסולסל הוא זה המגביה לעוף נושה כתבים ואגרות מארץ אל ארץ לחבונתו, <i>The dove, raising itself on high, that is, which is exalted in its flight, when by its intelligence it carries letters from place to place.</i> For, as <i>Alkazuinius</i> [=al-Qazwīnī, ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt] says, اذا ارسل من موضع بعيد يصعد نحو الهوا وتكون صعوده مدورا فلا يزال يصعد وينظر حتي يري شيا من علامات بلده فعند ذلك يهبط اليها بادني زمان [idhā ursila min mawḍiʿ baʿīd yaṣʿad naḥwa al-hawā wa-takūn ṣuʿūduhu mudawwaran fa-lā yazāl yaṣʿad wa-yanẓur ḥattī yarī shayan min ʿalāmāt baladihi fa-ʿinda dhālika yahbiṭ ilayhā bi-adnā zamān] <i>When it is sent from a very distant place, it climbs into the highest air, and its ascent is made in a circle. Nor does it stop climbing and looking about until it notices some sign of its own region. Then it descends to it</i> [II, 545] <i>in a short time.</i> And <i>Damir</i>: في طبعه انه يطلب وكره ولو ارسل من الف فرسخ ويحمل الاخبار وياتي بها من المسافة البعيدة في المده القريبة وفيه ما يقطع ثلاثة الاف فرسخ في يوم واحد [fī ṭabʿihi annahu yaṭlub wakrahu wa-law ursila min alf farsakh wa-yaḥmil al-akhbār wa-yātī bihā min al-masāfa al-baʿīda fī al-mudda al-qarība wa-fīhi mā yaqṭaʿ thalāthat ālāf farsakh fī yawm wāḥid] <i>And its nature is to seek its nest again, even if it is sent from a thousand parasangs, and to carry messages from the most remote places in the shortest time, so that some are said to have covered three thousand parasangs in a single day.</i> This exceeds all belief, and I think it was invented by those who tell of great things as still greater. The little letter that is tied to the dove is called in <i>Arabic</i> by a special name, بطاقة [biṭāqa] <i>bittaka</i>, that is, πιττάκιον [pittakion], <i>pittacium</i> [slip]. And زجل [zajala] <i>zagiala</i> means to send out a dove for this purpose; and a dove so sent is called زجال [zijāl] <i>zigial</i>. So it is in <i>Alcamus</i> <sup>R7</sup>.

‎R6 *(p. 541)* Compare ALB. Schultens, <i>Animadverss. Philologg.</i> on Esaj. XIX. 17., p. 265 of the <i>Opera minora</i>, Leiden 1769. ED. Pococke [=Edward Pococke, Commentary on Hosea (1685)] shows a similar use of the Arabic verb فزع [fazaʿa] in his <i>Commentar.</i> on Hos. III, 5.

‎R7 *(p. 545)* On <i>carrier doves</i>, whose service the Turks still use today for carrying letters, much is reported by DELLA VALLE in his <i>Travels</i> vol. I, letter 12, pp. 128–129 of the German translation (who asserts that a six days' journey is completed by a carrier dove in a single day), and by ALEX. RUSSELL in <i>the Natural History of Aleppo</i> p. 66. The plan of our work does not allow us to transcribe these passages here, since they are too long.
