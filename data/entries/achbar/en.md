# Book 3, chapter 34: The common mouse (vol. 2, pp. 429–435)

### ‎[II, 429] [col. 1017] Chapter 34.

### ‎<i>On the Common Mouse.</i>

‎The <i>Hebrews</i> call the mouse עכבר <i>achbar</i>, a word that is perhaps rather <i>Chaldee</i>, and which properly means <i>field mouse</i>. For עכבר <i>achbar</i> seems to be said for עכלבר <i>achalbar</i>, with the L elided, as in the words <i>Babel</i> for <i>Balbel</i>, and <i>Samuel</i> for שאול-מאל <i>Saul-meel</i>, as if you were to say <i>asked of God</i>. For in <i>Chaldee</i> עכל <i>achal</i> is <i>to consume;</i> and בר <i>bar</i> is <i>field:</i> therefore עכלבר <i>achalbar</i>, and by contraction עכבר <i>achbar</i>, is <i>consumer</i> or <i>devastator of the field</i> <sup>R1</sup>. The name fits the field mouse very well, which is a sure plague of the fields. <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>Hist.</i> book 6, ch. 37: Ἀπορεῖται δὲ καὶ ἡ τῶν ἐπιπολαζόντων μυῶν γένεσις ἐν ταῖς χώραις, καὶ ἡ φορά. πολλαχοῦ γὰρ εἴωθε γίνεσθαι πλῆθος ἀμύθητον τῶν ἀρουραίων, ὥστε ὀλίγον λείπεθαι τοῦ σίτου παντός. γίνεται δὲ οὕτω ταχεῖα ἡ φορὰ, ὥστε ἔνιοι τῶν μὴ μεγάλας γεωργίας ἐργαζομένων, τῇ προτέρᾳ ἰδόντες ὅτι θερίζειν ὥρα, τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ἕωθεν ἄγοντες τοὺς θεριστὰς, καταβεβρωμένα ἅπαντα καταλαμβάνουσιν. [Aporeitai de kai hē tōn epipolazontōn myōn genesis en tais chōrais, kai hē phora. pollachou gar eiōthe ginesthai plēthos amythēton tōn arouraiōn, hōste oligon leipethai tou sitou pantos. ginetai de houtō tacheia hē phora, hōste enioi tōn mē megalas geōrgias ergazomenōn, tē protera idontes hoti therizein hōra, tē hysteraia heōthen agontes tous theristas, katabebrōmena hapanta katalambanousin.] <i>The generation and increase of the mice that appear in the fields is also called into question.</i> [II, 430] <i>For often so great a multitude of field mice arises that little grain is left. And their increase is so sudden that some of those who have not ploughed much land, having decided the day before to reap, on bringing the reapers early the next morning find all the crops consumed.</i> Hence <i>Pliny</i>, having spoken of the prodigious fertility of mice, book 10, ch. 65, says: <i>And so it ceases to be a wonder whence so great a multitude of field mice ravages the harvests.</i> And in <i>Horus</i>, book 1, ch. 47: the <i>Egyptians</i>, ἀφανισμὸν δηλοῦντες, μῦν ζωγραφοῦσιν, ἐπειδὴ πάντα ἐσθίων μιαίνει καὶ ἀχρηστοῖ [aphanismon dēlountes, myn zōgraphousin, epeidē panta esthiōn miainei kai achrēstoi], <i>signifying destruction, paint a mouse, because in consuming everything it defiles and ruins it.</i>

‎Examples are met with everywhere. Thus at <i>1 Sam 5:6</i> in the <i>Greek</i> version it is written of those <i>Philistines</i> who held the captured ark: Μέσον τῆς χώρας αὐτῆς ἀνεφύησαν μύες [Meson tēs chōras autēs anephyēsan myes]. And in the <i>Vulgate</i>: <i>And the villages and fields in the midst of that region boiled up, and mice were born.</i> And again at ch. VI, 1. in the <i>Greek</i>: Καὶ ἐξέζεσεν ἡ γῆ αὐτῶν [col. 1018] μύας [Kai exezesen hē gē autōn myas]. <i>And their land boiled up with mice.</i> These are indeed glosses, since neither the <i>Hebrew</i> original nor the other versions, the <i>Chaldee</i>, I mean, the <i>Syriac</i> and the <i>Arabic</i>, recognize anything of the kind. Yet the thing is true in itself. For in verse five of the same sixth chapter the <i>Hebrew</i> too has: <i>You shall make</i>, etc. צלמי עכבריכם המשחיתים את הארץ, <i>images of your mice that ravage the land</i>; مثال الجرذان التي تسلطت علي الارض لتفسدها [mithāl al-jirdhān allatī tasallaṭat ʿalī al-arḍ li-tufsidahā], <i>images of the mice</i> <sup>R2</sup> <i>that fall upon the land to ravage it</i>, as the <i>Arabic translator</i> has it. Accordingly <i>Josephus</i>, <i>Antiquities</i> book 6, chapter 1, rightly says: Τὰ δὲ ἐπὶ τῆς χώρας μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀνελθὸν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς, κατέβλαψε, μήτε φυτῶν, μήτε καρπῶν ἀποσχόμενον· [Ta de epi tēs chōras myōn plēthos, anelthon apo tēs gēs, keteblapse, mēte phytōn, mēte karpōn aposchomenon] <i>And a multitude of mice, emerging from the earth, laid waste what was in the region, sparing neither plants nor fruits.</i> And <i>Philastrius</i>, on the Musorites: <i>When the ark of the Covenant had been captured by the Allophyli, and the earth, through the wrath of God, had produced shrews, so that their crops perished.</i> And <i>Maginus</i> in his <i>Palestine</i>: <i>In some places of</i> Palestine <i>the fields are almost deserted because of the abundance of mice and shrews, so that, if certain birds did not devour them, the inhabitants could make no sowing</i> <sup>R3</sup>.

‎[II, 431] Lest anyone think this evil peculiar to <i>Eretz Yisrael</i>, <i>Strabo</i>, book 3, records that in <i>Spain</i> too there has sometimes arisen μυῶν πλῆθος, ἀφ᾽ οὗ καὶ λοιμικαὶ νόσοι πολλάκις ἠκολούθησαν [myōn plēthos, aph᾽ hou kai loimikai nosoi pollakis ēkolouthēsan], <i>a multitude of mice, which has often been followed by a pestilential plague.</i> And in <i>Cantabria</i> the <i>Romans</i>, a reward being offered, μυοθηροῦντες πρὸς μέτρον ἀποδειχθὲν διεσώζοντο μόλις [myothērountes pros metron apodeichthen diesōzonto molis], <i>hunting mice to a fixed measure, were scarcely saved.</i> Nor are there lacking those who report the same of <i>Italy</i>. <i>Diodorus</i>, book 3: Περὶ τὴν Ἰταλίαν μυῶν πλῆθος ἀρουραίων. ἐγγενηθὲν τοῖς πεδίοις, ἐξέβαλέ τινας τῆς πατρίου χώρας· [Peri tēn Italian myōn plēthos arouraiōn. engenēthen tois pediois, exebale tinas tēs patriou chōras] <i>Around</i> Italy <i>a multitude of field mice, having emerged from the fields, drove some people from their native soil.</i> This very thing is in <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 17, ch. 41. And both follow <i>Agatharchides</i>, book 5, ch. 28. Further, it is most likely that the people of <i>Cosa</i> are meant, because <i>Rutilius Rufus</i> sings of <i>Cosa</i>:

‎<i>The citizens, it is said, once forced to emigrate, abandoned their homes, infested by mice.</i>

‎Likewise, to the <i>Abderites</i> of <i>Thrace</i>, who had left their native soil because of the multitude of frogs and mice and were seeking a place to settle, <i>Cassander</i> assigned lands in the farthest borders of <i>Macedonia</i>, as <i>Trogus</i> attests, book 15. <i>Heraclides</i> in the <i>Politiae</i> likewise relates that the <i>Chalcidians</i> settled in <i>Cleonae</i>, ἐν τῷ Ἄθῳ ἐξαναστάντας ἐξ Ἐλυμνίου ὑπὸ μυῶν, οἳ τὰ τ᾽ ἄλλα κατήθιον αὐτῶν, καὶ τὸν σίδηρον [en tō Athō exanastantas ex Elymniou hypo myōn, hoi ta t' alla katēthion autōn, kai ton sidēron], <i>having been driven out of</i> Elymnium <i>on Mount</i> Athos <i>by mice, which devoured not only other things but even iron itself.</i> Others report the same of <i>Gyarus</i>. Pliny, book 8, chapter 29: <i>M. Varro is the authority</i>, etc., <i>that the inhabitants of</i> Gyarus, <i>an island of the</i> Cyclades, <i>were put to flight by mice.</i> And in chapter 57: <i>Theophrastus is the authority that on the island of</i> Cyarus, <i>after they had driven out the inhabitants, they gnawed even iron.</i> Hence <i>Antigonus</i>, <i>Mirabilia</i>, chapter 21: Τῆς δὲ Καρυστίας καὶ τῆς Ἀνδρίας χώρας ἐστὶν πλησίον νῆσος ἡ καλουμένη Γύαρος· ἐνταῦθα οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι τὸν σίδηρον [Tēs de Karystias kai tēs Andrias chōras estin plēsion nēsos hē kaloumenē Gyaros; entautha hoi myes diatrōgousi ton sidēron]. <i>Near</i> Carystia <i>and</i> Andria <i>there is an island named</i> Gyarus, <i>in which mice eat iron.</i> And of the same island <i>Stephanus of Byzantium</i>: Ἀντίγονος δ᾽ ὁ Καρύστιός φησι, ὅτι ἐν ταύτῃ οἱ μύες διατρώγουσι σίδηρον [Antigonos d' ho Karystios phēsi, hoti en tautē hoi myes diatrōgousi sidēron]; <i>and</i> Antigonus of Carystus <i>says that on this</i> island <i>mice</i> [II, 432] <i>eat iron</i> <sup>R4</sup>. <i>Suidas</i> says much the same. Thus the text of Aristotle in the <i>Mirabilia</i> [ch. 24] is corrupt: Ἐν Κύπρῳ τῇ νήσῳ λέγεται τοὺς μέτας τὸν σίδηρον ἐσθίειν [En Kyprō tē nēsō legetai tous metas ton sidēron esthiein]; <i>On the island of</i> Cyprus <i>they say that the</i> metae <i>devour iron.</i> Corrupt also is Aelian, <i>History</i>, book 5, chapter 14: Ἐν τῇ Πάρῳ τῇ νήσῳ Ἀριστοτέλης λέγει μῦς εἶναι, καὶ μέν τοι καὶ γῆν σιτεῖσθαι τὴν σιδηρῶτιν [En tē Parō tē nēsō Aristotelēs legei mys einai, kai men toi kai gēn siteisthai tēn sidērōtin]; <i>Aristotle says that on the island of</i> Paros <i>there are mice which eat iron-bearing earth.</i> For in both places, instead of Κύπρῳ [Kyprō] or Πάρῳ [Parō], one should read Γυάρῳ [Gyarō]; and in the former passage μύας [myas] instead of μέτας [metas], in the latter τὴν σιδηρῖτιν [tēn sidēritin]. What of the fact that the inhabitants of the <i>Troad</i>, whom the <i>Greeks</i> barely subdued in a ten-year war, were forced to yield to this plague and abandoned their homeland? Pliny, book 10, [col. 1019] chapter 65: <i>Very many mice thus spring up in the</i> Troad, <i>and they have already driven out the inhabitants from there.</i> And at <i>Chryse</i>, a city of <i>Mysia</i>, <i>Apollo</i>, angered at the priest <i>Crinis</i>, ἔπεμψεν αὐτοῦ τοῖς ἄγροις μύας, οἵτινες τοὺς καρποὺς ἐλυμαίνοντο [epempsen autou tois agrois myas, hoitines tous karpous elymainonto], <i>sent mice into his fields, which destroyed the crops.</i> So the old <i>Scholiast</i> on <i>Homer</i>, on the word Σμινθεῦ [Smintheu] in book 1 of the <i>Iliad</i>. Of the same kind is what Aelian relates, <i>History</i>, book 12, chapter 5: Τῶν Αἰολέων καὶ τῶν Τρώων τὰ λήϊα πολλὰς μυῶν μυριάδας ἐπελθούσας ἄωρα ὑποκείρειν, καὶ ἀτελῆ τὰ θέρη τοῖς σπείρασιν ὑποφαίνειν [Tōn Aioleōn kai tōn Trōōn ta lēia pollas myōn myriadas epelthousas aōra hypokeirein, kai atelē ta therē tois speirasin hypophainein]; <i>that once very many myriads of mice invaded the crops of the</i> Aeolians <i>and</i> Trojans, <i>cut them down unripe, and made the harvest fruitless for those who had sown</i> <sup>R5</sup>.

‎From this it is abundantly clear that hardly any name could have been given more aptly to <i>mice</i>, and specifically <i>field</i> mice, than one taken <i>from the laying waste of fields.</i> Therefore עכבר <i>achbar</i> properly denotes this. This is confirmed by the usage of the <i>Arabs</i>, for whom عكبر [ʿkbr] <i>achbar</i> is not just any mouse, but a species of <i>field mouse</i>, namely the male <i>aljarbuo</i>, as we showed above <sup>a)</sup>. Accordingly, at <i>Esa. LXVI</i>, 17, for the <i>Hebrew</i> העכבר <i>haachbar</i> the Arabic has اليربوع [al-yarbūʿ] <i>aljarbuo</i>, as though these two were nearly synonyms. And the very thing with which <i>Isaiah</i> there reproaches the <i>Jews</i>, that some of them ate swine's flesh and העכבר <i>haachbar</i>, suggests that עכבר is a <i>field mouse</i>, and indeed the اليربوع [al-yarbūʿ] <i>aljarbuo</i>, which alone, the <i>Arabs</i> report, is eaten. For the μύωξος [myōxos], that is, the <i>glis</i> [dormouse], which <i>Jerome</i> understands here, is not [II, 433] reckoned by the <i>Arabs</i> among the mice <sup>R6</sup>. I admit, indeed, that in the Hebrew <i>History of the Second Temple</i>, p. 241, where the inhabitants of <i>Jerusalem</i>, besieged by the <i>Romans</i>, are said to have eaten נבלות הכלבים ועכברים, <i>the carcasses of dogs, and mice,</i> domestic mice must necessarily be understood, since in a city so closely besieged field mice were not at hand. And in extreme famine they had to accept as food whatever presented itself. But <i>Isaiah</i> is speaking of foods which the <i>Jews</i> of his time, in rebellion against God's law, chose for themselves of their own accord, under no compulsion of necessity. Moreover, no one seeks out the house mouse as food when other fare is available. Therefore in <i>Isaiah</i> the mice that were eaten were field mice, not house mice. The same point is supported by the fact that in the passage of <i>Samuel</i> we cited, the עכברים <i>achbarim</i> lay waste the fields <i>of the Philistines</i>. For that is characteristic of field mice. It is certain, however, that the later <i>Hebrews</i> take עכבר <i>achbar</i> for any mouse whatever. And <i>Moses</i> himself may have used it so, taking the word in a broader sense. To show this, <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases the <i>Hebrew</i> העכבר <i>haachbar</i> at <i>Lev 11:29</i> thus: ועכברא אוכמא וסמוקא וחיורא, that is, the <i>black</i>, <i>red and white</i> achbar: these are the three colours of mice. Of these the white is very rare, yet not unknown to the <i>Greeks</i>. <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 12, ch. 5, on the temple of <i>Apollo Smintheus</i> in <i>Hamaxitus</i>, a city of the <i>Troad</i>: Ὑπὸ δὲ τῷ βωμῷ φωλεύουσι λευκοὶ μῦες [Hypo de tō bōmō phōleuousi leukoi myes], <i>Under the altar white mice make their nests.</i> These are thought the most lustful of all; hence the proverb, Μῦς λευκὸς, ἐπὶ τῶν ἀκρατῶν περὶ τὰ Ἀφροδίσια [Mys leukos, epi tōn akratōn peri ta Aphrodisia]· <i>A white mouse, of those who are utterly incontinent in sexual matters.</i> See <i>Suidas</i>, and what <i>Aelian</i> reports from <i>Philemon</i>, <i>History</i>, book 12, chapter 10.

‎As to why the Law declares the mouse an unclean animal, besides the reasons that occur to anyone, these too are offered: that it is useless and harmful, and a type of those who say,

‎<i>We are mere numbers, born to consume the fruits of the earth.</i>

‎In <i>Cyril</i>, <i>Against Julian</i>, book 9: γαλῆ καὶ μῦς γράφουσί πως ἐφ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς τὰ δειλὰ, καὶ ἄνανδρα, καὶ ψοφώδη τῶν κλεπτῶν γένη [galē kai mys graphousi pōs eph᾽ heautois ta deila, kai ananda, kai psophōdē tōn kleptōn genē], [col. 1020] <i>the weasel and the mouse</i> [II, 434] <i>represent the timid, unwarlike and noisy kinds of thieves.</i> Since <i>noisy</i> is here entirely beside the point, I maintain that for ψοφώδη [psophōdē] we should read ψοφοδεῆ [psophodeē], that is, <i>afraid of every noise.</i> This word is used by <i>Lucian</i> in the <i>Scythian</i>, and by <i>Plutarch</i> in the book <i>On Preserving Health</i>; and the same author in the book <i>Utra anim.</i> says of the chameleon: Ὑπὸ δέους ἄλλως τρέπεται, φύσει ψοφοδεὴς ὢν, καὶ δειλός [Hypo deous allōs trepetai, physei psophodeēs ōn, kai deilos]· <i>From fear it changes into another colour, being by nature fearful and timid.</i> Hence in <i>Hesychius</i> ψοφοδεὴς [psophodeēs] is δειλὸς, κενοφόβος, ὁ καὶ τοὺς ψόφους καὶ τὰ ἐλάχιστα φοβούμενος [deilos, kenophobos, ho kai tous psophous kai ta elachista phoboumenos]. How well this suits the mouse the excellent <i>Poet</i> shows in his fable of the country mouse and the town mouse, <i>Satire</i> 6, book 2:

‎<i>The clatter of the doors shook both from their couches: in panic they ran through the whole room, and trembled the more, half dead, as soon as the lofty house rang with Molossian hounds.</i> — — — —

‎The <i>Hebrews</i> offer another reason, namely that eating mouse flesh induces forgetfulness. The most learned <i>Buxtorf</i> [=Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, Synagoga Judaica and Lexicon Chaldaicum Talmudicum et Rabbinicum] proves this from <i>Avoda Zera</i> ch. 3 (where, however, I do not find it) and from <i>Beth Iacob</i> fol. 181, where to the question why a dog recognizes its master but a cat does not, the answer is given that cats are forgetful because they eat mice, whose flesh, and indeed whatever they have gnawed, if eaten, disturbs the brain and the memory. Hence this conclusion is drawn: האוכל ממה שהעכבר אוכל משכח תלמודו האוכל עכבר עצמו לא כ״ש <i>He who eats of what a mouse has eaten forgets his learning: how much more, then, he who eats the mouse itself?</i>

‎The defeat of <i>Sennacherib</i>, which the sacred writers ascribe to an angel <sup>b)</sup>, and <i>Josephus</i> <sup>c)</sup>, following <i>Berosus</i>, to a plague sent by God, the <i>Egyptians</i> in <i>Herodotus</i> <sup>d)</sup> strangely refer to field mice. They relate that these, τοῖσι ἐναντίοισι αὐτοῖσι ἐπιχυθέντας νυκτὸς, κατὰ μὲν φαγέειν τοὺς φαρετρεῶνας αὐτέων, κατὰ δὲ τὰ τόξα, πρὸς δὲ τῶν ἀσπίδων τὰ ὄχανα· ὥς τε τῇ ὑστεραίῃ φευγόντων σφέων, γυμνῶν ὅπλων, πεσέειν πολλούς· [toisi enantioisi autoisi epichythentas nyktos, kata men phagein tous pharetreōnas auteōn, kata de ta toxa, pros de tōn aspidōn ta ochana; hōs te tē hysteraiē pheugontōn spheōn, gymnōn hoplōn, peseein pollous] <i>poured over the enemy themselves by night, gnawed through their quivers, their bows, and the handles of their shields, so that the next day, stripped of their arms and with many lost, they took to flight.</i> Is it relevant here that λοιμὸς [loimos] <i>plague</i> and λαμὰς [lamas] <i>mouse</i> in <i>Hesychius</i> are words so close that one could have been used for the other, and thus a defeat brought about by <i>plague</i> was ascribed to <i>mice</i>? However that may be, something of the kind is recorded to have happened to those <i>Cretans</i> who founded <i>Hamaxitus</i>, a city of the <i>Troad</i>. For when <i>Apollo</i> [II, 435], consulted about where to found their city, had answered that it must be founded ἔνθα ἂν αὐτοῖς οἱ γηγενεῖς πολεμήσωσι [entha an autois hoi gēgeneis polemēsōsi], <i>where the earth-born should make war on them</i>, they understood that <i>Hamaxitus</i> was meant; because, when they had pitched camp there, μυῶν ἄφατόν τι πλῆθος ἐφερπύσαν τά τε ὄχανα αὐτοῖς τῶν ἀσπίδων διέτραγε, καὶ τὰς τῶν τόξων νευρὰς διέφαγεν [myōn aphaton ti plēthos epherpysan ta te ochana autois tōn aspidōn dietrage, kai tas tōn toxōn neuras diephagen], <i>an immense host of mice, bursting forth, gnawed the handles of their shields and ate through the strings of their bows.</i> So <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i>, book 12, chapter 5. Not very differently the old <i>Scholiast</i> of <i>Homer</i> on book 1 of the <i>Iliad</i>: Νυκτὸς ἐπιγενομένης, μῦες ἔκοψαν αὐτῶν τοὺς τελαμῶνας τῶν ὅπλων [Nyktos epigenomenēs, myes ekopsan autōn tous telamōnas tōn hoplōn], <i>When night had come, mice cut through the straps of their arms.</i> Accordingly, in <i>Aristotle</i>, <i>Rhetoric</i>, book 2, chapter 24, <i>Polycrates</i> praises mice ὅτι ἐβοήθησαν διατραγόντες τὰς νευρὰς [hoti eboēthēsan diatragontes tas neuras], <i>because they brought help</i> (to his countrymen) <i>when they gnawed through the strings</i> of the enemy's bows. And in <i>Clement</i>'s <i>Protrepticus</i>, <i>Polemo</i> reports that the peoples neighbouring the <i>Troad</i> τοὺς ἐπιχωρίους μῦς, οὓς σμίνθους καλοῦσι θρησβεύειν, ὅτι τὰς νευρὰς τῶν πολεμίων διέτρωγον τῶν τόξων· [tous epichōrious mys, hous sminthous kalousi thrēsbeuein, hoti tas neuras tōn polemiōn dietrōgon tōn toxōn] <i>worship the mice of that place, which they call</i> sminthoi, <i>because they had gnawed the strings of the enemy's bows.</i> Since these accounts are very similar to what the <i>Egyptians</i> relate of the <i>Assyrians</i>, I would think that they worked this <i>Cretan</i>, or if you prefer <i>Trojan</i>, story into their own.

**Notes**

‎R1 *(p. 429)* Simonis, in his <i>Lexicon</i> and in the <i>Arcanum Formarum</i> p. 660, thinks the word עכבר is compounded from the verb עכר, which he supposes has the same meaning as the Arabic عرك [ʿarak] <i>he rubbed</i>, and from the word בר, so that it denotes the animal <i>rubbing grain</i>. How frigid this is, anyone will feel at once. Nor do I like any better what Michaelis [=J. D. Michaelis, Supplementa ad lexica hebraica (1784–92)] put forward in <i>Supplemm. ad Lexx. [=J. D. Michaelis, Supplementa ad lexica Hebraica]</i> vol. 5, p. 1908, referring the Hebrew עכבר to the Arabic عُكَبٌ [ʿukabun] <i>nimble, brisk</i>.

‎R2 *(p. 430)* The singular جُرَذٌ [juradhun] is explained in the lexicons as: <i>a species of field mouse</i>. Under the name جِرْذ [jirdh], <i>Dschird</i>, Shaw [=Thomas Shaw, Travels (1738)] described this animal in <i>Travels</i> p. 156 of the German trans., from which it is clear that it is the <i>mus Glis</i> with a hairy tail, <i>the body dark ash-grey above, whitish below</i>, or the <i>sciurus canus subtus albidus</i> of Linnaeus, p. 155, ed. 13, the μυωξον [myōxon] of <i>Oppian</i>, Cyneg. 2, 574.

‎R3 *(p. 430)* Bochart passed over the Syriac translator, who put ܥܘܟܒܪܐ [עוכברא], a word that signifies both a <i>mouse</i> and <i>a creeping disease, one that twists itself like a serpent</i>, on the testimony of the lexicographer Bar Bahlul. To this belongs the view of Hensler too, chief physician to the king [II, 431] of Denmark, that by the word עכברים in 1 Sam 6:5 not <i>mice</i> are meant but <i>carbuncles</i>, perhaps <i>venereal buboes</i>. Michaelis reports this conjecture, from a letter Hensler sent him, in the <i>Supplemm.</i> vol. 5, p. 1909, and not without approval: “There is,” he says, “something in the whole narrative that urges us to understand buboes or piles of this kind. Among the evils that had afflicted the Philistines, 1 Sam. V makes no mention of mice, which so troubled the translators that the LXX and the Vulgate translator added mice at 1 Sam 5:6; Houbigant approved this, but no manuscript has confirmed it. We are freed from all doubt if the mice of Chapter VI are much the same as עפלים, <i>carbuncles</i>, or a kind of <i>tumour</i> or ulcer.” But he soon adds: “Yet verse five of chapter VI seems to stand in the way and to favour mice that lay waste the field: עכבריכם המשחיתים את הארץ.”

‎R4 *(p. 432)* “It is certain that female mice gnaw metals and other hard bodies which they cannot eat, namely at the time when they are pregnant. Nor do only mice act so then, but also the females of the other animals that belong to the family of the Glires. — It may be that the teeth of pregnant females are severely affected, and the animals ease this pain by gnawing or biting.” So Beckmann on <i>Aristotle, On Marvellous Things Heard</i> ch. 24, p. 57.

‎R5 *(p. 432)* Examples of fields laid waste by mice in the West Indies, from travel accounts, will be given by Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde], <i>Vermischte Sammlungen</i> fasc. 2, ch. 3, p. 45.

‎R6 *(p. 433)* Oedmann, loc. cit., thinks that both <i>glires</i> [rats] (<i>Ratzen</i>) and <i>sorices</i> [shrews] are comprised under the Hebrew name עכבר, led chiefly by the fact that some of those who have visited Eretz Yisrael report that the fields there are laid waste by both these kinds of animals. This argument would hardly satisfy others. I would think the Hebrew names שפן and עכבר differ thus: the former signifies the <i>Syrian sorex</i>, Arab. وَبْر [wabr] (cf. note 3 to the preceding Chapter), the other, עכבר, the <i>mus jaculus</i>, on the authority of the Arabic dialect, in which عَكْبَر [ʿakbar] and يربوع [yarbūʿ] are nearly synonyms; see Bochart above and p. 1015.
