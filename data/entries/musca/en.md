# Part 2, Book 4, chapter 9: On the fly (vol. 3, pp. 341–352)

### ‎[III, 341] [col. 496] Chapter IX.

### ‎<i>On the Fly.</i>

‎Either in Scripture itself or in the ancient versions of Scripture, seven kinds of fly are found. I. The <i>fly</i> properly so called, in <i>Hebrew</i> זבוב <i>zebub</i>, in <i>Greek</i> μυῖα [myia], Eccl 10:1: <i>Dead flies make the perfumer's ointment give off a stench.</i> Isa 7:18: <i>The Lord will hiss for the fly that is in the uttermost part of the rivers of Egypt.</i> II. The bee, in <i>Hebrew</i> דברה debora, in <i>Greek</i> μέλιττα [melitta], Deut 1:44: <i>They chased you, as bees do.</i> Iud. XIV, 8: <i>And behold, a swarm of bees in the body of the lion.</i> Ps 118:12: <i>They surrounded me like bees.</i> Isa 7:18: <i>The Lord will hiss for the fly, etc. and for the bee that is in the land</i> of Assyria. III. The wasp or hornet, in <i>Hebrew</i> צרעה tsira, in <i>Greek</i> σφὴξ [sphēx] or σφηκία [sphēkia], as the <i>Greek</i> translators have it, <i>Exod 23:28: I will send the hornet before you, which will drive out</i> the Hivite, <i>etc.</i> Deut 7:20: <i>Moreover the Lord your God will send the hornet among them.</i> Josh 24:12: <i>I sent the hornet before you, which drove them out from before you.</i> IV. Οἶστρος [oistros], the <i>oestrum</i> or <i>asilus</i> [gadfly]. Alluding to its name, the <i>Greeks</i> say at <i>Ezek 2:6</i> Διότι παροιστρήσουσιν [Dioti paroistrēsousin], <i>For, as if stung by the gadfly, they will rage</i>, and at <i>Hos 4:16</i> Ὡς δάμαλις παροιστρῶσα παρεῖστρησεν Ἰσραήλ [Hōs damalis paroistrōsa pareistrēsen Israēl]: <i>Like a heifer struck by the gadfly, Israel has raged.</i> V. The κυνόμυια [kynomyia], or <i>dog-fly</i>, is often named in the <i>Greek</i> version. Thus <i>Exod 8:21</i> Ἰδοὺ, ἐγὼ ἐξαποστέλλω ἐπὶ σὲ κυνόμυιαν [Idou, egō exapostellō epi se kynomyian], and throughout the rest of the chapter; and <i>Ps 77:45</i> Ἐξαπέ- [col. 497] στειλεν εἰς αὐτοὺς κυνόμυιαν [Exapesteilen eis autous kynomyian]; and <i>Ps 104:31</i> Εἶπε, καὶ ἦλθε κυνόμυια [Eipe, kai ēlthe kynomyia]. But Jerome, at the end of his <i>Letter</i> to <i>Sunnia</i> and <i>Fretela</i>, wants κοινόμυιαν [koinomyian] to be read. For this <i>Aquila</i> has παμμυῖαν [pammyian], because the <i>Hebrew</i> ערב <i>arob</i> is thought to be taken from <i>mixture</i>. Accordingly there are also some among the <i>Hebrews</i> who interpret it so. But of these below, in their proper place. VI. To the kinds of fly may be referred κώνωψ [kōnōps], the <i>gnat</i>, of which <i>Matt 23:24</i> Οἱ διυλίζοντες τὸν κώνωπα, τὴν δὲ κάμηλον καταπίνοντες [Hoi diylizontes ton kōnōpa, tēn de kamēlon katapinontes], <i>Straining out the gnat, but swallowing the camel.</i> VII. As also σκνίπες [sknipes] (or, as others read, σκνίφες [skniphes]), <i>Exod 8:16</i> Ἔσονται σκνίπες ἔν τε τοῖς ἀνθρώποις, καὶ ἐν τοῖς τετράποσι καὶ ἐν πάσῃ γῇ Αἰγύπτου [Esontai sknipes en te tois anthrōpois, kai en tois tetraposi kai en pasē gē Aigyptou]. And <i>Ps 104:31</i> Καὶ σκνίπες ἐν πᾶσι τοῖς ὁρίοις αὐτῶν [Kai sknipes en pasi tois horiois autōn]. The Vulgate translator too and Jerome have <i>sciniphes</i>. But Josephus and the more recent <i>Hebrews</i> more correctly render the <i>Hebrew</i> כנים as <i>lice</i>. Now we shall speak of the fly properly so called.

‎The <i>fly</i>, as in <i>Hebrew</i> זבוב <i>zebub</i>, so in Chaldee is called דבובא <i>debuba</i>, in <i>Syriac</i> ܕܒܒܐ [דבבא] <i>debaba</i>, and in <i>Arabic</i> ذباب [dhubāb] <i>dsebab</i>, with slight change. The origin of the name, unknown to the <i>Hebrews</i> and <i>Syrians</i>, is disclosed by the <i>Arabs</i>, among whom ذب [dhabba] <i>dsabba</i> means <i>to wander about with no fixed abode</i> and not <i>to stay in one place</i>. That this suits the fly, there is no one who does not know. [III, 342] Hence Lucian in the <i>Encomium of the Fly</i>: Νεοττιὰν δὲ, ἢ καλίαν, οὐχ᾽ ἑνὶ τόπῳ κατεστήσατο, ἀλλὰ πλάνητα τὴν πτῆσιν κατὰ τοὺς Σκύθας ἐπανῃρημένη, ὅπου ἂν τύχῃ ὑπὸ τῆς νυκτὸς καταληφθεῖσα, ἐκεῖ καὶ ἑστίαν καὶ εὐνὴν ποιεῖται [Neottian de, ē kalian, ouch' heni topō katestēsato, alla planēta tēn ptēsin kata tous Skythas epanērēmenē, hopou an tychē hypo tēs nyktos katalēphtheisa, ekei kai hestian kai eunēn poieitai]: <i>She does not establish a nest or a little nest in one place, but, borne up in wandering flight</i> after the manner of the Scythians, <i>wherever she happens to be overtaken by night, there she makes her home and her bed.</i> To the same point belongs the fact that one who is extremely fickle and inconstant is said in an <i>Arabic</i> proverb to be اطيَش من ذباب [aṭyash min dhubāb], <i>more inconstant than a fly</i> <sup>R1</sup>.

‎[III, 343] Some of the <i>Arabs</i> permit the eating of flies, because they are often generated from permitted things: for instance from the bean, which the <i>Arabs</i> say, when corrupted, is so changed into flies that nothing remains but the husk. But the <i>Hebrews</i> forbade flies, relying on the law found in <i>Deut 14:19. Every creeping thing that flies is unclean: it shall not be eaten.</i> Which <i>Jonathan</i> [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrases [paraphrazei] thus: וכל דיבבי וזיבורי וזיזי דטלופחי ופולי דפרשי מן אוכלא ופרחין הי כעופא מסאבין אינון לכון לא יתאכלון, <i>all flies, and wasps</i> (or <i>bees</i>) <i>and the worms of lentils and beans which, separated from the food, fly out like a bird, shall be unclean to you; they shall not be eaten.</i> They conclude the same from these words, <i>Lev 11:20. Every creeping thing that flies and goes on four feet shall be an abomination to you.</i> Where <i>Jonathan</i> has: וכל ריחשא דעופא דמהלך על ארבע זני דיבבי וזני אורעי וזני זובורי שיקצא הוא לכון ברם דיבשא דזיבורי יתאכל, <i>And every creeping thing that flies, four-footed, the species of flies, and the species of hornets, and the species of bees, shall be an abomination to you: yet the honey of bees shall be eaten.</i> And in the same place <i>R. Selomo</i> [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)]: הם הדקים הנמוכים הרחושים על הארץ כגון זבובין וצרעין ויתושין וחגבין, <i>These are the minute little animals which creep upon the earth, such as flies, and hornets, and wasps, and locusts.</i> If you object that flies, hornets, wasps and bees are wrongly placed by the <i>Hebrews</i> in the class of four-footed creeping things, since the fly kind is not τετράπους [tetrapous] but ἑξάπους [hexapous], I shall reply that the two front feet are not counted as feet, because they rather serve as hands. For they do not walk on them as on the other four; rather with them they wipe their eyes when covered with dust, take their food, and carry off whatever has to be lifted. <i>Lucian</i> on the fly: Ἑξάπους δὲ οὖσα, τοῖς μὲν τέσσαρσι βαδίζει μόνοις· τοῖς δὲ προσθίοις δυσὶ, καὶ ὅσα χερσὶ χρῆται. Ἴδοις ἂν οὖν αὐτὴν ἐπὶ τεττάρων βεβηκυῖαν, ἔχουσάν τι ἐν ταῖν χεροῖν μετέωρον ἐδώδιμον ἀνθρωπίνως πάνυ, καὶ καθ' ἡμᾶς [Hexapous de ousa, tois men tessarsi badizei monois; tois de prosthiois dysi, kai hosa chersi chrētai. Idois an oun autēn epi tettarōn bebēkuian, echousan ti en tain cheroin meteōron edōdimon anthrōpinōs pany, kai kath' hēmas]. <i>Though it is six-footed, it walks on four feet only, and uses the other two in front as hands: so you may see it stepping on four feet and lifting up something edible in its hands,</i> [col. 498] <i>entirely in human fashion, as we do.</i> And the <i>Philosopher</i> too, book 4 <i>On the Parts</i>, chapter 6: Τοὺς δὲ πόδας τοὺς προσθίους μείζους ἔνια τούτων ἔχει, ὅπως διὰ τὸ σκληρόφθαλμα εἶναι, ἐπειδὴ οὐκ ἀκριβῶς τὴν ὄψιν ἔχει, τὰ προσπίπτοντα τοῖς προσθίον ἀποκαθαίρωσι σκέλεσιν· ὅπερ καὶ φαίνονται ποιοῦσαι αἵ τε μυῖαι, καὶ τὰ μελιττώδη τῶν ζώων· ἀεὶ γὰρ χαρακίζουσι τοῖς προσθίοις σκέλεσι [Tous de podas tous prosthious meizous enia toutōn echei, hopōs dia to sklērophthalma einai, epeidē ouk akribōs tēn opsin echei, ta prospiptonta tois prosthion apokathairōsi skelesin; hoper kai phainontai poiousai hai te myiai, kai ta melittōdē tōn zōōn; aei gar charakizousi tois prosthiois skelesi]. <i>Some</i> (animals) <i>have longer front feet so that, since because of the hardness of their eyes they do not see precisely, they may wipe off with those front feet</i> whatever [III, 344] <i>falls into their eyes. This you may see flies doing too, and all the honey-making little animals. For they always lift up</i> (as on stakes) <i>anything with their front feet.</i> So it should be rendered. For χαρακίζειν [charakizein] here is μετεωρίζειν [meteōrizein], as it is in <i>Hesychius</i>; not <i>vallare</i> [to fence with stakes] and <i>protegere</i> [to protect], as <i>Gaza</i> would have it. <i>Pliny</i>, book 11, chapter 48: <i>In insects that have hard eyes the front feet are longer, so that they may wipe them with their feet from time to time, as we observe in flies.</i> Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān]: والذباب ليس له اجفان لصغر احداقه ومن شان الاجفان ان تصقل مراة الحدقه من الغبار فجعل الله له يدين يصقل بها مراة احداقه فلهذا تري الذباب ابدا يمسح بيديه عينيه [wa-l-dhubāb laysa lahu ajfān li-ṣighar aḥdāqihi wa-min shaʾn al-ajfān an taṣqul mirāt al-ḥadaqa min al-ghubār fa-jaʿala Allāh lahu yadayn yaṣqul bihā mirāt aḥdāqihi fa-li-hādhā tarī al-dhubāb abadan yamsaḥ bi-yadayhi ʿaynayhi] <i>Flies have no eyelids because of the smallness of the pupil; but since it is the property of eyelids to wipe dust from the mirror of the pupil, God gave them hands with which to do this. So you may see them constantly wiping their eyes with these hands.</i>

‎Nor was it without reason that the eating of creeping things, and among them of flies, was forbidden to the <i>Jews</i>: for the fly is a foul and stinking animal, born from uncleanness and gladly clinging to unclean things. And whether it comes to the tables of men or to the altars of the gods, it shamelessly plunders the feast and defiles everything by its touch. What of the fact that the tiny beast, flying into a perfumer's shop and dying by chance in an ointment box, taints all the ointment stored in it? Hence that saying of <i>Solomon</i>, Eccl 10:1: <i>One of the dead flies makes the ointment of the perfumer send forth a stench.</i> So <i>a little folly</i> makes a man <i>eminent in wisdom and honour</i> stink. Just as <i>Paul</i> says that <i>a little leaven corrupts the whole lump</i>, 1 Cor 5:6. For what is good is corrupted much more easily than what is bad is amended. Accordingly <i>Haggai</i>, chapter 2, verses 12 and 13, notes that an unclean thing is not consecrated by the touch of a holy thing; but, on the contrary, a holy thing is polluted by the touch of an unclean thing <sup>R2</sup>.

‎[III, 345] It is asked why the <i>Egyptians</i> are compared with flies in these words of <i>Isa 7:18. The Lord shall hiss for the fly that is in the uttermost part of the rivers</i> of Egypt; and various writers offer various answers. Jerome: <i>But he calls</i> the Egyptians <i>flies because of the filth of idolatry and because they are an unwarlike people.</i> <i>Basil</i> thinks the reference is to τὸ εὐτελὲς τῶν ἀντικειμένων [to euteles tōn antikeimenōn] [III, 346] δυνάμεων· μυῖα γάρ ἐστι ζῶον ἀπὸ κόπρου τὴν γένεσιν ἔχον, ὀχληρὸν τοῖς ἀνθρώποις γένος, καὶ μυσαρὸν ὂν, καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ὀχληρὰ, καὶ ὁ θάνατος βδελυκτὸς [dynameōn; myia gar esti zōon apo koprou tēn genesin echon, ochlēron tois anthrōpois genos, kai mysaron on, kai hē zōē ochlēra, kai ho thanatos bdelyktos], <i>the worthlessness of the forces that are opposed</i> to the Israelites: <i>for the fly is generated from dung, and is a kind hostile and abominable to men, whose life is troublesome and whose death is loathsome.</i> But <i>Cyril</i> asserts that the <i>Egyptians</i> are compared with flies διά τοι τὸ πλῆθος, καὶ τὸ μὴ λίαν εὔηχες τῆς φωνῆς [dia toi to plēthos, kai to mē lian euēches tēs phōnēs], <i>because of their multitude and their discordant and uncouth speech</i>, as though it were not so much speech as the buzzing of flies. The simplest course seems to be to refer it to multitude, with the Chaldee [col. 499] translator, whose paraphrase runs thus: יכלי יי לעם קטרי משרית גבריא דסגיאין כרבביא וייתינון מארעא דמצרים, <i>The Lord shall call to the people of the bands of the army of mighty men</i> (that is, in which forces of mighty men are joined together and sworn in league), <i>who are many as flies, and he shall bring them from the land</i> of Egypt. R. Selomo too understands here חילות רבות כזבובים, <i>an army numerous like flies.</i> And <i>Abarbanel</i> [=Isaac Abarbanel (1437–1508), commentary on the Bible]: המשיל אותם בזבוב לקלות מרוצתם ולרוב האספם, <i>He compares them with flies because of the swiftness of their course and because of the multitude of their gathering.</i> The former of these comes from <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim], the latter from the <i>Chaldee</i> and <i>Solomon</i>; I think the latter is to be preferred, because <i>Egypt</i> was once populous beyond belief. <i>Herodotus</i>, book 2, chapters 60 and 177, relates that seven hundred thousand people assembled at <i>Bubastis</i> for the festival of <i>Diana</i>, and that twenty thousand cities in <i>Egypt</i> were inhabited under <i>Amasis</i>. In the kingdom of <i>Ptolemy Philadelphus</i>, of which <i>Egypt</i> was the greatest part, <i>Theocritus</i>, <i>Idyll</i> 17, counts more than thirty-three thousand towns. Rightly, then, are the <i>Egyptians</i>, whose multitude is immense, compared with <i>flies</i>. Hence <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Truculentus</i>, Act 1, Scene 1:

‎<i>For now there are almost more pimps and harlots than there once were flies, when it is hottest.</i>

‎And <i>Homer</i>, <i>Iliad</i> 2, line 469, says that the forces of the <i>Greeks</i> stood in great numbers in the <i>Scamandrian</i> meadow,

‎Ἠΰτε μυιάων ἁδινάων ἔθνεα πολλὰ [Ēute myiaōn hadinaōn ethnea polla], <i>Like the very many tribes of thronging flies.</i>

‎From the fly <i>Beel-zebub</i>, the god of the <i>Ekronites</i>, takes his name, 2 Reg. I, 2. The LXX render μυῖαν Θεὸν Ἀκκαρὼν [myian Theon Akkarōn], <i>the fly, the god of Ekron.</i> Many of the ancients follow this. Thus <i>Josephus</i>, book 9, chapter 1, relates that <i>Ahaziah</i> sent πρὸς τὸν Ἀκκάρων Θεὸν μυῖαν, τοῦτο γὰρ ἦν ὄνομα τῷ θεῷ [pros ton Akkarōn Theon myian, touto gar ēn onoma tō theō], <i>to the god of</i> Ekron, <i>the fly, for that was the name of the god.</i> And in <i>Suidas</i>, under the word Ἠλίας [Ēlias], an ancient writer says of the same <i>Ahaziah</i>: Ἐχρήσατο μυίᾳ τῶν ἐν Ἀκκάρων εἰδώλῳ [Echrēsato myia tōn en Akkarōn eidōlō], <i>He consulted the fly, the idol of the</i> Ekronites. Alluding to this, <i>Nazianzen</i> in his fourth <i>Oration</i> says: [III, 347] Οὐδὲ ζητήσουσι μυῖαν Θεὸν Ἀκκαρὼν, ἢ εἴ τι ταύτης ἐστὶ γελοιότερον [Oude zētēsousi myian Theon Akkarōn, ē ei ti tautēs esti geloioteron]. <i>Nor will they any longer seek the fly, the god of</i> Ekron, <i>or any other deity more ridiculous than that.</i> The <i>Greek Fathers</i>, that is, think that this god was worshipped under the image of a fly. <i>Theodoret</i> on this passage: Τί μυσαρώτερον μυίας; ἀλλ᾽ ὅμως τὴν εἰκόνα ταύτης Θεὸν ἀνηγόρευσαν [Ti mysarōteron myias? all᾽ homōs tēn eikona tautēs Theon anēgoreusan]. <i>What is more loathsome than a fly? Yet they declared its image to be a god.</i> And a little later: Ἣν ζῶσαν ταῖς ῥιπίσιν ἐλαύνουσι, ταύτης τὸν τύπον Θεὸν προσηγόρευσαν [Hēn zōsan tais rhipisin elaunousi, tautēs ton typon Theon prosēgoreusan]· <i>The creature which, when alive, they drive away with fly-whisks, its figure they called a god.</i> Following them, <i>Philastrius</i>, in his Catalogue of the heretics who lived before <i>Christ</i>, lists the <i>Muscaccaronites</i>, <i>who</i>, he says, <i>worship a fly in the city of</i> Ekron. <i>Procopius</i> too ascribes to this same god the image of a fly. Yet he doubts whether it was the name of a demon, or of an old woman raised to the gods. This fancy is refuted by the <i>Hebrew</i> name בעל־זבוב, <i>Beel-zebub</i>, which is compounded of masculine words. I prefer therefore to follow the <i>Latins</i>, for whom <i>Beel-zebub</i> is not, in the nominative, <i>the god fly</i>, but, with a genitive, <i>the god</i> or <i>lord of the fly. Augustine</i>, Tractate 1 on <i>John</i>: BEEL-ZEBUB <i>is said to be interpreted</i> prince of flies. <i>Jerome</i> on <i>Ecclesiastes</i>, chapter 10: <i>From these flies the prince of demons is also called</i> BEEL-ZEBUB, <i>which is interpreted either</i> idol of flies, <i>or</i> man of flies, <i>or</i> having flies. And on <i>Matthew</i>, chapter 10: <i>Beel-zebub is the idol of</i> Ekron, <i>which in the book of</i> Kings <i>is called</i> the idol of the fly. The <i>Book of Names</i>, on <i>John</i>: BEEL-ZEBUB, <i>having flies, or man of flies.</i> Isidore, <i>Origins</i>, book 8, chapter 11: BEEL-ZEBUB [col. 500] <i>was the idol of</i> Ekron, <i>which is interpreted</i> man of flies. I would rather say <i>god</i>, or <i>lord</i>, or <i>prince of flies</i>, as in <i>Augustine</i>. For בעל <i>Baal</i>, when it is the name of an idol, does not mean <i>man</i>, but <i>god</i> or <i>lord</i>. Nor is the reason for the name obscure, since the <i>Greeks</i> too had a god Μυιώδης [Myiōdēs], or Μυίαγρος [Myiagros], or Ἀπόμυιος [Apomyios], whether he was <i>Jupiter</i>, or <i>Hercules</i>, or some other god, so called <i>from driving away flies</i> <sup>R3</sup>. On this matter the passages of the ancients are explicit. <i>Pliny</i> [III, 348], book 10, chapter 28: <i>The Eleans</i> (invoke) <i>the god</i> Myiagrus <i>when a multitude of flies brings pestilence</i> <sup>R4</sup>; <i>and they die at once, on the day on which a sacrifice has been offered to him.</i> Others read: <i>once a sacrifice has been offered to that god.</i> And book 29, chapter 6: <i>No animal</i> (the fly) <i>is thought less teachable or of less understanding; all the more wonderful is it that at the sacred</i> Olympic <i>games, when a bull has been sacrificed to the god whom they call</i> Myiodes, <i>clouds of them depart out of that territory. Pausanias</i> in the <i>Arcadica</i>, on the festival of the <i>Aliphereans</i>: Ἐν ταύτῃ τῇ πανηγύρει Μυιάγρῳ προθύουσιν, ἐπευχόμενοί τε κατὰ τῶν ἱερείων τῷ ἥρωϊ, καὶ ἐπικαλούμενοι τὸν Μυίαγρον. Καὶ σφισι ταῦτα δράσασιν, οὐδὲν ἔτι ἀνιαρόν εἰσιν αἱ μυῖαι [En tautē tē panēgyrei Myiagrō prothyousin, epeuchomenoi te kata tōn hiereiōn tō hērōi, kai epikaloumenoi ton Myiagron. Kai sphisi tauta drasasin, ouden eti aniaron eisin hai myiai]. <i>At that festival they first of all offer sacrifice to</i> Myiagrus; <i>and when they have prayed to the hero over the victims and invoked the name of</i> Myiagrus, <i>after doing this the flies no longer trouble them.</i> And in the <i>first book of the Eliaca</i>: Φασὶ δὲ Ἡρακλεῖ τῷ Ἀλκμήνης, θύοντι ἐν Ὀλυμπίᾳ, δι᾽ ὄχλου μάλιστα γενέσθαι τὰς μυίας. ἐξευρόντα οὖν αὐτὸν, ἢ καὶ ὑπ᾽ ἄλλου διδαχθέντα, Ἀπομυίῳ θῦσαι Διῒ, καὶ οὕτως ἀποτραπῆναι τὰς μυίας περὰν τοῦ Ἀλφειοῦ· Λέγονται δὲ κατὰ ταῦτα καὶ Ἠλεῖοι θύειν τῷ Ἀπομυίῳ Διῒ ἐξελαύνοντι τῆς Ἠλείας Ὀλυμπίας τὰς μυίας [Phasi de Hēraklei tō Alkmēnēs, thyonti en Olympia, di᾽ ochlou malista genesthai tas myias. exeuronta oun auton, ē kai hyp᾽ allou didachthenta, Apomyiō thysai Dii, kai houtōs apotrapēnai tas myias peran tou Alpheiou; Legontai de kata tauta kai Ēleioi thyein tō Apomyiō Dii exelaunonti tēs Ēleias Olympias tas myias]· <i>They also say that when</i> Hercules, <i>son of</i> Alcmene, <i>was sacrificing at</i> Olympia, <i>the flies caused him very great trouble. So, either by his own discovery or on another's advice, he sacrificed to</i> Jupiter Apomyius (as if to say <i>the driver-away of flies</i>), <i>and thus the flies were routed across the</i> Alpheus. <i>And accordingly the</i> Eleans <i>are said to sacrifice to</i> Jupiter Apomyius, <i>who drives the flies away from</i> Olympia <i>in Elis</i> <sup>R5</sup>. Of this same rite <i>Aelian</i> says much in the <i>History</i> [III, 349], book 5, chapter 17, and book 11, chapter 8. So does <i>Antiphanes the comic poet</i> in <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 1, chapter 5. <i>Clement</i> too in the <i>Protrepticus</i>: Ἀπομυίῳ Διῒ θύουσιν Ἠλεῖοι, Ῥωμαῖοι δὲ Ἀπομυίῳ Ἡρακλεῖ [Apomyiō Dii thyousin Ēleioi, Rhōmaioi de Apomyiō Hēraklei]. And <i>Solinus</i>, chapter 1, on the shrine of <i>Hercules at Rome</i> in the <i>Forum Boarium</i>: <i>By divine power it is entered neither by dogs nor by flies. For when he was giving a distribution of sacrificial meat to the worshippers, he is said to have invoked the god</i> Myiagrus. <i>Pliny</i> reports the same in part, book 10, chapter 29: <i>At Rome neither flies nor dogs enter the temple of</i> Hercules <i>in the</i> Forum Boarium. So too at <i>Paphos</i>, περὶ τὰ θυρώματα τὰ τῆς Ἀφροδίτης [peri ta thyrōmata ta tēs Aphroditēs], <i>around the doors of</i> Venus (unless we should read περὶ τὰ θυσιάσματα [peri ta thysiasmata], <i>around the sacrifices of</i> Venus), <i>there is no fly.</i> So <i>Apollonius</i> records from <i>Andron</i> in the <i>History of Marvels</i>, 8. And at <i>Leucas</i> the flies, sated with ox blood, depart from the shrine of <i>Apollo Actius</i>, as is found in <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i>, book 11, chapter 8. Nor are flies found on the mountain of <i>Crete</i>, <i>Carina</i>, nine miles in circuit, if we believe <i>Pliny</i>, book 21, chapter 14. And the <i>Jews</i> report that some such miracle also occurred in the temple of God at <i>Jerusalem</i>, in <i>Pirke Aboth</i>, chapter 5, section 5. The words are לא נראה זבוב בבית המטבחים, <i>No fly was seen in the house of slaughtering.</i> If this is true, the devil, as usual, will have been God's ape in this respect. And just as <i>Apollo</i> at <i>Athens</i> was called PARNOPIUS, and <i>Hercules</i> on Mount <i>Oeta</i> CORNOPION, because of locusts driven away; and by the <i>Erythraeans</i> Ἰποκτόνος [Ipoktonos], because of the ἶπες [ipes] he killed, that is, the <i>small worms by which vines are infested</i>: so whatever god was believed <i>to drive away flies</i>, as in <i>Greek</i> μυίαγρος [myiagros], μυιώδης [myiōdēs] and ἀπόμυιος [apomyios], will in <i>Hebrew</i> have been called BEEL-ZEBUB, that [col. 501] is, <i>lord of flies.</i> Such he was believed to have shown himself, by removing and driving them away from his sacrifices. I therefore think this was the true name of that idol. Nor do I agree with a great man <sup>R6</sup>, who thinks that he was called by his worshippers בעל זבחים <i>Beel-zebachim</i>, that is, <i>god of sacrifices</i>, which the <i>Israelites</i> in contempt twisted into בעלזבוב <i>Beel-zebub</i>, that is, <i>lord of the fly</i>. For, to say nothing of the fact that <i>zebub</i> and <i>zebachim</i> are words so unlike that a play on them seems hardly to have been sought; nor that it is likely that any god took his name from the victims offered, since it was common to all to be worshipped by the offering of victims: it is certain that <i>Ahaziah</i> was in earnest when he said, <i>Go, and inquire of</i> Beel-zebub, <i>the god of</i> Ekron, <i>whether I shall recover from this sickness.</i> For <i>Ahaziah</i> will not have mocked the god whom, in his sickness, he ordered to be consulted about his recovery. I therefore think the name <i>Beel-zebub</i> was in use among the <i>Philistines</i> as much as among the <i>Israelites</i> [III, 350] <sup>R7</sup>. But since in the <i>Gospel</i> we read <i>Beel-zebul</i>, with L at the end, as if derived from the word זבל <i>zebel</i>, which means <i>dung</i>, I should think the later <i>Jews</i> altered [III, 351] the name, so as to relegate <i>Beel-zebub</i> in this way among the <i>dung</i> gods <sup>R8</sup>. Yet this is not necessary, since in those languages a final B is sometimes changed into L. Thus the mouth of the <i>Arabian</i> gulf is commonly called <i>Babelmandel</i> for the <i>Arabic</i> باب المندب [Bāb al-Mandab] <i>Bab-elmandab.</i> And the <i>nablium</i>, in Spanish <i>rabel</i>, is in <i>Arabic</i> رباب [rabāb] <i>rebab.</i> In the same way <i>Beel-zebul</i> could be said for <i>Beel-zebub.</i>

‎A more difficult question is why, in <i>Matth. XII</i>, 24. and <i>Luc. XI</i>, 15., the <i>Pharisees</i> held that <i>Beel-zebub</i>, or <i>Beel-zebul</i>, was the prince of demons, whom other <i>Hebrews</i> call אשמודי, <i>Asmodaeus.</i> I have no answer to give, except that the <i>Philistine</i> city <i>Accaron</i> [Ekron] is also written <i>Acharon</i> by Jerome and others. Since this closely echoes <i>Acheron</i>, the god of Acharon could have been confused by the ignorant with the god of [III, 352] <i>Acheron</i>, that is, with <i>Pluto</i>, whom the theology of the gentiles set over the underworld. For it is well known that the later <i>Hebrews</i> derived not a few words of their own language from the <i>Greek</i>. An example is that Josephus holds that τὰ Ἱεροσόλυμα [ta Hierosolyma] is so called because of τὸ ἱερὸν [to hieron], that is, the temple built there. And the same author calls the torrent <i>Iabok</i> <i>Iobacchus</i>, as though it were named after the god <i>Iobacchus</i>. The same was the intention of those who first said, for the torrents <i>Kedron</i> and <i>Kison</i>, χειμάῤῥους τῶν κέδρων [cheimarrhous tōn kedrōn] and τῶν κισσῶν [tōn kissōn], as if they were so called from cedars and pitch-pines. And the city <i>Scitta</i> was called <i>Scythopolis</i>, as though from the <i>Scythians</i>. And AR of the <i>Moabites</i> became Ἀρεόπολις [Areopolis], as if the <i>city of Mars</i>. And the name <i>Taphnes</i> was changed into <i>Daphnes</i>, so that it might be believed to be called παρὰ τῶν ἐκεῖ πολλῶν φυομένων δαφνῶν [para tōn ekei pollōn phyomenōn daphnōn], <i>from the many laurels planted there.</i> Finally, for the same reason, from the <i>Philistines</i> were made Ἀλλόφυλοι [Allophyloi], from <i>Pibeseth</i> Βούβαστος [Boubastos], from the <i>Giblites</i> the <i>Byblians</i>, from the <i>Horites</i> the <i>City of Heroes</i>, from <i>Iabneel</i> Ἰάμνια [Iamnia], as if it had this name ὅτι Ἰάμνους ἐκάλουν τοὺς καθύγρους, καὶ τεθηλότας τόπους [hoti Iamnous ekaloun tous kathygrous, kai tethēlotas topous], <i>because they called moist and verdant places</i> ἰάμνους [iamnous]. So <i>Stephanus</i>. And <i>Ptolemais</i>, for עכו <i>Accho</i>, was called Ἄκη [Akē], as though from τοῦ ἀκεῖν [tou akein], because <i>Hercules</i> was healed there.

‎R1 *(p. 342)* Yet since the species of <i>flies</i> and <i>gnats</i> are innumerable, it is not clear which in particular ذباب [dhubāb], זבוב designates. Perhaps it is a general name, comprehending all those insects which the Latins include under the name of <i>muscae</i> [flies] and <i>culices</i> [gnats]: for example, the <i>gnat of Egypt</i>, with white joints, the size of the common gnat, of a dusky grey colour; the <i>fly of the Buphthalmum</i>, common in Egypt, smaller in size than the common bee, with four wings, short, club-shaped antennae stretched forward and bearing a bristle on the back, an oblong, convex, shining, dark green thorax, and six legs on the thorax. These two insects were carefully described by Hasselquist [=Fredrik Hasselquist, Iter Palaestinum (1757)], in his <i>Travels</i> p. 470. 471. German trans. Further, the <i>troublesome gnat</i> of Forsskål [=Peter Forsskål, Descriptiones animalium (1775)] (in <i>Descript. Animall.</i> p. 85), in size and appearance like the common piping gnat, hairy over the whole body and the legs, with bristle-like antennae and dusky wings, which is found in immense numbers at Rosetta, Cairo and Alexandria, is a nuisance to sleepers at night, and can scarcely be kept off except by a well-closed curtain. Finally, the <i>black fly</i> also seems to belong here, with bristle-like antennae and a thorax with yellow spots, which Forsskål briefly lists among the insects of Arabia observed by him, p. 24. Eugène Roger, in his <i>Description of Palestine</i>, written in French (<i>La Terre Sainte</i>, Paris, 1664, 4to), p. 84, mentions small <i>black flies</i> called <i>Bargaches</i> (the sound of the word is to be rendered with French pronunciation, for it is the Arabic بَرْغَشْ [barghash], the same as بَعُوضْ [baʿūḍ], <i>gnat</i>, or <i>a certain species of gnats</i>, in Golius [=Jacobus Golius, Lexicon Arabico-Latinum (1653)] p. 259), whose swarms, borne in like dense clouds, attacked the camp of the Arabs with such violence that men and animals were almost deprived of the ability to breathe. And Oedmann [=Samuel Ödmann, Vermischte Sammlungen aus der Naturkunde], in the <i>Vermischte Sammlungen</i>, fasc. 4, ch. 9, p. 81 ff., thought it credible that the same species of flies is denoted by the Hebrew זבוב, chiefly on account of the passage Isa 7:18, where the incredible multitude of Egyptians who would invade the territory of Israel is set forth under the image of flies called זבוב. But in fasc. 6, ch. 9, p. 79, he himself changed his opinion, understanding it of that species of <i>gadfly</i> or <i>oestrus</i> which Bruce [=James Bruce, Travels to discover the source of the Nile (1790)], who often saw it in Ethiopia, described at length in his <i>Travels</i> vol. 5, p. 190 ff. of Volkmann's German trans., where you will also find a picture of this insect, plate 39. The Ethiopians call it <i>Tsaltsalia.</i> This species of oestrus was unknown to Linnaeus. But Gmelin [=J. F. Gmelin, 13th edition of Linnaeus, Systema naturae (1788–93)], in the <i>Notes on Bruce's Travels</i> edited by Hassencamp, p. 31, thinks it should be designated by the name <i>oestrus fusco-pilosus, alis immaculatis</i>. Compare, however, what Blumenbach remarked on Bruce's description of this insect, vol. 5, p. 291 f. German trans. Oedmann, however, considers that oestrus to be the זבוב of the Hebrews, moved by no other reason than that he thinks it more suitable to the passage Esaiah VII, 14 than that black fly which Roger described loc. cit. [III, 343] How uncertain this argument is, everyone will see for himself. With the same right, and perhaps greater, he could have referred here some species of the flies which we mentioned above from Hasselquist and Forsskål. It will be safest to regard זבוב, like the Arabic ذباب [dhubāb] and the Syriac ܕܒܒܐ [דבבא]?, as a general name for various species of flies and gnats.

‎R2 *(p. 344)* There is, however, a remarkable disagreement among interpreters in explaining these words. To begin with the ancients, the Alexandrians render this verse as follows: Μυιαι θανατουσαι σαπριουσι σκευασιαν ἐλαιου ἡδύσματος· τίμιον ὀλίγον σοφιας ὑπὲρ δόξαν ἀφροσυνης μεγαλην [Myiai thanatousai sapriousi skeuasian elaiou hēdysmatos; timion oligon sophias hyper doxan aphrosynēs megalēn]. They omit the Hebrew יביע, which they seem not to have understood, just as they did not grasp the sense of the whole simile. Yet the Vulgate translator follows them, rendering: <i>"Dying flies spoil the sweetness of the ointment: wisdom and glory are more precious than a little and short-lived folly"</i>. Among the more recent interpreters ZIRKEL follows them too. He thinks that the first member of the verse (זבובי-רקח) should be referred to the preceding chapter, so that the words יקר — מעט form a new sentence, yielding the sense the Alexandrians express: <i>a little wisdom is more precious than the glory of folly</i>. Yet he could draw out this sense only by altering the Masoretic text [III, 345]: he places the word מעט before מחכמה, following the Alexandrians, who have ὀλίγον σοφίας [oligon sophias], and holds that this מעט מחכמה, which he himself formed, should be rendered word for word ὀλίγον ἀπὸ σοφίας [oligon apo sophias], which he declares to be the same in sense as ὀλίγον σοφίας [oligon sophias]. This interpretive device is hardly likely to win anyone else's approval. I. E. CHR. SCHMIDT also refers the first part of the verse to the preceding chapter, but renders the other member, יקר מחכמה מכבוד סכלות מעט, thus: <i>more excellent than wisdom and honour is folly and poverty</i> (<i>Besser, als Weisheit und Ansehn, ist — Thorheit und Dürftigkeit</i>). He says the sense of this paradox [παραδόξου (paradoxou)] is that it is better to be needy and to be thought a fool than to put on a show of wisdom in order to excite people's admiration. The chief objection to this interpretation is that מעט nowhere occurs with the meaning <i>poverty</i>. DOEDERLEIN departs from all of these. In his <i>Scholia in Libros poeticos V. T.</i> on this passage, with an interpretation that is certainly unusual and, as he himself says, bold, he renders our passage: <i>He dries up deadly fluxes who opens springs of the perfumer's oil</i>. He thinks the words יבאיש and יביע correspond to each other by way of antithesis: יביע is said of <i>water bursting forth from springs</i>, as in Prov 15:2 28., while באש agrees in meaning with יבש, <i>to be dry</i>, in the fourth conjugation <i>to make dry</i>; זבובים are <i>fluxes</i>, from זוב <i>to flow</i>, and זבובי מות are <i>deadly fluxes</i>, wounds and ulcers, as כלי מות are <i>deadly weapons</i> and עץ מות <i>a death-bringing tree</i>. Opposed to these is שמן רוקח, <i>the perfumer's oil</i>, wholesome and refreshing. Hence the sense results: as he who pours out the perfumer's oil, whose great power in wounds is praised, dries up deadly fluxes, haemorrhages and the like, so a wise man who uses gentle words soothes and removes many harms. The last words he renders: <i>glory through wisdom surpasses honour acquired by folly, even the least</i>. You see that in this interpretation too new and unusual meanings are attached to the words, and the whole course of the discourse is altogether twisted in an extraordinary way. It will therefore be simplest to interpret our words in the way Bochart did above, or to follow the Syriac translator, the most learned of the ancient interpreters, who renders the first verse thus: ܐܝܟ ܕܒ̈ܒܐ ܕܡܝܬܝܢ ܡܣܪܚܝܢ ܠܡܐܢܐ ܕܡܫܚܐ ܒܣܝܡܐ [איך דבבא דמיתין מסרחין למאנא דמשחא בסימא]. ܝܩܝܪܐ ܗܝ ܡܢ ܚܟܡܬܐ ܘܡܢ ܣܘܓܐܐ ܕܬܫܒܘܚܬܐ ܣܟܠܘܬܐ ܩܠܝܠܐ [יקירא הי מן חכמתא ומן סוגאא דתשבוחתא סכלותא קלילא] <i>As dead flies make a vessel of sweet ointment stink, a little folly outweighs wisdom and much glory.</i> He did, however, omit the verb יביע. But there will be no difficulty if you join the two, יבאיש יביע, so as to render the second, יביע, adverbially: <i>makes it stink, belching forth</i>, or ceaselessly, i.e. makes the stench bubble up or belch forth like gushing water. On this Hebraism see GLASSIUS, <i>Philol. S.</i> p. 272, ed. Dath. Similarly A. Schultens [=Albert Schultens, Liber Jobi (1737)], in <i>Commentar. ad Prov. [=Albert Schultens, Commentarius in Proverbia Salomonis (1748)]</i> XIII, 5. p. 122, renders our words: <i>A dead fly makes the perfumer's balsam ferment with a stench and bubble up.</i> By זבובי מות, however, I would understand <i>deadly flies</i>, or harmful ones, just as כלי מות, Ps 7:14 are <i>deadly weapons.</i>

‎R4 *(p. 348)* „How the flies brought pestilence, although Pliny does not record it, is nevertheless easy to conjecture. He thinks the Phoenician בעל זבוב is the same as the <i>Pluto</i> of the Greeks, moved chiefly by this reason: that at Matt 12:24 and Luke 11:15 Βεελζεβουλ [Beelzeboul] or Βεελζεβουβ [Beelzeboub] is called ἀρχων δαιμονιων [archōn daimoniōn], <i>prince of demons</i>, and that such was <i>Pluto</i>, since he is called ἀρχων δαιμονων [archōn daimonōn] by Porphyry in Eusebius, <i>Praeparat. Evangel.</i> book 4, ch. 23, μεγαλοδαιμων [megalodaimōn] by Clement of Alexandria in the <i>Protrept. ad Gentes</i> p. 31, and βασιλευς ἐνερων [basileus enerōn], <i>king of the underworld</i>, by the Greek and Roman poets. Next, that to Beelzebub, as <i>prince of demons</i>, an immense power over demons is ascribed in the Gospels, and the same power is ascribed by the gentiles to Pluto, the ruler of demons. And just as people were wont to commend their welfare to Pluto, as <i>lord of death</i>, under whose rule were the <i>Parcae</i>, to whose decision the span of mortals' life was entrusted, and to vow victims to him, so Ahaziah, 2 Reg. I, 2., sent to inquire of <i>Beelzebub</i> about his health and life. Finally, that the meaning of the Hebrew or Phoenician name בעל זבוב also fits: for זבוב, which properly denotes any harmful insects, is often used by the Talmudic writers for <i>demons</i>; for instance in מדרש ילקוט שמעני, <i>Midrasch Ialkut Simeoni</i> section 2, fol. 14, col. 1, and section 3, fol. 111, col. 3, זבובין are <i>evil demons</i>; hence בעל זבוב is rightly explained in the Gospels as ἀρχων δαιμονιων [archōn daimoniōn]. But this whole view of Mill [III, 351] seems more ingenious and contrived than true. — For Noldius, in the <i>Notes and Vindicc. ad Concordantt. Particularr.</i> note 636, p. 802, Tympe's edition, Βεελζεβουβ [Beelzeboub] in the N. T. is not the same as בעל זבוב, the god of the Ekronites, but the Syriac ܒܥܠܕܒܒܐ ܒܥܠ ܕܒܒܐ [בעלדבבא בעל דבבא], <i>adversary</i>, <i>enemy</i>, or διαβολος [diabolos], Σατανᾶς [Satanas]; this view also pleased Storr, who in his <i>Dissertat. de sensu historico</i> p. 38 holds that Jesus was called by this name by the Jews because they considered him an enemy of the ancestral religion, compare John 7:12 47. VIII, 48. IX, 28. The chief obstacle to this view is that the genuine reading in the N. T. is not Βεελζεβουβ [Beelzeboub], as the Vulgate, altered by Jerome, has together with the Old Syriac, but Βεελζεβουλ [Beelzeboul]; this reading is constant in all the Greek codices consulted so far, and is also rendered by the Greek fathers, e.g. Theophylact, and by the versions: the Old Latin before Jerome, the Saxon, Gothic, Coptic, the later Syriac, the Russian, and both Arabic ones, that in the Polyglots and that edited by Erpenius.

‎R8 *(p. 351)* That among the later Jews it was held almost a matter of religion to give idols and idol worship opprobrious names, among which זבול, <i>dung</i>, was a common one, Lightfoot has proved from the Talmudic writers in his <i>Horae Hebraicae on Matth.</i> XII, 25. But Michaelis, in the <i>Supplemm. ad Lexx. Hebrr.</i> vol. I, p. 205, and in the <i>Notes on the vernacular version of Matth.</i> X, 25, holds that Βεελζεβουλ [Beelzeboul], which is read in the N. T., is altogether different from בעל זבוב, <i>the god of the Ekronites</i>, and not formed from it, but is to be explained from judicial astrology. "This," he says, "divides the heavens into 12 <i>houses</i>, setting one of the planets over each house, which in the terminology of that art is called the <i>lord of the house</i>. זבול is a <i>dwelling</i>, but above all a dwelling <i>of the stars</i>; properly, then, בעל זבול is a <i>planet</i>, the <i>lord of a heavenly house</i>, as Venus is of the house of Virgo. And since these planets were held to be <i>gods</i> by the heathen, who closely joined astrology with the worship of vain deities, and since the Jews held the gods of the nations to be <i>evil demons</i>, what was more natural than to call the prince of the demons (perhaps not the one prince of all, but yet the chief of some order, and more powerful than the rest) the <i>lord of the heavenly house</i>? Now the words of Christ will also become wonderfully clear, and will have a certain allusion and point: εἰ τον οἰκοδεσπότην [ei ton oikodespotēn] (רב הבית, or, as it is in the Hebrew gospel, בעל הבית) Βεελζεβουλ [Beelzeboul] (בעל זבול) ἐκάλεσαν [ekalesan]." This opinion would not displease me either, provided Michaelis had proved by examples that the word זבול is used in astrology in the sense that he himself assigns to it.
