# Book 3, chapter 36: Kippod is the hedgehog and the porcupine (vol. 2, pp. 454–462)

### ‎[II, 454] [col. 1035] Chapter 36.

### ‎<i>It is proved that</i> קפוד <i>kippod is the hedgehog and the porcupine.</i>

‎Three elements contend over the animal קפוד <i>kippod</i>: some assign it to the earth, others to the water, others to the air along with the birds. In the first rank I place the <i>Greeks</i> and <i>Jerome</i>, for whom קפוד <i>kippod</i> is ἐχῖνος [echinos], the hedgehog, namely the land hedgehog, which lives in hedges in summer and in winter in tree trunks or in holes in the ground. Others infer that it is an aquatic animal from <i>Isa 14:23</i>, on <i>Babylon</i>: <i>I will make it a hereditary possession</i> of the kippod <i>and pools of water.</i> But the ranks of these are divided in two, or even in three. Some hold it to be the <i>tortoise</i>, some the <i>beaver</i>, some the <i>otter.</i> All rely on the etymology of the word. For in <i>Isa 38:12.</i> קפד means <i>to cut off</i>, where the weaver cuts off the warp when the whole web has been woven. And these animals have a remarkable power of cutting. With the beaver the matter is very well known. <i>It is an animal with a fearsome bite</i> (says <i>Pliny</i>, book 8, chapter 30); <i>it cuts down trees by rivers as if with iron.</i> But <i>Varro</i> too, in book 4 <i>On the Latin Language</i>, would have the otter [<i>lutra</i>] named from λύειν [lyein], <i>because it is said to undercut the roots of trees on the bank and to loosen them.</i> The same power in the tortoise is all the more remarkable because, <i>though it has no teeth</i>, it nevertheless <i>crushes everything with the edge of its beak.</i> That is, <i>the edges of the beak are sharp, the upper part closing over the lower in the manner of a box.</i> So <i>Pliny</i>, book 11, ch. 37, and book 9, ch. 10. Similar to this is what is read of the sea turtle in <i>Porta coeli</i>, fol. [col. 1036] 34a: ויש לה כח גדול בפיה ובשיניה ואם תקח אבן בפיה תשבור אותה בחוק גדול לחלקים קטנים, <i>It has enormous strength in its mouth and teeth. And if it takes a stone into its mouth, it crushes it with great force into the tiniest pieces.</i> Add to this that in <i>Arabic</i>, if both <i>Kimchis</i> are to be believed, the name of the <i>tortoise</i> is קנפאר, which does not differ much from the <i>Hebrew</i> קפוד. But those who hold the third opinion, namely that קפוד <i>kippod</i> is a species of bird, object that elsewhere <i>kippod</i> is named by <i>Isaiah</i> and <i>Zephaniah</i> together with birds. The passage of <i>Isaiah</i>, chapter 34, verse 11, reads thus: <i>The pelican and the</i> kippod <i>shall possess it.</i> And <i>Zephaniah</i>, chapter 2, verse 14, not very differently: <i>Both the pelican and the</i> kippod <i>shall lodge on its lintels.</i> Again, these are in doubt as to what kind of bird <i>kippod</i> is: some explain it as the <i>owl</i>; others as the <i>vulture</i>; others as the <i>blackbird</i>; others as the <i>bittern</i>, or <i>starry heron</i>; others as the <i>cypselus</i>, which is also the <i>apus</i>; others as the <i>plancus</i>, or <i>duck-eagle</i>[?]; and what not? And the most learned <i>Fuller</i>, in his <i>Miscellanea</i>, book 1, chapter 18, compares most of those interpretations with one another in such a way that he rejects none except the one which alone is true. I mean that most ancient one, by which קפוד <i>kippod</i> is explained as the hedgehog; and many things serve to confirm it.

‎First, the authority of the <i>Greeks</i> and of <i>Jerome</i>, who consistently explain it so. I do not think it safe to depart from them unless there are weighty reasons, and here [II, 455] I see none. 2. To the <i>Greeks</i> I add the <i>Hebrews</i>, among whose most learned men this opinion has long prevailed, that קפוד is the <i>hedgehog</i>. <i>R. Nathan</i> explains it with the <i>Spanish</i> word ריצ"ו <i>rizo</i>, or <i>erizo</i>; <i>Poniarius</i> with the Italian ריצ"יו <i>riccio</i>; <i>Rabbi Selomo</i> with the French הריצ"ון <i>herisson</i>; <i>Elias</i> with the German איג"יל <i>Igel</i>. And in the <i>Talmud</i> קפוד <i>kippod</i>, or קופד <i>kuphad</i>, which is the same in the <i>Chaldee</i> inflection, is often placed among land quadrupeds. Thus in <i>Berachoth</i>, chapter 9, folio 57b: כל מיני חיות יפות לחלום חוץ מן הפיל והקוף והקפוד, <i>All species of wild beasts are good for a dream</i> (that is, of good omen when seen in sleep), <i>except the elephant</i>, <i>the ape</i> and the <i>kippod</i>, that is, the <i>hedgehog</i>. For <i>R. Selomo</i> talks nonsense when he explains it as the <i>long-tailed monkey</i>. And in the tractate <i>Kelaim</i>, ch. 8, folio 32a: והקופד וחולדת הסניים, <i>And the</i> kuphad (<i>hedgehog</i>) <i>and the bramble weasel.</i> And in <i>Bava Kama</i>, chapter 7: אין מגדלין חתולים וקופדים, <i>They do not raise cats and</i> kuphadim (<i>hedgehogs</i>). And in <i>Sabbath</i>, chapter 5, folio 57b: ואין העגל יוצא בגימון ולא פרא בעור הקופד, <i>A calf may not go out</i> (on the <i>Sabbath</i>) <i>with a</i> gemon, <i>nor a cow with the skin of a</i> kuphad. I think the <i>Hebrew</i> גימון <i>gemon</i> is κημὸν [kēmon], a <i>muzzle</i>, and that the word is derived from it; although by this term they do not mean any muzzle whatever, but the one by which a calf is prevented from sucking. The <i>Aruch</i> defines it: לוח בפיו שלא יוכל לשתות החלב, <i>A board that is put in its mouth so that it cannot suck the milk.</i> In <i>Greek</i> you would call it πύσσαχον [pyssachon]. <i>Hesychius</i>: Πύσσαχος· ξύλον καμπύλον τοῖς μόσχοις περὶ τοὺς μυκτῆρας τιθέμενον, ὃ κωλύει θηλάζειν· [Pyssachos; xylon kampylon tois moschois peri tous myktēras tithemenon, ho kōlyei thēlazein] <i>A curved piece of wood placed around the nostrils of calves, by which they are prevented from sucking.</i> And the skin of a <i>kuphad</i> (that is, of a <i>hedgehog</i>) was tied to the cow's udder for the same purpose, that is, so that neither the calf nor any other animal might suck it. <i>Rabbi Selomo</i> on this passage: קופד שרץ הוא שנימיו חדים כמחט וקורין לו הריצון וקושרין עורו בדדי הפרה שלא יניקוה שרצים, Kuphad <i>is a creeping thing</i> (so the <i>Hebrews</i> call the smallest quadrupeds) <i>whose hairs are sharp like</i> [col. 1037] <i>needles</i>; <i>it is commonly called</i> herisson. <i>Its skin is tied to the cow's udder so that creeping things may not suck it.</i> For the milk had to be guarded not from the calf alone, but also from serpents, among which <i>boas are fed at first on the juice of cow's milk</i>, <i>whence they took their name.</i> So Pliny, book 8, chapter 14. And Solinus, who follows him: <i>It</i> (the boa) <i>lies in wait for herds of cattle, and fastens itself to the udder of whichever cow is most abundant in milk, and fattened by continual sucking</i>, etc. 4. Finally, in <i>Arabic</i> قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i>, plural قنافذ [qanāfidh] <i>kanaphido</i>, is the <i>hedgehog</i>. Barbarous translators write <i>caused</i>, <i>ceufud</i>, <i>ceufund</i>, etc. The matter is too well known to need proof. I will only advise the reader that the name قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> includes, besides the <i>hedgehog</i>, also the <i>porcupine</i>. This is clear from <i>Avicenna</i>, who says that among the species of <i>kunphud</i> one is the <i>mountain</i> kind, <i>having spines that can be shot</i>, whose proper name is دلدل [duldul] <i>duldul</i>, that is, the <i>porcupine</i>. Hence in Damir [=al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān] the female hedgehog is called أم دلدل [umm duldul] <i>mother</i> τοῦ [tou] <i>duldul</i>, that is, of the <i>porcupine</i>. And <i>duldul</i>, or <i>porcupine</i>, is defined in the same place [II, 456] as عظيم القنافد [ʿaẓīm al-qanāfid], <i>the larger hedgehog</i>. And according to <i>Algiahid</i>: الفرق بين الدلدل والقنافد كالفرق بين البقر والجوامس والبخاتي من الابل العراب [al-farq bayna al-duldul wa-al-qanāfid ka-al-farq bayna al-baqar wa-al-jawāmis wa-al-bakhātī min al-ibil al-ʿirāb] Duldul <i>and</i> kanaphido (that is, the <i>porcupine</i> and <i>hedgehogs</i> properly so called) <i>differ no otherwise than oxen from buffaloes, and</i> Bactrian <i>camels from</i> Arabian ones. Finally <i>Elmacinus</i>, in the <i>Saracen History</i>, book 1, chapter 5, seems to have meant by القنفد [al-qunfud] <i>alkunphud</i> the <i>porcupine</i> rather than the <i>hedgehog</i>, when he writes of <i>Aisha</i>'s camel: صار كالقنفد من السهام [ṣāra ka-al-qunfud min al-sihām] <i>It became like an</i> alkunphud <i>from the darts</i>, that is, from the multitude of darts with which it was pierced. But in this the <i>Arabs</i> had Pliny and <i>Oppian</i> to follow: the former, in book 8, chapter 35, expressly places porcupines <i>in the hedgehog kind</i>; the latter, in book 2 of the <i>Cynegetica</i>, line 603, making two kinds of land hedgehogs, some small and unwarlike, others μεγέθει πολὺ μείζονας [megethei poly meizonas], <i>far larger</i>, and bristling all round — ἀρειοτέρῃσιν ἀκωκαῖς [areioterēsin akōkais], <i>with stronger points</i>, seems to understand <i>porcupines</i> by those larger hedgehogs. And for <i>Suidas</i> too ἡ ὕστριξ [hē hystrix], the <i>porcupine</i>, is ὁ ἀκανθόχοιρος, ὁ χερσαῖος ἐχῖνος [ho akanthochoiros, ho chersaios echinos], <i>the spiny pig, the land hedgehog</i>.

‎Now, lest anyone object, on the authority of both <i>Kimchis</i>, that the <i>Arabs'</i> قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> is the <i>tortoise</i>, I say that they are gravely mistaken on this point. For in <i>Arabic</i> the <i>tortoise</i> is not called <i>kunphud</i> but سلخفاة [sulaḥfāh] <i>sulachpha</i>, or سلخفية [sulaḥfiya] <i>sulachphia</i>, as if you were to say <i>hiding in a shell</i> or <i>husk</i>: for خفا [khafā] <i>chapha</i> means <i>to hide</i>, and سلخ [salkh] <i>silch</i>, or <i>sulch</i>, is φολὶς [pholis], a <i>covering</i>, properly of a <i>serpent</i>, but also of a <i>tortoise</i>; just as γῆρας [gēras], a word most use for the <i>leberis</i>, or <i>slough of a serpent</i>, but some also for χελωνίω [chelōniō], as <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 2, ch. 37, and <i>Suidas</i> under Φολίδωτον [Pholidōton]. But that قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> is the <i>hedgehog</i> no one will deny who has read <i>Damiri</i>, where قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> is surnamed ابو الشوك [abū al-shawk] <i>abussuchi</i>, that is, <i>father of thorns</i>; and انقد [anqad] <i>ankado</i>, that is, <i>the stripper of bark</i>; and ابو سفين [abū safīn] <i>abusaphin</i>, that is, <i>the father who scrapes</i> or <i>polishes</i>, because garments are polished with its [col. 1038] skin; and العساعس [al-ʿasāʿis] <i>alasaiso</i>, because it roams at night. For that is properly عس [ʿassa] <i>assa</i>, as also سري [sarā] <i>sara</i>; hence the proverb in use among them, اسري من قنفذ [asrā min qunfudh], <i>More night-roaming than the</i> kunphud. In the same place you may find many things pertaining to its description, among which these are remarkable: القنفذ ان جاع صعد الكرم منكسا فيقطع العناقيد ويرمي بها ثم ينزل فياكل منها ما اطاق واذا كان له فراخ تمرغ في العناقيد لتشتبك في شوكه ويذهب به الي فراخه وهو لا يظهر الا ليلا وهو مولع باكل الافاع [II, 457] ولا يتالم لها وانا لذعته الحية اكل الصعتر البري فيبري وله خمسة اسنان في فيه والبرية منها تسفد قايمة وظهر الذكر لاصق بظهر الانثي [al-qunfudh in jāʿa ṣaʿida al-karm munakkasan fa-yaqṭaʿu al-ʿanāqīd wa-yarmī bihā thumma yanzilu fa-yaʾkulu minhā mā aṭāqa wa-idhā kāna lahu firākh tamarragha fī al-ʿanāqīd li-tashtabika fī shawkihi wa-yadhhabu bihi ilā firākhihi wa-huwa lā yaẓharu illā laylan wa-huwa mūlaʿ bi-akl al-afāʿ wa-lā yataʾallamu lahā wa-anā ladhaʿathu al-ḥayya akala al-ṣaʿtar al-barrī fa-yabraʾ wa-lahu khamsat asnān fī fīhi wa-al-barriyya minhā tusfadu qāʾima wa-ẓahr al-dhakar lāṣiq bi-ẓahr al-unthā] Alkunphud, <i>when it is hungry, climbs a vine upside down and plucks off the clusters, which it throws down. Then, coming down from there, it eats of them as much as it can. If it has young, it rolls about in the clusters so that they become entangled in its spines, and with them it hurries to its young. It does not appear except at night. It has a wonderful appetite for eating vipers, and is not harmed by them. When bitten by a serpent, it is healed by eating wild marjoram. It has five teeth in its mouth. The land</i> kunphud <i>copulates upright, so that the back of the male clings to the back of the female.</i> Everyone sees that these things pertain to the hedgehog, although among them there are some that you would seek in vain in the <i>Greeks</i>. Now, if we consult the <i>physicians</i>, say <i>Avicenna</i>, <i>Ibn al-Baitar</i> and that same <i>Damiri</i> mentioned above, we shall find not only that they attribute to the <i>kunphud</i> the same virtues that the <i>Greek</i> writers attribute to the ἐχίνῳ [echinō], but also that, in rendering the passages of <i>Dioscorides</i> and <i>Galen</i> on the land and sea hedgehog, they consistently call the former القنفذ البري [al-qunfudh al-barrī] <i>alkunphud albarri</i>, or <i>land</i> hedgehog, the latter القنفذ البحري [al-qunfudh al-baḥrī] <i>alkunphud albahari</i>, or <i>sea</i> hedgehog. Not to mention that the translators of the <i>Prophets</i>, who made the <i>Arabic</i> version from the <i>Greek</i>, everywhere rendered the <i>Greek</i> ἐχίνους [echinous] by القنافذ [al-qanāfidh] <i>alkanaphido</i>, which is the plural of <i>kunphud</i>. For examples see <i>Isa 14:23</i> and <i>XXXIV, 11.</i> and <i>Zeph 2:14.</i> Accordingly, among the <i>Hebrews</i>, <i>R. Nathan</i>, far more learned in <i>Arabic</i> than <i>Kimchi</i>, readily acknowledges that قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> is the <i>hedgehog</i>. Further, that the <i>Hebrews'</i> קפוד <i>kippod</i>, the <i>Chaldeans'</i> קופד <i>kuphad</i>, and the <i>Arabs'</i> قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> are the same animal, anyone who has some taste of these languages will at once recognize. The long vowel in קופד <i>kuphad</i> makes up for the lack of the <i>Daghesh</i> point found in קפוד <i>kippod</i>. And that very <i>Daghesh</i> is replaced by the letter <i>Nun</i>, which shows itself in the <i>Arabic</i> قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i>, as also in [col. 1039] the <i>Ethiopic</i> ቀንፈዝ፡ <i>konphoz</i> of the same meaning. Thus חטה <i>chitta</i>, that is, <i>wheat</i>, stands for חנטה <i>chinta</i>, as the <i>Arabs</i> write it. The wood of which the ark of the testimony was made is called שטה <i>sitta</i>, for שנטה <i>sinta</i>; as in <i>Arabic</i> سنط [sanṭ] <i>sent</i>. The <i>spider</i> is עכביש <i>accabis</i>, for <i>ancabis</i>; as in <i>Arabic</i> عنكبوث [ʿankabūth] <i>ancabuths</i>. And עז <i>ez</i>, that is, <i>goat</i>, plural עזים <i>izzim</i>, is in <i>Arabic</i> عنز [ʿanz] <i>enz</i>. This is to be noted here against those who trace the word קפוד <i>kippod</i> to the root קפד <i>kaphad</i>, so that it would signify an animal endowed with some peculiar power of <i>cutting</i>. But the true root is not קפד <i>kaphad</i>, but the unused קנפד <i>kanphad</i>; unless someone thinks that the word is a compound, with the gutturals elided (as happens), of קנה <i>kana</i>, which is a <i>dart</i>, and אפד <i>aphad</i>, which is <i>to clothe</i>, because this animal is clothed and fenced about with spines and prickles as with darts. Or, if you prefer that the <i>Nun</i> in قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>kunphud</i> is epenthetic, even the root קפד <i>kaphad</i> has certain meanings which [II, 458] you could refer here. As when קפדן <i>kaphdan</i> is used of a harsh and irascible man. In this sense, in <i>Sanhedrin</i>, chapter 11, the prophet <i>Elijah</i> is said to have been קפדן. And in the tractate <i>On the Sabbath</i>, ch. 2, fol. 30b: לעולם יהא אדם ענותן כהלל ואל יהא קפדן כשמאי, <i>Let a man always be gentle like</i> Hillel, <i>and not</i> kaphdan (or <i>irascible</i>) <i>like</i> Shammai. Because, as the <i>Pomarius</i> says, a harsh and irascible man is כמו הקפוד עם קוצים, <i>like a hedgehog with spines.</i> So these <i>Greek</i> proverbs, Ἐχῖνου τραχύτερος [Echinou trachyteros] and Ἅπας ἐχῖνος τραχὺς [Hapas echinos trachys], signify a morose and intractable man. To the same point belongs the fact that קפד <i>kaphad</i>, or הקפיד <i>hikphid</i>, means <i>to be attentive</i> and <i>anxious</i>, and הקפדה <i>hakkephada</i> means <i>attention</i> and <i>anxiety</i>; because, as is in the <i>Pomarius</i>, <i>when the hedgehog</i> קורץ כל גופו, <i>covers its whole body with spines, its face hiding between its feet, and raises its prickles, with which it defends itself as with a shield and wounds those who wish to lay violent hands on it,</i> פעולת הזאת היא השגחה והרגשה נמרצת, <i>this action is one of providence and the keenest attention.</i> Moreover, ܩܦܕ [קפד] for the <i>Syrians</i> is <i>to shudder</i>, or <i>to bristle</i>, as when the hairs stand on end from fear, as in hedgehogs. So in the <i>Syriac Psalm</i> 119, verse 120: ܩܦܕ ܒܣܪܝ ܡܢ ܕܚܠܬܟ [קפד בסרי מן דחלתך], <i>My flesh shuddered for fear of you</i>, that is, <i>the hairs of my flesh stood on end</i>, as is found in <i>Iob. IV</i>, 15. In French you would say <i>herisser</i> by the same metaphor. For <i>herisser</i>, that is, ὀρθοτριχεῖν [orthotrichein], <i>to bristle</i>, and <i>herisson</i> are cognates.

‎Now, to reply to the objections: <i>Fuller</i> denies that there is anything in the nature of the hedgehog that suits the <i>kippod.</i> This I cannot sufficiently wonder at, since <i>Isaiah</i> and <i>Zephaniah</i> always describe the <i>kippod</i> as a solitary animal and φιλέρημον [phileremon], dwelling in the desolate cities of <i>Babylon</i>, <i>Bozrah</i> and <i>Nineveh</i>. That the hedgehog is such, there is no one who does not know: ζῶον χῆρον, ἤτοι ἔρημον [zōon chēron, ētoi erēmon], an animal which its spines, which no one touches with impunity, keep from all society with other animals. Hence it came about that in <i>Greek</i> it was called χὴρ [chēr]. <i>Hesychius</i>: χήρ· ἐχῖνος [chēr: echinos], namely from χηρώσις [chērōsis], that is, ἐρημώσις [erēmōsis]. Hence also the <i>Latin</i> name; not <i>ab haerendo</i> [from clinging] or <i>horrendo</i> [from bristling], as some would have it. For just as <i>hir</i> for <i>vola manus</i> [the palm of the hand] in <i>Probus</i> and <i>Charisius</i> was made from the <i>Greek</i> χεὶρ [cheir], so <i>her</i> for <i>hericius</i> [hedgehog] comes entirely from the <i>Greek</i> χήρ [chēr]. The animal which a later age, as the word grew longer, called <i>herix</i>, <i>hericius</i>, <i>herinacius</i>, the ancients called <i>her</i>. <i>Nemesianus</i> in the <i>Cynegetica:</i>

‎<i>And the hedgehog wrapped up in the fold of its spiny body.</i>

‎And long before, <i>Plautus</i> in the <i>Captivi</i>, Act 1, as emended by the distinguished <i>Salmasius</i>:

‎<i>Go now, hunt a hare: now you hold a hedgehog.</i>

‎Or, if by the name <i>kippod</i> in the <i>Prophets</i> we understand the <i>porcupine</i> (which [?] [col. 1040] we have proved from the ancients to be included in the same genus as the hedgehog), the matter will seem[?]

‎[II, 459] all the more fitted to inspire horror, if the <i>Prophets</i> threaten that populous cities, such as <i>Babylon</i> among the <i>Chaldeans</i>, <i>Nineveh</i> among the <i>Assyrians</i>, and <i>Bozrah</i> among the <i>Idumaeans</i>, will pass as an inheritance to porcupines. For the porcupine is both larger and fiercer than the hedgehog, and, because of the missile spines which it hurls at dogs and hunters with a sure and well-aimed stroke, far more terrible <sup>R1</sup>. Accordingly the excellent <i>Poet</i>, after vividly describing the porcupine bristling with hairs sharp like arrows, teaches in these words how far the porcupine surpasses the echinus, that is, the hedgehog:

‎<i>But this array does not stay fixed, in the manner of the woodland hedgehog.</i> <i>It fights on the offensive with frequent volleys,</i> <i>and guards its limbs from afar, and, whirled through the air,</i> <i>its native missile flies out from its shaken back.</i>

‎And whereas <i>Ion</i> in the <i>Phoenix</i> <sup>a)</sup> mocks the οἰζυρὰς τέχνας [oizyras technas], the <i>wretched arts</i>, of the hedgehog, which, when attacked by other beasts,

‎Στρόβιλον ἀμφ' ἄκανθαν εἰλίξας δέμας, Κεῖται δακεῖν τε καὶ θιγεῖν ἀμήχανος· [Strobilon amph' akanthan eilixas demas, keitai dakein te kai thigein amēchanos] <i>Keeping its body under a dense ball of thorns,</i> <i>it lies there, unable to bite or to touch in return:</i>

‎the porcupine's courage and skill in fighting so carried the most illustrious <i>Poet</i> away in admiration that it drew these verses from him:

‎<i>Sometimes, fleeing in the manner of the</i> Parthians, <i>it wounds its pursuer: sometimes, as if pitching camp in order,</i> <i>it lashes out its terrifying point in a dense wave,</i> <i>and roughens its shoulders with kindred shafts.</i> <i>The whole body of the beast does battle, and its back, shaken,</i> <i>resounds with a harsh crash; you would think columns roused by the goad of trumpets</i> <i>were clashing with standards joined.</i> <i>So great a din rages in so small a space. To its arms is added</i> <i>cunning, and a fear sparing of itself, and an anger never</i> <i>prodigal of its darts, cautiously content to threaten,</i> <i>and spending its shots only to save its life.</i> <i>Error is absent; skill directs a sure stroke,</i> <i>the distance never deceiving its measure; and the mute skin</i> <i>keeps its course and guides the efforts of a practised eye.</i>

‎And a little later,

‎<i>Behold, a small beast is armed with its own weapons,</i> <i>and seeks no outside help; it carries everything with it;</i> [II, 460] <i>it is its own quiver, its own javelin, its own bow.</i> <i>One animal possesses all the arts of war.</i>

‎Finally he concludes that we learned the art of shooting from this beast. And of the same the emperor <i>Hadrian</i>:

‎<i>With missile spines it kept dogs and men</i> <i>far off, and put them all to flight.</i>

‎And <i>Phile</i>, ch. 59, <i>On Animals</i>:

‎Ὕστριξ δὲ τὰς ἄνωθεν ἐκπέμπει τρίχας, Ἃς ἡ φύσις γ᾽ ἔθηξε καὶ πρὸ τῆς μάχης. Φρίξασα γὰρ τὰ νῶτα βάλλει τοὺς πέλους Οἷον ἀπὸ νευρᾶς εὐτυχῶν <sup>b)</sup> τις τοξότης. [Hystrix de tas anōthen ekpempei trichas, has hē physis g' ethēxe kai pro tēs machēs. Phrixasa gar ta nōta ballei tous pelous hoion apo neuras eutychōn tis toxotēs.] <i>As the rough porcupine hurls its hairs like darts,</i> <i>which shrewd nature has sharpened for battle,</i> <i>for when it draws in its back, as with a bowstring</i> <i>drawn taut, it hurls darts at its enemies from afar.</i>

‎And <i>Oppian</i>, book 3 of the <i>Cynegetica</i>, v. 398:

‎— — — Θοὰς δ᾽ ἔφριξεν ἐθείρας Καί τ᾽ ὀπίσω νώτοισιν ἀκαχμένον ὠκυπέτῃσιν Ἰθὺς ἀκοντίζει μαλερὸν βέλος. — — [Thoas d' ephrixen etheiras kai t' opisō nōtoisin akachmenon ōkypetēisin ithys akontizei maleron belos.]

‎<i>It raises its sharp spines, and shoots straight backwards the deadly dart fixed on its swift-flying back.</i> And a little later,

‎Δηθάκις ἔκτεινεν κύνα κάρχαρον, [Dēthakis ekteinen kyna karcharon,]

‎<i>it has often killed a dog rough with teeth.</i> Accordingly hunters, since they do not dare to set dogs on the porcupine, take it by a certain stratagem, which the same <i>Oppian</i> professed he would describe in what follows, but, prevented by death, he did not keep his promise. Nor is there reason for anyone to think that these things are said on <i>poetic</i> authority, since all who have written on the nature of animals report the same. Thus the <i>Philosopher</i>, <i>History</i>, book 9, [col. 1041] chapter 39, mentions certain animals τὰ βάλλοντα ταῖς θριξὶν, οἷον αἱ ὕστριχες [ta ballonta tais thrixin, hoion hai hystriches], <i>which shoot their hairs, like porcupines.</i> And <i>Pliny</i>, book 8, chapter 35: <i>The porcupine has longer quills, and, when it stretches its skin, they can be shot. It pierces the mouths of dogs pressing on it, and shoots a little farther off.</i> Solinus also: <i>Bristling on its back with spines</i> (the porcupine), <i>which, often loosened, it discharges by voluntary shooting, so as to wound attacking dogs with continual showers of quills.</i> And <i>Aelian</i>, book 1, chapter 31: Τὰς ἄνωθεν τρίχας οἱονεὶ βέλη ἐκπέμπει, καὶ εὐστόχως βάλλει πολλάκις, τὰ νῶτα φρίξασα, καὶ ἐκεῖναί γε πηδῶσιν. ὥσπερ οὖν ἔκ τινος ἀφειμέναι νευρᾶς· [Tas anōthen trichas hoionei belē ekpempei, kai eustochōs ballei pollakis, ta nōta phrixasa, kai ekeinai ge pēdōsin. hōsper oun ek tinos apheimenai neuras] <i>It sends forth its upper hairs like arrows, and often aims them straight as at a target, contracting its back as it bristles. And they leap out as if shot from a bowstring.</i> The <i>Scholiast on Aristophanes</i> on the <i>Peace</i>, and from him <i>Suidas</i>: Ἔστι δὲ ὕστριξ [II, 461] θηρίον τρίχας ἔχον ὑὸς, ἂν ἐν τῷ διώκεσθαι ἐπακοντίζει κατὰ τῶν διωκόντων· [Esti de hystrix thērion trichas echon hyos, an en tō diōkesthai epakontizei kata tōn diōkontōn] <i>The porcupine is a wild beast bristling with swine's bristles</i> (whence its name), <i>which, when someone pursues it, it shoots out at its pursuers.</i> <i>Porta coeli</i> p. 19 d: גם יש מין מחוירי הבר שיש להם שערות גסים כמו חצים וזה לסבת גסות העור, <i>There is a species of wild swine whose hairs are thick like arrows, namely because of the thickness and hardness of the skin.</i> <i>Avicenna</i>, book 2 of the <i>Canon</i>, in the chapter قنفذ [qunfudh] <i>Kunphud,</i> والجبلي هو الدلدل ذو الشوك السهمي [wa-l-jabalī huwa al-duldul dhū al-shawk al-sahmī] <i>And the mountain one</i> (kunphud) <i>is the</i> duldul (<i>porcupine</i>), <i>which has arrow-like spines,</i> that is, missiles. <i>Damir</i> in the chapter دلدل [duldul] <i>Duldul:</i> واذا راي ما يكرهه انقبض فيخرج منه شوك كالمسال يخرج من اصابه والشوك الذي علي ظهره بحو الذراع [wa-idhā rāy mā yakrahuhu inqabaḍa fa-yakhruju minhu shawk ka-l-masāl yakhruju min aṣābahu wa-l-shawk alladhī ʿalī ẓahrihi bḥw al-dhirāʿ] <i>And when it sees anything hostile, it contracts itself. And spines like needles come out of it, by which those pressing on it are wounded. And the spines that are on its back are about a cubit long.</i> <i>Marco Polo</i>, book 1, chapter 33, on <i>Scassem</i>, a city of <i>Tartary</i>: <i>In that region there are many spiny swine</i> (porcupines), <i>which, when they are caught, often injure men and dogs with their spines. For the dogs, set on the swine by the hunters, so provoke and stir up those beasts that, running together</i> (contracting) <i>their backs, on which the spines rest, they shake them violently, and with a certain violence discharge the very quills and hurl them at the nearer men and dogs.</i> Gesner [=Conrad Gesner, Historia animalium]: <i>It strikes whomever it wishes with a sure and aimed blow, with a wound sometimes almost incurable, being narrow and deep, dangerous indeed if a nerve is pierced.</i> So that <i>Oppian</i> not without reason sings,

‎Ὑστρίγγων οὔπω τι πέλει κατὰ δάσκιον ὕλην Ῥίγιον εἰσιδέειν, ὅτ' ἀργαλεώτερον ἄλλο, [Hystringōn oupō ti pelei kata daskion hylēn Rhigion eisideein, hot' argaleōteron allo]

‎Which <i>Stephanus</i> condensed thus in his <i>Notes</i> on <i>Callimachus</i>:

‎<i>Nothing in the shady woods is more dreadful to behold than the porcupine.</i>

‎That the <i>Prophets</i> spoke of this animal may be the more probable because I learn from the <i>Arabs</i> something hitherto unknown, that the porcupine is an animal common in <i>Syria</i>. <i>Damir</i>, in the chapter دلدل [duldul] <i>Duldul</i>, or <i>On the Porcupine</i>: هو كثير ببلاد الشام والعراق وبلاد المغارب [huwa kathīr bi-bilād al-Shām wa-l-ʿIrāq wa-bilād al-Maghārib] <i>It abounds in the region of</i> Syria <i>and</i> Iraq (<i>Mesopotamia</i> and <i>Babylonia</i>), <i>and</i> [col. 1042] <i>in the region of Megareb</i>, that is, <i>western Africa</i>. He teaches the same about the hedgehog, thus: وهو صنفان قنفد يكون بارض مصر قدر الفار وقنفد يكون بارض الشام وعراق في قدر الكلب القلطي والفرق بينهما كالفرق بين الفار والجرذ [wa-huwa ṣinfān qunfud yakūn bi-arḍ Miṣr qadr al-fār wa-qunfud yakūn bi-arḍ al-Shām wa-ʿIrāq fī qadr al-kalb al-qalṭī wa-l-farq baynahumā ka-l-farq bayn al-fār wa-l-jurdh] <i>The hedgehog is of two species. One is in the land of</i> Egypt, <i>of the size of a shrew; the other in the lands of</i> Syria <i>and</i> Iraq (<i>Mesopotamia and Babylonia</i>), <i>of the size of a</i> Maltese <i>dog, so that they differ as much as the shrew and the mouse differ.</i> [II, 462] Those who infer that the kippod <i>kippod</i> קפוד is an aquatic animal from these words of <i>Isaiah</i> about <i>Babylon</i>, <i>I will make it a possession</i> of the kippod, <i>and pools of water</i>, do not even so adduce anything that stands in the way of our view. For among aquatic creatures too there is a species of hedgehog, which by <i>Avicenna</i>, <i>Damir</i> and <i>Ibn al-Baitar</i> is called قنفد بحري [qunfud baḥrī], by others ἐχῖνος θαλάσσιος [echinos thalassios], <i>sea hedgehog</i>, or else ποτάμιος [potamios], <i>river</i> hedgehog. For <i>Paul of Aegina</i> reported that there are also river hedgehogs. The <i>Euphrates</i> near <i>Babylon</i> could have teemed with them. At least we learn from <i>Nearchus</i> that hedgehogs of remarkable size are found near the islands lying off the mouth of the <i>Euphrates</i>. <i>Strabo</i>: Λέγει δὲ (ὁ Νέαρχος) ἐν ταῖς πρὸ τοῦ Εὐφράτου νήσοις φύεσθαι παγούρων καὶ ἐχίνων μεγέθη· [Legei de (ho Nearchos) en tais pro tou Euphratou nēsois phyesthai pagourōn kai echinōn megethē]. But we interpret the passage of <i>Isaiah</i> very differently, namely in this way: <i>I will make it a possession</i> of the <i>kippod</i> (that is, <i>of the land hedgehog</i>), <i>even the pools of water</i>. That is, not only will the city be laid waste, but the neighbouring pools too will dry up, so that they will be as fit a dwelling as the city for the land hedgehog, which delights above all in dry places. Thus in <i>Zeph 2:13</i> <i>Nineveh</i> is said to be made לשממה וציה, <i>a desolation and dryness</i>, and only then does it come about that the <i>kippod</i> lodges in it. And in <i>Isa 34:11</i>, before <i>Idumaea</i> is handed over as a possession of the <i>kippod</i>, the streams are turned into pitch and the dust into brimstone.

‎To prove that the kippod <i>kippod</i> קפוד is a bird, it carries little weight that in <i>Es. XXXIV</i>, <i>11.</i> it is named together with birds. For right in verse thirteen dragons and ostriches are joined together, although they are animals of entirely different kinds. And <i>Zephaniah</i> gathers flocks, beasts, the pelican and the <i>kippod</i> together into one bundle. This affects the reader all the more, since in a description of devastation the things that inspire horror are set forth in such a jumbled order. When <i>Sophocles</i>, in this line of the satyr play <i>Amycus</i>, mentions <i>hares</i> among many birds,

‎Γέρανοι, κορῶναι, γλαῦκες ἴκτινοι, λαγοὶ, [Geranoi, korōnai, glaukes iktinoi, lagoi,]

‎<i>Cranes</i>, <i>crows</i>, <i>owls</i>, <i>kites</i>, <i>hares</i>: who would conclude from this that the <i>hare</i> is a kind of bird? I know that someone has substituted λάροι [laroi], a kind of water bird, for λαγοὶ [lagoi]. But in vain. For <i>Athenaeus</i>, book 9, chapter 14, cites this line to prove that λαγοὶ [lagoi] is as much <i>Attic</i> as λαγώ [lagō]. So λαγοί [lagoi] must be retained. Conversely, in <i>Lucian's</i> <i>Syrian Goddess</i> you may see <i>eagles</i> named among many quadrupeds thus: Ἐν δὲ τῇ αὐλῇ ἄφετοι νέμονται βόες μεγάλοι, καὶ ἵπποι, καὶ αἐτοὶ, καὶ ἄρκτοι, καὶ λέοντες· [En de tē aulē aphetoi nemontai boes megaloi, kai hippoi, kai aetoi, kai arktoi, kai leontes] <i>In the court, moreover, there graze freely great oxen</i>, <i>and horses</i>, <i>and eagles</i>, <i>and bears</i>, <i>and lions</i>. From which it would be absurd to infer that the eagles there are not birds but a kind of quadruped.
