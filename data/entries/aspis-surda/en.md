# Part 2, Book 3, chapter 6: On the deaf asp, which is said to stop its ear so as not to hear the voice of the charmers (Ps 58:5–6) (vol. 3, pp. 161–177)

### ‎[III, 161] [col. 384] Chapter VI.

### ‎<i>On the deaf asp, which is said to stop its ear so as not to hear the voice of the charmers, Ps 58:5–6.</i>

‎It remains for us to speak of the charming of asps, and indeed of all serpents, [col. 385] on the much-discussed (πολυθρύλλητα [polythryllēta]) words of the <i>Psalmist</i>, <i>Ps 58:5–6. Their poison is like the poison of a serpent: like that of the deaf asp, which stops its ear so as not to hear the voice of the whisperers, and of the diviner skilled in incantations.</i> Here the first question is why the asp is called <i>deaf</i>. <i>Bustamantinus</i>, in book 3, chapter 11 of <i>De Scripturae animantibus</i>, answers that it is called <i>deaf</i> because, as its sight is very sharp, so its hearing is very dull. But those versed in nature hold exactly the opposite: that the asp is much stronger in hearing than in sight. <i>Nicander</i>, in the <i>Theriaca</i>, line 162:

‎— — — — ὑπναλέοις δὲ Αἰὲν ἐπιλλίζουσα φαείνεται ἐνδυκὲς ὄσσοις. Ἀλλ᾽ ὅταν ἢ δοῦπον νέον οὔασιν, ἠέ τιν᾽ αὐδὴν Ἀθρήσῃ, νωθρὸν μὲν ἀπὸ ῥεθέως βάλεν ὕπνον. [— — — — hypnaleois de / Aien epillizousa phaenetai endykes ossois. / All᾽ hotan ē doupon neon ousin, ēe tin᾽ audēn / Athrēsē, nōthron men apo rhetheōs balen hypnon.]

‎— — — — <i>sluggish, it seems to blink continually</i> <i>with eyes weighed down by sleep.</i> <i>But when with ready ear it catches either a murmur</i> <i>or some voice, it casts the lazy sleep from its body.</i>

‎Pliny, book 8, chapter 23: <i>First of all</i> (nature) <i>gave this pest dull eyes, and made them see not forwards from the front but from the temples; and so it is roused more often by hearing than by sight.</i> In vain does <i>Bustamantinus</i> retort that this is said of the land asp, not of the ptyas and the chelidonia. For <i>Nicander</i> and Pliny treat the whole genus of asps. Nor has anyone else distinguished land asps from ptyades and chelidoniae in such a way as to dream that the latter excel in sight and the former in hearing. For all asps have the same dullness of eyes and sharpness of ears. Mercurialis: <i>Common to all asps are these: weak sight, slow gait, keen hearing</i> <sup>R1</sup>.

‎[III, 162] Others therefore hold that the asp is called <i>deaf</i> not by nature but by design, because, as the <i>Psalmist</i> adds, it stops its ears so as not to hear the voice of the charmer. Theodoret: Προσφυῶς αὐτὸν ἀσπίδι παρέβαλλεν, οὐ κωφευούσῃ κατὰ φύσιν, ἀλλὰ καὶ βυούσῃ τὰ ὦτα, καὶ τῆς ἐπῳδῆς ἀποθουμένῃ τὴν προσβολήν [Prosphyōs auton aspidi pareballen, ou kōpheuousē kata physin, alla kai byousē ta ōta, kai tēs epōdēs apothoumenē tēn prosbolēn]; <i>He aptly compared him to an asp, not deaf by nature, but stopping its ears so as to ward off the force of the incantation.</i> So also <i>Suidas</i> under Κωφῆς [Kōphēs]. And Augustine: “Like the deaf asp” <i>Why deaf? Whence</i> “And stopping its ears?” <i>It is deaf because it stops its ears.</i> R. Selomo [=Rashi (R. Solomon ben Isaac)] takes a middle course, writing that the asp in old age grows deaf in one ear and fills the other with dust so as not to hear the charmer's voice, which is a pure fiction. But neither is it certain enough what Augustine, <i>Cassiodorus</i>, Bede, <i>Isidore</i> and others report, as taken from popular rumor: that the asp, when it begins to suffer the charmer, presses one ear to the ground [col. 386] and stops the other with its tail, so that no way lies open to the incantation.

‎But to make the whole matter clearer, I must say a few things first about the charming of serpents. <i>Augustine</i> <sup>a)</sup> holds that serpents are moved by the songs of men more than any other kind of animal, so that this would be a proof that the first man was seduced by the devil through the serpent in <i>Paradise</i>. And so you may see whole nations notorious for this kind of enchantment. Among them are the <i>Marsi</i> and the <i>Psylli</i>, as everyone knows. But you may also read in <i>Aelian</i>, <i>History</i> book 6, chapter 33, that the <i>Egyptians</i> do the same μαγείᾳ τινὶ ἐπιχωρίῳ [mageia tini epichōriō], <i>by a certain magical art peculiar to that nation</i>. And the <i>Nubian Geographer</i>, first <i>Clime</i>, part 7, reports this very thing of the people of <i>Melinde</i>: يزعمون انهم يسحرون الحيوان الضار حتي لا يضر الا لمن ارادوا ضره [yazʿumūn annahum yasḥarūn al-ḥayawān al-ḍārr ḥattā lā yaḍurr illā li-man arādū ḍarrahu] <i>They are said to charm noxious animals so that they harm no one except whom they themselves wish.</i> And Cadamosto [=Alvise Cadamosto, Navigazioni (1455-56)] of the <i>Negroes</i>: <i>There are also enchanters, that is, outstanding charmers. They enchant serpents above all.</i> And Spitillus <i>in</i> his Collectanea of the <i>Peruvians</i> in the <i>New World</i>: <i>At festival time a boy, crowned, with a spear in one hand and a live viper hanging from the other, takes a seat placed in a very richly adorned spot. From this it is easily inferred that these are arts of the demon, since he is not bitten by the viper.</i> And <i>Teixeira</i> [=Pedro Teixeira, Relaciones (1610)] of the <i>Indians</i>, <i>History of Persia</i> book 1, chapter 29: <i>Los vemos comunmente en la</i> India <i>adò suelen los gentiles traher culebras encantadas, algunes muy grandes, y terribles, por las calles, y casas, haziendo las baylar al son de una flauta, y las rebuelven al cuello, y hazen con ellas otros semejantes ademanes, tratando las con las manos sin</i> [III, 163] <i>offensa suya. Y aunque algunos los attribuen a no tener dientes</i>, <i>porque dizen se los sacan siendo chiquitas. La experienzia muestra lo contrario, puez siendo algunas vezes ensannadas</i>, <i>o no tan ligadas con el encanto como convenia</i>, <i>se ha visto, que hazian grande estrago en los aspectantes.</i> In <i>Latin</i> this runs: <i>We have often seen this in</i> India, <i>where the heathen are accustomed to lead charmed serpents, some of them very large and terrible, through the streets and houses; they handle them unharmed, make them dance to the sound of a pipe, twist them round the neck, and perform other gestures of this kind. Some keep saying this happens because they are toothless, and that their teeth, which are small, are easily pulled out. But those who think so are refuted by experience, since these same serpents, when provoked and not bound by the charm as firmly as they should be, have sometimes been seen to cause great slaughter among the spectators</i> <sup>R2</sup>.

‎Nor was this superstition born today or yesterday. You may see traces of it everywhere in the most ancient writers. Thus <i>Orpheus</i> is fabled to have recalled <i>Eurydice</i>, struck by a serpent, from the underworld, because

‎— — ὑπὸ ὄφεως αὐτὴν τῷ ὄντι δεδηγμένην Καὶ κυνδυνεύσαν θανεῖν, ταῖς ἐπῳδαῖς, αἷς οἶδε, Καὶ ἀγχινοίᾳ, μέσῃ τε, καὶ πολυμαθείᾳ Αὐτὴν ἐζωοποίησεν· — — — — [— — hypo opheōs autēn tō onti dedēgmenēn / Kai kyndyneusan thanein, tais epōdais, hais oide, / Kai anchinoia, mesē te, kai polymatheia / Autēn ezōopoiēsen · — — — —]

‎<i>She had in truth been bitten by a snake and was in danger of her life; by the incantations he knew, by his skill, his muse and his great learning he restored her to life.</i> So <i>Tzetzes</i>, <i>Chiliad</i> 2, <i>History</i> 54. And the same writer proves that this art was not unknown to <i>Orpheus</i> [III, 164] from the words of <i>Orpheus</i> himself in the work <i>On Stones</i> (a work which, however, I would rather ascribe with <i>Suidas</i> to <i>Timocles of Syracuse</i>),

‎Ῥοῖζόν τε στῆσαι χαμαὶ ἐρχομένοιο δράκοντος, Εἴσεται, ἠδ' ὄφεων ἰὸν σβέσαι ἑρπυστήρων· [Rhoizon te stēsai chamai erchomenoio drakontos, / Eisetai, ēd' opheōn ion sbesai herpystērōn ·]

‎<i>He will know how to stop the hissing of the dragon coming along the ground, and to quench the poison</i> (or rather the <i>dart</i>) <i>of creeping serpents.</i> I cite these words the more gladly because <i>Paul</i> plainly alludes to them in <i>Ephes. VI, 16.</i> You will find them, moreover, in the proem of the work Περὶ [col. 387] λίθων [Peri lithōn], but corrupted. For instead of ῥοῖζόν τε στῆσαι [rhoizon te stēsai], our editions, against both metre and sense, have Ῥοιζῶντες, καὶ [Rhoizōntes, kai], etc. To the same point belongs what we read of <i>Medea</i> in <i>Apollonius</i>, <i>Argonautica</i> book 4, line 147:

‎Ἡδείῃ ἐνοπῇ θέλξαι τέρας, [Hēdeiē enopē thelxai teras,]

‎<i>that she soothed the monster with sweet voice</i>, that is, the dragon, guardian of the golden fleece. Accordingly there follows not much later,

‎— — — αὐτὰρ ὅ γ' ἤδη Οἴμῃ θελγόμενος δολιχὴν ἀναλύετ᾽ ἄκανθαν, [— — — autar ho g' ēdē / Oimē thelgomenos dolichēn analyet' akanthan,]

‎<i>So, soothed by her song, his long spine was relaxed.</i> But <i>Ovid</i>, ascribing the same thing to <i>Jason</i> in book 7 of the <i>Metamorphoses</i>:

‎<i>After he had sprinkled him with herbs of</i> Lethean <i>juice,</i> <i>And thrice spoken the words that bring peaceful sleep.</i>

‎To this very kind of magic Plato alludes in these words in book 2 of the <i>Republic</i>: Θρασύμαχος φαίνεται ὑπό σου ὥσπερ ὄφις κηληθῆναι [Thrasymachos phainetai hypo sou hōsper ophis kēlēthēnai]· <i>Thrasymachus seems to have been charmed by you like a serpent.</i> But whoever wants something more explicit should read, at the end of the <i>Mirabilia</i> attributed to Aristotle [ch. 162], the long story of a serpent called <i>sacred</i>, killed by the spells of a <i>Thessalian</i> witch. Of the same kind is the report that the <i>Indian</i> ἐπῳδοὺς [epōdous], charmers, cure the bites of serpents, as <i>Nearchus</i>, the companion of <i>Alexander</i>, relates, and by flute-players, as Theophrastus relates: the former in <i>Strabo</i>, book 15; the latter in <i>Gellius</i>, book 4, chapter 13. And what <i>Antigonus</i> wrote under the son of <i>Lagus</i>, that the <i>Psylli</i>, when struck by asps, suffer nothing, <i>Mirabilia</i>, chapter 19.

‎Of this enchantment, moreover, there are many kinds. Some were accustomed to enchant serpents by hand and touch. Virgil, <i>Aeneid</i> book 7, line 754:

‎<i>Who was wont to scatter sleep by song and by hand.</i>

‎<i>Silius Italicus</i>, book 1, line 411:

‎<i>And Atyr, skilled to disarm serpents of their dire poison</i> <i>and by touch to lull the noxious chelydri to sleep.</i> [III, 165] And book 8, line 500:

‎<i>They say that Anguitia, offspring of Aeetes, first</i> <i>showed baneful herbs, and tamed poisons by touch.</i>

‎<i>Symmachus</i>, book 7, letter 19: <i>What Colchian or Thessalian would have relieved these injuries by song or by hand?</i> Others drew a circle and applied drugs. Thus Aristotle, or whoever wrote the <i>Mirabilia</i>, relates that a <i>Thessalian</i> woman in the city of <i>Tenos</i>, in order to kill the sacred serpent, κύκλον γράψασαν, καὶ τὰ φάρμακα θεῖσαν, εἰσβῆναι εἰς τὸν κύκλον αὐτὴν καὶ τὸν υἱὸν [kyklon grapsasan, kai ta pharmaka theisan, eisbēnai eis ton kyklon autēn kai ton huion], <i>having drawn a circle and placed drugs in it, entered that circle with her son.</i> And in Lucian's <i>Philopseudes</i> the <i>Chaldaean</i>, to call forth serpents, came into the field in the morning and read seven sacred names from an old book, θείῳ καὶ δαδὶ καταγνίσας τὸν τόπον, περιελθὼν εἰς τρὶς [theiō kai dadi katagnisas ton topon, perielthōn eis tris], <i>having purified the place with sulphur and a torch, walking round it three times.</i> Again, <i>Seneca</i> describes another rite of enchantment in these words in the <i>Medea</i>, Act 4:

‎<i>And folding the grim rite with her left hand,</i> <i>she summons the plagues, whatever the sand</i> <i>of burning</i> Libya <i>breeds.</i> — — —

‎Metal plates, also stones, pieces of wood, and so-called <i>talismanic</i> figures were employed for this use. Arnobius, book 2: <i>Against harmful strokes and the poisonous bites of snakes we often seek remedies, and we protect ourselves with plates sold by the</i> Marsi <i>and</i> Psylli. (Others read, I doubt whether better, <i>We protect ourselves with the lips, with the teeth of the</i> Marsi <i>or</i> Psylli.) And the <i>Nubian Geographer</i> (<i>Clime</i> 1, part 3) tells the fable that near the <i>Nigritian</i> city of <i>Cucu</i> there grows a certain wood which they therefore call عود الحية [ʿūd al-ḥayya] <i>serpent's wood</i>, because اذا وضع علي حجر الحية خرجت اليه مسرعة [idhā wuḍiʿa ʿalā juḥr al-ḥayya kharajat ilayhi musriʿa] <i>when it is placed at a serpent's lair, the serpent immediately comes out to it.</i> Indeed, if we believe it, whoever holds this wood in his hand ياخذ من الحيات ما شا بيده من غير ان يدركه [col. 388] شي من الجزع ويجد في نفسه قوة عند اخذها [yākhudh min al-ḥayyāt mā shā bi-yadihi min ghayr an yudrikahu shay min al-jazaʿ wa-yajid fī nafsihi quwwa ʿinda akhdhihā] <i>will take as many serpents as he wishes in his hand, in such a way that meanwhile he is struck by no fear; rather, in catching them he will feel a certain strength within himself.</i> Others, however, report it differently: namely, that if anyone holds this wood in his hand or hangs it from his neck, لم تقربه حية البتة [lam taqrabhu ḥayya al-batta] <i>no serpent at all approaches him.</i> The same author has this about the city of <i>Emesa</i>, <i>Clime</i> 3, part 5: وهي مطلسمة لا يدخلها حية ولا عقرب ومتي ادخلت علي الباب المدينة هلكت علي الحال [wa-hiya muṭalsama lā yadkhuluhā ḥayya wa-lā ʿaqrab wa-matā udkhilat ʿalā al-bāb al-madīna halakat ʿalā al-ḥāl] <i>It is so</i> talismanized) that is, [III, 166] οὕτω τετελειωμένη [houtō teteleiōmenē], furnished with such τελέσμασι [telesmasi], or <i>talismans</i>), <i>that no serpents or scorpions enter it; rather, if they are brought to the gate of the city, they immediately expire.</i> He adds that in a certain vault a stone is set in which the figure of a scorpion is carved, by touching which a person whom a scorpion has stung or bitten is healed. With the same credibility, in Pliny, book 28, chapter 3, <i>Attalus</i> affirms that <i>if anyone on seeing a scorpion says TWO, it is checked and does not dart its stroke.</i> And Alcamus [=al-Qāmūs al-muḥīṭ, the Arabic dictionary of al-Fīrūzābādī] says that اوس [aws] <i>os</i>, or اوسه [awsah] <i>osoh</i>, is كلمة تقال للحية فتنخضع [kalima tuqāl li-l-ḥayya fa-tankhaḍiʿ] <i>a word which, when said to a serpent, makes it lower itself.</i> Is it because it seems to imitate the hissing of a serpent? For thus in the book of <i>Mirabilia</i> the <i>Thessalian</i> witch, in order to call the sacred serpent into her circle, is said μιμεῖσθαι τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ θηρίου [mimeisthai tēn phōnēn tou thēriou], <i>to imitate the voice of the beast.</i> This is expressed in <i>Arabic</i> by one word, صدا [ṣadā]. Namely, صدا [ṣadā] means <i>to imitate a serpent's hissing in charming it</i>, as Echo imitates our voice. For صدي [ṣadā] is the noun for <i>echo</i>, from which noun the verb صدا [ṣadā] is formed.

‎But the chief power of this fascination is believed to lie in chants and songs, which the <i>Hebrews</i> call לחשים, that is, <i>whisperings</i> or <i>mutterings</i>, because magicians are accustomed to murmur their chants between their lips <sup>R3</sup>. The <i>Psalmist</i> treats of these in this place, when he says that the deaf asp stops its ear so as not to hear the voice of מלחשים, <i>the mutterers</i>, or <i>charmers</i>. For by magic chant serpents were believed, 1. first, to be drawn out and called forth from their holes. Thus <i>Pliny</i>, book 8, chapter 16, says that they <i>are drawn out by chant and compelled to punishment</i>. And <i>Aelian</i>, on the <i>Egyptians</i>, book 6, chapter 33: Τῶν φωλεῶν τοὺς ὄφεις, ἐπαοιδαῖς τισι καταγοητεύσαντες, εἶτα μέντοι προσάγουσι ῥᾶστα [Tōn phōleōn tous opheis, epaoidais tisi katagoēteusantes, eita mentoi prosagousi rhasta]; <i>Serpents, first charmed with certain songs, they afterwards draw out of their lurking places very easily.</i> And <i>Seneca</i> in the <i>Medea</i>:

‎— — <i>drawn by magic chants</i> <i>the scaly throng is present, its lairs deserted.</i>

‎As in <i>Lucian's Philopseudes</i>, to the <i>Chaldaean</i> magician ἧκον, ὥσπερ ἑλκόμενοι πρὸς τὴν ἐπῳδὴν, ὄφεις πολλοὶ, καὶ ἀσπίδες, καὶ ἔχιδναι, καὶ κεράσαι, καὶ ἀκοντίαι [hēkon, hōsper helkomenoi pros tēn epōdēn, opheis polloi, kai aspides, kai echidnai, kai kerasai, kai akontiai], <i>there came, as if drawn by the incantation, many serpents, namely asps, and vipers, and cerastae, and javelin-snakes.</i> Lampridius <i>in</i> the Heliogabalus: <i>He is said to have gathered serpents by means of priests of the</i> Marsian <i>people.</i> Jerome <i>on</i> Psal. LVII.: <i>That man who charms is called a</i> Marsus, <i>and he draws them from darkness to the light.</i> <i>Augustine</i> [III, 167], book 11 <i>On</i> Genesis <i>according to the Letter</i>, chapter 28: <i>Serpents are thought to hear and understand the words of the</i> Marsi, <i>so that, when these charm them, they very often spring out of their lurking places.</i> And in the book of 65 <i>Questions</i>, Question 44: <i>That they come out of their caves at the words of the</i> Marsi <i>they are compelled by the devil, God permitting.</i> And in <i>On Heresies</i>, chapter 17, on the <i>Ophites</i>: <i>They also have a real snake, which they feed and venerate. And, when the priest charms, it comes out of the cave and</i> [col. 389] <i>climbs up on the altar set against the cave.</i> <i>Cadamosto</i>, on the nephew of the prince <i>Budomel</i>, <i>Navigations</i>, chapter 28: <i>Having drawn a circle on the ground, by fascination he compels the serpents to come into it.</i> <i>Scaliger</i> [=J. C. Scaliger, Exercitationes (1557)] on the books on <i>animals</i>, book 1, chapter 28, section 123: <i>We have sometimes seen serpents roused from their holes by chants.</i> Many believe it points the same way that the <i>Hebrews</i> call a magician חובר <i>chober</i>, as it were an <i>associator</i>, because he associates and gathers together into one the animals he has called forth. Thus at <i>Deut 18:11</i>, חובר חבר, according to <i>Solomon</i> [Rashi], is שמצרף נחשים או עקרבים או שאר חיות למקום אחד, <i>one who associates serpents, or scorpions, or other animals into one place.</i> This is taken from <i>Jonathan's</i> [=Targum Jonathan] paraphrase: ומחברין ואסרין חיוין ועקרבין וכל מיני רחשין, <i>who associate and bind</i> (namely by magic words) <i>serpents, and scorpions, and all kinds of reptiles.</i> The <i>Jerusalem</i> paraphrase is not unlike it. Nor does the <i>Psalmist</i> seem to have had another meaning when he says in this very place, <i>It does not hear the voice of the charmers, nor</i> חובר חוברים מחכם, (word for word) <i>of the skilled associator of associations</i>, that is, of him who knows the art of calling serpents to himself so that they gather into one. Accordingly the <i>Chaldee</i> renders here דלא מקבל לקל מלי חרשיא אסורי נחשין, <i>Which</i> (the asp) <i>does not hear the voice and words of the magicians who bind serpents</i> <sup>R4</sup>.

‎[III, 168] But they also hold that serpents are put to flight by chant. <i>Lucan</i>, book 9, line 913:

‎<i>First the sands which the space of the rampart enclosed</i> <i>he purges with chant, and with words that put snakes to flight.</i>

‎So he; but he alone, as far as I remember.

‎Snakes are also said to be halted, soothed and stupefied by chant. <i>Tibullus</i>, <i>Elegy</i> 9:

‎<i>Chant also checks the course of the angry snake.</i>

‎A certain witch in <i>Petronius</i>:

‎— — <i>and dragons bidden to stand still.</i>

‎<i>Virgil</i>, in the place cited above:

‎<i>Who on the viper brood and the foul-breathing water-snakes</i> <i>was wont to shed sleep by chant and by hand,</i> <i>and soothed their wrath, and by his art relieved their bites.</i>

‎<i>Columella</i> in the <i>Garden</i>:

‎<i>Thus once the snake lulled to sleep by magic chants.</i>

‎<i>Seneca</i> in the <i>Medea</i>:

‎<i>First, serpent, be lulled to sleep by my chants.</i>

‎<i>Silius</i>, book 3, line 300, on the <i>Marmaridae</i>:

‎<i>At whose chants the serpent forgot its poison,</i> <i>at whose chants the cerastae lay down tame.</i>

‎And book 5, line 352, on <i>Synalus</i>:

‎— — — <i>in anointing a wound</i> <i>with the juices of herbs, and in drawing iron out of the body by chant,</i> [III, 169] <i>and in sending sleep upon the whole chelydrus</i> <i>he surpassed all.</i> — — — —

‎And book 8, line 497:

‎<i>These battle-lines knew how to wage war, and the</i> Marsian <i>youth</i> <i>knew both how to war by hand and to chant sleep upon water-snakes,</i> <i>and to blunt the viper's tooth with herbs and song.</i>

‎<i>Plutarch</i> in the <i>Cato</i>, on the <i>Psylli</i>: Τὰ θηρία κατεπάδοντες ἀμβλύνουσι, καὶ κηλοῦσι· [Ta thēria katepadontes amblynousi, kai kēlousi] <i>By song they stupefy and soothe the serpents. Philostratus</i> in the <i>Apollonius</i>, book 3, chapter 2: <i>Written incantatory words are said to induce sleep in dragons</i>: in Greek, ὕπνον ἐγγοητεύσαντες τοῖς γράμμασιν [hypnon engoēteusantes tois grammasin]. So <i>Theodoret</i>, on <i>Psalm</i> 57, says that the asp stops its ear while the magician sings, ὥστε μὴ θέλγεσθαι ταῖς ἐπῳδαῖς [hōste mē thelgesthai tais epōdais], <i>so that it may not be soothed by the incantations.</i> And on the same passage <i>Hilary</i>: <i>Nor does he here mention the chants of the</i> Marsi, <i>or the lulled hissing of vipers. Cadamosto</i>, in the place cited above: <i>He went and restrained the serpents with a spell, and returning at once he addressed his guest thus: The serpents had besieged the house to lay it waste, had I not met so great an evil with an incantation, which we use rather frequently.</i>

‎Moreover, serpents were believed to be purged of all poison by song, so that they do no harm by their bite. Thus <i>Isidore</i>, <i>Etymologies</i> book 9, chapter 2, writes that the <i>Marsi</i> <i>are unharmed by the evil effects of charms.</i> [col. 390] To this belong these lines of <i>Alcimus</i>, book 2, <i>On Original Sin</i>:

‎<i>Within he wields the weapons of a secret charm</i>, etc. <i>Soon the snake is handled with impunity by an unarmed hand:</i> <i>And in the snake only the bite, not the poison, is feared.</i>

‎And <i>Maimonides</i> [=Moses Maimonides (Rambam), Mishneh Torah], <i>On Idolatry</i>, chapter 11, section 10: הן אומרים שח׳ אומר כך וכך על הנחש או על העקרב אינו מזיק, <i>If a magician, as they say, has said this or that to a serpent or a scorpion, it will do no further harm.</i> Indeed, in <i>Delrio</i>, book 2 of the <i>Disquisitiones magicae</i>, Question 13, <i>Pedro Cieza</i> asserts (whether truly or otherwise, let the reader judge) that all the huge serpents of the <i>Andes</i> mountains have laid aside all their ferocity and harmful power, having once been disarmed by the charms of a certain old woman: so that even now they terrify only by their appearance, and are otherwise harmless.

‎The power of a charm is also thought to heal those whom a <i>serpent</i> has injured by its bite or touch. On this account <i>Nearchus</i>, in <i>Strabo</i>, commends the <i>Indians</i>. For since in <i>India</i> serpents and reptiles of every kind swarm in great numbers and trouble the inhabitants in many ways, he reports that there ἐπῳδοὺς περιφοιτᾷν πεπιστευμένους ἰᾶσθαι, καὶ εἶναι σχεδόν τι ταύτην μόνην ἰατρικὴν [epōdous periphoitan pepisteumenous iasthai, kai einai schedon ti tautēn monēn iatrikēn], <i>charmers wander about who</i> [III, 170] <i>are believed to heal, and that this is almost the only medicine.</i> So of <i>Umbro</i>, in the <i>Poet</i>, book 7:

‎— — — <i>and by his art he relieved bites.</i>

‎And <i>Lucan</i>, book 9, line 933:

‎— — <i>the plague implanted in the black marrow</i> <i>perishes, charmed away.</i> — — —

‎The chief point is this: snakes, when the magician sings, are said to <i>burst</i> and to <i>split in the middle.</i> Lucilius:

‎— — — the Marsian <i>bursts the snake</i> <i>with song, when he has stretched out all its veins.</i>

‎<i>Virgil</i>, Eclogue 8:

‎<i>The cold snake in the meadows is burst by chanting.</i>

‎<i>Ovid</i>, <i>Metamorphoses</i> book 7, Fable 2, in the person of <i>Medea</i>:

‎<i>I burst the jaws of vipers with words and charm.</i>

‎And <i>Amores</i> book 2, <i>Elegy</i> 1:

‎<i>By a charm snakes burst asunder, their jaws torn apart.</i>

‎And <i>On Cosmetics for the Face</i>:

‎<i>Nor let snakes be split in the middle by</i> Marsian <i>chants.</i>

‎<i>Horace</i>, Epode 7:

‎<i>And that the head bursts asunder at a</i> Marsian <i>dirge.</i>

‎<i>Lucan</i>, book 6, line 488:

‎— — — <i>for these the snake unrolls its cold coils,</i> <i>and is stretched out on the frosty field;</i> <i>the viper's knots join again, its body broken.</i>

‎<i>Nemesianus</i>, <i>Eclogue</i> 4, of the sorceress <i>Mycale</i>:

‎<i>She has chanted what the Moon fears, by which the snake is burst.</i>

‎And the <i>Philosopher</i>, in the place cited above: Ὡς δὲ προσῆλθεν ὁ ὄφις εἰς τὸν κύκλον, λύσιν εὐθὺς γενέσθαι αὐτου [Hōs de prosēlthen ho ophis eis ton kyklon, lysin euthys genesthai autou]. <i>As soon as the serpent entered the circle, it</i> (they say) <i>was at once dissolved</i>, that is, <i>killed, its joints loosened.</i>

‎Meanwhile, that there are some serpents which cannot be charmed seems deducible even from Scripture. For in <i>Jer 8:17.</i> God threatens that he will send among the <i>Israelites</i> [III, 171] נחשים צפעונים אשר אין להם לחש, <i>serpents, reguli</i>, <i>against which there is no charm.</i> The Complutensian edition has οἷς οὐκ ἔστιν ἐκσπᾶσαι [hois ouk estin ekspasai], <i>from which one cannot tear oneself away</i>; but others better, οἷς οὐκ ἔστιν ἐπάσαι [hois ouk estin epasai], <i>which one cannot charm.</i> And in <i>Eccl 10:11</i> <i>Solomon</i> says that a בעל לשון, a <i>slanderer</i>, is no better (that is, just as pernicious) than אם נשך הנחש בלא לחש, <i>if a serpent bites without charm</i>, that is, <i>uncharmed</i>, or <i>one that cannot be charmed.</i> And in the passage of the <i>Psalmist</i> with which we are concerned, the <i>deaf asp</i> is said <i>to stop its ear</i>, <i>so as not to hear the voice of the charmer.</i> Indeed, a serpent is thought to resist a charm in two ways: either when it sings back, or when it grows deaf to the song. <i>Recanere</i> [to sing back], ἀντᾴδειν [antadein], or <i>recantare</i> if you prefer, is <i>to repel and turn back the charm by a contrary hissing.</i> <i>Pliny</i>, book 28, ch. 2: <i>Not a few</i> (believe) <i>that the serpents themselves sing back</i>, [col. 391] (so I read with <i>Turnebus</i> <sup>b)</sup>), <i>and that this is the only understanding they have.</i> <i>Aristotle</i>, of the <i>Thessalian</i> witch: Εἶτα μιμεῖσθαι τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ θηρίου· τὸ δὲ ἀντᾴδειν, καὶ προσιέναι· [Eita mimeisthai tēn phōnēn tou thēriou; to de antadein, kai prosienai;] <i>Then</i> (the witch began) <i>to imitate the voice of the serpent; but the serpent sang back and approached.</i> A serpent against which the charm is ineffective, because it sings against the song in this way, is called صل [ṣil] <i>sil</i> in <i>Arabic</i>, as one that is not affected by the song is called اصم [aṣamm] <i>asammo</i>, that is, <i>deaf</i>. Properly صل [ṣil] <i>sil</i> is a <i>ringing</i> or <i>shrill</i> serpent, that is, one which, uttering a certain shrill sound, sings and hisses against the magician. But by a phrase as familiar to the <i>Hebrews</i> and <i>Arabs</i> as it is unknown to the <i>Greeks</i> and <i>Romans</i>, a serpent is said <i>to grow deaf to the song</i>, or, what is the same, <i>to stop its ear</i>, when it is no more affected by the song than if it were deaf or had its ears stopped. So <i>Kimchi</i> [=David Kimḥi (Radak), Sefer ha-Shorashim] took it in the passage of the <i>Psalm</i>, where he says of the asp: זה הנחש הקשה לא יועיל בו לחש כאילו הוא חרש וכאילו יאטם אזנו שלו ישמע מה שלוחשים לו, <i>That serpent is hard</i>, <i>against which a charm avails no more</i> <i>than if it were deaf</i>, <i>or hardened its ear</i>, <i>so as not to hear the songs with which they charm it.</i> By the same phrase, one who is hard and merciless toward the poor is said <i>to harden his ear to the cry of the poor</i>, Prov 21:13. And the pious man <i>stops his ear</i>, <i>lest he hear of bloodshed</i>, Isa 33:15, that is, he is so averse to cruel and bloodthirsty counsels, and attends to them no more when they are proposed by the wicked, than if he had his ears stopped. So also when it is said of <i>Aeneas</i> in book 4 of the <i>Aeneid</i>,

‎— — — <i>but he is moved by no weeping, nor does he listen yielding to any words. The Fates stand in the way, and a god blocks the man's gentle ears.</i>

‎That is, he made him as unbending to the prayers of <i>Dido</i> and <i>Anna</i> as if he had blocked his ears. In like manner the asp is deaf to the song and stops its ear when the song has as little power over it as if it were entirely deaf and had stopped its ears. The antecedent stands for the consequent. [III, 172] Nor, I think, is it taken otherwise by <i>Avicenna</i> [=Ibn Sīnā, al-Qānūn fī l-ṭibb (the Canon)] when he so often repeats that this or that serpent is of the kind of the deaf, as is shown more fully below.

‎Furthermore, whether the serpent sings back or grows deaf to the song, it is believed not only to escape, but also to create danger for the magician. So in <i>Aristotle</i>, when the sacred serpent sang against the <i>Thessalian</i> sorceress, she very nearly sank into a deadly sleep. And, as <i>Alcimus</i> testifies in the place cited above,

‎<i>Sometimes the charmer perishes, if the deaf serpent has scorned the cunning murmurs of the adjurer.</i>

‎Either the whole force of the charm, by the just judgment of God, turns to the destruction of its author, or the serpent inflicts a lethal wound on the charmer. To this belongs the celebrated example <sup>c)</sup> of that magician of <i>Salzburg</i> who, when he was striving to drive all the serpents within one mile into a certain pit, was at last killed by a single huge and aged one that leapt upon him. Accordingly the <i>Son of Sirach</i> teaches that magicians too are bitten by serpents, in these words of <i>Ecclesiasticus</i>, chapter 12, verse 13: Τίς ἐλεήσει ἐπαοιδὸν ὀφιόδηκτον, καὶ πάντας τοὺς προσάγοντας θηρίοις; [Tis eleēsei epaoidon ophiodēkton, kai pantas tous prosagontas thēriois?] <i>Who will pity a charmer bitten by a serpent, or any of those who go near</i> venomous <i>beasts?</i> For there θηρία [thēria] signifies not any <i>wild beasts</i> whatever, but <i>venomous</i> ones, as we have already proved earlier <sup>d)</sup>.

‎Which serpents, moreover, sing back, or grow deaf at the chant, or whose poison is believed to be stronger than any spell, none of the <i>Greeks</i> explains. Except that <i>Aelian</i>, book 1, chapter 54, seems to assign this to the asp as its peculiar property, that against its bite neither medicines nor incantations are of any avail: Καὶ ἔχεως, he says, δῆγμα, καὶ ὄφεως, ἀλλ᾽ οὖν, φασὶν, ἀντιπάλων μὴ διαμαρτάνειν φαρμάκων. Καὶ τὰ μὲν αὐτῶν ἀκούω πόματα εἶναι, τὰ δὲ χρίσματα· καὶ ἐπῳδαὶ δὲ ἐπραΰναν τινῶν ἐγχεισθέντα ἰόν. Ἀσπίδος δὲ ἀκούω μόνον δῆγμα ἀνίατον εἶναι, καὶ ἐπικουρίας κρεῖττον. [Kai echeōs … dēgma, kai opheōs, all' oun, phasin, antipalōn mē diamartanein pharmakōn. Kai ta men autōn akouō pomata einai, ta de chrismata; kai epōdai de epraynan tinōn encheisthenta ion. Aspidos de akouō monon dēgma aniaton einai, kai epikourias kreitton.] <i>The bites of the viper and of other serpents, they say, are not without remedies to be set against the evil.</i> [col. 392] <i>Of these I hear that some are given to drink, others are rubbed on; in some cases even incantations have mitigated the injected poison. But I have learned that the bite of the asp alone is incurable and beyond all help.</i> But the matter, left untouched by the <i>Greeks</i>, is set out accurately and clearly by <i>Avicenna</i>, following the <i>Arab philosophers</i>, in the chapter <i>On the species of serpents</i>, which begins thus <sup>e)</sup>: ان العلما بامر الحيات وطبايعها قسموها ثلثة اقسام شديدة الحدة [inna al-ʿulamā bi-amr al-ḥayyāt wa-ṭabāyiʿihā qassamūhā thalathat aqsām shadīdat al-ḥidda] [III, 173] لا تمهل من الحال الي فوق ثلث ساعات ولا علاج للسوعها وهي الصم والاصلال ولا ينفع فيها الا قطع العضو في الحال او الكي البالع النافذ بالنار فانه يحرق السم ويضيق المجاري [lā tumhil min al-ḥāl ilī fawq thalath sāʿāt wa-lā ʿilāj li-lusūʿihā wa-hiya al-ṣumm wa-al-aṣlāl wa-lā yanfaʿ fīhā illā qaṭʿ al-ʿuḍw fī al-ḥāl aw al-kayy al-bāliʿ al-nāfidh bi-al-nār fa-innahu yaḥriq al-samm wa-yuḍayyiq al-majārī] <i>The philosophers, in their books on serpents and their nature, divide them into three kinds.</i> In the first species <i>the force of the poison is so intense that</i> death <i>is not delayed beyond three hours from the time of</i> the bite, <i>nor is there any cure. For they are of the class of the deaf and the hissing</i> (that is, as we have shown, of those which either seem to grow deaf at the chant, because they are no more affected by it than if they were deaf, or even τῷ ἐπαοιδῷ ἀντᾴδουσι [tō epaoidō antadousi], <i>sing back to the charmer</i>, so that the charm is ineffective against them). <i>Therefore nothing helps except amputation of the limb at the same moment, or a burning that penetrates to the inmost parts; because it burns out the poison and constricts the passages.</i> He adds that to this species belong الملكة [al-malika], the <i>regulus</i>, which kills by sight and hearing alone; and الخطاف [al-khaṭṭāf], the <i>chelidonian asp</i>; and الاسفيس اليابسة [al-asfīs al-yābisa], the <i>dry asp</i>, or χερσαίαν [chersaian]; of which the latter kills within three hours, the former within two; and البزاقة [al-bazzāqa], the <i>ptyas asp</i>, from which the bitten man perishes قبل ان توجع [qabl an tūjiʿ], <i>before he feels pain</i>, that is, <i>he dies without pain</i>; the <i>Latin</i> translator absurdly has <i>before it returns</i>, because he read ترجع [tarjiʿ] for توجع [tūjiʿ]. Of the same sort is that a little later the same translator places in this same class <i>arched deaf ones, which multiply in the lands</i> of Egypt. <i>Arched deaf ones</i>, that is, الصم المقوسة [al-ṣumm al-muqawwasa]. So he seems to have read, without any sense, instead of الصم المقعصة [al-ṣumm al-muqʿiṣa], which are <i>deaf</i> serpents <i>that bring death swiftly.</i> Of this kind, besides the regulus and the asp, are المقرنة [al-muqarrana], the <i>cerastes</i>, and اوبريس [ūbrīs], the <i>hydrus</i>, and كرسودروس [kursūdrūs], the <i>chersydrus</i>, all of which <i>Avicenna</i> counts among the deaf in what follows. Thus in the chapter <i>On the basilisk</i>: هو الاول من الصم [huwa al-awwal min al-ṣumm] <i>It is the first of the deaf.</i> And in the chapter <i>On the chelidonian asp</i>: وهي من الصم [wa-hiya min al-ṣumm] <i>This too is of the deaf.</i> And below: وعلاجها علاج الصم [wa-ʿilājuhā ʿilāj al-ṣumm] <i>And its cure is the cure of the deaf.</i> He says the same of the <i>chersaean asp.</i> And of the <i>ptyas</i>: علاجها من جنس علاج الصم [ʿilājuhā min jins ʿilāj al-ṣumm] <i>It is cured in the same way as the deaf.</i> And in the chapter <i>On the cerastes</i>: هي جنس من الصم [hiya jins min al-ṣumm] <i>It is of the class of the</i> [col. 393] <i>deaf.</i> And <i>On the hydrus</i>, or <i>chersydrus</i>: هي اصغر من الاصله صما [hiya aṣghar min al-aṣla ṣamman] <i>It is smaller than the dryinus, deaf.</i> So that it is now obscure to no one that the <i>Arabs</i> call <i>deaf</i> all serpents which they believe to be immune to incantation. Hence <i>Alcamus</i> says expressly: الاصم من الحية لا يقبل الرقي [al-aṣamm min al-ḥayya lā yaqbal al-ruqī] <i>A deaf serpent is one</i> [III, 174] <i>which does not admit incantation.</i> Such, moreover, they hold to be all those whose poison is intense in the highest degree, namely the regulus, asp, cerastes, hydrus, chersydrus. With this doctrine it will seem to agree that the royal <i>Psalmist</i> places the asp, and <i>Jeremiah</i> the regulus or basilisk, in that class. As if it were established by sure experience either that magicians never attempt to charm that kind of serpent, or, if they attempt it, that they succeed so little that it often even turns to the ruin of those who try.

‎But two objections can be raised against this. First, no reason appears why magicians should not be able to do against asps, hydri, reguli and cerastae the same as against the other serpents. For why should a regulus or an asp not be called out by the power of a demon, or grow numb, or burst asunder, or be disarmed of its poison, as easily as any other serpent? And who would believe that their reckoning sticks here, when they seem to give greater proofs of their art in other things? Further, many write that these serpents too are subject to charming. In Lucian's <i>Philopseudes</i> a magician calls out ἀσπίδες, καὶ ἔχιδναι, καὶ κεράσαι, καὶ ἀκοντίαι [aspides, kai echidnai, kai kerasai, kai akontiai], <i>asps, and vipers, and cerastae, and darting snakes.</i> Virgil says that <i>Umbro</i> with song and hand spread sleep

‎— — — <i>on heavily breathing hydri.</i>

‎And <i>Silius</i>, book 8, says that the <i>Marsi</i>

‎— — — <i>sing sleep upon the chelydri.</i>

‎And of the <i>Marmaridae</i>, or <i>Psylli</i>, book 3:

‎<i>At whose songs the cerastae lay down tame.</i>

‎The cerastae, namely, have πρὸς τοὺς καλουμένους Ψύλλους ἔνσπονδα, οἵ περ οὔτε αὐτοὶ δακόντων ἐπαΐουσι τῶν δηγμάτων, καὶ τοῖς τῷ τοιούτῳ κακῷ περιπεσόντας ἰῶνται ῥᾷστα [pros tous kaloumenous Psyllous ensponda, hoi per oute autoi dakontōn epaiousi tōn dēgmatōn, kai tois tō toioutō kakō peripesontas iōntai rhaista], <i>a treaty with those who are called</i> Psylli, <i>who neither feel the bites of those that bite them, and easily heal those who fall into that evil.</i> So <i>Aelian, History</i> book 1, ch. 57. Nor are the asps otherwise disposed towards the same <i>Psylli</i>. Antigonus, <i>Mirabilia</i> ch. 19: Ὑπὸ τῶν ἀσπίδων οὗτοι μὲν (Ψύλλοι) οὐδὲν πάσχουσι τυπτόμενοι· τῶν δὲ λοιπῶν οὐκ ἔστιν, ὅστις διαφεύγει δηχθείς [Hypo tōn aspidōn houtoi men (Psylloi) ouden paschousi typtomenoi; tōn de loipōn ouk estin, hostis diapheugei dēchtheis]; the Psylli, <i>when struck by asps, suffer no harm, whereas none of the others</i>, when <i>bitten</i> by them, <i>escapes</i> death. <i>Varro</i> in <i>Priscian</i>: <i>If an asp, when brought near, has stung him and does not kill him, let him know that he is of the stock of the</i> Psylli. <i>Cinna</i> in <i>Gellius</i>, book 9, ch. 12:

‎<i>As the</i> Punic Psyllus <i>the drowsy asp</i>.

‎supply <i>hunts</i>, or <i>handles with impunity.</i> These things do not seem possible otherwise than by magic arts. Accordingly <i>Lucan</i> of the <i>Psylli</i>: [III, 175] — — <i>a tongue equal to potent herbs.</i>

‎And <i>Silius</i>:

‎<i>At whose songs the serpent forgot its poison.</i>

‎And <i>Plutarch</i>: Αὐτά τε τὰ θηρία κατεπάδοντες ἀμβλύνουσι, καὶ κηλοῦσιν [Auta te ta thēria katepadontes amblynousi, kai kēlousin]; <i>and the serpents themselves they stupefy and soothe with incantations.</i> And <i>Hesychius</i>: Ψυλλικὸς γόης, ὁ τῶν Ψύλλων, οἱ δὲ Ψύλλοι ἔθνος Λιβύης [Psyllikos goēs, ho tōn Psyllōn, hoi de Psylloi ethnos Libyēs]; a Psyllic <i>magician, that is, one of the</i> Psylli, <i>which is a people</i> of Libya. Finally <i>Sulpicius</i>, who had travelled through all <i>Egypt</i>, where asps are in great abundance, believed they could be charmed; otherwise he would not have said what we have already <sup>f)</sup> quoted from him: <i>An asp of wondrous size,</i> etc. <i>as if charmed by spells, laid down its blue neck.</i>

‎Now, to refute the two objections brought: to the first we answer that the hydrus, the asp, the cerastes and the basilisk [col. 394] can be restrained by the devil as easily as any other serpent, provided God permits it; but that God does not will that everything in the world be allowed to the devil. For however proudly that impure spirit boasts <sup>g)</sup> <i>that all things have been delivered to him</i>, yet God has said to him the very thing he said to the sea <sup>h)</sup>: <i>Thus far shalt thou come, and no further.</i> Hence it is that in <i>Egypt</i> the magicians, who had produced blood, serpents and frogs in appearance, are stuck when it came to the lice <sup>i)</sup>, since God did not permit them to go on mocking <i>Pharaoh</i> with their illusions. And in the <i>Gospel</i> <sup>k)</sup> the demons enter the swine of the <i>Gergesenes</i> only with <i>Christ's</i> leave. To the second we answer that Lucian's <i>Philopseudes</i> is objected in vain, since by its very title it professes to lie. In vain also the <i>Marsi</i> and <i>Psylli</i>: what is reported of them, if true, ought to be attributed to nature rather than to magical power. I see that most of the ancients held this view. <i>Pliny</i> of the <i>Psylli</i>, book 7, ch. 2: <i>In their body there was an innate poison deadly to serpents, so that by its smell they put them to sleep.</i> And a little after: <i>A similar thing persists in</i> Italy <i>in the people of the</i> Marsi, <i>whom they say are descended from a son of</i> Circe, <i>and that for this reason this natural power is in them.</i> And Solinus: <i>That the people of the</i> Marsi <i>is unharmed by serpents is no wonder. They derive their descent from a son of</i> Circe, <i>and know that from their ancestral power the service of poisons is owed to them; therefore they despise poisons.</i> And elsewhere: <i>Beyond the</i> Garamantes <i>were the</i> Psylli, <i>fortified against noxious poison by an incredible firmness of body. They alone did not perish from the bites of snakes, and, though attacked by a lethal tooth, remained in unimpaired health.</i> So <i>Strabo</i>, book 17: Τοὺς Ψύλλους τοὺς πρὸς τῇ Κυρηναίᾳ φυσικήν τινα ἀντιπάθειαν ἔχειν πρὸς τὰ ἑρπετά [Tous Psyllous tous pros tē Kyrēnaia physikēn tina antipatheian echein pros ta herpeta]; <i>that the</i> Psylli <i>who are in</i> Cyrene <i>have a certain natural power against serpents.</i> And of the same <i>Lucan</i>, book 9, verse 894.

‎[III, 176] <i>Their very blood is safe, and can admit no poison</i> <i>even when the chant has ceased; the nature of the place</i> <i>has ordained that they be immune, though mingled with serpents.</i> <i>It has profited them to have set their dwelling amid poisons.</i> <i>Peace has been granted them with death. So great is their trust</i> <i>in their blood: when a little infant has dropped to the ground,</i> <i>fearing lest there be some admixture of foreign</i> Venus, <i>they test the doubtful offspring with the deadly asp.</i>

‎Around <i>Parium on the Hellespont</i> the same power was inborn in the <i>Ophiogenes</i>, of whom, after <i>Crates of Pergamum</i>, <i>Varro</i>, <i>Strabo</i>, <i>Pliny</i>, <i>Priscian</i>, etc. have written. What of the fact that <i>Celsus</i>, book 5, ch. 27, attributes nothing whatever to the <i>Psylli</i> beyond the common run of men, except <i>boldness, confirmed by practice itself?</i> As if the same had happened to them as to <i>Mithridates</i>, of whom the <i>Poet</i> says:

‎<i>By often drinking poison</i> Mithridates <i>brought it about</i> <i>that cruel venoms could not harm him.</i>

‎Moreover, although the <i>Arabs</i> have written that some serpents can be charmed, while others, which they call <i>deaf</i>, are wholly immune to incantation, it is nevertheless not necessary to refer the passages of <i>David</i>, <i>Solomon</i> and <i>Jeremiah</i> to this. For the holy <i>Prophets</i> nowhere distinguish serpents in this way. From their words you may gather only this: that magicians often attempt it in vain, whether they try to soothe and calm with their chant asps, or reguli, or any other kind of serpent. Therefore, for these passages of Scripture to hold true, there is no need to imagine that there are certain species of serpents against which the magicians' spells have no power. For in the same species of serpent the outcome can differ, according as God variously draws in or slackens the reins on the devil. Nor does the familiar demon, on whom the whole power of the incantation depends, always accommodate himself to the magician's nod. Hence it is that the magician's attempt does not always answer to his wish.
